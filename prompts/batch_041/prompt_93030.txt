A colleague of mine defends epistemicism about vagueness, and the analogy she leans on hardest is with blurred vision. On her telling, our predicates are like a camera whose lens smears edges. There is, she says, a perfectly sharp boundary in the world — some exact number of hairs at which a person stops being bald, some exact number of grains at which a heap begins — and vagueness consists wholly in our inability to resolve it. A borderline case is like the edge of a shadow seen through poor optics: the shadow does end somewhere, we simply cannot see where. Better eyes, or a better instrument, would see the line.

She puts the analogy to four jobs at once. First, it makes vagueness a species of ignorance rather than a failure of determinacy. Second, it preserves bivalence and classical logic for borderline cases, since every borderline predication is determinately true or determinately false, merely unknowably so. Third, it dissolves the sorites: each step of the argument is either true or false, and the felt pull of the paradox is an artefact of our not being able to tell which. Fourth, it implies that hard cases have determinate answers — a bylaw banning vehicles from a park either does or does not reach skateboards — and that a judge who is said to fix the line is only discovering where it already lay.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on epistemicism as such.

First, give me a correspondence table: for each element of the source — the scene and its edges, the lens, the blur itself, the observer, the improved instrument, and the fact that a blurred image is nonetheless a fully determinate distribution of light — say what in the target is meant to play that role, and flag any element of the target that has no counterpart in the source. Second, set out the disanalogies, ranked by how much they matter for the four uses above. I am especially interested in four candidates, and I want you to say of each whether it is a real defect or a cheap shot:

(a) Blur is itself a determinate state of affairs. A blurred image has a definite intensity at every point and is fully described by a point-spread function; the blurring is a fact of the same kind as the edge it obscures. But a sharpening of a vague predicate comes with no metric: nothing tells us how far a given sharpening departs from the truth, or how to rank two admissible sharpenings against each other. If the analogy is supposed to explain how the underlying fact could be determinate while our grasp of it is not, does the absence of any such measure for precisification matter?

(b) Blur is remediable, and the analogy advertises it as such. A better lens, a steadier hand, a longer exposure, and the edge appears. The ignorance the epistemicist needs is not of that kind: no improvement in our instruments or our powers of discrimination could locate the hair at which baldness gives out, and the view would be misdescribed if it promised otherwise. If the analogy predicts that better optics will help, has it conceded more than the view can afford? And if it does not predict that, what in the analogy was doing the work?

(c) Higher-order vagueness. A blurred edge has a definite blur profile, so there is a sharp fact about the degree of blur at every point. The picture seems to leave no room for the boundary between the clearly bald and the borderline being itself vague, nor for the boundary of that boundary. Is this a real gap, or does the analogy iterate gracefully?

(d) Whether the analogy simply assumes the disputed premise. It gets going only by granting that there is an edge for the lens to smear. How much of the argumentative work is done by the picture, and how much by that grant?

Third, tell me what the analogy smuggles in: which claims it presents as common sense that in fact carry the conclusion, and which of her four jobs would collapse if those claims were withheld. Fourth, and most important: once the disanalogies are on the table, which of the four conclusions survive, which were being carried by the analogy all along, and which could be had more cheaply by a different route?

Finally, I would like you to assess two rival pictures, and to improve on them or replace them if neither works. The first is the supervaluationist picture of a boundary that has not yet been drawn — an unsurveyed frontier, with a sentence counting as true just in case it comes out true on every admissible way of drawing the line. The second is the length of a coastline, which is not a blurred fact about a sharp fact but is not a single determinate fact at all, since it depends on the scale at which it is measured and no scale is privileged. For each rival picture, say what it captures that the blurred lens misses, what it gets wrong, and which of the four conclusions, if any, it would support. I do not need citations or reading suggestions; I want the argumentative anatomy.
