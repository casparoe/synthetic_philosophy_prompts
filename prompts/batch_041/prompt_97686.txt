I'm writing an essay on whether self-deception is even possible, and I want to use The Remains of the Day as my main test case. Stevens looks like the textbook self-deceiver — he narrates his own life in a way that keeps him from ever admitting what he wanted and what he gave up for the job — but the closer I look the less it seems like he holds two contradictory beliefs at once, and the more it seems like something else: a refusal to avow what he already knows, or a kind of pretense he keeps up in front of himself. So read the novel through the actual philosphy here — the paradox that self-deception seems to require knowing and not-knowing the same thing, and the deflationary accounts that try to dissolve the paradox (self-deception as pretense, as a failure of avowal, as an emotional rather than a doxastic state). Where does Stevens land? Which scenes should I be working with — the bantering, the missed chances with Miss Kenton, the way he handles his father's death, the way he talks about Lord Darlington near the end — and does the book dramatize a case the paradox view can't handle, or does it actually vindicate the idea that you can half-know and not-know at once? I want a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophy seriously, and that ends by saying what the novel adds to the debate that the arguments on their own don't.
