Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of phenomenal combination must satisfy. The target is narrower than panpsychism as a whole: assume, for the sake of argument, that micro-level entities have experiences or at least the categorical bases of experience, and ask what a theory must deliver to explain how some collection of these becomes the experience of a single subject. Treat that question, and not the prior question of whether micro-experience exists, as the area whose standard of adequacy you are specifying.

Present the requirements as a numbered list. For each, give the requirement stated precisely enough to be checkable, the case, datum, or argument that motivates it, and a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. Among the requirements you should consider, and may add to, revise, or reject:

1. Subject combination: the theory must yield one subject where there were many, rather than a mere aggregate of subjects or a subject plus its parts.
2. Quality and structure: it must explain how the determinate, localized qualities of micro-experiences produce a seamless phenomenal field with its own structure, rather than a sum of minute qualities or a smear with no internal articulation.
3. No brute emergence: it must avoid the very explanatory gap that motivates the position, without covertly reintroducing it under another name.
4. Delimitation: it must say which collections compose a subject. Every arbitrary set of micro-experiences, only causally integrated ones, only those with some further relation? And it must not license an absurd profusion of arbitrary or gerrymandered subjects.
5. Empirical fit: it must be consistent with what we know of unity and its failures, including cases where a single brain appears to sustain more than one stream of experience, cases where unity is graded or partial, and cases where experience is absent altogether.
6. Persistence and nesting: when micro-subjects combine, do they survive as components of a nested subject, are they extinguished, or are they transformed? Each answer carries costs, and the theory should own them.
7. Formality of the combination relation: is combination a relation or a process, all-or-nothing or graded, symmetric or asymmetric, transitive or not? Specify what the theory requires and what follows if the relation fails to hold between otherwise suitable parts.
8. Non-epiphenomenality: the theory must not leave macro-experience without a causal or explanatory role, or must argue that this does not matter.
9. No question-begging unification: the theory may not presuppose the unity of a subject in the machinery that is supposed to produce it.

To force the list to do work rather than summarize familiar positions, your requirements must return verdicts on two constructed cases. First, a system in which the combination relation holds between some micro-experiences and not others, so that one physical system momentarily hosts two partially overlapping macro-subjects. Second, a system whose constituents combine into one subject and are then redistributed so that the subject fuses with another and splits again. State what each requirement implies about these cases.

Then, and this is the part I most want: identify any pair of desiderata you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain the tension. Say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories. Close with a brief assessment of how constitutive phenomenal bonding, panprotopsychism or Russellian monism, cosmopsychism's decomposition strategy, and straightforward emergence fare against the list.

Argue from the requirements and the cases. Do not cite literature, and do not appeal to what any named author has said.
