I'm taking a historiography seminar and I'm stuck on this whole Whig history thing. As I understand it, the complaint is that Whig historians write the past as a story of progress toward the present, and that's supposed to be a fallacy because they judge the past by present-day standards and ignore contingency. But here's my problem: don't all historians write from the present? We can't exactly climb out of our own moment. And when a historian says slavery was wrong, or that the 1832 Reform Act was a step toward democracy, aren't they judging the past by present standards too? So isn't the critique of Whig history just a way of telling historians not to make moral judgments, or a way of pretending we can be neutral? I've been reading a bit about the 'presentism' debate in history -- the idea that all history is written for present concerns -- and it seems to me to vindicate my point.

I want to write a paper arguing that Whig history is unavoidable, and maybe that the classic critique is itself Whig history: it tells a story of historians gradually overcoming bias and getting more sophisticated, which is exactly a progress narrative. My professor says I'm missing something, but she won't tell me what. Can you help me see where the confusion is, and whether there's definately a defensible version of my argument? I'd especially like to know what the Whig fallacy is supposed to be, if it's not just 'using present-day categories' or 'having a moral point of view.'
