I've been chewing on the old evidence problem on and off for like a year and I think I've finally landed on a view, so I want someone to try and break it.

Quick version of the problem, for anyone who needs it: if you already know E, then learning that your theory T explains E can't raise your credence in T, because E is already at probability 1, so the likelihood ratio is 1. Mercury's perihelion is the stock example -- the anomaly was known for decades before general relativity accounted for it, and everyone treats that as a big win for GR.

The move I used to like is the logical-uncertainty one: the agent isn't certain of its own derivations, so "T entails E" is itself news, and it can raise credence. Fine, I get it. But I dont think that's where the action is anymore.

My actual view: the old evidence problem is real, it isn't about p=1, and it's basically overfitting. A theory built or patched to fit data you already had is like a model trained on its training set -- its "prediction" of that data is nearly worthless, because the data went into making it. A theory that stuck its neck out on something nobody had looked at yet is like a model that generalizes to a held-out set. Standard Bayesianism is blind to the difference, because it only looks at fit, not at the causal history of how the hypothesis got built. So confirmation should be sensitive to how much the hypothesis was set up to fit, not just to how well it fits.

What counts against this? I want the real hits, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a defender would concede rather than dismiss. In particular:

- Any historical case where a theory was pure accommodation, no novel prediction, and yet clearly earned a big confirmation bump. And if the reply is always "well, ruling out the alternatives was itself informative", is the view doing any work at all?

- Does it just collapse back into ordinary Bayesianism? Someone will say: conditionalize on the fact that you came up with T after seeing E, i.e. put the discovery process itself into your evidence, and then standard Bayes gives you the penalty for free. If that's right, is my view just a heuristic about being careful what counts as evidence? And is that a problem, or fine?

- Timing. Two agents with the same evidence end up with different credences because of when they heard E relative to when they built T. That feels like a bug -- unless the timing is itself evidence about the process. Which way does it cut?

- Can the penalty even be specified, or is any "discount for construction" an arbitrary fudge with no principled prior behind it? If it can't be specified, is the view unfalsifiable, and is that fatal or just a limitation?

- Does the overfitting analogy actually hold, or is it carrying the argument for me? ML has a fixed distribution and a ground truth to generalize to; science has neither, and the hypothesis space keeps growing. If the analogy breaks, does the position survive without it?

- Does it predict anything its rivals dont, or has it been qualified into "confirmation is just Bayes done carefully"?

Give me an honest read at the end on how insulated this is, and if it is, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind. What would move me most is one clean case of pure accommodation, no novel prediction, where we still think the confirmation was legit and not just "it happened to be the only game in town".
