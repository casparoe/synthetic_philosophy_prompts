I'm a PhD student in political theory (I hang out with the ethics people alot) and two people in my cohort, Dana and Marcus, have been going at each other for a week over Nozick's views on animals. I can't definately tell who's winning and I'd like you to referee it move by move.

Setup: in Anarchy, State, and Utopia Nozick says almost nothing about animals — a couple of paragraphs at most, tucked into the rights discussion. What he seems to say is that people get Kantian side-constraints (you can't use them as mere means, even for good aggregate outcomes) while animals get utilitarian treatment (their interests can be aggregated and traded off). I've seen it summarized as utilitarian treatment for animals, Kantian treatment for people. I don't have the book in front of me so take my reconstruction with a grain of salt — if I'm misremembering his position, say so.

Dana's opening: the asymmetry can't survive. What grounds the Kantian constraint, on any standard reading, is rational agency — the capacity to be a source of ends, to choose, to make claims. Infants and people with severe cognitive disabilities don't have that, at least not currently. So Nozick has three options: (1) say those humans only get utilitarian treatment, which he clearly doesn't want; (2) extend the Kantian constraint to at least the cognitively comparable animals, which he doesn't do; or (3) ground the constraint in something other than current capacities. She says he never does (3), so the position is just unstable.

Marcus: that's the standard marginal-cases objection and it assumes the grounding has to be current capacities. Nozick could say the constraint attaches to all members of a species whose typical members have the capacity, or to beings with the potential for it, or to beings already inside a community of mutual recognition. Marginal humans are inside because they're one of us, not because they pass a test.

Dana: those are all rescue moves. Species-typical capacity is speciesism with a nicer name — why should a typical capacity protect an individual who lacks it? Potentiality proves too much, since fetuses have it and arguably some animals do in some sense. And one of us is relational, not a capacity, which means Nozick's Kantianism isn't about autonomy at all; the slogan is just a description of who we happen to care about.

Marcus: maybe the slogan is descriptive rather than a defended theory. Maybe the boundary is vague, like bald. Vagueness doesn't make a boundary arbitrary — no sharp line, but clear cases on each side and marginal cases in the penumbra. That's fine.

Dana: vagueness works for bald because the underlying facts form a continuum and nothing practical hangs on the line. Here the stakes are whether you can be used without consent. Vagueness at that level is a license for whatever we already do. And the penumbra isn't symmetric: marginal humans are clearly inside for Nozick and cognitively comparable animals are clearly outside, so it's not vagueness, it's a discontinuity dressed up as one.

Marcus: then the discontinuity could be grounded in something other than capacities. The moral community is a cooperative scheme of mutual protection and animals aren't participants. Marginal humans are protected because others represent them in the scheme; animals can be represented too, by humans who care about them. So this reading actually gets some animals inside the Kantian wall. I don't think Nozick says this, but it's a direction his view could go.

Can you go through this move by move: who's right about what, where each of them is missing something, and what the strongest version of Nozick's position looks like once you patch it? And tell me how confident you are in each call, singling out the point you're least sure about.
