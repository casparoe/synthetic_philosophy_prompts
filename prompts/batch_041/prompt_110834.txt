Bit of an unusual request, but here goes. I'm in a philosophy of science seminar that spent a week on truthlikeness — Popper's idea that even a false theory can be closer to the truth than another false theory, and the 1974 result (Tichý and Miller, I think) that wrecked his content-based definition, since you can't crank up a false theory's stock of true consequences without cranking up its false ones too. I wrote a short essay for the seminar arguing that we should stop trying to repair the definition and instead relativise the whole notion, and my supervisor said it's either trivially right or trivially wrong and I should definately work out which before I present it. I don't want reading suggestions or references, I've got the reading list. What I want is someone to tell me whether the central move survives contact, which parts are hand-waving, and whether the toy example is doing real work or just decorating. Here's the essay, roughly as it'll be handed in:

Let a theory be the set of possible worlds it permits. Now suppose the community also supplies a partition of logical space into cells, where a cell is a complete answer to a question the community cares about. For a theory T and a cell C, say T rules out C if nothing in C is permitted by T, rules in C if everything in C is permitted by T, and is silent about C otherwise (it permits some but not all of C). Give T a score: +1 for every cell it correctly rules out or rules in, −1 for every cell it incorrectly rules out or rules in, and 0 for silence. Verisimilitude is then the two-place score(T, Q) rather than a property of T alone. That's the whole thesis, and I claim the old impossibility results are just what you get when you insist on a ranking that doesn't move when the questions move.

Toy example. Three valves, each open or shut, so eight worlds. Truth: A open, B and C shut. T1 = A is open. T2 = A and B are open (false). T3 = at least two are open (false). T0 = the tautology. On a coarse partition Q1 whose cells are given by how many valves are open (cell sizes 1, 3, 3, 1): T1 = +1, T2 = 0, T3 = 0, T0 = 0. Refine to Q2, the eight singleton worlds: T1 = +4, T2 = +4, T3 = +2, T0 = 0. Same theories, same world, same truth; T2 goes from strictly worse than T1 to level with it, purely because the questions got finer. The complete truth scores +7 on Q2 and the tautology scores 0, which I take as sanity checks.

Three things I already see and don't know what to do with. First, the measure pays for exclusions rather than assertions — T1's only point on Q1 came from ruling out the all-shut world — and silence is never punished, so a theory that says nothing is never worse than 0, which means a bold false theory can end up ranked level with the tautology (T2 and T3 do exactly that on Q1). I'd fix it by weighting cells, but I can't see how to do that without smuggling the grain back in. Second, someone will say I've just reinvented accuracy scores over a partition and stuck Popper's word on top, in which case the interesting claim isn't about truthlikeness at all. Third, on the finest partition a false but specific theory can catch up with a true one (T2 ties T1), which looks like the old trivialization problem reappearing in miniature, and if it's the same problem then I've moved it rather than dodged it.

So: which of those three is fatal and which is a feature I should lean into? Is "verisimilitude is two-place" a real thesis, or just the truism that any comparison needs a space of alternatives? Is the toy rigged — I picked the actual world myself, and I'm worried that choice is doing the work the definition is supposed to be doing. And give me the strongest objection from someone who thinks truthlikeness has to be an objective, grain-invariant property of a theory, then tell me honestly whether I can answer it or only restate my position louder. Be blunt, I'd rather find out now than in front of the seminar.
