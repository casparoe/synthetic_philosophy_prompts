I need a poem, and the form has to be a round: a canon for four voices, each entering a line later than the one before, all four lines sounding together by the end.

The subject is what happens to a verdict about a work of art when it is handed from one person to another. Some things about a work travel without loss. That the quartet is in four movements, that it runs twenty minutes, that the slow movement returns to the opening theme: say these to me and I have them, as good as if I had heard the thing myself. Other things do not travel. That it is heartbreaking, that it is beautiful, that it is worth the evening: say these to me and I receive a sentence I can repeat without having acquired anything the sentence is about, and I can go on repeating it indefinitely, which is more or less what I do. A round is the right form for this because of something it does on its own. Every voice ends up singing every line, first-person lines included, so that by the fourth entry the words of someone who was there are coming out of the mouths of people who were not. Do not leave that as an accident of the form. Write the lines so that the stacking is the argument.

What the poem should carry, without ever using the vocabulary of the subject, and without naming any philosopher, any theory, or any actual work of art:

- the line between what can be passed along and what cannot, and the fact that the line does not fall where you would expect it to;
- the double face of a verdict, which is at once a claim about the thing and a report of a response, so that one face ought to travel as easily as a fact about the movements and the other cannot travel at all;
- the verdict I keep on file after standing in front of the thing and feeling nothing: my friend's judgment retained like an unpaid debt, together with the question of whether the failure was the work's, the friend's, or mine, and the further fact that the kept verdict still functions as a reason to go back and try again;
- the way a second-hand verdict grows louder with each retelling while its warrant stays exactly where it started.

Formal requirements. Four lines, no more. Each line has to stand as a sentence on its own and also answer the line before it, since in performance it will be heard against all the others at once. The whole thing has to be singable by people with no training. The line you choose to enter last should be the one that, once it is in, makes the earlier lines sound like something other than what they sounded like alone. The poem itself should stay out of the dispute it is about: no verdict on whether a verdict can be transmitted, no moral about credulity, nothing that resolves the question either way.

Leave it open at the end. I do not want the round to settle whether the last voice has earned the claim it is making or is merely echoing it. The poem should finish with the claim sounding at full strength and the warrant still missing, so that a philosopher who has argued about this for years can say which way the form leans, while someone who has never thought about it once hears only a good song. Close with a sentence or two on which line you had enter last, and why that order is the right one.
