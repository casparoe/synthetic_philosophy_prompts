I retired about four years ago after 35 years as a geologist (mostly basin stratigraphy, a lot of it on old drill cores), and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group at the university extension. We've drifted onto infinity and I'm out of my depth in a very enjoyable way. I need a handout.

Here's what I want. A decision tree that takes someone from a starting commitment to a named position on actual infinity. If you think a quiz with a scoring key works better for this, do that instead, but the tree is what I picture. The trick is that it has to sort people by what they say about cases, not by which -ism they'd tick on a survey. So every node has to be a question somebody can answer without already knowing where it leads. No node that just asks 'do you believe in actual infinities?' — that's the thing we're trying to get behind.

I keep informally sorting us into three: infinity as a property of an object you can quantify over, infinity as a property of a process you can always continue, and infinity as a rule of thumb for generating things you'll never finish. If the tree can start somewhere near that and split each branch, good. If my three are wrong, say where they break.

The people I want the tree to be able to seperate, described by what they actually say rather than by a label:

- The one who says 'endless' only ever means 'name any number and there's a bigger one', and won't hear it as a claim about a completed thing.
- The one who is fine with a completed totality but balks at Hilbert's hotel — thinks that if you move everybody up a room, the hotel is now short a room's worth of guests, i.e. a part can't be the same size as the whole.
- The one who accepts the pairing criterion for 'same size' but then asks what these infinite things are made of, and is not joking.
- The one who thinks the whole question is about language, and that 'there are infinitely many primes' is a shorthand for a procedure that never gets contradicted.
- The one who says there is no single universe of sets, so 'how many reals are there' has no settled answer to give.
- The one who thinks there is no completed infinity anywhere, but holds that space and time are infinitely divisible. I think that combination is real and it's the one I find hardest, so I want it as a leaf even if it makes the tree lopsided.
- The one who allows infinity only for things you can build, not for things you can merely prove must exist.

Cases I want to be the actual content of the nodes, since I think our disagreements are about cases and not definitions: Hilbert's hotel with the doubling trick that leaves all the odd rooms empty; pairing the naturals off against the perfect squares; the fact that no number is the largest; the diagonal argument on the set of all subsets of the naturals; a hotel with one room for every real number, where you cannot shuffle anyone to make room; and a rock you keep cutting in half, where the question is whether the halves run out before the cuts do.

Each leaf should carry the name of the position if it has one, or 'unnamed' if it doesn't, plus one sentence on that leaf's most awkward commitment. Flag any two leaves your tree can't really separate, and note any combination of answers that nobody seems to occupy, with a short guess at why — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Ten leaves or fewer, please. Audience is our group: two biology grad students, a retired judge, a high school math teacher, and me, so keep it short enough to fit on two sides of a page.

One last thing: don't just hand me the standard textbook spread of -isms. I can get that from the encyclopedia. The value here is in the questions.
