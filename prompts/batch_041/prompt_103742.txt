Suppose Jeremy Bentham could be briefed on the modern World Happiness Report — an annual ranking of countries by average self-reported life satisfaction on the Cantril ladder, with national scores decomposed into six explanatory factors (GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity, and perceptions of corruption). Would he accept this as a measurement of happiness in his own hedonic sense, given that it averages individuals within a nation rather than attending to the "extent" dimension of his felicific calculus? Would he think a government should try to move its country up the ranking, and if so, by which levers? How would he adjudicate the tension between maximizing aggregate pleasure and his formula of the greatest happiness of the greatest number, when the two pull apart — for instance, when a policy raises the mean while widening the spread? And where would his hedonism put him on the dispute over whether self-reports measure felt pleasure or merely reflect culturally shaped expectations? Answer as exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion; state your confidence in each claim and identify the single point you are least sure of.
