I moderate a large online discussion group about politics — a few thousand members, three volunteer moderators — and the most common complaint we receive is that someone is arguing in bad faith. I have no training in philosophy or psychology; everything I know about this I have worked out from years of watching these fights, and I am tired of inventing a standard from scratch each time one breaks out. I would like you to write a decision procedure that my moderators and I can actually run when such a complaint arrives.

Here is what I have concluded so far, and where I get stuck. The people we most want to remove often sound entirely sincere, while some of the smoothest talkers are plainly performing, so sincerity cannot be the test. We cannot see anyone's motives — only what they do when they are handed evidence and objections — yet if I judge only by behavior I fear I am simply labeling as bad faith anyone who will not agree with me. Some members who say outrageous things turn out to be repeating what an entirely different community told them was true; they are not lying so much as sealed inside a different picture of the world, and I cannot decide whether that should count as bad faith or as something else. A member recently asked me how to tell a belief that is merely well defended from one that has been arranged so that nothing could ever count against it, and I had no answer. And when I run the test honestly it seems to indict a great deal of what my own side does, which makes me wonder whether the procedure should be symmetrical or whether that is naive.

Please give me the procedure as a sequence of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns a no, and where it hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of values rather than evidence. Distinguish a single bad-faith move from a person who is habitually in bad faith, and say which of the two the procedure is entitled to judge. Mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Assume a moderator has ten minutes and no expertise, and that I have the final say. Then run it on three complaints: a member who demands sources, dismisses every one offered, and insists he is only being rigorous; a member who posts relentlessly one-sided material but appears to be genuinely trying and whom people mainly find tiresome; and a member who made one inflammatory claim, apologized, and whom the group now wants gone. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I will not like. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do.
