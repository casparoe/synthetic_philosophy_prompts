I'm a teaching assistant for a second-year philosophy of mind course, and last week's seminar on Hofstadter's dialogue "Prelude… Ant Fugue" went badly. You may recall the setup: Achilles, the Tortoise, the Crab, and the Anteater talk about fugues and about wholes and parts, and the Anteater — who earns his living repairing nervous disorders in anthills by pulling individual ants out of them — explains that Aunt Hillary, the colony, converses with him by writing in the pheromone trails her ants leave, that the ants themselves are stupid and know nothing of the conversation, and that a colony can be killed outright by a rainstorm that drowns not a single ant, because what dies is the pattern rather than the tissue. One of my students asked the obvious question — whether an anthill can be a patient — and I answered with three minutes of hand-waving about levels of description. I want to atone by writing the handout for next week myself, and I've decided the honest way to do it is to write the thing that people in that world would actually have: not a treatise but a piece of institutional prose.

So: draft a section of a clinical practice handbook, the sort of document a professional body circulates to its members, for practitioners who treat anthills. Invent the professional body, the handbook, and the editor; attribute nothing to any real person or institution, and cite nothing. Write it entirely straight — deadpan, second person, addressed to the practitioner, organised as numbered recommendations each followed by a short rationale — at roughly 800 words. The humour has to come out of the clinical content, so get the following features of the case right and let them collide: the handbook must treat the colony as the patient (it has a case history, it relapses, it is discharged, it dies) while conceding that its tissues are ants and that the ants are not the patient; that the patient survives the complete turnover of its parts; that its memories are stored outside its body, in trails, where any passer-by can read them; that a routine intervention for the colony is a lethal injury for the ant it removes; that the practitioner's own approach produces a greeting, which one chapter must record as a symptom and another as an expression; and that consent can only be obtained by reading trails which the practitioner's own presence has partly written. Where the guideline cannot resolve something, let it do what real guidelines do — a scope limitation, a footnote, a sentence beginning "this document takes no position on". Two further constraints. First, the body of the handbook must never use the words mind, consciousness, or experience; where a practitioner would need them, it must write around them, and the only place those words appear is a short dissenting annotation by a named (fictional) practitioner, whose use of them the majority evidently regards as unprofessional. Second, do not let the handbook say that the ants think, and do not let it say that the colony is merely a manner of speaking either — if it flirts with the latter, it must do so in the register of professional convenience, as a statement about what is clinically useful rather than about what is true, which is the more damning version.

After the handbook section, give me a JSON object, and nothing after it, with exactly these fields: "target" (string: the practice or position the piece satirises, one sentence); "feature_exaggerated" (string: the real feature of the original thought experiment that the handbook magnifies); "levels" (array of two or three objects, each with "level" and "same_event_reads_as", describing how one and the same clinical event looks at a different level of description); "professional_convenience" (string: the single sentence in which the handbook trades truth for usefulness); "dissent" (object with "practitioner" and "objection"); "refused_question" (string: the question the handbook explicitly declines to settle); and "specialist_verdict" (object with "fair" as a boolean and "why" as one sentence). The JSON should be valid and should quote lines from your own handbook where the schema calls for it.
