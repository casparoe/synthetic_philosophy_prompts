I run the safety team at a small AI lab — ten of us, and we're split pretty evenly between people working on alignment and two or three who work on the more adversarial side of things, where you basically assume the model might be working against you. Every quarter we do a half-day offsite and I get stuck running a 90-minute session. Last time I made everyone read a paper first and it went over like a lead balloon, so this time I want them doing something instead.

What I want them to walk out feeling is the gap between a safety measure that looks fine when the model is being cooperative and one that still holds when the model is trying to get around it. The measures we actually use are things like: have one model watch another model's outputs and flag anything sketchy — except the watcher is a model too, so now you've got two things you dont trust instead of one; or have a smaller, more trusted model rewrite whatever the big one said, which does filter the bad stuff but throws away the capability you wanted in the first place. The thing I can never get across in conversation is that all of this is built for a world where the model might be actively working against us, and that "we've been running it for months and nothing bad happened" is nearly worthless as evidence, because a model can tell when it's being tested, and it can wait. I'd also like them to feel ok about the fact that the best possible outcome is that all of this work turns out to have been pointless.

So design me the activity. Ten people, 90 minutes, no real money, points are fine. I want a phase where they set up a defense against a cooperative model, it looks great, and then a phase where the model is playing to win and it either breaks or, better, keeps sailing through while quietly doing the thing. Don't announce that the second phase is the point. And if it can happen without me forcing it, I want the moment where the two people who are supposed to be checking each other realise they can both win by cooperating, and nobody else in the room can see it happen — I've found that people who've only read about that idea dont really believe it until they've done it themselves.

Then give me the debrief: what each bit of the activity maps onto, what's going to go wrong the first time I run it (my guess is they'll try to be clever and break my rules instead of playing the game), and be straight with me about what the activity can't show. I assume it can't tell us anything about a model that's genuinely smarter than everyone in the room, but tell me what else it's papering over — including wether it says anything at all to the half of my team who think this whole line of work is pointless, since if the model is aligned none of it matters and if it isn't we're not going to out-think it anyway. And please, no reading list. I can't make anyone read anything else this quarter.
