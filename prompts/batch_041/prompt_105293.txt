For several decades the question of whether philosophy is of any use to science has been a recurring skirmish between working physicists and philosophers, and it shows no sign of resolution. The physicists point to the absence of any physical result that was actually produced by philosophical argument, and to the conceptual muddle they say philosophers import into live research; the philosophers reply that the concepts the physicists rely on — simultaneity, field, probability, symmetry, the vacuum — were and remain philosophically contested, and that the physicists' own methodological pronouncements are themselves philosophy of an unexamined kind. My suspicion is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about a single proposition: one is making a claim about the causal history of physics (whether physics would have reached its present state without the philosophy), the other a claim about the rational reconstruction of physics (whether its concepts can be made sense of without philosophy), and both claims could be true at once. Is that suspicion right? Diagnose the disagreement — verbal, substantive, or two different questions travelling under one name — and say what, if anything, would count as progress.
