I'm in a grad seminar on the epistemology of testimony and I keep bouncing between the two standard camps without ever landing, so I'm going to just write out my actual gut verdicts on a pile of cases and have you tell me what I actually believe. Here they are. (1) In a city I've never been to, I ask a stranger at a bus stop which way the river is and I believe her on the spot; no track record for her, no way to check, and it doesn't feel like I'm being sloppy. (2) Same trip, a different stranger volunteers that the guy who runs my hostel is a thief; I don't believe it, and not because I have evidence the hostel guy is honest -- the accusation just doesn't get in. (3) My doctor, who's been right about me for fifteen years, tells me my thyroid numbers mean something, and I believe her even though I can't evaluate the claim at all. (4) Someone with a big reputation in one field says something sweeping in a field he's clearly wandered into from the outside, and I notice I believe him anyway, and I think I shouldn't have. (5) A four-year-old is told by her mother that there are invisible germs on her hands and believes it, and I can't see that she's being epistemically careless, even though she has nothing like a track record to go on. (6) I believe there's a country called Uruguay and that its capital is Montevideo, purely because I've been told so my whole life by people who were themselves just told, and I'd say I know it. (7) A forecaster whose predictions I've actually checked for years tells me it'll rain and I believe it; but the same forecaster, on a day I know he's got a book coming out, tells me a huge storm is coming, and I discount it heavily even though his track record is unchanged. (8) Someone from a community I know nothing about tells me in detail what his community believes about the afterlife, and I believe him about what they believe but not about the afterlife itself. (9) An anonymous commenter on a forum gives me a specific-sounding fact about a niche subject I happen to know well, and I reject it instantly -- not because of any track record, just because it clashes with what I already think I know.

What I want is the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that gets all nine, and if none does, which verdict has to go and why. My own half-formed version is basically: default to believing people unless something specific points the other way, where the something-specific can be about the speaker or about how the claim sits with the rest of what I think I know. I already suspect that's smuggling in two seperate rules and that cases (1) and (2) are what will break whatever I say, since they're both a single stranger, one shot, nothing to check, and I go opposite ways. I'd also like you to say what your repaired principle predicts about one case I haven't listed: I'm at a party, a stranger tells me confidently and with no hedging that my close friend was badmouthing me last week -- do I believe him, on your account, and does that verdict feel right or does it show the principle needs another clause? Last thing, since I have to write a response paper on this: is there anything left of the reductionist idea that testimony has to earn its keep from non-testimonial evidence, or does my (6) about Uruguay just kill it outright? My roommate thinks I'm overthinking all of this, which is probably true, but I'd rather have the principle nailed down.
