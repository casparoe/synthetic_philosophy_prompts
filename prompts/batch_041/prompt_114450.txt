Next month I'm giving a talk at a workshop hosted by a history of science department, and I'm the odd one out — I spent six years as an ML engineer before moving into AI governance, and the organizer invited me because he thinks I'll be a useful provocation. The room is basically all historians of science and technology: a couple of STS people, one whose entire career is early modern print and the book trade, one economic historian who works on the British Industrial Revolution and has opinions about the Great Divergence, and an emeritus who I'm told thinks "technology" as a causal agent is a category error. My thesis is that the printing press is the right analogy for transformative AI and nuclear weapons is the wrong one, but not for the reason people usually give. The usual reason — information abundance — seems like bad history to me: what actually mattered about print was that it made a text *stable*, so the same words could be checked and cited in many places at once, and the real effect was to move the bottleneck from producing texts to trusting them. I want to say AI does the same thing one level up, that the nuclear case misleads because its central fact is a decision not to use the thing, i.e. a non-event, and that you can't generalize from a non-event. I'm also going to say the Industrial Revolution is the worst of the three analogies, on the grounds that the period is contested to the point where the analogy has no determinate referent. And I'm going to propose a criterion for when an analogy is carrying a mechanism rather than a mood, which I've been calling the no-peeking test: you have to be able to state the mechanism in terms that a contemporary of the earlier episode could have verified without already knowing how it turned out. I suspect the criterion is the thing they'll actually go after, and I'm not confident it survives contact with a room of historians. I have twenty minutes and then the rest is Q&A.
