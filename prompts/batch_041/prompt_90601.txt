A colleague in my game theory reading group has produced the following argument, and I have been unable to locate its fault. I set it out as he does, with my numbering.

(1) A rational player never plays a strategy that is strictly dominated, and this holds whether the dominating strategy is a pure strategy or a mixture of strategies: a strictly dominated strategy is not a best response to any belief whatever about the other players' play.

(2) Mixed dominance is not a cosmetic extension of the idea. In the matrix below, which gives only the row player's payoffs, the middle row M is strictly dominated by the even mixture of T and B, although no pure strategy dominates it:

  L   C   R
T  3   0   3
M  1   1   1
B  0   3   3

(3) So we may strike out strictly dominated strategies and repeat the operation on the smaller game. The order of the deletions cannot matter, for the following reason. If a strategy is dominated at some stage, then the strategy or mixture that dominates it is at least as good as it against every profile of the opponents' surviving strategies — strictly better against every such profile in the strict case. Deleting further strategies only removes some of those profiles; it cannot manufacture a profile against which the struck-out strategy beats its dominator. Nothing deleted could therefore have been part of a rational outcome, and the same strategies survive however the deletions are ordered.

(4) Now nothing in (3) turns on the word "strictly". Run the same argument with "weakly dominated" in place of "strictly dominated" — that is, with strategies that are never better than some alternative and sometimes worse — and every clause goes through verbatim. A weakly dominated strategy is never strictly better than its dominator against any profile, so a player who drops it gives up nothing; and by the reasoning of (3) the deletion cannot destroy the domination on which it rested.

(5) Hence iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies is also path-independent, and "the" set of weakly rationalisable strategies is well defined, just as in the strict case. Since (2) shows that mixtures genuinely extend the reach of dominance, this conclusion carries over to elimination by mixtures as well.

(6) It follows that whenever a strategy is weakly dominated — even if it is dominated only by a mixture, and even if its only shortfall is a tie against a single profile — it may be struck out, and the outcome of the procedure is independent of the order in which we strike things out.

I am fairly confident that (6) is false, since the standard textbooks insist that weak dominance is order-dependent; but I cannot find where the argument breaks, and I would sooner keep (1)–(3) than (6). Tell me which step to reject. Explain why that step looked so plausible, and what is paid by rejecting it — in particular, what becomes of the claim that a rational player must never play a weakly dominated strategy, and what, if anything, takes the place of the tidy path-independence result once (5) is given up. If you judge that the trouble lies not in a single step but in what (3) is quietly assuming, or in the work it is being asked to do, say so and say what the repair costs.

State where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what would change your mind: which further consideration would lead you to reject a different step, or none.

End with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" that names the step you reject and gives your reason in a few words.
