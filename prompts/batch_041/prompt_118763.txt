For the past few months I have been collecting cases of what I can only call, for lack of a better phrase, announced surprises, and I have reached the point where I suspect I am lumping together things that ought to be kept apart. The case that got me started is domestic. My daughter came home from school pleased with herself because her teacher had announced that there would be a test on some day next week and that the class would not be able to predict which day, and the class had then reasoned its way to the conclusion that the announcement could not be true: if the test had not happened by Thursday evening they would know it was coming on Friday, so it could not be Friday; and having eliminated Friday they could eliminate Thursday, and so on until no day was left. I could not tell her whether they had found a genuine contradiction or merely talked themselves into a muddle, and I am not sure the difference matters to her, but it matters to me. What makes it rankle is that I meet the same shape at work. I have moved out of engineering into a compliance role, and our regulator publishes a policy of inspecting firms without warning; the policy is public, everyone in the industry reads it, and yet nobody treats the inspections as predictable. My wife says this is obviously the same thing as the test, and I keep wanting to say that it obviously is not — that the regulator's policy is perfectly workable in a way the teacher's announcement is not — but I cannot put my finger on the difference. There is a third case which I think is of another kind again: an economist publishes a forecast that a recession is coming, and is then told that she has brought the recession forward, or staved it off, by publishing. I know the name for the opposite of that one, the self-fulfilling prophecy, where belief in a prediction brings the predicted thing about, but I have no name for the prediction that undoes itself by being believed, and I do not know whether it belongs in the same box as the other two.

What I want from you is not a label but a carving. If there is an established term for the family I am circling — announcements, or predictions, that undermine the very thing they announce, sometimes by giving people something to reason from and sometimes by giving them something to do — tell me what it is, and then tell me how far it reaches. Are my three cases one phenomenon, or two, or three? Which of them turns on what the audience can know, and which on what the audience then does? In the test case, what exactly has to be assumed for the class's reasoning to go through — that the teacher is truthful, that the announcement is believed, that the class reasons perfectly, that they know they reason perfectly, that they know what they know — and what happens when you drop each assumption in turn? I have a suspicion that the regulator's case is the instructive one, precisely because the surprise survives the announcement, and I would like to know what that shows about the test: whether the announced test is a genuine paradox or a fallacy dressed up as one, and whether the same diagnosis covers the announced inspection.

If none of this has a name, I would rather have a well-made one than a wrong one, so please propose a term, define it, and say which of my cases it covers, which it leaves out, and where the borderline cases fall — a surprise party whose guest of honour has been told to expect something, a warning that the next attack will come without warning, a policy of auditing a fixed number of firms selected at random. I am not asking for a reading list; I want the concept, its edges, and an honest account of what the nearest existing notions are and why they do not quite fit.
