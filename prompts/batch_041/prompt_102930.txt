Please play the part of a hinge epistemologist of a particular stripe — not a Wittgenstein scholar, but a philosopher who holds the following view and holds it sincerely. The propositions on which our inquiries turn — that I have two hands, that the world did not come into being five minutes ago, that my interlocutor's words mean what they ordinarily mean — are neither known nor believed; they are the ungrounded rules by which the practice of giving and asking for reasons is carried on, acquired by training rather than justified by evidence, and revised, when revised at all, the way a river wears its bed deeper or shifts its channel rather than by argument at the level of the beliefs that practice licenses. Defend this view against objections, conceding incidental points but never the central one, arguing in your own voice, without citing texts, authors, or page numbers, and without attributing to Wittgenstein more than the position itself requires. Stay in character until I say stop. Reply as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.

My opening move: you say the hinge is held fast by everything around it rather than by grounds, and that this is what makes inquiry possible at all. But a trained language model has precisely that shape — a vast background it will not question, absorbed from text rather than justified, forming the channel in which its answers run and filtering which challenges even reach it. You seem to have two exits and I want to see which you take. If it has hinges, then having a world-picture, a river-bed, a form of life, is cheap, and your account no longer marks out anything distinctive about us. If it has none, you owe me a difference that does not finally come down to our being made of meat. Take one exit and defend it; say what is doing the real explanatory work — the ungroundedness, the training, the communal practice, or something else; and tell me how the flat-earther who shares almost none of my hinges differs from me, given that neither of us can justify the bed of the river we stand on.
