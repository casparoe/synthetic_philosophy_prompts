I teach the free will and moral responsibility unit in a graduate seminar, and every year my students can recite Galen Strawson's Basic Argument from memory without being able to say which step of it they actually reject. I want a decision tree that forces the choice.

Take the argument as having this spine: (i) you act as you do because of how you are; (ii) so being ultimately responsible for what you do would require being ultimately responsible for how you are; (iii) being ultimately responsible for how you are would in turn require being responsible for the process that made you that way, and so on without end — the only way to stop the series being a self that caused itself, which is impossible; (iv) therefore no one is ultimately responsible for what they do; and (v) ultimate responsibility is what basic-desert moral responsibility requires — the kind that would make someone deserve blame or praise just for having done the thing, apart from any forward-looking justification.

Build me a flowchart whose starting point is acceptance of step (i) as stated, and whose internal nodes are the argument's own steps and presuppositions. Each node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer about her own view without already knowing which leaf it leads to. I want at least one node on each of the following, though you should add whatever else the logic requires: whether "because of how you are" is being read causally (your character pushes you) or constitutively (your act just is the exercise of your character); whether the regress needs a principle licensing the move from "not responsible for the source" to "not responsible for the outcome," and whether that principle is a logical truth or a substantive moral claim; whether step (v) is a report of what ordinary blaming presupposes or a stipulation; and whether the stopping point is a fact about the agent, a fact about our practices of holding one another responsible, or a fact about how the word "responsible" is used.

I also want two nodes that work by case rather than by principle, so that people who talk past each other get sorted:

- The engineered agent. A person's entire character was designed and installed by a team of neuroscientists. She now deliberates, cares about reasons, endorses her desires, and does something cruel. Ask whether this agent's responsibility status differs from that of an ordinary cruel person, and if so whether the difference is one of degree.
- The self-revised agent. A person spent decades deliberately rebuilding her character from cruel to kind, using nothing but ordinary effort, and now acts kindly. Ask whether the fact that she made herself matters, given that she did not make the self that did the making.

The leaves should include, at minimum: the position that the argument is sound and no one is ever responsible in the desert-involving sense; the position that the argument is sound about ultimacy but that ultimacy was an empty notion, so nothing is lost; the position that the conclusion is correct and responsibility-talk should be kept but rebuilt around forward-looking purposes; the position that an action can be the agent's own because it expresses her evaluative stance, with no regress ever arising; the position that the agent must be able to answer for the action, where answering is a matter of being responsive to reasons rather than of having authored herself; agent causation, where a substance causes without being caused to cause; self-forming action, where some of what the agent is results from her own undetermined effort and the regress therefore terminates in something she did rather than something that merely happened to her; the position that character and action are not two separable items, so the regress never gets a foothold; the position that the demand for ultimacy belongs to philosophers rather than to the practice of blaming; and the position that the self is a process or bundle with no owner, so the question who made the self is ill-formed. Leave room for a leaf that no one occupies — an agent that literally caused itself in the relevant respect — and say why it is empty: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

For each leaf, give one sentence stating what it concedes and what it denies, one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, and one line saying how it handles each of the two cases above. Note explicitly which single node, if a reader answers it the other way, moves the most readers to a different leaf. Flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate, and say whether the difference between them is substantive or verbal. Where a leaf has a standard label, use it, but write the position out in plain prose first and do not include citations, dates, reading lists, or the names of defenders.
