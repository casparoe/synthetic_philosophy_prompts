One of my colleagues on the university assessment committee keeps ending arguments with "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" — she deployed it last week against a proposal to add a "critical reasoning gains" score to our program review, meaning, I take it, that any such number is doomed to be gamed and we might as well not bother — and I need a menu of what that sentence could actually be claiming, sorted from the trivially true to the ones that would genuinely license her conclusion. What makes me suspicious is that the same idea shows up in at least three formulations that aren't equivalent: the economics version, roughly that any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes; the evaluation-research version, that the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor; and the coffee-mug version she actually says. I also remember an alignment reading group I sat in on once where someone split the phenomenon into four flavors — something about regression to the mean, something about optimization pushing you into the tails of the distribution, something about the causal chain between the proxy and the goal getting severed, and something about an actual adversary working against you. I don't remember the names they used and I don't want a bibliography, but if that four-way split is a real distinction, fold it in; if it collapses into fewer distinctions than it claims, say so.

So: lay out for me the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making. I'd like them as a menu rather than a single preferred reading, ordered from the ones that are nearly analytic or trivially true, through the ones that are substantive empirical generalizations about incentives, up to the ones that are almost metaphysical — for instance the idea that some things we want to measure (reasoning ability, say, or the quality of a dissertation) are partly constituted by the practice of measuring them, so that there's no incentive-free fact of the matter to have been tracking in the first place. For each reading, tell me whether it's plausible, what would have to be true in the world for it to hold, and whether it actually supports her move of killing the assessment proposal rather than just reshaping it. Finally, tell me which reading I should assume she means when she says it, and which reading is doing the work in the standard "specification gaming" or "reward hacking" talk that my CS colleagues use — I suspect those are not the same reading and that the ambiguity is where the argument goes wrong. Bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of my question. American English, please — I'm on a committee, not in a seminar, so keep it usable.
