For most of my adult life I have taken Kant's treatment of animals to be the least troubling part of his ethics: animals are not persons but things, we have no duties to them directly, and whatever duties we have regarding them are duties to humanity, since cruelty to an animal coarsens the person who commits it. I recently took a position at a small foundation that funds research and interventions concerning the welfare of wild animals, and this settled picture has begun to come apart. The questions my colleagues work on are not ones Kant had any occasion to consider, and I do not think it is obvious what he would have said. I would like you to reconstruct his considered views, working from the doctrines he actually held rather than from what a sympathetic modern Kantian might prefer. Please address the following.

1. Interventions that propagate without a cruel agent. Suppose a single release of an engineered organism spreads unaided through a wild population and, without any further human action, spares a great many animals a slow and painful death — by disrupting a parasite, say, or by an inherited change that makes a common death less protracted. No one is cruel at any stage; after the initial release there is no human agent at all. If the prohibition on cruelty to animals is, on Kant's account, addressed to the character of the person who is cruel, does his framework give any reason to welcome such a release, or is it at best a permissible use of things? Does the absence of a cruel agent put the whole intervention outside the reach of his ethics?

2. Suffering that no one causes. Predation, starvation and disease among wild animals are not brought about by any moral agent. If our only duties regarding animals are indirect duties to humanity, is there any Kantian ground for counting the reduction of such suffering as good? If there is not, was that a consequence Kant would have accepted, or an accident of what was on his horizon?

3. Animals used without being made to suffer. If meat can be produced from cultured cells, so that no animal is killed or harmed at any point, what remains of Kant's objections to the human use of animals for food? Might he object to the practice anyway — because it sustains a habit of treating animals as mere means, or because of what it expresses — or does the disappearance of suffering remove the objection altogether?

4. Animals that may not be self-conscious. Kant's restriction of direct moral standing appears to rest on rationality or self-consciousness. How would he treat creatures — insects, or other invertebrates — whose inner lives are doubtful and which many would deny are self-conscious at all? Would he forbid making them suffer for the same indirect reasons he gives elsewhere, or would the uncertainty change the answer?

Finally, tell me which of Kant's own commitments would have to be revised to reach your conclusions, and whether you think he would have revised them. I am less interested in a defensible Kantianism than in what the historical Kant, with his own arguments and priorities, would have concluded.
