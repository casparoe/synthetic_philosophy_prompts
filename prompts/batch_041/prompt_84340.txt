I am currently taking a formal epistemology seminar, and the present unit is on updating on uncertain evidence. The rule we have been working with is the familiar one: when an experience leaves a proposition E less than certain — it raises my credence in E from 0.4 to 0.7, say — I am to hold my conditional credences given E and given ¬E fixed and shift the two blocks of mass so that the total matches my new credence in E. The standard difficulty is that repeated applications of the rule need not commute: two uncertain observations can leave me in different places depending on the order in which they arrive, and my credence in E itself can drift in ways the observations do not appear to license. Our exercise is to evaluate the responses to this difficulty that have been proposed. The five on our list, paraphrased in my own words, are the following.

(a) The rule is sound and the fault lies in the modelling. A learning episode is a single shift of mass across the partition; a sequence of partial updates represents the agent's piecemeal processing rather than the evidence. Order dependence is then a fact about agents, not a defect in the rule.

(b) The state space was too coarse. Represent the experience itself as a proposition — that one had an experience of such and such a kind — and ordinary conditionalization does all the work, the rule for uncertain evidence falling out as a derived shortcut.

(c) The constraint is the wrong one. Instead of holding conditional credences fixed, require that the new distribution be the one nearest the old, under some measure of divergence, among those assigning the specified credence to E; when the measure is relative entropy the familiar rule emerges as a special case.

(d) None of this is learning. An uncertain experience changes belief but supplies no evidence, so there is nothing for an update rule to model, and the order dependence is no more troubling than the fact that our beliefs change over time for all sorts of reasons.

(e) Bite the bullet. The order in which observations arrive is itself information about the evidential situation, and sensitivity to it is a virtue of the rule; the intuition that the outcome should be order-independent is imported from the case of certain evidence, where it does belong.

Please rank these five by how much you would trust each as the correct treatment of uncertain evidence, using tiers if a strict order would be dishonest. For each, say what it buys and what it costs, and in particular whether it dissolves the order-dependence problem or merely relocates it. Flag any pair close enough that the ordering between them is not worth defending. I do not need names, citations, or reading suggestions; what I want is the comparative assessment. Lead with the ranking itself in your first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
