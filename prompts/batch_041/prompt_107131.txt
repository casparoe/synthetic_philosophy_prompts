For some time I have been trying to get to the bottom of a claim I have seen attributed to the statistician I. J. Good, namely that you can never expect to be worse off for having more information. The reasoning, as I understand it, is that anything you would choose to do after learning some new fact was already open to you beforehand — you could simply have acted the same way and ignored what you learned — so additional information can only widen your set of options, and a wider set of options can never lower the best outcome you can secure. The logic strikes me as compelling, yet it collides with things I actually observe: a friend who will not check a lump, someone who would rather not learn what a partner was doing years ago, an investor who refuses to open their statements during a downturn. Please explain the conceptual issue here. I want to understand precisely what the claim does and does not assert, why it is framed in terms of expectations formed in advance rather than how matters feel once the information is actually in hand, and what the main ways are of reconciling the logical point with the genuine sense in which some knowledge can be unwelcome or harmful to possess. I am not asking for a formal proof; I want a clear account of where the apparent conflict really comes from and which of the proposed resolutions I ought to take seriously.
