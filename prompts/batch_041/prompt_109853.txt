I am the character and behaviour lead for a group of state secondary schools, and I have to turn two rival pictures of human nature into something my form tutors can actually run. The pictures, the ones usually labelled after Mencius and after Xunzi: on the first, children already have the beginnings of compassion, shame, deference and a sense of right and wrong, and the teacher's task is to keep those beginnings from being obstructed, worn down or starved — the trouble is bad soil and repeated cutting, not a bad seed. On the second, the native equipment is partiality, appetite and the pursuit of advantage; decency is acquired, deliberately and laboriously, through ritual, repetition, explicit rules and imitation of good models, so that the settled disposition is largely downstream of the practice. Both pictures insist that people can change and that surroundings matter enormously; they differ over where the change comes from. My tutors have no philosophy and twenty minutes per case, decisions come up daily, and anything outside our standard tariff of sanctions has to be signed off by the head teacher.

Write me a decision procedure they can run. It must take a single case — one pupil, one class, or one whole-school policy — and output an intervention rather than a verdict about anyone's nature. It should put its questions in a fixed order, say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and mark where it stops with a no. It needs an explicit test for telling the two pictures apart in a particular case, plus a rule for the common situation where they prescribe the same action anyway, and a rule for what to do when they genuinely conflict. It should flag which steps are judgement dressed up as procedure, and name the point at which the remaining question is one of values — how far a school may go in manufacturing a child's dispositions — rather than of fact, and hand that to a named person. Finally, it must forbid any intervention that would manufacture the evidence for its own diagnosis: if we treat children as naturally bent and build the school accordingly, we will get the behaviour we predicted and will have no way of telling whether the prediction was true.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a fourteen-year-old caught passing answers to a friend in a test she would have passed on her own; a corridor litter problem that no amount of reminding has fixed; and a Year 9 tutor group whose attendance collapses every February. Include at least one case where the procedure returns something I will not want to hear. End with what the procedure cannot do, and say which records we already keep would show us, after a year, that its diagnostic step is misfiring.
