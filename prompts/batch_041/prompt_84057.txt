In my ethics seminar this term we have been working through Augustine's On Lying — the treatise he wrote before he became a bishop — and I have volunteered to lead next week's discussion of it. What I keep tripping over is how little Augustine seems willing to concede. He begins by setting jokes aside on the ground that nobody ever counted a joke a lie, and the whole burden of his account falls on a kind of double-mindedness, one thing held in the mind while another is uttered; the translation we are using renders his definition as "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind." I would like to know what a theory of lying actually has to get right before I can judge whether a position that strict is defensible, so before I read anything else on the subject I want the specification itself: the requirements any adequate account of lying must satisfy, each with the case or consideration that puts it on the list, and a note on which of them are genuinely non-negotiable and which are really commitments of one camp rather than conditions of adequacy — I suspect, for instance, that a requirement to the effect that a lie is an offense against God belongs in the second pile, but I want to be told why or why not. I am not asking for a reading list or for who said what; I want the standard, argued for on its own terms.

Some cases I want the list to be able to handle, since they are the ones I plan to put to the class: a doctor who tells a patient with dementia something false in order to spare her terror; a statement that is literally true and uttered precisely in order to produce a false belief in the hearer; a falsehood addressed to someone the speaker knows will not believe it, or to someone who on any ordinary view has no claim on the truth at all, as with the murderer asking where my friend is hiding; the joke and the novel, which Augustine wants to exclude by stipulation and which I suspect do more work for him than he admits; a form or a chatbot answered falsely, where the "hearer" is an institution or a machine; a politician's carefully true statement, or her refusal to answer, or her deliberate ambiguity; and the ordinary polite falsehood about how much one enjoyed the evening, which harms no one and which hardly anyone thinks worth much blame. Tell me which distinctions the theory cannot be allowed to collapse — between lying and being mistaken, between lying and withholding, between deceiving and lying, between the wrongness of the act and the badness of its upshot, between what is owed to the hearer and what the liar does to himself — and flag the pair of requirements on your list that you think cannot be satisfied together, because that is the part I most want to see: I suspect the real disagreement lives in the list rather than in the cases. Finally, since my purpose is practical — I want a standard that a hospital ethics committee or a journalist's code of conduct could actually apply, not a taxonomy for its own sake — say which single requirement does the most to make Augustine's absolute prohibition untenable, and whether there is any requirement on the list that his position satisfies better than its permissive rivals do.
