So here's the setup: Aristotle gets revived and given a crash course in modern macro — not the textbook version, but the actual plumbing: commercial banks creating deposits when they lend, central banks setting interest rates rather than quantities, fiat currency with an inflation target, repo markets, derivatives, insurance, the whole thing. He's then asked to advise a mid-sized modern city-state (own currency, central bank, commercial banks, stock market) on monetary and financial policy. What does he say?

I dont want the stock answer that he'd condemn usury and leave it there. I want you to work out what his actual distinctions imply for things he never saw. Specifically:

1. He says money exists by convention (nomos), not by nature. Does modern fiat money vindicate that, or break it? Because the convention is no longer a single political decision — it's a decentralized byproduct of profit-seeking bank lending, with a central bank trying to steer it after the fact. Is that still nomos, or is it something else?

2. He treats money as a measure (metron). But a measure that deliberately shrinks at 2% a year — inflation targeting — seems to undermine its function as a measure. Would he see this as a corruption of money's nature, or as a legitimate tool of household management (oikonomia) for steering the economy? And would he treat the Cantillon effect — new money going first to those closest to the source, redistributing wealth in a way that has nothing to do with need or merit — as a decisive objection to the whole system, or just a fixable defect?

3. Rank the following from most to least natural on his view, with reasons: index funds, credit default swaps, payday lending, sovereign wealth funds, universal basic income, carbon credits, insurance, mortgages, savings accounts with interest, and a central bank's lender-of-last-resort function. Where exactly does the line between natural acquisition (chrematistike bounded by household needs) and unnatural acquisition fall for each?

4. Is there any modern financial instrument he'd actually endorse? And which of his positions here would be most surprising to someone who only knows the philosopher-who-hated-usury version?

Finally, be honest about where his framework just doesn't have the resources to say anything determinate. Don't force him into a position if the texts don't support one — but do tell me what the most defensible Aristotelian line would be, even if it's not something he himself said.
