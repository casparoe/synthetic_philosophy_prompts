I'm a second-year PhD student sitting in on a political theory seminar on sortition and lottocracy, and I've been sent back to the ancient sources to get clear about what selection by lot was supposed to be doing before the moderns got hold of it. I thought I knew what I was looking for, but Aristotle has stopped me cold, and the seminar's assumption that he is simply the first lottocrat has not helped. The passage I keep returning to is Politics IV.9 (1294b), where he is explaining how a mixed constitution can be assembled out of democratic and oligarchic parts: "In the third place is a combination of the two systems, taking some features from the oligarchical law and some from the democratic; I mean, for example, that it is thought to be democratic for the offices to be assigned by lot, for them to be elected oligarchic, and democratic for them not to have a property-qualification, oligarchic to have one; therefore it is aristocratic and constitutional to take one feature from one form and the other from the other, from oligarchy that offices are to be elected, and from democracy that this is not to be on a property-qualification." I can follow the recipe for mixing well enough, but I cannot see what licenses the labels. Why should the method of appointment — lot rather than vote — be the thing that carries the democratic or the oligarchic character of a constitution? What is the connection supposed to be between appointment by lot and the equality that democracy is meant to embody? Is Aristotle asserting that the lot expresses a conception of the citizens as equals, that it produces an equal distribution of office, or only that the many happen to favour it? And what work is the hedge "it is thought to be" doing: is he reporting the conventional markers by which Greeks classified constitutions, or endorsing them as the correct criteria?

What makes me doubt that I have hold of a single doctrine is Book VI.2 (1317b), where, immediately after announcing that "one factor of liberty is to govern and be governed in turn" and that the popular principle of justice is "equality according to number, not worth", he sets out the institutions that are "democratic in character" and puts "election of officials by all from all" first, only a few lines later adding "election by lot either to all magistracies or to all that do not need experience and skill". So election figures as a democratic device in VI.2 and as the oligarchic marker in IV.9; and the lot, which IV.9 treats as the democratic device as such, is in VI.2 confined to offices that require no experience or skill — a restriction that looks like a concession that the lot's democratic credentials depend on what is being filled rather than on the method itself. Are the two passages inconsistent, or is "election" doing different work in each (the method of choosing versus who chooses and from whom), and is the lot democratic for Aristotle intrinsically, as an expression of equality, or instrumentally, as a way of keeping the notables from monopolising office?

I'd like a patient reconstruction rather than a survey of what people say about sortition: what the argument in each passage actually is, which of its steps are load-bearing, where the two passages can be reconciled and where they genuinely pull apart, and what a reader trying to get Aristotle right should and should not carry over into the contemporary debate.
