I'm assembling the readings for a graduate seminar on the emotions, and before we weigh any particular account I want a clean statement of what a theory of emotion has to deliver to count as adequate. Please write that desiderata list: the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, and the distinctions it cannot collapse — with the case or consideration behind each requirement — and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp (feeling theories, appraisal theories, perceptual theories, and so on).

Test the list against a case I'm stipulating, so it can't be answered by reciting a familiar menu. Suppose a person who, after surgery, exhibits the complete appraisal, physiological, and behavioral profile of fear — avoidance, vigilance, autonomic arousal, the lot — and whose fear reliably tracks genuine danger, yet who reports sincerely and consistently that he feels nothing at all, not even a muted version. The theory must either count this as fear or explain why it isn't; run the same test in reverse for someone who feels the full felt and bodily turbulence of an emotion but makes none of the appraisals the emotion is supposed to require. Beyond that, I want the list to take a stand on: whether object-directedness is essential, given moods and free-floating anxiety; whether the theory must allow us to be systematically wrong about our own emotions; how it handles the continuity between human and animal affect; and what it must do about cross-cultural variation in emotion concepts.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular contenders, and flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that tension interests me more than a scorecard. Write in American English.
