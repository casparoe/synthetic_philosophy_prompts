An essay I recently read in a general-interest magazine offers the following account of the debate over "equality of what," and I would like your help in deciding how much of it to believe. The essay says, in substance:

"Since the 1970s the central question of distributive justice has been: equality of what? The literature now recognizes three answers. The first is Rawls's: the currency is primary goods — the all-purpose means that a rational person wants whatever else he wants — and justice consists in their equal distribution. The second is Dworkin's: the currency is resources, and the fair distribution is the one that would emerge from a hypothetical auction combined with a hypothetical insurance market, so that people bear the costs of their ambitions but are compensated for unchosen misfortune such as handicap or poor native talent. The third is Sen's: the currency is capabilities — what a person is actually able to do and to be. Sen's argument against the first two is the empirical fact of human diversity. People differ in their capacity to convert resources into functioning; the disabled, the chronically ill, the malnourished, and those facing social discrimination need more resources to reach the same level of functioning as others. A wheelchair user and an able-bodied person on the same income are not equally situated, since the wheelchair user must divert part of that income to mobility merely to reach the same starting line. This is the problem of conversion factors, and it is decisive. The same style of argument disposes of welfare as a currency: the person with expensive tastes, who needs champagne and caviar to reach the same level of satisfaction as his neighbour, would acquire a claim on everyone else's resources under welfare egalitarianism, which is absurd. Sen's conclusion is that the correct currency of distributive justice is the capability set, that the task of a just society is to equalize it, and that Nussbaum's list of ten central capabilities supplies its content."

The essay is fluent and confident, and I am not equipped to tell where it is reliable. I would like an audit of it, move by move: for each of its main claims, say what a specialist in the field would accept as correct, what is a legitimate simplification that would not mislead a careful reader, what is a distortion that a defender of the position in question would reject, and what is plainly mistaken. I am particularly concerned about two things. First, the essay's closing claim — that the debate is effectively settled in favour of capabilities — strikes me as stronger than the argument that precedes it; I would like to know whether that conclusion follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate, or whether it inflates a more modest set of results. Second, I suspect the essay is harder on Rawls than on the other two figures, but I cannot say precisely how.

A related puzzle of my own, which may bear on the same material. I work in disability policy, and I have noticed that when a city installs curb cuts, ramps, and accessible transit, it is spending more resources on some citizens than on others in order to bring their capabilities up to a common level. A colleague takes this to show that the whole "currency" framing is confused: if resources are deliberately distributed unequally so as to equalize capabilities, then capabilities cannot be a target of which resources are merely the means, since resources are partly constitutive of capabilities; the two cannot be cleanly separated, and the debate the essay summarizes is therefore a pseudo-problem. His positive view is that the only thing that matters is whether people can actually do and be the things they have reason to value. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine objection or a confusion, and I would like you to say which, and whether it follows from anything true in the essay's summary or is invited by the summary's own way of framing the issue.
