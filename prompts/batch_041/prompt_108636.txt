So I've been reading philosophy since I retired (condensed matter guy, thirty-odd years on phonons, and now I have the time) and a dinner argument broke out that I honestly couldnt referee. Wondering if you can.

It started because my granddaughter said the dog knows he isn't allowed on the couch. Ruth, who's read a lot of philosophy, said well, strictly speaking the dog doesn't know anything, and neither do we, not in the sense that there's some inner state called a belief sitting behind the behaviour. She said 'believes' is a placeholder word, like 'caloric' or 'vital force' used to be, and once we actually understand the brain the word gets dropped, not translated. Her line was: it's not that beliefs turned out to be neurons, it's that there was never anything there to turn out.

Owen pushed back and said the analogy is doing all the work and it's a bad one, because nobody's day-to-day life ever ran on caloric. Belief talk isn't a scientist's hypothesis, it's the bookkeeping we use to hold each other to things: you promised, so you knew, so you lied, so you owe me an apology. You can't discover that the vocabulary of promising was a mistake. And he said the physics analogy cuts the other way anyway, since when we found the molecules, temperature and pressure didn't get eliminated, they got grounded. So maybe belief is temperature, not caloric.

Ruth came back with what I thought was a decent point: temperature survived because there are laws stated in it that are exact and projectable, gas laws and so on, where as belief talk has no exceptionless laws at all, it's 'all else being equal' the whole way down. That's the signature of a placeholder. If neuroscience ever gets its act together, she said, belief talk goes the way of the four humors, not the way of temperature.

Owen said two things. First, that all-else-being-equal isn't disqualifying, since biology and economics are full of it and nobody concludes that selection or prices don't exist. Second, and this is the bit I keep turning over, he said belief talk isn't mainly for predicting the movements, it's for saying the movements are right given what the person knew. When you say she thinks the keys are in the drawer, you're not just betting on where her hand goes, you're saying that's the thing to do given her information. Neurons can't be right or wrong about where the keys are, beliefs can. So whatever belief talk is tracking, it isn't the same thing the neuroscience is tracking.

Ruth said that's just redescription, that 'right given her information' is another way of describing the machinery that produces the reaching. And she added that Owen's own position eats itself: when he says beliefs don't exist, is he asserting that, which would mean he thinks it's true, which would mean he believes it? Then his theory is false by its own lights. And if he isn't asserting it, he's just making noise. Owen said that's a trap and not an argument. He said if you say in 1750 that there's no such thing as a vital force, nobody gets to answer that saying so takes a vital force. He can describe his own state in whatever terms the true theory uses; the true theory just won't use the word belief.

Anyways. What I can't sort out is who has the better of it at each step. Could you go through it move by move, the caloric analogy, the temperature reply, the no-laws point, the right-given-her-information point, and the self-refutation bit, and say for each one who has it right and why? Not a verdict on the whole thing, just each move.

And one more. Before any of this I'd been explaining quasiparticles to the table, phonons and holes and such. Not real particles, you can't bottle one, but not fake either, you'd be lost without them and they predict things. I said maybe beliefs are like that: not fundamental, and not eliminable. Ruth said no, quasiparticles are just a convenient way of bookkeeping the real interactions. Owen said no, quasiparticles are a counterexample to Ruth, since they show you can have something that's nowhere in the fundamental inventory and still be an indispensable part of a real science. Was that comparison doing any work or was I talking nonsense? And if it does work, does it break the tie, or is it a third position neither of them was running?

I don't want a reading list by the way, I won't read it. I want to know who's right about what.
