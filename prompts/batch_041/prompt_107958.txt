A friend of mine in the mathematics department has spent two weeks arguing that the classical random-chord paradox is not a curiosity about circles but a proof that the principle of indifference is bankrupt, and that the argument generalizes until nothing we ordinarily call statistical inference survives. I can see that some step must give, since I am not prepared to conclude that no credence about a continuously varying quantity is ever warranted. But every step looks to me like something I already believe, and I cannot find the joint. Here is his argument as I have reconstructed it.

Set a circle of radius 1 and the equilateral triangle inscribed in it, whose side is √3. A chord is drawn at random, and we ask for the probability that its length exceeds √3. Three descriptions of the drawing each look like the canonical application of indifference, and each is invariant under every rotation of the figure: (a) the endpoints are chosen independently and uniformly on the circumference, which gives 1/3; (b) the midpoint of the chord is chosen uniformly over the disk, which gives 1/4; (c) a point is chosen uniformly on a radius and the chord is drawn perpendicular to the radius there, which gives 1/2. I have checked all three computations; they are correct.

(1) A credence is answerable to the possibilities it is about, not to the words used to describe them: where two descriptions pick out the same possibility space, they license the same credences.
(2) Indifference is (1) applied to evidence. Where the evidence leaves the possibilities symmetric, credence must treat them symmetrically, and the verdict of indifference is the verdict those symmetries force.
(3) Each of (a), (b) and (c) is the verdict so forced. Each of the three sampling descriptions is perfectly symmetric under the rotations of the circle, so each falls under (2); and all three describe the same drawing of the same chord, since a chord and its length are what they are however the experimenter arrived at them.
(4) So indifference licenses 1/3, 1/4 and 1/2 for one and the same event.
(5) A rule that returns three answers is either inconsistent, and so not a rule, or it is a permission. If it is a permission, then every rotation-invariant distribution over chords is permitted. Those are exactly the distributions of the form (any distribution for the chord's distance from the centre) times (uniform angle), so the permitted answers to our question are every number between 0 and 1. A permission that licenses every answer says nothing about this event.
(6) Either way, indifference cannot be the bridge from symmetry in the evidence to a determinate credence.
(7) Nothing else can serve as that bridge. Finite evidence cannot determine a distribution over a continuum, and data can at best wash out a prior rather than fix it.
(8) Therefore no credence about a continuously varying quantity — a physical constant, a rate, a coin's bias, the next measurement — is ever warranted by evidence.
(9) That conclusion is absurd; it would make measurement, estimation and prediction unjustifiable.
(10) Therefore one of (1) to (7) is false.

He adds two riders. He says he has already heard the standard repair: demand invariance not of the rotations alone but of some larger group, for instance the rigid motions of the plane, treating the chord as a line that happens to meet the disk, and the larger demand picks out a single answer. He replies that nothing in the evidence selects that group over the rotations, or over any other group that happens to act transitively on chords, and that a principle which must be told in advance which transformations are irrelevant is not deriving anything from the evidence. Second, he says the coin is worse for us than the circle: a uniform prior over a coin's bias and a uniform prior over the logarithm of the bias are equally natural, disagree about the posterior, and are never separated by any finite run of tosses.

I would give up a good deal to keep (1) and (2), and I will not give up (9). Tell me which step fails and why it looks so much better than it is. If the fault is in (1) or (2), say what a credence could be answerable to instead. If it is in (3), say exactly where the three descriptions stop being descriptions of one thing, and whether the same fault would also rescue the coin. If it is in (5) or (6), say what indifference survives as and what takes over its work as a principle. If it is in (7), say what a credence about a continuum can be justified by, if not by evidence that fixes it. Then tell me the price of the cut: which further claims, in each case, I would have to abandon along with the step. I do not need references.
