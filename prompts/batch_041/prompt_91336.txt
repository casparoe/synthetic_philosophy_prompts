I'm a philosophy prof, and I've managed to tangle myself up over the Sphex wasp while reworking a lecture on free will. You know the one: the digger wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it home by the antennae, leaves it at the burrow mouth while she goes inside to check the nest is empty, and then — if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's in there — comes out, hauls it right back to the same spot, and dutifully checks the nest all over again. Do that a few times and she'll loop forever, never once catching on that the thing being messed with is you. Hofstadter gave us the wonderful word 'sphexishness' for this kind of behavior, and Dennett leans on it a lot (quoting Wooldridge's Machinery of the Brain, I think) to make the point that conduct can look deeply thoughtful and still be mindless. Fine. Here's where I got stuck. I told my students the wasp is sphexish because she's a mechanism, running a fixed routine that's insensative to what's actually going on. Then a sharp student asked: aren't *we* mechanisms too, at least on the story Dennett tells? So if the wasp is sphexish because she's a mechanism, doesn't it follow that we're sphexish as well, and free will is just dead? And if we're meant to be different, isn't that only to say that some of us are less sphexish than others — which would make free will a matter of degree, which sounds absurd? I mubbled something about compatibilism and moved on, and I'd like to do better next week.

So, putting on your philosopher-of-mind hat: think carefully here before you answer, because I suspect I've committed some sort of category error and I can't see where. Start by brainstorming the different things 'sphexish' might actually mean or contrast with, then untangle for me (a) whether being a mechanism is even the right thing to blame for the wasp's rigidity, (b) whether determinism has anything to do with sphexishness at all, and (c) what the wasp is and isn't doing in the free-will debate. Don't just reassure me that compatibilists have an answer — show me precisely which step in my little argument is the broken one, and why the student's "so it's a matter of degree" move doesn't land the way he thinks it does.
