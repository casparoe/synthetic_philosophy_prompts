Next month I am presenting at the departmental workshop attached to my philosophy of mind course, and I would like a question-and-answer preparation sheet for the discussion that follows. The thesis I will defend is this. The literature standardly treats pain asymbolia — the condition in which patients report that a stimulus hurts while failing to withdraw from it, and sometimes while smiling or laughing — as proof that the badness of pain lies in its affective-motivational component rather than in the sensory experience itself. I think that reading is too quick. The cleanest thing the cases show is a dissociation between the felt badness of pain and the disposition to escape it: on my reading, the patients who are most informative still register the stimulus as bad, but the badness no longer moves them. If that is right, then pain's badness cannot be identified with its motivational pull, and theories that reduce the badness of pain to an imperative to stop or escape are in trouble; badness is better treated as a felt evaluative property that can come apart from what it disposes you to do. I will also argue that the cases put pressure on the view that pain is bad because it represents the body as damaged.

The audience is specific, and I want the objections from them rather than from the field in general. It includes two philosophers of mind who are representationalists about pain and will want to know what asymbolia shows about the sensory state as opposed to the affect; a clinical pain researcher who distrusts lesion evidence and will press me on how heterogeneous the asymbolic patients are and how much weight their self-reports can bear; a psychologist who works on affect and motivation and suspects I am conflating unpleasantness with aversion; and the course instructor, who works on value theory and will likely charge that I slide between intrinsic, relational, and dispositional senses of badness.

Please give me the prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way that audience member would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest; what I should concede at the outset; and the one question I am dreading, which I should prepare for rather than hope goes unasked. No citations, reading lists, or references to particular papers — I need arguments I can deliver from the podium, not a bibliography — and please flag any point where my own position is more fragile than I seem to realize.
