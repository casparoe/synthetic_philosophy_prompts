I run a fortnightly "puzzles in practical reason" night for our MA students — usually 14–18 people, 90 minutes, and we have a small budget for snacks, which matters. Next week is Kavka's toxin puzzle and I don't want the usual thing where I sketch the billionaire and everyone nods and says "huh, intention is weird" and we go home. I want them to actually get stuck. So the plan is: participants can earn a real small reward — a chocolate from the bowl, a token worth a few points toward something silly, whatever, no actual money — not for doing a mildly unpleasant thing later, but for having a genuine intention now to do it, with zero penalty for skipping at the appointed time. Since I obviously can't read intentions, I want the detector to be a person: each participant nominates someone in the room who knows them decently well, that person predicts whether they'll actually do the thing, and the reward is paid on the strength of that prediction. There should also be a separate stage where the same people are rewarded for predicting their own behaviour, and I want the difference between the two stages to be the whole point — intending vs. predicting, and what happens to an intention when the only thing hanging off it is a payoff that doesn't depend on the act. Please build this out: rules, materials, how the reward scheme works without money, the timeline, what the facilitator does at each step, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the session onto the puzzle itself (why "I'll take the reward and just not do it" is supposed to be the rational move, what that implies about whether intentions can be generated at will, why the standard escape hatches — precommitment, public promises, making it a matter of your word — are doing so much work).

One thing I want baked in, because it's the part I'm actually unsure about: a second condition in which nobody is allowed to announce or promise anything and the predictor is someone they don't know at all. My guess is that nobody in that condition can collect, and I'd like the activity designed so that we find out rather than assume it. My colleague thinks the whole puzzle is just a verbal trick about the word "intention" and that the practical-reason framing is decoration; I don't want you to settle that for me, but I do want the debrief to give him a fair run so we can argue about it afterwards. Mild unpleasantness only, by the way — nothing anyone could actually get hurt by, and nothing that would embarrass a shy student.

And please tell me straight what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — my suspicion is that everyone just says "sure, I intend it," the nominated friend plays along out of politeness, the reward gets paid to everybody, and the whole thing becomes a lesson in nothing. Then be honest about which part of the real puzzle the activity can't reproduce at all and that I'll have to supply myself by talking.
