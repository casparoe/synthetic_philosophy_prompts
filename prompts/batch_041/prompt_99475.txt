I am a second-year PhD student in a seminar on the comparative history of mass violence, and we keep circling a dispute I cannot settle. A classmate of mine, a historian who works on testimony, insists that any attempt to rank atrocities is not merely difficult but obscene — that to say one genocide was worse than another, or to grade such events on a single scale of evil, is already to have betrayed the victims — and when pressed he falls back on the proverb "comparisons are odious," treating it as the end of the matter, much as other people in the room fall back on the claim that one particular atrocity was unique in kind. I am not asking you to decide whether he is right in the end. What I want is a disambiguation: the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making, and an account of which of them would actually license the conclusion he draws from it.

Please lay the readings out as a numbered menu, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, with a short verdict on each — trivially true, substantive but defensible, contentious, or simply false. Among the things I would like sorted out: whether the claim is about the structure of value (that evils are not commensurable, so that comparative judgments are not false but meaningless), about our epistemic position (that we cannot know or weigh what a ranking would require), about the standing of the comparer (that no one has the authority to rank the dead), about the act of comparing (that it insults, instrumentalizes, or invites minimization and denial), or about the words themselves — since "incomparable" in English can mean both "having no common measure" and "unmatched, without equal," and I suspect a good deal of the disagreement in the seminar is that slide. For each reading, say whether it is a claim about the world, about us, or about the propriety of an act, and whether it supports refusing the ranking question, answering it, or something in between — in particular, whether it rules out all comparative judgment or only certain uses of it, in contexts of triage, restitution, or memorialization where some ordering seems practically unavoidable. End by saying which reading my classmate most likely has in mind, and whether the slogan as he wields it is doing any work beyond marking that he would rather not answer.
