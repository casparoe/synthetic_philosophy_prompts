I chair the ethics panel at a mid-sized foundation. We make maybe eight genuinely hard grant decisions a year — grants between fifty grand and two million, real people on the recieving end — we get an hour per case, and the room is mixed: one philosopher, our lawyer, two program officers, a community rep, me. The recurring problem is that we don't agree on the moral framework and we've been improvising, case by case. I want a procedure we can actually run in that hour.

Our house rules, and the procedure has to respect them. Each of us gives a coarse credence — high, medium, low, or "don't know" — to a short list of views we keep on the whiteboard: a welfarist one, a rights/autonomy one, a prioritarian one, a community-consent one, and a "this isn't ours to decide" one. "Don't know" is a real bucket, not a zero, and it can be the swing vote. Then, instead of just averaging, we want two screens: first something like a moral-parliament or expected-choiceworthiness step, then a second screen that asks what happens if we've got the credences wrong — including the nightmare case where the view we all ranked last turns out to be right. The second screen should be able to veto the first.

Write it as a procedure: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, where it hands the decision back to a person because the step is values all the way down. Be blunt about which steps are arithmetic on incommensurables dressed up as method — I'd rather have the seams visible than a tidy flowchart that's lying to me. Don't give me a reading list or tell me who said what; I won't read it and I won't remember it.

Then run it on two cases: (a) deworming in a high-prevalence region versus a cage-free campaign, and (b) a donor who wants us to fund something he cheerfully admits is harmful, "offset" by something good. I expect to hate the answer on at least one of those, and I'd rather find out now than in the room.
