I am a novelist, and for the past several years I have sat on the board of a small literary foundation. We run a month-long summer residency and a ten-day autumn festival, and between the two we feed roughly two hundred people — residents, staff, visiting writers, volunteers, and whoever wanders in from the town. At our next meeting the board must adopt a standing food-procurement policy, and the discussion has gotten away from us.

A subcommittee has come back recommending that we go plant-based by default: vegetarian and vegan menus as the baseline, meat available only on request. The reasoning is the familiar one — animals are morally considerable, industrial meat is hard to defend, and a plant-based menu is the harm-free option. Two of my fellow trustees have now dug in against it, and their objection is not the usual carnivore complaint. Their argument, as I understand it, runs like this. Crop agriculture is not harm-free either. Ploughing, harvesting, and pesticides kill enormous numbers of field animals — mice, voles, ground-nesting birds, and insects — and if invertebrates have any moral standing at all, those deaths count, and they may count in the billions. If that is right, then how harmful a menu is depends on how much cropland it consumes, and beef may not be the worst item on the list. Cattle grazed on pasture that would never be ploughed might be responsible for fewer field-animal deaths per calorie than the wheat or soy that would replace them. Chicken runs the other way: it takes far less feed and land per calorie, so it may do less crop damage — but it takes many more individual animals to feed the same number of people, and each of those is a bird reared and killed deliberately rather than incidentally. On this view our vegan baseline is not the neutral option it appears to be; it is a choice for one particular pattern of wild-animal death, made on behalf of people who never agreed to it.

I do not know what to do with this, and I suspect no one else on the board does either. What I need from you is the memo I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a specific policy — not a menu of options, a policy — with a date by which we would implement it and a date by which we would review it. It should engage the crop-death argument on its merits, including the parts I have summarized too briskly, and say plainly which of the trustees' claims you think are sound, which are overstated, and which are not the board's business at all. It should be honest about what the foundation gives up in each direction: the guests who will be annoyed, the kitchen staff who will have to execute it, the money, and whatever moral cost attaches to the choice we do not make. If you conclude that the evidence is too uncertain to justify any policy, say so, and tell us what would have to become true for you to change your mind.

Write it as a memo to the board: the recommendation first, then the reasoning, then the dissent. I will be the one defending it at the meeting, so it needs to survive someone smarter than I am objecting to it.
