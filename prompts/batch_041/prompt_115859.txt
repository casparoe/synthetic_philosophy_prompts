I am putting together the closing item for a graduate seminar on moral trade, and I want it to be a poem rather than a summary: something the students can carry out of the room, and something that would still work for a reader who has never encountered the idea.

The idea, as I would put it to them. Two people who hold different moral views can often make an exchange that each regards as an improvement by her own lights. The engine is a difference in valuation, not an overlap in values: each party does, funds, or refrains from something that the other ranks higher than she does, and receives in return something she ranks higher than the other does. That is what separates trade from compromise, where each side gives up part of what it wanted; here both sides get more of what they wanted, each measured by her own standards, and no shared scale is required to see that both come out ahead. It is also why trade is troubling. Each party deliberately does something she takes to be of little weight, or perhaps to be wrong, in order to move the other toward something she takes to be of great weight, and an exchange rate has to be struck — so many hours of one kind of work per so many of another, this many animals per that many bednets — without either party conceding the other's values. And trade has a limit worth pressing: if your view takes the form of an absolute constraint rather than a ranking, there is nothing to trade with respect to the forbidden act, since a constraint cannot be exchanged for more of anything else. So the gains from trade are, oddly, a function of disagreement, and would shrink toward nothing as moral views converged.

What I want is a pantoum of five or six quatrains, twenty to twenty-four lines. The form's rule is that the second and fourth lines of each quatrain reappear as the first and third lines of the next, and in the closing quatrain the second line is the third line of the first quatrain while the fourth line is the poem's opening line, so the poem ends where it began; minor rewording of a repeated line is permitted and, here, welcome. Use it for two voices alternating line by line throughout, so that by the form's own rule each speaker is obliged, a stanza or two later, to deliver as her own line something the other first said. Let the migrating lines be the goods traded, and let the reader feel them change meaning when they change mouths.

Two constraints on the content. First, accuracy: someone who knows the idea should be able to identify the difference in valuation as the engine of the trade, the mutual gain by each party's own lights, the worry about complicity or self-betrayal in doing the low-ranked thing, and the thought that an absolute constraint leaves nothing to trade. Second, legibility: no technical vocabulary, and no dependence on the reader's prior acquaintance with the concept — the argument should be carried by the images and by the form. Take as the concrete case one speaker who holds that the gravest ongoing wrongs are done to animals and another who holds that the gravest are done to the poor, unless some other pair of committed views serves the poem better; if you substitute, say which pair you chose and why in the commentary.

Do not resolve the poem. I want the ending to leave open whether the return of the opening line, now spoken by the other party, marks a closed circle — both of them where they started, having merely swapped — or a gain that the circular form conceals. Do not moralize in either direction, and do not let the poem break into argument in the last stanza. No citations or references are needed; nothing will be printed alongside it.

Then, under the heading Commentary on the choices made, write roughly three hundred words on the choices: why the pantoum suits this idea, and what the migration of lines does that free verse could not; which lines you intend as the traded goods and how their sense shifts in the other speaker's voice; how the final return to the opening line is meant to bear on the circle-or-gain question; and one place where fidelity to the idea and the demands of the form pulled against each other, and how you decided.
