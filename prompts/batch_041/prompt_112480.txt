You are a philosopher of international law and an intellectual historian of the concept of genocide, with a specialist's grasp of Raphael Lemkin's original category, the 1948 Convention that narrowed it, and the political history of how the word has been applied and withheld. Work carefully and at length. Before you write the analysis proper, open with a short, deliberately unpolished brainstorming section in which you lay out the different jobs the word genocide does when it is used — at minimum the legal job (it is a term of art that triggers duties), the moral job (it renders a verdict), and the interpretive job (it organizes a mass of events into a single kind of event) — and note where those jobs pull apart. Then set the brainstorm aside and write the disciplined analysis.

Facts you may take as given, since you cannot consult anything. Lemkin coined the term in the 1940s to name a coordinated plan of different actions aimed at destroying the essential foundations of the life of a national group, not only by killing its members but by dismantling its political and social institutions, its culture, its language, its religion, and its economic existence — a destruction of a group's national pattern that he distinguished from the subsequent imposition of the oppressor's pattern. His category was broad: it bundled physical, biological, cultural, and economic techniques. The 1948 Convention that he campaigned for narrowed the concept sharply. Its definition requires acts committed with the intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group as such; it lists killing, causing serious bodily or mental harm, deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about physical destruction, imposing measures to prevent births, and forcibly transferring children; it excludes political groups; and it requires a specific intent aimed at the group, which is the hardest element to prove. The cultural dimension Lemkin cared about was dropped in drafting. The Convention obliges its parties both to punish and to prevent.

Now the episode. Between April and July 1994, in roughly a hundred days, some eight hundred thousand people, overwhelmingly Tutsi but also moderate Hutu, were killed in Rwanda by a state-directed campaign — the interim government, the army, and militia — that was openly aimed at a group. The killings were not a surprise in kind: warnings had circulated for months, and the scale and organization were visible to foreign governments as they unfolded. The international response is the part I want you to analyze. The Security Council, after ten Belgian peacekeepers were killed, reduced rather than reinforced the peacekeeping force. The United States, still marked by the deaths of its soldiers in Somalia the previous autumn and operating under restrictive peacekeeping policy, spent weeks avoiding the word genocide, speaking instead of acts of genocide while resisting the conclusion that genocide was underway; a reporter's question about how many acts of genocide it takes to make a genocide became the emblem of the evasion. The administration eventually conceded the label while continuing to decline to intervene. By the time the Council authorized a reinforced mission it was too late; the killing ended when the rebel forces took the capital.

I want the episode analyzed through the theory, and the theory tested by the episode. Specifically:

1. Mapping. What does Lemkin's category say this episode was, and what does the Convention's category say it was? Are they the same event? Where the killers' intent, the group's status, the techniques used, and the scale fit each category, and where one category sees genocide and the other sees an evidentiary problem or a different crime.

2. The naming as a move. Treat the avoidance of the word as itself an object of analysis rather than mere cowardice. If the legal job of the word is to trigger duties, what does it tell us that the actors most likely to bear those duties were the ones most invested in withholding the label? Is the evasion evidence that the category works as a trigger, or that it fails as one? Say which reading the case supports and why.

3. The gap between the moral and the legal category. By the end, was there any serious disagreement that the moral category applied? What does it show about the Convention that the moral verdict, once finally rendered, produced no action? Distinguish what Lemkin's broader category would have licensed from what the narrow legal category obligated.

4. Testing the theory. Does Rwanda confirm Lemkin's account of genocide as the destruction of a group's foundations, or does it expose his breadth as a liability — too much bundled together to be actionable? Does it support or undercut the drafting choices (specific intent, the group list, the omission of cultural genocide)? Would Lemkin have recognized this as his paradigm case, and would the drafters?

5. Where the theory is silent. Identify what the concept does not and cannot explain about how the episode went — the role of institutional incapacity, of great-power interest, of the structure of the Security Council — and resist the temptation to make the theory speak to those.

Close with a judgment: is the lesson of the case that the legal category should be reformed (broadened, or severed from the trigger, or backed by an enforcement mechanism), that the moral category should be kept distinct from the legal one, or that the two cannot be reconciled? Argue for one.

Throughout, be explicit about your assumptions, flag any place where the historical record is contested rather than treating a contested point as settled, and say plainly where the theory is being stretched to fit rather than manufacturing a fit. The deliverable is a case analysis that runs in both directions — theory illuminating the case and the case testing the theory — not a history of the killings and not a survey of the literature.
