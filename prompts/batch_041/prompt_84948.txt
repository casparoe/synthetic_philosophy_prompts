I have been reading, for background on a novel I am writing, one of those popular books on the mind that a general reader picks up when a science supplement recommends it, and its chapter on eliminative materialism has left me unsure how much of it I can use. The novel is narrated by a cognitive scientist who has concluded that the vocabulary of belief and desire is a dead end and that a mature science of the brain will describe her, and everyone else, without it; the action is mostly the friction between her way of speaking and the way every other character speaks, and I would like her to be someone a philosopher of mind could respect rather than a caricature. Since that chapter is the only account I have, I would be grateful if you would audit it for me sentence by sentence. Here is its gist, in its own words as best I can render them: "Eliminative materialism is the view that neuroscience has shown that beliefs and desires do not exist. Its defenders point out that folk psychology — our everyday practice of explaining behaviour by citing beliefs and desires — is really just a primitive theory, like the physics of demons or the humours of medieval medicine, and that a mature science of the brain will have no use for it. Since no one has ever seen a belief under a microscope, and we now know the brain runs on electrochemical signals, talk of beliefs and desires is at best a convenient fiction, a way of speaking we keep for social purposes; when I say I believe it will rain, I am not reporting an inner state but signalling a disposition to behave, which is why eliminativists deny that there is any such thing as the content of a thought. The view is usually credited to the Churchlands, who also expect consciousness, free will and the self to go the same way. Critics say it is self-refuting, since to assert that there are no beliefs is itself to assert a belief, but eliminativists reply that this is a verbal trick, and that once neuroscience is complete we will simply stop using these words. The upshot is that the scientific view of the mind is incompatible with the ordinary view, and that anyone who has understood the science ought to stop talking about what they believe."

What I want is an audit I can act on, not a reading list. Tell me which of those sentences a careful defender of eliminative materialism would sign, which would make them wince, and which are simply wrong, and flag any place where a standard objection is dressed up as a refutation or where some quite different position — I have a suspicion about the talk of "convenient fiction" and "signalling a disposition," but I cannot say what it is — has been smuggled in under the eliminativist's name. Two things matter most to me for the book. First, whether that last sentence follows from anything true in the rest: could my narrator, having understood whatever the science actually shows, be entitled to treat anyone who keeps talking about beliefs as confused, or does the summary's conclusion need something the summary has not supplied? Second, and more important for the prose, whether someone can deny that beliefs exist while still holding that her own thoughts have content, since I have been trying to write her interior monologue without the vocabulary of belief and have been told by one colleague that the whole idea is incoherent because a thought with no content is not a thought at all, and by another that the interesting question is whether content has to be sentence-like in the first place. I would like to know what the defensible version of my narrator's position is, what it commits her to about her own mind, and which of the formulations I am tempted to give her would betray that I had learned the view from a popular book.
