My flatmate Bram is Dutch — grew up just outside Utrecht, still has the accent, still thinks a bicycle is a personality — and last night at a leaving-do for one of his colleagues we ended up in a two-hour argument about politeness, of all things, that got genuinely heated somewhere around the second pint. His line is that the Dutch are morally in the right and everybody else is running a con, and he's got an argument for it that I cannot find the hole in. So before you answer, take the role of someone who actually works on this area — lying, tact, and what speech is doing when it isn't quite saying — and take a few minutes to lay out the candidate moves before you commit to one. I'm not after citations or a reading list; I want the thing dissected.

His arguement, as near as I can reconstruct it:

(1) If you say something you believe to be false, meaning your hearer to take it as true, then you've lied to them.
(2) Polite speech is stuffed with exactly that. 'Lovely to see you.' 'I'd love to, but I'm completely swamped that week.' 'What an interesting idea.' 'You're looking well.' 'I'll definately think about it.' In each case the speaker doesn't believe what they're saying, and expects to be taken at their word.
(3) A lie doesn't stop being a lie by being small or kindly meant. What's wrong with lying isn't the size of the damage; it's that you've reached into someone else's picture of the world and put a false item in it, and that's the same act whether the item is 'your haircut really suits you' or 'your money's safe with me'.
(4) So any culture that runs on politeness runs on a steady drip of small lies, and a culture that has turned it into a fine art — Japanese indirectness, the whole business of never saying the blunt thing and letting the refusal be read off the tone — has put it on an industrial footing.
(5) Dutch directness, which refuses the game altogether, is therefore the more honest arrangement: everyone knows where they stand, nothing has to be decoded, nobody's being managed.
(6) So bluntness is morally better, and the fact that it's rude doesn't tell against it, because rudeness is at worst a matter of taste while dishonesty is a matter of principle.

Step (6) looks to me like it proves too much: it turns tact into a vice and convicts a whole lot of speech that I don't think anyone seriously believes is wrong, and if that's where we land then something upstream must be broken. But I can't say which step, and when I went for the obvious escape he'd already got there before me and said: fine — then tell me why 'the cheque's in the post' isn't equally fine when the person you're stalling half-suspects that it isn't. I couldn't answer that. And before you tell me it's just a cultural difference: he knows it's a cultural difference, he says the question is whether it's a wrong one. I'd rather not give up (3) if I can help it, it feels like the heart of the thing, but if that's the price then say so. So: which step do I reject, why did it look so plausible in the first place, and what does rejecting it cost me — and if there's a way to keep all six and simply swallow (6), tell me honestly what that would commit me to next time someone asks whether I liked their novel.
