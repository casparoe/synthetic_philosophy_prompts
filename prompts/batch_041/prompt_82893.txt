I am a PhD student in philosophy of religion, and next week I have the session of our graduate seminar — twelve students, ninety minutes — on grace, foreknowledge, and freedom. I would like to do something more ambitious than a tour of the standard positions. What I want the students to feel rather than hear described is the bind that Molinism is meant to relieve and that its critics press: that God is said to know, before any act of his own will, what each free creature would freely choose in any circumstances in which it might be placed — and that this knowledge is supposed to be neither grounded in the creature's actual choice nor in any divine decree, while still being knowledge of a genuinely free act. Two pressure points should be felt rather than stated: the grounding question (what makes such counterfactuals true, if nothing does?) and the providential dilemma (if the ledger is both fixed and free, no planner can arrange a world in which everyone freely ends well; either some are lost, or the choices are scripted).

Please design one ninety-minute activity that stages this, together with the debrief. Here is a partial design; keep what is good, repair what is not, and supply everything I have left vague.

Before the session I email the students a short form: four hypothetical situations (say, an offer of gain at a friend's expense; an opportunity for anonymous revenge; a public humiliation; a request for costly help), each with a binary choice, and the single question, for each, what would you freely do? I collect the answers. In the session the students are not themselves but an advisory council to a planner who must arrange the circumstances of fifteen souls. Twelve of the souls are the students, anonymised; three I invent, designing them so that they refuse every circumstance on the menu. The council receives the ledger — for each soul, what that soul would freely do in each of the four circumstances — and must assign each soul exactly one circumstance, aiming to bring as many as possible into communion. The rules: the ledger is fixed and independent of what the council decides; no soul may be assigned a circumstance off the menu; a soul's outcome is exactly its ledger entry. As a separate deliverable the council must also draft a short statement to be read to any soul left outside communion, explaining why its placement was just.

I want the recognition to arrive through the structure, not through me. The council should first notice that the three stubborn souls cannot be saved by any placement; then reach for remedies — add a fifth circumstance, amend the ledger — and find that the first is forbidden and futile and the second turns a free refusal into a script. Only then do I reveal the ledger's provenance: the rows are their own answers from the emailed form, and the three impossible souls are mine. I want to leave them with the question of what could make those rows true.

So please give me: the complete rules and materials, including the wording of the pre-session form and a concrete ledger that will actually generate the impossibility (assume I can adjust the planted rows once I have the real answers); a minute-by-minute timeline; a success condition that lets the council feel the failure without real stakes; one or two secret roles I can hand out to stage the rival positions — I am thinking of one student instructed to argue that the council should simply make the stubborn souls come (this is the Dominican alternative, a divine premotion that secures the outcome) and another instructed to argue that the ledger cannot be knowledge of anything at all; the debrief, mapping each beat of the activity onto the corresponding piece of the position — the ordering of divine knowledge into natural, middle, and free, the distinction between middle knowledge and foreknowledge or decree, the grounding objection, and the dilemma — while holding off on the names Molinism, Molina, and middle knowledge until that point, and giving a fair hearing to the answers a Molinist might offer (that such counterfactuals are brute, or grounded in the creature's nature considered apart from its choices); the predictable failures the first time I run it, each with a specific response (I expect students to insist they would simply choose differently, to try to game the ledger, to read the whole exercise as predestination, and to be uneasy about the invented souls); and a frank account of what the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know which part of the position I must still supply in my own voice.

Two constraints: no reading lists or citations, and the students will have looked nothing up, so the activity must stand on its own.
