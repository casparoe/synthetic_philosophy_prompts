I am writing a novel about a woman who agrees, before the embryo exists, to carry a child for her sister, and the more law I read the less I understand what the law believes. In California the agreement must be signed and notarised before the transfer, and a court can declare the intending parents to be the child's parents before the child is born. In Germany the same arrangement is unlawful and egg donation is banned outright, paid or unpaid. In Britain the surrogate may be paid her expenses, but the arrangement may not be brokered for profit, and the agreement itself cannot be enforced. In Spain the eggs may be paid for and the surrogacy may not. And British adoption law will not treat a mother's consent to an adoption order as valid if she gave it less than six weeks after the birth, on the view that consent given earlier is not real consent at all — yet the surrogacy agreement that settles the same question, who the parents are, is signed before conception. What does each of these arrangements presuppose about who a parent is, about whether parenthood can be created by intention or only recognised after the fact, and about what may be sold of a person's body? Which of those commitments does each regime genuinely need, and which parts would sit equally well with rival theories? And if one picture of parenthood were swapped for the other, what else would have to change?
