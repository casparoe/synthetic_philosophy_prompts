I'm a longtime reader of LessWrong and the old SSC comment threads — more lurker than poster — and I've been nursing a half-formed idea that I'd like turned into a short research proposal: the sort of thing you'd submit for a modest philosophy-of-practice grant, say eight to ten pages with a one-page abstract. The idea concerns what people call consequentialist cluelessness, and my suspicion is that the phrase runs together two things that come apart. The first is the ordinary epistemic problem: the causal chain fanning out from any act is so wide and tangled that I can't compute the downstream effects, but the effects are still a determinate matter, so a sufficiently good model with enough time would at least tell me the sign of the expected value — call that resolvable cluelessness. The second is subtler and harder to state: the suspicion that for some choices the expected value is not merely unknown but unsettled, because what the "right" answer is depends on facts about a distant future that our present choices partly constitute, so that there may be no fact of the matter now about which act is better, even in principle. I want a proposal that (a) makes that distinction sharp enough to be a real distinction rather than a verbal trick, (b) shows why the usual consequentialist escapes — rule-consequentialism, satisficing, restricting the expected value to tractable horizons, and the move from acts to decision procedures — each tend to address one flavor while leaving the other untouched, and (c) sketches a concrete way to test whether the second flavor is a genuine problem or dissolves under pressure.

Please state the central question so that it has a determinate answer, give a month-by-month plan assuming a single semester, say what a positive and a negative result would each look like, and offer a candid assessment of whether this is really one project or two smuggled together under one title. I'd also like the framing to take seriously the possibility that I'm just redescribing the first problem in fancier words, and to say what would have to be true for the second flavor to be more than that. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I'll fill in the references myself — but do name the positions and arguments you're leaning on, so I can go check them. Finally, say how confident you are in the framing as a whole, and single out the one step you're least sure survives scrutiny.
