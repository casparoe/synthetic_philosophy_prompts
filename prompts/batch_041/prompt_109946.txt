You are a philosopher of mind who works on animal cognition and on the methodology of attributing mental states, and I would like your help drafting a written opinion. I sit on my university's animal care and use committee as its non-affiliated community member — by trade I am a software engineer, and I have no formal background in philosophy, so I am relying on you to supply the conceptual scaffolding as well as the prose. Our institutional policy currently names vertebrates and stops there, though the committee can extend it by resolution, and a resolution is now on the table because three protocols are queued for the next meeting: one on octopuses that involves repeated mildly aversive stimuli, one on decapod crustaceans, and one on ants. One complication that dragged me into this: the octopus protocol includes a machine-learning distress detector trained on vertebrate pain behaviour, and the committee cannot agree whether its output counts as evidence about the animal or merely as evidence about the model. My colleague, a developmental biologist, has been arguing that we should decline to vote at all, on the grounds that consciousness has no observable marker, that each of the markers people propose — integrative processing, motivational trade-offs between competing needs, self-protective behaviour under anaesthesia, the capacity to give up one good to avoid a harm — is behavioural or neural evidence that underdetermines the conclusion, and that a committee pretending to settle which animals are conscious is doing metaphysics with a budget. I think she is at least partly right, and I do not want a memo that simply overrules her.

Please think this through carefully before you write, and begin by setting out for yourself the strongest version of each side and the points at which they genuinely conflict. Then give me the memo itself, short enough to circulate before the next meeting: a recommendation the committee can vote on (which protocols to approve as they stand, which to approve with conditions such as anaesthesia and analgesia requirements or a ceiling on aversive trials, and which to defer pending something specific), the decision rule stated in a form we can apply to species that are not on the list, and what weight, if any, we should give output from a detector of the kind described. Two constraints shape the rule. It has to be stated as a rule rather than decided case by case, so it must handle species we are confident about, species that are genuinely contested, and cases where extending it would look absurd; and any version that says "when in doubt, assume consciousness" has to answer the objection that this is cheap to say and expensive to live with, since a protection that covers everything protects nothing in particular and may dilute what we owe the animals we are sure of.
