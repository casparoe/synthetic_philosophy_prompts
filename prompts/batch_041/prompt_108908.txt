I spent most of Tuesday evening arguing with a friend — a philosopher of religion and a committed Molinist — about whether the grounding objection to middle knowledge is fatal, and I would like to build this year's graduate final around the part of the dispute we could not settle. Please write one exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme, in that order and under those headings.

The question is for a graduate seminar in philosophical theology: ninety minutes, twenty marks. It must not be answerable by reciting the doctrine of the three kinds of divine knowledge. The crux I want students to reach is that a counterfactual of freedom cannot be grounded in the creature without either collapsing middle knowledge into natural knowledge or making the creature unfree, and therefore has to be either brute or grounded in something the Molinist cannot have.

To force that, build the question around a case I invented during the argument. Suppose God considers two creatures who are perfect duplicates of one another down to the last intrinsic feature and who are placed in exactly the same circumstances. On the libertarian assumption the Molinist needs, it must be possible for one to accept a temptation and the other to refuse it, so the two counterfactuals of freedom have opposite truth values even though there is nothing about either creature to make them so. Then add the second step: God creates neither, or creates only the one, so that the free act the counterfactual is about never occurs at all. Students should have to say what, if anything, makes the counterfactual true, and whether the Molinist can pay that price without losing either divine sovereignty or the free will defence.

Keep the question itself to about 150 words and the model answer to about 700, written in the voice of a strong graduate student who takes a clear verdict at the end. Give the marking scheme as five numbered must-hit points of four marks each, and after each point a single line saying how candidates typically get that point wrong. No citations or reading lists — the students have no library access during the exam.
