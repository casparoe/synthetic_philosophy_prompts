Ok this is a wierdly specific thing to get hung up on but I need to get it out of my system. I'm a first year PhD student and I just started TAing our intro to proofs course, and last week a student in office hours asked me whether 2 is an element of 3. I'd just put the von Neumann definition of the naturals on the board, so the honest answer is yes, and I said yes, and she asked whether that's actually true or just how we happened to build them. I said it doesn't matter, any construction gives you the same structure, use whichever is convinient. She said, ok, then which one is the number 2? And I completely floundered and changed the subject. It's been eating at me for a week now.

What's bugging me is that the whole way we run the course seems to presuppose that the answer doesn't matter, and I genuinely don't know what that commitment is. We define the naturals as those particular sets, then two chapters later we prove things about the natural numbers like there's one object sitting there, and we never once say what we think 2 is. We put a homework problem on the exam where 2 ∈ 3 comes out true, mark it correct, and then in the same breath tell students the construction is arbitrary. Same deal in the grad seminar I sit in on, where the professor slides between Dedekind cuts and Cauchy sequences for the reals and calls it the same thing without blinking. And a friend who does formal verification tells me that in the set theory based proof assistant he uses, the naturals literally are the von Neumann ordinals, so membership facts like that are theorems you can prove, but if you switch to a library that builds them a different way those theorems just aren't there anymore and you have to redo everything. Which makes the choice sound a lot less innocent than we pretend.

So what I want to know is: what philosophical commitments does this practice actually presuppose? Defining the numbers by a particular set theoretic construction, proving theorems about them, and then treating the choice of construction as a matter of taste. Does it presuppose that numbers are positions in a structure rather than objects with their own identity? Does it presuppose that we can know things about abstract objects at all, and if so, how, given that nobody has ever bumped into a 2? Which rival views about what numbers are does it quietly rule out? Is it internally consistent, or does it need two incompatible things at once? And where is it just underdetermined, i.e. what's the range of views about numbers that would all license exactly what we do? Finally, if we stopped hedging and took the construction seriously, so that the number 2 really just is that set, what would actually have to change about how we teach and grade this stuff?
