I recently served on a mass-casualty triage panel where we had to choose between a plan that would save more lives and a plan that gave each identified patient an equal chance, and the disagreement has followed me home. Please act as a philosopher of ethics and decision theory who knows the debate over whether numbers count, and help me draft the joint statement that a committed Taurekian—someone who denies that numbers as such give any reason to save more rather than fewer—and a committed aggregator—someone who holds that each additional life saved adds a reason—could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. Start with a brief private brainstorm of the candidate common ground and the possible merely verbal disputes, then produce the document itself: a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table. The agreed claims should cover what each side can accept about the separateness of persons, the moral status of averted deaths, the difference between a reason to save a person and a reason to prefer a state of affairs in which more are saved, and the legitimacy of lotteries and weighted lotteries in rescue decisions. The table should state each residual disagreement in wording that the side holding it would accept, identify the cruxes, and for each crux give a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. To keep this from turning into a rehearsal of the familiar five-versus-one example, build the statement around the Split Ward: a fixed rescue budget can either save 100 people who would each live another 20 years in full health, or save 101 people who would each live another 19 years and 11 months, with no promises, special obligations, or prior relationships on either side. Both sides should say whether the numbers count here and why, and whether the answer changes if the 101st person can be saved only by an act that foreseeably kills one of the 100. If you find that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why; if a crux cannot be tested even in principle, say so rather than inventing evidence.
