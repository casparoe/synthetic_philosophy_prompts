In a committee meeting last month we were deciding whether an employee hardship fund should cover a colleague who had run up substantial debts after declining the subsidised insurance the scheme offers and after a stretch of what everyone present agreed was reckless spending. The chair closed the discussion with the remark that you make your bed and then you lie in it, and most of the room treated that as settling the matter. I recognised the sentiment as the folk version of the position philosophers call luck egalitarianism, but I could not tell whether the proverb was doing any real work or merely marking a refusal to think the case through, and I suspect the same sentence can be read as several quite different claims, some of them too obvious to be worth uttering and some of them very strong indeed.

Please treat the proverb as a slogan and lay out as a menu the distinct precise claims it could be making about choice, responsibility, desert, and what we owe the imprudent. For each reading, state what it asserts, whether it is trivial, plausible, contentious, or simply false, and what further premises it needs to do any work. I want to know which readings are genuine articulations of luck egalitarianism and which belong instead to neighbouring positions it is commonly confused with — strict egalitarianism, desert-based justice, and the libertarian thought that a person's imprudence forfeits their claim on the resources of others — and, among those, which would actually license the chair's conclusion that the fund should refuse help rather than merely explain why the colleague's position is unfortunate. I should also like you to say which reading the chair most plausibly meant and which one the room probably heard. Two further matters. First, say which reading leaves luck egalitarianism exposed to the familiar objection that it abandons the imprudent to their misfortune, and which readings defuse or evade that objection. Second, say what becomes of each reading once we notice that the recklessness was itself shaped by unchosen factors, so that the line between choice and luck threatens to shift indefinitely; where a reading collapses under that pressure, say so plainly rather than rescuing it. Write your answer in British English.
