My friend and I spent half of last night arguing about crisis triage — you know, the protocols hospitals use when there arent enough ventilators or ICU beds, where the rule is save the most lives, or the most life-years, and during covid some states actually published scoring sheets that ranked patients by how many years they'd probably get back. What I want to know is what that practice presupposes about persons. Does save-the-most require treating people as a pool whose claims get added together — which is basically the separateness-of-persons objection, that utilitarianism treats the distinctness between persons like the distinctness between one person's moments, so it can sacrifice one for five? Or can you run a maximize-survivors protocol while still holding that each person is a separate locus of claims that can't be traded off, with something else doing the work (ex ante agreement, consent, the fact that triage only allocates and doesn't kill)? Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it's merely compatible with, and where it's underdetermined — my hunch is that several very different views of the person license the exact same scoring sheet, and I'd like to know if that's right. And then the part that really bugs me: the same system that will let one patient die to save five in a crunch absolutely will not take one healthy person's organs to save five people on the transplant list — it runs a waiting list, and urgency sometimes beats life-years. Is that a genuine inconsistency in what we presuppose about persons, or just the killing/letting-die difference? And if the triage practice does presuppose aggregation, what would a non-aggregating version of it even look like — is that a coherent thing to build, or does scarcity just force the aggregation?
