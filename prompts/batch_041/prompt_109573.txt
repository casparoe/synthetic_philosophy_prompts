Assume, for the sake of argument, that Hannah Arendt had lived to the present decade. I am not asking what she would have enjoyed saying, or what her temperament would have led her to; I am asking what her concepts compel once they are turned on material she never saw. Treat this as reconstruction, not ventriloquism.

Consider four developments that postdate her.

1. The synthetic particular. A fabricated audio or video record — a head of state apparently ordering an atrocity, a candidate apparently accepting a bribe — becomes indistinguishable in practice from a genuine one. It is released days before a vote and reaches tens of millions before any authentication is possible, and by the time it is debunked the vote is over.

2. The decay of the record. The mere existence of (1) means that genuine records can now be waved away as fabrications. The damage is not a false belief about a particular event but the loss of standing of any evidentiary record: whatever the tape shows, it can always be denied, and the denial costs the denier nothing.

3. The self-sealing story. A public claim that an entire apparatus — elections, a medical establishment, a press corps — is a coordinated fiction, sustained not by any single assertion that could be refuted but by a narrative that absorbs each disconfirming fact as fresh proof of the conspiracy.

4. Falsehood without a liar. Claims that circulate with no identifiable author: generated, recombined, and re-amplified by automated systems and by crowds indifferent to their origin, so that no one can be said to have told the lie, and no one can be held to have believed it either.

I want your reconstruction of her verdicts on the following.

(a) She distinguishes, in her writing on truth and politics, between the ordinary political lie — a specific falsehood told by someone who knows better, to a limited audience, and therefore parasitic on the very distinction between true and false that it violates — and a modern form of lying that does not target a particular fact but the context that makes facts intelligible, aiming to make the fiction into the world. Sort 1–4: which are lies in her sense, and which belong to a different category — ideology, propaganda, self-deception, or a pathology of the common world rather than an act at all?

(b) Her account of the totalitarian fabrication seems to require a liar: the lie is a deed, attributable to someone whose freedom to depart from reality is exactly what makes it possible. Does (4) break that requirement? If no one authors the claim, has she the resources to call it a lie? And if not, is the authorship condition doing real work in her theory, or is it an artifact of the cases she happened to have?

(c) Would she have ranked (2) — the general collapse of the record's authority — as graver than any particular falsehood in (1), or would she have thought that putting the crisis of truth at the center mistakes the political problem, given her insistence that factual truth is in tension with a realm whose currency is opinion and persuasion rather than demonstration? I want the tension between her defense of factual truth and her suspicion of truth's political authority resolved, not smoothed over.

(d) Her framework was written before the remedies now on offer: provenance and authentication standards for media, platform liability, criminal penalties for synthetic deception of officials. Which remedy does her position favor, and which does it forbid as an attempt to make truth politically authoritative?

(e) One prediction I want pinned down. How would she distinguish a synthetic fabrication from a genuine record of a real event, cropped and captioned so that it asserts the opposite of what occurred? Many people find the second worse than the first. Does her account agree, and if it does, on what principle?

State the principle you attribute to her, the arguments it rests on, and the exact point at which you are extrapolating beyond anything she wrote. Where her commitments pull against each other, say which one you take to be load-bearing and why.
