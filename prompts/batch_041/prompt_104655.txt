For most of my adult life, the question whether an unjust rule can still be law belonged to the world of examinations. It stopped being academic this spring, when a disciplinary panel applied such a rule to me — a rule I had declined to comply with, on grounds I still hold. I do not want my own case relitigated here. I want adjudicated an argument that broke out at the hearing between two members of the panel, which I have set down as faithfully as I can. I have read enough to recognize the outline of the old quarrel between legal positivism and natural law, and I do not want a recapitulation of that literature; I want these two arguments, as actually made, judged.

The first member said: "The rule is valid law. It was adopted by the body that had authority to adopt it, it stands on the books, and it has been applied even-handedly. That it is unjust is a separate question, and a serious one, but it does not alter the rule's legal status. Keeping validity and morality apart is precisely what forces us to say the honest thing — this is law, it is unjust, and we must now decide whether to obey it anyway. The alternative, declaring that unjust rules simply are not law, lets us feel righteous while concealing the real choice we are making."

The second replied: "I call that laundering. A rule that reaches a certain pitch of injustice forfeits its claim to be law; calling it law confers an authority it has already lost. And here we need not even reach the moral threshold. Consider how the rule was made and used: it was never properly promulgated to those it binds, it was applied retroactively to conduct that predated it, and it demanded something that could not reliably be done. A rule that fails those requirements is defective on the law's own terms — not merely unjust, but no rule at all. It is the institution's own craft, not my moral preferences, that disqualifies it."

The first then said: "Your second argument surrenders your first. Generality, promulgation, prospectivity, clarity, consistency — a legal system can satisfy every one of those and still be monstrous; the most orderly persecutions are perfectly clear and perfectly consistent. So the inner discipline you invoke shields no one from injustice; it protects the law's own operation. And the moment you fall back on a threshold of injustice, you have traded the test of legality for a test of morality, and you owe us a line you cannot draw, since there is no non-arbitrary place to put it. My separation, by contrast, draws no line it cannot defend: validity is one thing, obedience another, and I never promised that valid law would be good law."

The second answered: "Yet you persist in treating validity as a switch that is either on or off. In practice we reason about a rule's authority by degrees — how it was made, whether it can be known, whether it can be followed — and those degrees are not arbitrary; they track whether the rule can claim the allegiance of those it binds. You want a bright line so that you may avoid the judgment. I say the judgment is the law's business."

I am not telling you which of them I favor, because I genuinely do not know. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, rating each step and saying where each speaker is right, where each overreaches, and what both are missing. In particular: (1) Is the first speaker right that separating validity from morality is a virtue because it is honest, or is the second right that it launders? (2) Is the first speaker's objection to the "inner discipline" — that it is compatible with great iniquity and therefore morally inert — decisive, or does the second speaker's requirement do work the first is not seeing? (3) When the second speaker introduces a threshold of injustice, has he in fact shifted to a moral test, and if so, is the first right that no non-arbitrary threshold exists? (4) Is the second speaker's closing move — that authority comes in degrees — a genuine refinement or an evasion? Finally, tell me what both of them are missing, and whether this is a disagreement about the concept of law at all, or whether the vocabulary of validity and morality is concealing a different question — about the ethics of obedience, the role of an institutional decision-maker, or the nature of complicity — that the two of them would do better to argue directly.
