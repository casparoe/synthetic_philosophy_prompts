I hold a view I'll state in my own words before asking you to attack it. Call it the strategy-stealing assumption: an agent that can identify and model another agent's decision procedure can "steal" that procedure and adopt it as its own policy; and because the stolen policy, played against its owner, produces whatever the owner would do when facing itself, a reflective agent can always secure an outcome at least as good as the outcome its opponent secures. On that basis I also accept the further claim that two sufficiently reflective agents facing a symmetric cooperation problem — a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, or a stag hunt with no communication — will cooperate. Each can steal the other's strategy; among the policies a reflective agent might settle on, only the cooperative one is stable when played against itself; so the pair lands on mutual cooperation rather than mutual defection. I care about this because the same reasoning is often used to argue that sufficiently capable AI systems will end up coordinating with one another rather than competing, and I want to know how much weight it can actually bear.

Tell me what would count against the view, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they'd be, and I want you to distinguish the ones a committed defender would themselves concede from the ones only critics press. Please cover, at minimum:

- the step from "you match your opponent's payoff" to "you cooperate" — whether stealing ever yields cooperation as opposed to mere symmetry, and whether mutual defection is not also a fixed point of the stolen strategy played against itself, in which case what selects cooperation over it and whether that selection is an extra premise rather than a consequence of stealing;
- the fixed-point or regress problem: if each agent's policy is defined in terms of what the other does, what makes the description well-posed, and what happens when an agent will cooperate only if it can establish that the other will too, while the other is running the same reasoning;
- whether "stealing" helps only when the opponent's action is correlated with mine rather than merely copied by me, and how the view fares if the correlation has to be evidential rather than causal;
- the idealization itself: which real agents, human or artificial, would have to fail for the view to be testable, and whether the view risks becoming unfalsifiable — every failure reassigned to insufficient reflectivity — and, if so, whether that is a fatal defect or an acceptable feature of a normative ideal;
- any behavioral, evolutionary, or machine-learning considerations that bear on it, with an honest verdict on how much they can show.

I'd be most moved by a demonstration that the argument proves too little — that it delivers only symmetry, not cooperation — so tell me whether that is the real weak point or whether the deeper trouble lies elsewhere. Do not give me a reading list; argue it on the merits. For each substantial claim, say how confident you are, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
