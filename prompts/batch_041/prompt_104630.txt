Ok, so I've fallen down the "did they actually collapse or did they just... change" rabbit hole again, and I want to watch the two positions actually fight it out instead of reading another essay that splits the difference.

Here's the case I want them to fight over, and it's made up on purpose so nobody can just pattern-match it onto the Maya or Easter Island. Call it the Corridor: a chain of river valleys that by around 800 CE had cities, a writing system, monumental architecture, and long-distance trade. By around 1100 CE the cities are empty, the script is undeciphered, and the population is maybe a fifth of what it was, living in small farming villages. Two things make it weird. First, it took about 250 years and nobody alive experienced a fall — no chronicler writes "and then it ended"; the grandchildren of city-dwellers just think village life is life. Second, by most measures the average person in 1100 ate better, worked less, and died less violently than the average person in 800.

Now write the dialogue between two people who both know all of that and still disagree about whether the Corridor collapsed.

The realist thinks it's a real collapse and that the "but they were fine" move is cope. Her line is that collapse should be defined by the loss, not the speed or the vibes: a society has collapsed when it loses capacities — the script, the trade network, the ability to build the big things — that it can't regenerate within, say, the lifetime of its own institutions. Slow is fine, no event required. And she's happy to bite the bullet that the villagers eating better is irrelevant, or even that eating better is just what collapse usually looks like from below.

The continuist thinks "collapse" is a folk category that does no explanatory work. His line is that all that happened is some variables fell and others rose, that "collapse" always smuggles in a baseline — complexity? cities? elites? — and that once you ask "collapse of what, measured how, valued by whom" the word falls apart. He thinks the 250-year timeline actually helps him, because there's no event to point at, just a slope you'd only even notice in hindsight.

Make them actually engage, not talk past each other. The realist has to give a non-cheap answer to "collapse of what" — not just "obviously complexity." The continuist has to give a non-cheap answer to "so nothing ever goes badly for a society, it's all just change?" — no lazy relativism. Let them press each other on whether the slow timeline is a point for or against, on whether the peasants eating better is evidence about the collapse or just a distraction, and on whether any conceivable evidence could settle it. I want the real crux between them, and each one should land at least one hit the other has to absorb rather than deflect — neither of them is an idiot and neither is a strawman.

Don't end with "both are right in their own way." I want them to stall in a real place, with one of them saying something the other has to admit he can't answer yet — that's the interesting part. Casual voice, no citations, don't bother reciting the real historical cases, the Corridor is invented and that's definately the whole point.
