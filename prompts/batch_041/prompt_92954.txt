I write novels for a living (or near enough), but this year I've somehow ended up as writer-in-residence in a philosophy department, and the woman who runs the masters module on decision theory has asked me to contribute one essay question to the final assessment — her reasoning being that it's good for the students to get a question from someone outside the discipline, mine being that I get a nice dinner out of it. The topic she's given me is Good's theorem on the value of information. I know roughly what it says: for a Bayesian expected-utility maximiser, the expected value of information can't be negative, because whatever you learn you can always ignore it and choose the act you would have chosen anyway. I also know that 'you can always ignore it' is doing an enormous amount of unexamined work in that sentence, and that's where I want the question to live. Assume the exam is sat in a hall with no internet, so please don't build anything into the question that needs them to cite or look anything up — no reading lists, no references; it has to stand on its own.

What I'd like from you: the question itself; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme out of 20, with the marks attached to specific must-hit moves and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each of them wrong. It's a 90-minute essay paper, and I'd like the question to have two or three short parts as long as they hang together, rather than three separate questions stapled together. The thing I care about most is that a student can't scrape a decent mark by reciting 'free information is always worth having' and padding it out for a page and a half. I'm not a philosopher, so don't give me a question that's secretly just a vocabulary test.

I've been fiddling with a little two-state example and I'd like the question built on it. A character has finished a novel. The market will either be hungry for it or glutted, fifty-fifty as far as she can tell. If she publishes now she gets 10 in the hungry state and 0 in the glutted one; if she holds it back a year she gets a steady 4 either way. So she publishes, and the decision is worth 5 to her. If she knew the state in advance she'd publish when hungry and hold back otherwise, which is worth 7, so on the face of it the information is worth 2. Now the version I actually want to use: she can commision a market report that will tell her the state, and she's the sort of person who cannot bear knowing her book would have flopped. Learning that the market is glutted costs her 8 of misery; learning it's hungry gives her 1 of pleasure. That makes the report's net value negative even though the decision problem hasn't otherwise budged. I genuinely can't tell whether that's a counterexample to Good's theorem or simply a case of information that isn't free in the relevant sense, and that argument is exactly what I want the exam question to stage, so the marking scheme should tell me which side a strong answer has to be able to argue and why the other side isn't stupid, and it should seperate the A from the B by naming the moves. Beyond that, please have the question force them apart on the ex-ante/ex-post thing — what the theorem does and doesn't promise about how things turn out — and on what exactly the 'ignore it' step presupposes, with at least one part where they have to say where the theorem's premises come apart once you leave the single-agent expected-utility picture. I'd rather they got there through an example of their own (a game where knowing more hurts, a committee whose members start from different priors, someone who can't stand ambiguity) than by naming papers, which they won't be able to do anyway. And in the marking scheme, please flag the errors I can already see coming: students thinking the theorem promises that information is good for you once you've got it, and students thinking risk aversion breaks it. British English throughout, please.
