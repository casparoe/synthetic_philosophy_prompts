I'm assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of consciousness, and I want a dialogue my students can argue about for a full session. The exchange should be between two philosophers: one who holds that phenomenal character is a genuine, introspectively accessible feature of experience, and one who follows Dennett in thinking that the notion of qualia as intrinsic, private, ineffable, and incorrigibly given properties is not merely false but incoherent — a posit that dissolves under pressure rather than a hypothesis that gets refuted. Neither character may be a strawman. The realist should be able to press the strongest version of her case, and the eliminativist should not be allowed to win by quietly redefining the target out of existence; if the eliminativist's position is really just the claim that experiences have functional roles, the realist is entitled to say so and to demand that the eliminativist own it.

To keep this from being a reheated tour of the standard intuition pumps, build the dialogue around a case I've been toying with and would like you to develop rather than merely mention. Suppose a subject undergoes a nightly phenomenal recalibration: each night a device nudges the tuning of her sensory transducers by an imperceptible increment, calibrated so that all of her downstream responses — color naming, matching, discrimination thresholds, memory reports, aesthetic preferences, and even her verbal avowals about how things look — remain exactly what they would have been without the nudge. She notices nothing, then or later, and no test can detect any change; over a year the increments compound into a large cumulative shift. The two characters should fight over whether there is any fact of the matter about whether what red looks like to her now is the same as what red looked like a year ago, and what that verdict does to the realist's claim that qualia are introspectively accessible intrinsic properties. The realist will want to insist that the case is perfectly intelligible and that its intelligibility is itself evidence for qualia, since a difference can be real even if no one could ever know it; the eliminativist will want to insist that the case exposes the notion as idle machinery, since if no possible observation, report, or disposition could distinguish the two hypotheses, the alleged difference is not a hidden fact but a piece of metaphysical furniture we built and then forgot we built.

Have them actually engage rather than talk past each other. The realist should get to deploy the objection that the eliminativist has simply changed the subject from phenomenal character to functional role, and the eliminativist should have to answer that objection without simply asserting that everything mental is functional. Let the argument reach a genuine crux — ideally one where the realist's position begins to look as though it requires something the case renders unintelligible, and the eliminativist's position begins to look as though it requires denying something the realist finds self-evident. Do not resolve it with a tidy handshake, and do not have either character concede more than the argument actually forces. Aim for roughly 1200 to 1800 words. Begin directly with the dialogue itself — no scene-setting preamble, no title, and no commentary before or after.
