I have been trying for some time to find a name for a pattern that I keep meeting whenever the relations between churches and states are argued about, and nothing I have found fits it exactly. I shall describe it as I have actually met it, since I suspect the description will have to do most of the work. I have lived long enough in countries with quite different settlements to have seen all of the following.

First, where there is an established church — bishops by right in the upper house of the legislature, the head of state its supreme governor, its buildings and its rites taken for granted at national occasions — the case for keeping the arrangement is made most forcefully by people who neither believe nor attend. They defend it as public infrastructure: a fabric of parishes and buildings, a supply of funerals, weddings and civic ceremonies, a body the state can hold to public account. The case for dismantling it is made, quite as forcefully, by people who do belong — clergy and laity who argue that the connection has compromised their church and that it cannot say what it believes so long as it is an arm of the state.

Second, where there is no establishment, but the state collects a levy on behalf of religious bodies as a surcharge on income tax, and where leaving a body requires a formal act registered with the civil authorities, the arrangement is defended by people who have registered that act or were never members, because the money pays for hospitals, schools and social work that they use; and it is attacked by devout members of small bodies that stand outside the levy, who hold that a congregation whose funds arrive through the tax office has already given up the thing that made it a congregation.

Third, where the constitution forbids establishment, or where a doctrine of strict separation is treated as the settlement itself, that doctrine is defended with something like religious fervour by people who hold no religious belief, and is experienced as an imposition by believers whose practice it restricts; while the surviving traces of public religion — an exemption, a prayer at the opening of a session — are defended by people who say they are not religious and regard the practices as part of the furniture of public life.

What I think I am seeing has two parts. The first is that attachment to the settlement is a poor guide to the strength of anyone's belief, and can run exactly inverse: those who use the arrangement least defend it hardest, and those who use it most attack it hardest. The second is that the two sides are not answering one another. One defends the arrangement as infrastructure, the other attacks it as doctrine; the arguments pass without meeting, and yet each is aimed precisely at the other's exposed flank — the defender at his having no use for the church he is defending, the critic at his dismantling a service he does not pay for.

My questions are these. Does this pattern have a name? If it has not, I should rather have a considered coinage and a clear account of its edges than a bare label. In particular: is the inversion essential to it, or incidental? Does it require an established church, or can the same structure arise wherever the state merely funds, exempts, consults or protects religious bodies? How is it distinguished from ordinary coalition politics, in which people support arrangements that suit them for reasons that have nothing to do with belief? And is it a species of hypocrisy, or does something escape that description — for the defenders need not be insincere, since they may genuinely hold the arrangement to be valuable for reasons that have nothing to do with belief? Finally, what would count as a clear case and a clear non-case, so that I can tell when I am seeing this pattern and when I am seeing something else?

The nearest things I have come across seem to me to be about which people practise and which merely belong, or about religion taken as a badge of nationhood, and neither is quite what I mean, since my puzzle concerns who defends the settlement rather than who is religious. Please answer in American English.
