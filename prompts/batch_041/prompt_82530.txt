In the virtual world Athanor, the engine that runs the world is itself simulated inside Athanor: the physics of the world is a program that denizens can, with sufficient skill, read and rewrite. There is no external scoring and no goal. What there is, is property, residence, reputation, and history, all stored as facts about the world's own state. Athanor is not sealed off from ordinary life: holdings are bought and sold for ordinary currency, and a large number of denizens conduct most of their friendships, their income, and their standing there.

A guild, the Lapidaries, rewrites the local rules of a district so that a rival's title deed, an object whose existence conditions are stated in that district's rule-set, has never existed there. They succeed completely. Every record inside Athanor now agrees that the rival never held the deed, and no denizen can assemble evidence to the contrary. Only the operator's out-of-world logs show what was done. The rival loses her residence, her livelihood, and, in the eyes of those who knew her, her history.

Write a dialogue between two philosophers who have both studied this case and disagree about what it shows.

The first holds that virtual objects are real objects: abstract artifacts whose existence conditions are fixed by the rules and the sustained practices of the world that hosts them. On this view virtual harms are real harms, harms to real things, and not merely harms to people's feelings about fictions.

The second holds that virtual objects are fictional objects: what exists in Athanor exists in a fiction, and what the Lapidaries changed was what the fiction says, not what there is. On this view the rival was genuinely wronged, but the wrong is located entirely in the ordinary losses it caused, and no object was destroyed, because there was no object to destroy.

Let each speaker argue the case rather than survey the literature; neither should cite or gesture at existing work, and neither should be given a position the other can dismantle with a slogan. The dialogue should satisfy the following.

The realist must say what fixes the deed's existence conditions once we grant that the rules stating them are themselves editable from inside the world, and must answer whether the deed was real at the time if nothing inside Athanor now retains a trace of it. The fictionalist must say who authors the fiction when the world's rules can be rewritten by its inhabitants, and must answer the objection that if the wrong is exhausted by the ordinary losses, then a lawful eviction would have wronged the rival just as much, leaving the erasure of the deed itself to do no work.

Require each speaker to state a criterion for when a virtual harm is real, and then to apply that criterion to a contrasting case: a denizen who loses an object she cared about greatly but whose income, residence, and friendships are unaffected. Each speaker should accept at least one of the other's points and say what it costs them, and each should press at least one distinction the other has to either take on board or reject outright. Let them disagree about whether the self-hosting feature of Athanor makes the fictionalist's position unstable or the realist's position trivial, and about whether the operator's external logs matter to the metaphysics or only to the evidence.

End with the point of deepest remaining disagreement stated plainly, rather than a synthesis. Do not let either speaker win by rhetoric or by caricature, and do not resolve the case on the reader's behalf.

Give the speakers invented names that are not the names of real philosophers. Use speaker labels and continuous prose; keep stage directions to a minimum. Aim for roughly 1200 to 1800 words.
