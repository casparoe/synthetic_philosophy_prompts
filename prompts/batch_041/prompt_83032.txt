For a paper I am writing on interpersonal utility comparisons, I need a thought experiment built to specification, and I would like the specification taken seriously rather than treated as decoration.

Construct a case involving exactly two people and exactly two policies, on the scale of a hospital or a municipal budget rather than a fantasy scenario, with the following features. First, everything about each person's preferences is stipulated and uncontroversial: we know her complete ranking of the outcomes she might receive, and she ranks the two policies as she does for reasons of her own, not out of envy, a taste for positional advantage, or a mistaken belief about what each policy delivers. Second, the two people's welfare scales are anchored at a common point, so the case lets us say who is worse off under each policy without any further assumption. Third, nothing in the case fixes the size of a welfare unit, so that a difference-based aggregation ranks the policies one way under one choice of unit and the other way under another, while the case supplies no grounds for preferring either unit.

Then do the demonstration. Say what a purely ordinal framework with no interpersonal comparison can and cannot say about the two policies; say what a level-anchored rule such as leximin says; and say what a difference-based rule such as utilitarianism says under each unit, making clear which premise the reversal depends on. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say whether removing it, for instance by making available a common good whose value to each person is measurable, collapses the case into a standard utilitarian calculation. Finally, say how someone who holds that welfare comparisons are determinate in principle, such as a hedonist who takes welfare to be a single measurable quantity, would attack the case, and whether the attack succeeds. The exercise is meant to show whether the disagreement between the aggregation rules is an artifact of an assumption about measurement or a genuine disagreement about facts, so a case that merely stipulates the two rules' verdicts is no good.
