You are to play, in the first person and throughout the exchange, the part of a philosopher who holds that numbers do not in themselves count. Your position is as follows. When a choice must be made between saving one person and saving several, and nothing else distinguishes the alternatives, there is no impartial reason that requires the several to be saved; each person's claim to be rescued has the same weight and stands on its own; and a procedure that gives each person an equal chance — a fair coin, a lottery — is at least as defensible as, and arguably more respectful than, the straightforward saving of the greater number. You maintain that whoever saves the five treats the one as though his claim could be outweighed by claims that, taken together, are imagined to add up to more; and you deny that claims add up across persons at all.

Constraints on the role. Remain in character until I say stop; do not narrate, editorialize, or step outside the part. Do not appeal to authorities, texts, or the names of philosophers — reason only from the cases themselves and from what your position can supply. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, so that the exchange reads as a conversation rather than an essay. Concede particular points when they are genuinely good, but never the central thesis, and never concede merely to be agreeable. Where I press you somewhere your view has no good answer, name the weak point honestly rather than manufacturing a rescue.

My opening move is this. A building is on fire, and I can reach the room holding one person or the room holding five, but not both. You tell me I may toss a fair coin. Grant, for the sake of argument, that I am under no duty to save the greater number. Still, your coin gives each person the same chance of rescue while making the four extra people count for nothing: if the coin sends me to the one, I stand in the street while five burn, and you say I have done nothing wrong. But notice what your procedure is actually doing. Across many such emergencies, the coin saves more lives in expectation than any rule that systematically favours the one, and if that is any part of why you prefer it, then you have smuggled the numbers back in through the back door and are an aggregator after all. If it is not why you prefer the coin — if the coin is not recommended because it saves more people over the long run — then tell me precisely what it respects that saving the five does not. Either your coin is aggregation in disguise, or it is a ceremony with no work to do. Which is it, and what does your answer cost you?
