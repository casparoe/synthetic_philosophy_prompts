I am a novelist, and each book I write rests on a claim about evolved human nature the way another novelist's book might rest on a piece of family history. That means I stake two years of work and my editor's goodwill on a premise before anyone qualified tells me it was a just-so story. I would like you to turn the dispute over evolutionary psychology — the charge that its explanations are stories told after the fact, and the defence that they can be tested by assembling independent lines of evidence — into a procedure I can actually run at the kitchen table, from reasoning alone. I cannot consult the literature and do not want a reading list or citations; anything that depends on knowing who said what is of no use to me.

Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to me because what is at stake is taste or values rather than evidence. It has to handle the rival explanations its critics keep raising, and it has to take a position on whether a hypothesis that cannot be tested directly is disqualified or merely weaker — if that dispute cannot be settled without the literature, say so and build the ambiguity in rather than hiding it. Tell me which distinctions you had to sacrifice to make the procedure usable, and where it will misfire. The end product should be a verdict I can defend in one sentence at an acquisitions meeting.

Then run it on three candidate premises: that our appetite for stories with endings is an adaptation for transmitting information useful to survival; that men and women differ in the kind of jealousy they find unbearable, as a consequence of differing parental investment; and that a reluctance to eat in front of strangers is an evolved anti-theft adaptation, on the grounds that the eater's hands were occupied. I expect one of these to fare worse than I want and another better than I expect, and I would rather know now. Finish by saying what the procedure cannot do, and how a premise that fails it might still earn its place in a novel — as something a character believes rather than something the book quietly asserts.

Please write in British English.
