I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of language and mind, and the fifth session is on Davidson's account of radical interpretation. My students can all recite that belief and meaning have to be interpreted together, and that a good interpreter must find the speaker largely right about the world; what they cannot do is feel why this is supposed to be a constitutive constraint on interpretation rather than a piece of methodological politeness, or why Davidson treats the residual indeterminacy as harmless. I want a 90-minute session for fourteen students in which the constraint is something they are forced to obey in order to finish the task at all, and I would much rather they arrive at it by discovering they cannot proceed without it than by being told about it. The group is mixed -- some are here for mind, some for language -- so the activity should not presuppose that anyone has already read the primary texts closely, and it must run without me lecturing up front. What they should come away seeing is (i) that any manual they build already embeds a verdict about what the speakers believe, so there is no way to fix meanings first and beliefs afterwards, (ii) that when the data underdetermine the manual, charity is what makes one manual usable and the other unusable rather than merely less elegant, and (iii) that this looks like a worrying indeterminacy from inside the exercise even though Davidson insists it is not one.

My rough plan, which you should improve on but not replace: half the group plays informants who speak a small invented language with a fixed lexicon and fixed dispositions to assent, dissent, or say nothing; the other half plays field linguists who may only record which utterances the informants produce or accept in which observable scenes. No questions, no pointing, no negotiation, no miming, no translating, and the informants work from a one-page sheet they can play without understanding any of the philosophy. Build the informants' sheet so that a purely bottom-up, word-by-word dictionary yields at least two manuals that fit every scene the linguists will actually see, and so that one of those manuals makes the informants massively mistaken about the world -- denying what is plainly in front of them, or failing to distinguish a live animal from a picture or a statue of one -- while the other makes them merely different in what they attend to or how they hedge. The linguists must publish a manual stated as rules that generate truth conditions for sentences, not a word list, since I want the holism to bite when they revise one entry and watch it propagate. At the end I want to spring a held-out scene on which their manual and the rival one diverge, so the choice of charity becomes a real choice with consequences. Design the lexicon, the scene cards, and the held-out card concretely enough that I can photocopy them, tell me roughly how many scenes each side needs to see before the puzzle bites, and build in some small, non-monetary stake so the linguists are scored rather than merely amused -- for instance, their manual is graded against scenes they were not shown, and the informants' sheet carries a hidden condition they are trying to satisfy.
