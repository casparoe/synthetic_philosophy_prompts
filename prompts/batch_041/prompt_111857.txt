You are a philosopher who works on nineteenth-century Danish thought and, separately, on the legal treatment of conscientious objection in modern liberal states. I want you to predict how the pseudonymous author of Fear and Trembling, Johannes de Silentio, writing in 1843, would analyse a legal arrangement that did not exist in his lifetime. Restrict yourself to the conceptual resources available to that author plus the description I give you below; do not import later scholarship, later ethical vocabulary, or anything the author could not have understood.

Think carefully before answering. Begin with a short numbered list brainstorming which features of the case a follower of Silentio would treat as philosophically salient, and which he would treat as distractions. Then give your predicted analysis, in the author's own idiom as far as you can reconstruct it.

The arrangement. A liberal state enacts a statute under which a public official may be exempted from performing a duty of her office on grounds of conscience. The exemption is not automatic. The official must lodge with an administrative board a written statement of the ground of her objection, and the board may refuse the exemption if it judges that the stated ground is not coherent with the official's other publicly avowed commitments. The stated ground must also be one that the board can grasp as a reason, since the board must record reasons.

The case. An official is required to authorise a form of legal union that she believes God forbids. She lodges a statement whose content is true but deliberately incomplete: she omits her actual ground, partly because she believes that stating it would be to abandon the very relation that grounds it, and partly because she expects the board to find it unintelligible and to read it as fanaticism. She supplies instead a secondary ground that she does not really hold.

Address the following, in this order. First, would Silentio classify her as a knight of faith, as a knight of infinite resignation, or as something else — and by what right could he classify her at all, given his own insistence that he cannot make the movement of faith himself and cannot recognise it from the outside? Second, does the board's coherence test merely give administrative form to the standing incommensurability of the single individual with the universal, or does it impose something genuinely new: a demand that the absolute relation mediate itself in the universal, which is exactly what Silentio denies can be done? Third, is her partial and misleading statement a betrayal of the paradox, or is it continuous with the silence he describes? Fourth, given his treatment of the demonic in the third Problema, could any procedure whatsoever let a board distinguish the knight of faith from the demonic individual, or is the statute's real achievement to make legally operative a distinction that was always invisible from outside? Fifth, and briefly, where would Silentio's answer come apart from what the book's author, as distinct from its pseudonym, might have said?

Do not supply citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the argument. Write in British English, and make the reasoning explicit at each step rather than gesturing at conclusions.
