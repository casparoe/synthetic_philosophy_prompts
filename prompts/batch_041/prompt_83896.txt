I teach the social-choice unit of a graduate democratic-theory seminar, and every year the discussion collapses into a recitation of escape routes from Arrow's theorem that students can name but not tell apart. I want a diagnostic instrument instead of a lecture.

Write a decision tree that takes a reader from one starting commitment — that Arrow's theorem is a valid proof, i.e. that no rule aggregating the preferences of two or more voters over three or more alternatives can simultaneously satisfy unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship — to a named position about what to do with the result.

Two constraints on how the tree is built. First, build it from the logical space rather than from a list of authors: fix the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, and only then locate the positions in that space. Second, every branch node must be a concrete question about a case or a claim, phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to; no node may simply ask which axiom the reader is willing to give up.

Make the first branch a question about what the reader takes the theorem to be a theorem about — the possibility of a fair voting rule, the coherence of a collective will, the adequacy of welfarist evaluation, or the informational basis of social evaluation — since different targets license different responses. Let later branches ask which feature of the setup the reader treats as revisable, and let the positions sit at the leaves.

Format the tree as an indented outline with numbered nodes, so it can be printed as a two-page handout. Each branch label states the question that splits it from its siblings; each leaf states the position, its standard name if it has one, and one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

The tree must contain leaves for all of the following, wherever your logic puts them:

- a position that restricts the domain of admissible preference profiles rather than the aggregation rule;
- a position that keeps the rule but gives up independence of irrelevant alternatives;
- a position that rejects the informational assumptions built into the theorem — ordinal and non-comparable preferences — rather than any of its four conditions;
- a position that lets certain outcomes be blocked even against unanimous preference, keeping the rest;
- a position that accepts a privileged final decider;
- a position that abandons the demand for a single social ordering and settles for a decision procedure;
- a position that accepts the theorem as a final negative result about collective rationality rather than as a problem to be solved;
- a position that treats the theorem as a reductio of the question rather than of any axiom.

Use at least nine and no more than thirteen leaves in total.

Where a node needs a concrete case, draw on these and add others as needed: a jurisdiction whose voters' preferences over a single tax rate are single-peaked; a rule that ranks the alternatives by summing each voter's ranking position; a constitution that forbids a majority from infringing certain rights even when every voter would prefer that it did; a lottery that selects one citizen at random and adopts that citizen's ranking as the social ranking.

Then add four short sections after the tree:

1. Two or more pairs of leaves that the tree as drawn cannot separate. Say what further question would separate each pair, and whether the difference is substantive or merely verbal.
2. Any combination of answers the tree permits that no one occupies, with a diagnosis of each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
3. For each required leaf listed above, one sentence on why your tree places it where it does rather than somewhere else.
4. A closing paragraph on whether the tree has a single trunk — whether all of these positions really descend from the starting commitment, or whether some are reachable only by rejecting it outright.

No reading list and no citations: the instrument has to work for a reader who cannot look anything up.
