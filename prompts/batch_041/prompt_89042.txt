So I'm taking this seminar on early modern women philosophers and we just spent two weeks on Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz -- the Mexican nun, 17th century, mostly self-taught because she couldn't get into the university (she apparently asked her mother to let her dress as a boy and enroll, and got turned down), who got condemned by the bishop of Puebla for sticking her nose into a theological controversy, and then wrote back this enormous letter that's really a defense of women's right to study -- and who in the end sold off her books and instruments and gave the money to the poor. The move I keep chewing on is that she doesn't just assert 'women can learn'; she goes after the scriptural warrant for shutting them up, arguing that Paul's line about women not teaching is about preaching in the pulpit, not about a woman reading Aristotle or instructing other women privately. Our professor keeps insisting the whole argument is 'situated' -- that it only works inside a scholastic, Catholic, 17th-century frame and that a modern secular feminist can't just pick it up and run with it. I want to test that claim.

So: sit Sor Juana down in 2025, walk her through the state of the question, and tell me what she'd actually say -- reasoning from what she argued and from the intellectual furniture she actually had (scholastic Aristotle, the church fathers, Paul, whatever was in her grandfather's library and her own), not from what a 21st-century person would say while wearing her name. Four things in particular. (1) The way girls' education gets argued for now in development policy -- the World Bank / NGO line that educating girls raises GDP, cuts child mortality, lowers fertility. Would she take that as an ally or as a concession, given her own case for women's learning was frankly theological and about the soul's business with truth rather than about economic output? (2) The reversal: in alot of countries women now outnumber men at university and a chunk of the discourse has flipped into a 'what about the boys' crisis. Does her argument have anything to say once the exclusion runs the other way, or is it structurally one-directional and not repurposable? (3) The fights at women's colleges over admitting trans women -- I have no idea what she'd do with that, and I'd honestly rather you tell me 'her premises don't reach this far' than fake a position, but if they do push somewhere, where? (4) The women-in-philosophy numbers and the implicit-bias literature, which feels like the modern cousin of her complaint that women get kept out of the rooms where the arguing actually happens. And tell me where she'd be out of her depth entirely. Flag your extrapolations as extrapolations -- I want a careful 'here's what her commitments force and here's where I'm guessing' rather than a confident character sketch, because the whole point for me is whether the situatedness objection is right.
