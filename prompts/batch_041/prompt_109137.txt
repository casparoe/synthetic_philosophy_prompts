I'm in a political theory reading group and we've spent most of this term stuck on the same fight, the luck egalitarian one, and I want to break the loop. The shape of it, as I understand the two sides: one camp holds that an inequality is unjust when and only when it tracks brute luck rather than option luck, so if you gamble, or squander what you were given, or take a job you knew was precarious and end up worse off than your cautious neighbour, that gap isn't an injustice, it's the upshot of your choice — compensate for the unchosen misfortunes, bad health, bad parents, bad weather, and let the rest stand. The other camp says that view, taken seriously, tells the imprudent and the reckless and the merely unlucky-in-a-way-that-looks-like-a-choice that they can be left to sink, and that the whole project of carving the world into chosen and unchosen and paying out on that basis gets equality wrong from the start, because what equality is about is standing in relations of equality with others rather than getting a fair share of stuff. Both sides know each other's arguments by heart at this point. We can all recite the abandonment objection and we can all recite the reply that it only lands against a cartoon version, and then we go round again.

What I want from you isn't a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is it substantive, or verbal — are the camps loading different senses onto 'equality' and 'justice' and talking past each other? Are they answering different questions under one heading, one asking what distributive justice requires of a distribution and the other asking what it takes to relate to others as an equal, in which case the apparent clash is an artefact of the shared word? Or is a hidden empirical question doing the work — how much of actual inequality is luck versus choice, whether responsibility attribution is reliable enough to build a theory on — that neither side wants to treat as empirical? Or is it bedrock intuition about whether desert and responsibility belong in a theory of justice at all, which would explain why the arguments never bite?

My own hunch, and I'd like you to push on it rather than just agree with it: that the fight is really about whether the luck egalitarian apparatus is a criterion of what justice is or a doctrine about when the state may leave you to your fate, and that once you seperate those two, what's left is a much smaller disagreement about institutional design. If that's right, tell me what's left over after the split; if it's wrong, tell me exactly where it breaks. One more thing we couldn't settle and that might bear on your answer: we came up with a distinction between being responsible for an inequality existing at all and being responsible for its magnitude — you chose to take the gamble, but the size of the loss the world hands you when it goes wrong isn't yours in the same way. Does that cut the cake differently from the brute/option luck line, or is it the same line in different clothes? And finally, given whatever diagnosis you land on, tell me what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a case one side can't accommodate, a change of question — or whether this is the kind of dispute where no progress should be expected and we should stop meeting about it.
