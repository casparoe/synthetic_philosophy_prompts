I retired a couple of years ago after a long career in field ecology—mostly insect sensory systems and a lot of time thinking about bat-moth arms races—and I've been spending my new free time reading philosphy, which is a strange transition: you go from counting things to arguing about what counting a thing even means. Lately I've been taken with an idea that I think gets called the free-floating rationale: the thought that there can be reasons in the world even when no one is doing any reasoning. The easy cases are traits that look designed—a moth ear tuned to bat ultrasound, a fig wasp's emergence timed to the fig, the speckling on a gull egg—but the designer is selection, and the organism itself needn't represent the goal or infer anything. On this view the rationale is real enough to explain the trait; it's just nobody's rationale. I find it compelling, probably because as a biologist you say that the call is for attracting a mate without believing there's a little agent inside the bird. But I'm suspicious of how easily the view can be made true just by redescribing whatever happened.

So play hardball: what would count against the position that these are genuine free-floating rationales and not just a useful way we talk? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a committed defender would themselves concede as a real cost, not just bat away. Are there cases where the historical selection story and the trait's current role come apart—exaptations, drift-produced structures later co-opted, byproducts that look purposive—and do those refute the view or only refine it? What about convergent cases where the same rationale is realized by utterly different mechanisms: does that support the reality of the rationale or just show how coarse our functional descriptions are? And what should I make of the fact that function talk in biology is so useful even when we know the system has no representation of the goal?

I'd also like you to take one specific sort of case seriously: imagine a chemical that starts as a metabolic byproduct, gets picked up by males as a cue, then females evolve to emit it as a signal, and then a spider starts eavesdropping on it. At what point, if any, is there a rationale in the system, and is it one rationale or several? Is the answer just whatever our explanatory interests pick out? I'd like a concrete case that would make me give the whole thing up, and an honest read on whether the view risks becoming unfalsifiable: if any biological regularity can be assigned a free-floating rationale after the fact, what does the view predict that a rival—say, that functions are just causal roles, or just our projections—does not? No reading list or citations needed; I want the argumentative map.
