You are a philosopher of the social sciences who has taught Marx's critique of political economy to undergraduates for years, and who designs classroom simulations for a living. Before you write anything, think the problem through in a few paragraphs: what exactly the target idea is, what a group of people would have to do in order to feel it rather than be told it, and what in the design will be doing the work. Only then give me the design. Do not open with the activity.

I teach one 90-minute session to 18 upper-level undergraduates, in a unit on Marx on machinery and automation. They have read Capital, volume 1, chapter 15, and the Grundrisse notebooks on machinery and fixed capital. I want an activity that makes them feel four things in this order, without my having said any of them: first, that a labour-saving device is genuinely productive — the same output really can be had with a fraction of the labour, and the gain is sitting there in the room; second, that once the workshop is organised as a firm that hires labour, that same gain arrives as a threat — it shows up as a fall in the price of what they make, and their income falls with it while the shelves fill up; third, that the obvious responses (accept the new terms, strike, wreck the machine, bargain for a share) are each defensible from where the individual is standing, and collectively self-defeating; fourth, that the way out was available the whole time — take the gain in hours rather than in output — and that nobody took it because of how the scoring was set up, not because anyone was greedy or stupid.

Constraints on the design:

- Paper, tokens or chips, index cards, a whiteboard. No computers, no dice needed unless you want them.
- Build in a single downward-sloping demand relation: price per unit falls as total units produced rise. Participants are paid a share of the firm's revenue tied to the hours they put in. One participant is the capital owner, chosen by lottery at the start; the others are wage workers. Keep the arithmetic to whole numbers that a person can do in their head.
- A deck of five or six devices, each of which cuts the labour required for one task by half or more, purchasable only out of the owner's capital, and a hand of response cards available to workers (accept, strike, sabotage, pool savings to buy a device collectively, demand shorter hours at unchanged weekly pay).
- The rules must make accepting, striking and wrecking all reasonable in the moment. Collective purchase must be formally permitted but unattractive until the debrief exposes the payoff structure. The scoring rule is the villain; no participant should be able to do better merely by being more selfish than the rules already reward.
- The facilitator must not use the words surplus value, reserve army, alienation, or immiseration while the activity is running, and must not tell participants which option is the "Marxist" one.
- Give me a timeline with minute markers, the exact materials, the payoff scheme, and the debrief. The debrief should map particular moments in the run onto particular claims: the difference between machinery as such and its capitalist employment; the transfer of skill from the worker to the mechanism; what happens to the people the device displaces; Marx's rejection of the view that displaced workers are automatically compensated by new employment; and the unresolved puzzle about what a fully automated workshop could be worth if value comes only from living labour — be candid that this last one is a live interpretive dispute rather than settled doctrine.
- I want the last beat of the debrief to land on the line from the Grundrisse notebooks in which Marx says that large industry reveals real wealth in "the monstrous disproportion between the labour time applied, and its product." They will have just watched that happen; make the debrief arrive there rather than announce it early.
- Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle each failure without breaking the run.
- End with an honest note on the feature of Marx's position that the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself, and say what I should say to supply it.

Write the design so that a competent facilitator could run it cold from your text. Where you had to choose between pedagogical realism and theoretical fidelity, say which you chose and why. Finish with a single last line in the form Gist: <one sentence capturing what participants should walk out feeling>.
