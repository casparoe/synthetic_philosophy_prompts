Two Windows: a note on decisions you get to make twice

Many decisions give you exactly two chances to act, and the two chances are hard to tell apart from the inside. A guest at a reception wants to leave at the right moment: leaving early looks rude, leaving late is fine, and never leaving is worse than leaving late but better than leaving early. A researcher wants to abandon a doomed project: quitting after the first discouraging result looks hasty, quitting after the second is sensible, and grinding on to the end is mediocre. In each case you would, if you could, act at the second chance and not the first.

Formalize it. You pass through two windows. Acting at the first is worth 0, acting at the second is worth 4, acting at neither is worth 1. While in a window you cannot tell whether it is the first or the second. So whatever policy you adopt is a single probability p of acting, applied at both windows. Expected value:

p·0 + (1−p)[p·4 + (1−p)·1] = (1−p)(1 + 3p),

which is maximized at p = 1/3, for a value of 4/3.

Two things follow, and I think they are the real content of the puzzle usually told with a driver and two exits.

First, the tempting reasoning — at the second window acting is best, so I will act then; at the first I will wait — is unavailable, because then and now are indistinguishable from the inside. What looks like a plan with two different instructions is a plan with no instruction, namely never act, value 1. The unit of choice is the plan, not the node, and the plan that treats both windows alike beats the plan that pretends to distinguish them.

Second, and this is the part I care about, resolving to wait for the better moment is not merely risky; it is beaten by deliberate randomization. Acting with probability one-third at each window beats waiting, 4/3 against 1. The randomization is not a failure of nerve; it is the optimum, given that you cannot label the windows.

I take the lesson to be that much of what we call a commitment device works not by adding willpower but by making the windows distinguishable — by turning a two-window problem into a one-window problem.

My worry is that the formalization proves too much. In most real two-chance situations you can in fact tell the windows apart: you know how long the meeting has run, how many discouraging results you have had. Once you can, the puzzle evaporates and waiting is optimal after all.

What I want from you: (1) whether the expected-value analysis and the claim that randomizing beats waiting are stated correctly and precisely; (2) whether the move from the toy case to the general moral about commitment devices is legitimate, or whether it slides over something important; (3) whether the closing worry can be answered, and if so how, without giving up the thesis; and (4) anything in the essay that is simply wrong, plus the strongest objection I have not anticipated.
