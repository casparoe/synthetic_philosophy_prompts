I'm a philosopher of mind presenting a work-in-progress paper at a small departmental seminar next week, and what I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a summary of my own view. Take the role of someone who has watched this exact paper get taken apart in rooms like this one. Think carefully about who is in the room before you draft anything: start by brainstorming their priors, then work out what they will actually say to me.

My thesis: phenomenal properties are epiphenomenal — they never enter into the causal production of any behaviour, including my utterances and my judgments — and yet the standard objection to epiphenomenalism, the paradox of phenomenal judgement, is not a paradox, because it rests on a principle that should be rejected, namely that a state can be about a phenomenal property only if that property causally influences it. My move is to replace the causal model of aboutness with a constitutive one. A judgement about a quale is not caused by the quale; the quale is part of the vehicle that carries the judgement, in something like the way a belief is individuated by its content rather than caused by it. The causal work — the tokening, the transition, the utterance — is done at the level of the physical realiser, and the phenomenal character rides along as a non-causal aspect of that very same state. So I claim to keep both epiphenomenalism and the plain fact that we talk about our experiences.

The room: about a dozen people. Four or five are physicalists who regard the hard problem as a confusion and think introspective reports are just reports of representational states, and they will keep asking what my view predicts that theirs does not. Two are illusionists who will hear my position as a half-hearted version of theirs and press me to go all the way. One or two are qualia realists who accept the conceivability of zombies and will notice exactly where my constitutive move puts pressure on that argument. One is a cognitive scientist who studies metacognition and confidence and will want to know what, if anything, would count as evidence. Nobody in the room works on the semantics of the word 'about', so no one will help me with the distinction I am leaning on.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no introduction, no closing summary, no advice about tone. Each item should be compact — a sentence or two of the objection in the words of the person who would raise it, then the reply I should give in two minutes, then one sentence naming where that reply is soft. Order the first four by how much damage they would do; make the fifth the question I am hoping nobody asks, whatever its damage. Write in British English, and do not tell me to consult, read or cite anything, since I cannot look anything up.
