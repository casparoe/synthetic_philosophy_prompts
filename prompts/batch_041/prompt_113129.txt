A freind in my reading group dropped this on me last week and I still can't find the loose thread. (1) To say "the world is becoming less violent" is to say some quantity has fallen over time. (2) Any such quantity is built out of two choices: what counts as a violent event — does a siege count once or once per death, is a famine caused by a blockade a violent act, is a police killing and a battlefield death the same kind of thing — and what you divide by, people or states or years or nothing at all, and since absolute counts can rise while rates fall this isn't a minor detail. (3) Make defensible but different choices and you get different answers: some measures fall, some rise, some flip depending on where you start the clock. (4) So whether the claim is true depends on choices that nothing in the world forces on us. (5) So it isn't really a claim about the world; it's a claim about which choices we made. (6) But the same argument runs for "the world is warming," "child mortality is falling," "the oceans are acidifying" — those are measured too, and nobody thinks they're merely claims about our choices. (7) So (5) has to go. Where's the mistake? I keep thinking it's (3) and that he's just cherry-picking measures, but he says he'd run it for whatever measure you name, so I don't think that's it. If I have to choose I'd rather keep (5) than give up (2) or (3), since the point about measurement choices seems to me actually right and kind of corrosive — but then I want to know what I'm buying. Which step do you reject, why did it look fine, and what does each way out cost? And is there a narrower version of the argument that survives and still does real damage to the decline-of-violence literature, one that doesn't also take out thermometers?
