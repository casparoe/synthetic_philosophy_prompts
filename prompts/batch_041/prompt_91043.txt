I retired a few years ago after thirty-odd years as a field ecologist — mostly beetles, mostly one genus that has given me more taxonomic grief than I care to admit — and since then I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars at the local university, which has been a strange and mostly wonderful way to spend a Tuesday. I've gotten friendly with two of the regulars there. One is convinced that species are real divisions in nature and that the fact we're still juggling half a dozen competing species concepts is a scandal we should be able to clean up; the other is equally convinced that species categories are our own partitions laid over a branching, messy, reticulating world, and that pluralism isn't a problem to be solved but the permanently correct answer. They've been circling each other for two years now, definately talking past each other more than to each other, and it's finally occurred to me that what they need isn't another debate, it's a document. So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them. I want the claims both of them would actually sign — my hunch is they agree on far more empirical biology than either will admit in public, ring species, cryptic species, the plain fact that different concepts hand you different counts, asexual lineages, all the hybrid zones I've spent my working life staring at — then the precise statements of where they still differ, each one phrased the way that side would want its own view phrased, and then the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would genuinely move at least one of them. For each crux give a case, argument, or observation they'd both agree bears on it, plus what each side predicts about it, written down in advance so nobody can wriggle out afterwards. Number the agreed claims, put the disagreements in a table.

Two things I'd like beyond the obvious. First, tell me whether any part of their dispute is verbal — I have a suspicion that at least one round of it is about what the word "real" is being asked to do rather than about anything with six legs. Second, they're happy to argue in the seminar room, but the reason I care is that I've watched a listing decision and a museum re-curation both turn on this, and the field guide people need an answer too, so say which cruxes would actually change what a regulator, a collections manager, or a guide author should do, and which are honestly philosophy that makes no difference to anyone holding a net and a pin. And if there's an empirical result either of them would have to accept as a defeat, I want that in writing — a prediction with no possible refutation is just a preference, as my old supervisor used to say, or words to that effect, I'm probably mangling him.
