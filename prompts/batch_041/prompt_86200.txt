I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, and I've ended up in a long-running argument with a colleague that I would like audited properly, because neither of us can say where the analogy we keep reaching for actually holds.

The background: our team is building an agent whose behaviour is governed by a written set of rules, and we are debating whether to let it rewrite those rules at runtime under a procedure the rules themselves specify. My colleague thinks this is nothing to be frightened of, because we already have a working example of a rule system that contains, inside itself, the instructions for changing itself, and it has kept going for centuries, namely a constitution with an amendment clause. His line is that a legal order is essentially a program that can rewrite its own source code, that it has been running in production for two hundred years without the sky falling, and that the sensible design for our agent is therefore not to freeze its rules but to give it a proper amendment procedure, with quorum, delay and publicity, and let it evolve. He says anyone who wants a frozen rule set is like someone who refuses to ship version two.

I can see why the analogy feels good and I can also feel it doing work it has not earned, but I cannot articulate where the joints are. What I want from you is an audit. First, the correspondence, in prose rather than as a table: what in a legal order plays the role of the source code, the interpreter, the hardware, the compiler, the runtime, the version control, the test suite. Say plainly which of these have a genuine counterpart and which do not. Then the break points, the places where the legal case has a feature the software case simply lacks, or the reverse, with an eye to the ones that bear on his conclusion rather than the ones that are merely decorative. I would also like you to take seriously a few things I have noticed that make me uneasy about the picture of law as self-modifying code. The amendment clause is written in the same language as everything else it governs, so it is not obviously a compiler sitting outside the code, and there is no machine underneath that we can point to as the thing making the rules take effect. Whether an amendment is valid seems to depend on people treating it as valid, which is not how the validity of a program works. And there is a puzzle I keep getting tangled in. If the clause says it may be amended by a two-thirds majority, and a two-thirds majority uses it to raise the bar to unanimity, is that amendment valid, and valid under which version of the clause? Suppose instead they use it to repeal the clause entirely, or to make the clause unamendable. In software I would say the process either halts or it does not and I can point at the semantics; in law I do not know what the analogous thing to point at is.

So: how far does his analogy actually hold, what does it quietly import from the legal case that the software case lacks, and does his conclusion, that we should not freeze the rules but give the agent a proper amendment procedure, survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If it survives, say what is really carrying it, because I suspect it is not the analogy. If a better analogy is available for the thing we are actually trying to reason about, tell me what it is and why it does better. No reading list, please; I want the argument, not the bibliography. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold and no tables, just paragraphs the way you would write a long email. American English, please.
