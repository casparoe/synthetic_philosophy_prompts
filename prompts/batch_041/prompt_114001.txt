Quick recap so we're on the same page, and because i want your answer to be about the thing i'm actually asking about: if every truth is knowable, then every truth is known. The argument: take some truth p that in fact nobody knows. Then "p and nobody knows that p" is true too, so by the principle it's possible that someone knows it. But knowing a conjunction means knowing both halves — so that someone knows that p, and also knows that nobody knows that p. Knowing the second one entails that it's true, so nobody knows p, them included. Contradiction. So there's no such p, which means every truth is known. Which is nuts.

The ask: i want the ways out of this ranked. Here's what i can see, add anything i've missed:

(a) restrict knowability to basic/atomic truths, and give up closure of knowledge under conjunction — otherwise the restriction buys you nothing.
(b) restrict the principle to propositions where "someone knows p" isn't already ruled out a priori. The Fitch conjunction gets excluded, because "someone knows that p and nobody knows that p" is contradictory on its face.
(c) drop factivity. Swap "knows" for something like "has a true justified belief" or "is in a position to truly believe," so the second half of the conjunction doesn't clash with the first.
(d) let the contradiction stand — go paraconsistent, or give up classical logic somewhere upstream.
(e) reinterpret the modal. "Possibly known" doesn't mean "there's a possible world where it's known," it means "not ruled out by what we could in principle verify," or "knowable by an unlimited community given unlimited time."
(f) shrink the principle's scope to truths we could in principle settle by some finite procedure.
