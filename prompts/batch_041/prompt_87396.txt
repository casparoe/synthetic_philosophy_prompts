I'm a second-year PhD student in a language acquisition lab, and my supervisor has voluntold me to write up the joint statement from a two-day workshop we ran last month on innateness and the poverty of the stimulus. It was meant to be an adverserial collaboration between the two camps that have been talking past each other for decades: the nativist side (domain-specific representational constraints on the learner's hypothesis space, the child as a learner whose prior does most of the work) and the empiricist / usage-based side (a domain-general learner with powerful distributional and Bayesian machinery, working over an input far richer than the nativists will concede). What I need from you is the document those two camps could actually both put their names to. It has to be non-vacuous — both sides agreeing that children acquire language is exactly the sort of filler that made the workshop useless. And please don't adjudicate: I'm not asking who's right, I'm asking for the text they'd both sign, with the residual disagreement made exact.

So: a set of agreed claims that are load-bearing rather than decorative — what the phenomenon to be explained actually is, what the induction problem is and isn't, what would count as evidence for either side, what's a red herring, and a disambiguation of the word innate (domain-specific versus domain-general bias, representational versus architectural, present at birth versus maturationally scheduled), because half the shouting seemed to be that one word doing four jobs at once. Then the disagreements, each phrased so that the side which holds it would accept the wording as fair — no strawmen, no weasel. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. I suspect one is whether a domain-general learner with realistic priors could in principle get there from the input alone, and another is whether the error and recovery patterns in children carry the signature of a constrained hypothesis space at all — but tell me if I've missed the real ones. For each crux I want the case, argument or experiment that both sides would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down in advance. Two things to build in. First, a labmate keeps waving around a corpus figure for how much of child-directed speech is disfluent or outright ungrammatical, and I've noticed both camps quote the same number as though it definately settled their case, which can't be right — deal with that. Second, we've got a study running that both sides grudgingly agreed to treat as relevant: adults are trained on a miniature artificial language whose training items are fitted equally well by a linear adjacent-dependency rule and by a hierarchical one, and the test items are precisely the ones where the two generalisations come apart; the nativists say it's the structure-dependence point over again, the empiricists say it's a rigged demo because the training set is tiny and there's no semantic anchor. Write both predictions down as if before the data came in, and say what each side would have to concede if the other's prediction comes out.

Format, and please stick to it: a section called Scope and ground rules (~120 words, saying what the statement covers and what it deliberately doesn't); then Claims we both sign, numbered, fifteen to twenty of them, roughly 700 words; then a table called Where we still differ, with columns for the question, the nativist formulation, the empiricist formulation, and what actually turns on it — six to nine rows; then Cruxes and what would move us, roughly 600 words, one sub-heading per crux, each with the agreed bearing case and both predictions; then What's probably just words, ~200 words, translating any dispute that turns out to be terminological; then a two-line signature block. No reading list, no citations, no references to who said what in the literature — I don't need them, and half of us would end up arguing about the attributions anyway.
