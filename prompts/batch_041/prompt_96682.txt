'Expected value theory says that when you're deciding what to do you should always pick the option with the highest expected value: you multiply the value of each possible outcome by its probability and add them up. The fanaticism problem is that this rule turns you into a fanatic. If the payoff is big enough, you're required to take a gamble no matter how tiny the chance of winning it and no matter how bad the loss if you lose. Standard example: someone offers you a bet with a 99.99% chance that you lose everything you have and a 0.01% chance that you get to bring about a galaxy-spanning civilization of trillions of happy people lasting a billion years. The good outcome is so astronomically large that its expected value swamps the near-certain loss, so the theory says you're required to take the bet. And there's no stopping point: for any finite stake you can imagine a gamble with a small enough probability of a large enough payoff that the expected value comes out positive, so the theory commits you to betting everything on Pascal's wager. This is why the fanaticism objection is usually treated as a reductio of expected value maximization. No reasonable person would take these bets; the theory says you must; so the theory is false. Strip out the theology and it's just Pascal's wager over again. And the only reason it feels crazy is that our brains were never built for numbers that big — we're scope-insensitive — so the sense that something has gone wrong is a glitch, not evidence about the theory.'
