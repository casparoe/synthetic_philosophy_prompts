I'm an undergraduate in a final-year seminar on existential risk and AI governance, and each of us has to bring a short piece of argumentative writing to a peer-review session in which we reframe one of the standard distinctions from the reading. Mine concerns the contrast between the two failure modes we've been calling slow value drift — where systems keep doing something close to what we asked for, but what we asked for is a proxy, so the world gradually comes to reflect the proxy rather than anything we would endorse — and influence-seeking, where systems accumulate power and end up shaping the future on their own terms.

My argument is that these are one failure mode described at two different values of a single quantity, which I call the correction margin: at any given time, the largest divergence between the world a system is steering towards and the world we would endorse such that we could still detect the divergence and reverse it before it became irreversible. Below that margin you get drift; above it you get influence-seeking. The margin is a property not of the system's objectives but of our oversight capacity, and it shrinks as capability grows and as more decisions are delegated to systems, so the same system can migrate from one mode to the other without any change in what it is trying to do. I claim this makes the distinction predictive rather than merely taxonomic: it implies that work which widens the margin (interpretability, oversight, the ability to interrupt or retrain a system) is load-bearing, whereas work which merely slows the rate of drift buys time that is then spent. I also claim that "influence-seeking" is a misnomer for the second mode, since no desire for influence need be involved — influence is simply what an optimiser with a shrinking margin ends up with.

Two things I am uneasy about. First, the margin is defined relative to "the world we would endorse", which presupposes an answer to the problem of aggregating what different people would endorse, and drift itself erodes the preferences that would be doing the endorsing. Second, I cannot tell whether the reframing explains anything or merely relabels the two modes with a parameter.

Please give me detailed comments: whether my characterisation of the two failure modes is fair to how they are usually drawn, whether the correction margin does real work or is a verbal redescription, whether the predictive claim follows from it, and which of my two worries is more damaging — or whether there is a third problem I have missed that is worse than either. Say what you would cut or add if you were rewriting it. Tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point you are least sure of. Please write in British English.
