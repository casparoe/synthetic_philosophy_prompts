I'm teaching a seminar on Ulysses contracts and constitutional precommitment and I want to use Nolan's Memento as the case study. Leonard's tattoos, Polaroids and notes are basically his entire apparatus for keeping a promise to himself, and I want a reading that takes the philosophical literature seriously instead of just saying "memory is unreliable, cool twist."

Two things I'd like the reading to work with. First, a distinction I find useful: precommitment by incentive versus precommitment by information. Ulysses ties himself to the mast, so acting on the desire becomes too costly -- but Leonard's device runs the other way, it removes the alternative from his knowledge, so his future self can't even entertain it. Which is the stronger kind of binding, and does the film expose a failure mode specific to the informational kind? Second, the standard worry about constitutional precommitment is that the people who bind themselves are the same people who are bound, so there's no external enforcer standing behind the promise -- the enforcer is always also a party. Leonard is his own trustee, his own enforcer and his own beneficiary, and nobody outside the loop ever audits the record. So does Memento dramatize the trustee problem better than the political theory does, or does it cheat by making the protagonist's case pathological rather than structural?

And where does the reading strain? I have a nagging feeling the honest answer is that Leonard isnt really a Ulysses at all -- he isn't fighting weakness of will, he's lost the very capacity the contract is supposed to work on, so the device is a prosthesis rather than a self-binding. Tell me if that's right, and what the film adds to the debate that argument alone doesn't. The ending seems crucial: he doesn't merely forget his own commitments, he edits them.
