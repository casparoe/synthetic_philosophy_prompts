I'm teaching the Mill stretch of my phil of social science seminar again in a few weeks and I keep getting stuck on the same passage in Book VI, ch. 7 of the System of Logic (the one where he's arguing you can't run experiments on societies). It's the paragraph where he rolls out the whole individualist card, and it reads so smoothly that I always think I've got it until I have to say out loud what each sentence is actually claiming. Here it is:

"The laws of the phenomena of society are, and can be, nothing but the laws of the actions and passions of human beings united together in the social state. Men, however, in a state of society, are still men; their actions and passions are obedient to the laws of individual human nature. Men are not, when brought together, converted into another kind of substance, with different properties; as hydrogen and oxygen are different from water, or as hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, and azote are different from nerves, muscles, and tendons. Human beings in society have no properties but those which are derived from, and may be resolved into, the laws of the nature of individual man. In social phenomena the Composition of Causes is the universal law."

And then he goes straight on: "Now, the method of philosophising which may be termed chemical overlooks this fact, and proceeds as if the nature of man as an individual were not concerned at all, or were concerned in a very inferior degree, in the operations of human beings in society."

What I want is a proper walk-through: what each of those sentences commits him to, which of them are doing real work and which are rhetorical throat-clearing (the hydrogen/oxygen/azote stuff — azote is just nitrogen, by the way, that one derailed a whole seminar once). Then the parts I can't fix myself. I have a hunch the last sentence is the load-bearing one and that it's quietly in trouble: if I remember the Book III doctrine right, composition of causes is supposed to be the case where the joint effect is the sum and the laws of the separate causes survive the union, as against the chemical case where they don't — and yet later in Book VI he describes society as a consensus in which every element acts on every other and effects react back on their causes. So is the last sentence inconsistent with his own picture of society, or am I reading "composition" too narrowly? Tell me if my hunches are wrong; I'd rather find that out now than in front of twenty grad students.

Also: is this an ontological claim about what social facts are, or a claim about how social science has to be built, and which reading does the text actually support? How does it sit with what he stacks on top of it later — the science of character formation he wants to bolt onto psychology, the talk of empirical laws of society, the inverse deductive method in the historical chapters? If I hand someone the strongest available objection to the passage that doesn't need any vocabulary Mill wouldn't have recognised, what is it, and where exactly does it bite — the ontology, the resolution claim, or the inference from "no new substances" to "no new laws"? And give me a plain-English version I can put on a slide, plus the two or three spots where I should just tell the room this bit is genuinely ambiguous instead of pretending to settle it. No references needed, I have the text in front of me, and please don't just tell me it's the founding statement of methodological individualism — I know that, I need to know what it actually commits him to and where its exposed.
