I'm a second-year PhD student, mostly phil of mind, but I keep getting pulled into ethics seminars and now I've promised someone a chapter, so I need a case built. Heres the thing I'm chasing.

Take panpsychism seriously for a second — experience goes all the way down, the fundamental stuff has some form of it. The lazy ethical takeaway is "everything suffers, rocks matter", which is boring and probably wrong. The interesting fork is one level up. If micro-experiences exist, you need some story about whether they combine into bigger subjects. Two options: either they combine into lots of little subjects (and us, and maybe nothing bigger), or the cosmos as a whole is the one fundamental subject and individual subjects are derivative of it — what people call cosmopsychism. I want to know whether that choice, and only that choice, can flip a moral verdict.

So: build me a thought experiment with two scenarios that differ only in how the combination goes. Same fundamental physics, same arrangement of constituents, same behaviour, same everything a physicist or a psychologist could measure — different facts about which subjects there are. And make it a case where the moral verdict actually turns on that. The point of the case is to test whether panpsychism makes moral status non-functional: if the ethics tracks the subjects rather than the behaviour, then two things that behave identically can differ in how much they matter, and that's the claim I want the case to either support or embarrass.

Specs (I'm being picky because otherwise it just collapses into a normal argument about functionalism):

1. No zombies. Both scenarios are panpsychist. The only variable is whether the micro-experiences unify into a subject, stay as an uncombined heap, or get absorbed into one cosmic subject.
2. Functionally identical. Same inputs, outputs, dispositions, self-reports, same reaction to damage. Poke either one and it says ouch.
3. The physical description has to be stateable without anything a physicist would call new physics. You can say a relation holds or fails to hold between constituents; you can't bolt on a new field to make the ethics come out right.
4. A physicalist has to be able to read the whole thing without being forced to admit the two scenarios really differ. So it should work as a conditional: if panpsychism is true, then this is the difference.
5. One paragraph for the scenario itself. No sci-fi, no vats, no labs. Two situations that could be true of the actual world.

Then, after the scenario: go through the specs one by one and show it meets them — if it can't meet one, say which and why, I'd much rather know than have you fudge it. Then say what the panpsychist who believes in many little subjects says the verdict is, what the cosmopsychist says, and what the physicalist says, and seperate where they disagree about the ethics from where they're just disagreeing about the metaphysics. Tell me which single feature is doing the work, and what happens if I drop it — say if the behaviour differs between the two scenarios, or if one scenario is allowed to be non-experiential after all. Then the honest bit: what's the strongest move for someone who thinks the case is rigged? I expect it's something like "you smuggled in a notion of moral status that already assumes subjects are the unit", but give me the best version of that objection, not the strawman. And finish by actually picking a side: does the case show that "suffering in fundamental physics" is a coherent ethical idea, or does it show the ethics is hostage to the combination problem until the metaphysics is settled? Don't hedge on that last one.
