I'm on the curriculum commitee for our district's 9th-grade world history course — thirty weeks, four units, rewrite due in the spring, and the school board signs off in June. The fight we keep having isn't about coverage; everyone agrees we need more Asia, Africa, and the Americas in there. It's about framing. When we just slot new regions in, the story still bends around Europe: the only unit that's supposed to explain the modern world is the one on industrialization, everything before 1500 gets filed under 'medieval' or 'traditional,' and the regions we add end up described by what they supposedly lacked (no wheels, no iron, no horses). One colleague wants to delete the Industrial Revolution unit and replace it with 'knowledge and technology across Afro-Eurasia, 1200-1700'; another says that's just trading one center for another, and he's not wrong — a course centered on China or on the Islamic world would have its own blind spots. Meanwhile the state standards mandate a strand called something like 'the expansion of Western civilization,' and a group of parents are already convinced we've gone too far the other way. So I don't want a list of buzzwords or a sermon about why Eurocentrism is bad. I want a decision procedure my committee can actually run, unit by unit, to decide (a) whether a given framing is Eurocentric and (b) whether a proposed fix is an improvement on it: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and gives a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the disagreement is about what history is for and not about the facts. It should keep straight the difference between Europe showing up as one region among others and Europe showing up as the axis the whole thing turns on, since I think my colleagues on both sides conflate those. And it has to be honest that no frame is neutral — that we can't tell the past from everywhere at once — so it should say which distortions we're accepting and why, rather than pretending the right framing will have none. Draw on whatever is actually settled in the field about periodization and framing; I don't need a reading list and please don't cite things, I need the machinery.

Then run it on three cases: our existing Scientific Revolution unit, with the proposed replacement above; an Industrial Revolution unit a teacher wants to keep but rebuild as 'coal, colonies, and cotton,' with Britain still the site but slavery, Caribbean plantations, and Indian textiles doing the explanatory work; and the periodization itself, i.e. whether labeling 500-1500 'the Middle Ages' is something we can fix or just a name we're stuck with. At least one of those should come out the way I don't want it to — I'd rather find that out in committee than at the board meeting — and tell me plainly where the procedure can't decide anything and a teacher is just going to have to pick.
