I've been sitting in on a year-long seminar that is nominally about social explanation but keeps collapsing into the same fight about methodological individualism, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of the fight rather than another round of it. The individualists in the room hold that social entities — classes, states, markets, cultures, norms — are nothing over and above individuals and their interactions; that talk of such entities is either convenient shorthand or a placeholder awaiting microfoundations; that an explanation which stops at a structure or a rate has simply not finished; and that treating collectives as agents is bad method and politically dangerous, since it licenses talk about what a nation wants. The holists hold that social facts have explanatory autonomy: that suicide rates, price levels, or patterns of state formation can be explained by other social facts; that positions and institutions constrain and partly constitute the individuals who occupy them; that there is downward causation from structures to behaviour; and that the individualist programme quietly assumes a ready-made individual with settled preferences, when the interesting question is where those come from. Everyone grants, when pressed, that you could not change any social fact without changing some individual facts — someone called this supervenience, everyone nodded, and it changed nothing, because each side hears the concession as coming from the other. Nobody has moved in a year, and the same exchanges recur whether we are reading about rational choice, about the longue durée in history, or about whether social structures exist at all.

My hypothesis, which I floated once and was shouted at from both sides for, is that three different claims are being run together, each side defending one while attacking another: an ontological claim about what exists, an explanatory claim about what an explanation may appeal to, and a semantic claim about whether social predicates can be defined in individual terms. I suspect the first is nearly settled and does almost no work; that the second is where the real disagreement lives and may not be a disagreement about the world at all but about what an explanation is supposed to deliver — mechanisms all the way down, versus subsumption under regularities at whatever level the regularities are stable; and that a genuine empirical question hides underneath, namely whether macro-level regularities can actually be recovered from individual-level models. I gather there are formal results in general equilibrium theory showing that aggregate demand need not inherit any of the nice properties of individual demand, which some of my colleagues treat as a proof that the reductionist programme cannot work and others dismiss as beside the point because it concerns prediction rather than explanation.

So tell me what kind of disagreement this is. Which parts are verbal, which substantive, which a disguised empirical question, and which bedrock — a difference over what counts as an explanation that neither side can argue for from neutral ground? Is it one debate, or several restaged under a single name in different fields? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — and is there a diagnosis under which no progress should be expected at all? I don't want a survey of the literature or a reading list; engage with the positions as I have stated them and with the structure of the disagreement, and be specific about what each side is actually committing itself to. End with a single final line of the form \answer{LABEL — one-sentence summary}, where LABEL is your short diagnosis (verbal, substantive, hidden-empirical, different-questions, ill-posed, or a combination) and the sentence names the crux.
