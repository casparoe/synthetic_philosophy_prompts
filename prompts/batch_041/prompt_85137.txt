I am a retired plant systematist. For forty years I argued about what counts as a species, and I thought I had seen every variety of classification puzzle. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy of language and typography, and I have become fixated on a deceptively simple question: what makes every a an a? I am not asking for a history of the alphabet, a survey of famous theorists, or a reading list. I want to know what principle, if any, unifies my verdicts on the following cases.

1. A standard double-storey a in a serif font: clearly an a.
2. A single-storey a, as in many sans-serif or handwritten forms: clearly an a.
3. A glyph that is exactly the shape of a standard a but is used in a secret code to stand for the letter e: I say it is an a-shaped mark functioning as an e, not an a. The shape is the same, but the letter is not.
4. A glyph in a non-Latin script that happens to be visually indistinguishable from a Latin a: I say it is not the Latin letter a, though it may be an a-like glyph. It belongs to a different system.
5. A child's first attempt at writing a, a wobbly loop with a tail that only a parent could read: I say it is an a. Intention and participation in the practice matter more than exact shape.
6. A rock formation that, by erosion, is visually indistinguishable from a perfect a: I say it is not an a. It was not produced or used as a letter.
7. A mirrored a, as in a mirror image: I say it is not an a, though it is an a-derived shape. Orientation is part of the type for me.
8. A rotated a, lying on its side: I say it is not an a in ordinary text, but if a font or a game systematically rotates all letters, I am inclined to say the rotated token is still an a in that system. This is one of the cases that makes me uneasy.
9. An a whose bowl is filled in, or whose stem is detached, in a highly stylized display face: I say it is still an a if it is part of a font that uses those distortions systematically and readers recognize it.
10. A shape that is halfway between an a and an e, with no context: I say it is not determinately either. Context might resolve it.
11. An a in a word, where the surrounding letters make the word unmistakable, but the a itself is smudged beyond recognition: I am torn. I lean toward saying the token is an a because it occupies the a-slot in a known word, but I can also see saying it is an illegible mark.
12. A perfect a generated by a random process, then placed in a text by a typesetter who thinks it is just a decorative mark: I say it is not an a in that use, even though it looks exactly like one.
13. A perfect a in a font designed by an AI that has no communicative intentions, but the font is used by people to write: I say the tokens are a's. The system of use, not the designer's inner life, is what matters.
14. A letter a that has been so abstracted that it is just a vertical stroke and a dot, but it contrasts systematically with the other letters in a minimalist alphabet: I say it is an a if the system treats it as the a and users read it as such.

My tentative principle is something like this: a token is an a if it is a token of a type that plays the a-role in a system of graphic contrasts used by a community to encode a language, and the type is historically or causally connected to the Latin letter a. But this principle seems to make the secret-code case come out as an a, because the mark is historically connected and plays a role, just the role of e. It makes the rotated-a-in-a-rotating-font case come out as an a, which I want. It makes the rock formation not an a, which I want. But it may make the non-Latin lookalike an a if that community's system is historically connected, which I do not want. And it may make the child's wobbly mark an a only if the child counts as a community, which feels like a stretch. I am not sure whether the principle should be functional, historical, intentional, or some mixture.

Please act as an expert in philosophy of language, philosophy of categorization, and the theory of typefaces. Think carefully and proceed in stages. First, restate the puzzle in your own words and brainstorm the candidate criteria that might be in play: shape, function, intention, convention, causal history, system of contrasts, community use, and anything else you think is relevant. Then state as precisely as you can the principle or principles that best capture my verdicts. For each clause of the principle, tell me which of my cases motivates it. Then test the principle against cases I have not listed, for example: a letter a in a dream; a letter a in a language game played by one person; a letter a that evolves gradually across generations of scribes until it looks like an o; a letter a that is produced by a machine that copies the form but is used as a symbol for a mathematical variable; and a letter a that is recognized as an a by everyone but has no historical connection to the Latin alphabet. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up the verdict or complicate the principle. Do not stop at saying that the category is a family resemblance or that it is prototype-based; those are labels, not principles. Show me the clauses and the cases.
