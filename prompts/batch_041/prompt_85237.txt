I'm the incoming chair of a national animal-welfare organisation, and I have to give the opening address at our annual conference. The hall is mostly veterans of farmed-animal work, several of whom regard insect welfare as a distraction; there are a few entomologists and neuroscientists on the programme, and a large lay membership. I want the address to argue that insects belong in our remit, but with the argument doing the structural work rather than appearing in it as a slogan: it has to keep the probability of insect sentience separate from the moral weight of an insect's experience, since most of the real disagreement lives in the second factor and conflating the two is how the case gets oversold, while still conveying that the expected stakes are vast once a low probability is multiplied by the numbers of animals and by whatever weight is warranted. It cannot assert what nobody knows, and it cannot imply that the room's existing work is rounding error — which is more or less where the multiplication seems to point. It should also meet two objections head-on rather than dodge them: that the same reasoning runs on to nematodes, of which the standard estimate puts some 4.4 × 10²⁰ in the world's topsoil alone, sixty billion for every human, and then to whatever comes next, with no natural stopping point; and that a rule of always deferring to tiny probabilities of astronomical goods would leave us unable to justify the modest, near-certain improvements this organisation actually delivers. Write the address, ten minutes, pitched to land for those who know the debate without losing those who don't.
