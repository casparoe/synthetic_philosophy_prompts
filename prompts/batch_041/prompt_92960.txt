So my department just switched on the AI-detection feature in the plagiarism software, and as a first-year TA I'm the one who gets to read the flags. Here's the actual setup, because I think the details are the whole point. Every submitted essay gets a score from 0 to 100 for "percent AI-generated." Under 20, nothing happens. Between 20 and 60, the instructor has to sit down with the student for a "developmental conversation." Over 60, it goes to the academic integrity office as suspected misconduct. The software's own docs, which I read because I'm that kind of person, say the score "should not be used as the sole basis for adverse action" and isn't proof that AI was used at all. My department read that and kept the thresholds anyway, just adding a line to the handbook that the score is "one input among many." Also the score is per essay, but obviously if a student used a chatbot it might be for one paragraph, or for the outline, or to fix the grammar, and the number doesn't tell you which.

I can't stop chewing on this and I dont have the vocabulary for what's bugging me. It feels like the whole apparatus is quietly assuming things about what writing is and what a mind does, and then the disclaimer is assuming something else, and nobody notices they don't match.

What I want from you: what philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Does treating the score as a measurable magnitude with meaningful thresholds commit us to there being a real, continuous property of "AI-ness" in a text -- and if so, is that property about the text or about the process that produced it? Does the detection project itself presuppose that human and machine writing leave different fingerprints, and how does that sit with the "stochastic parrots" / fancy-autocomplete line (no understanding, just next-token prediction -- so maybe no signature to find) versus the view that these models really do understand (so maybe there's no principled difference in the output at all)? Which view of authorship, intention, and understanding does the misconduct rule need, and which rival views does it quietly rule out? And is the practice internally consistent given its own disclaimer, or is the disclaimer doing something else -- liability-shifting, maybe -- rather than describing the epistemology?

Please give it to me in four sections, in this order:

1. "What it presupposes" -- about a paragraph, the implicit theory spelled out.
2. "What it rules out" -- about a paragraph, the rival views the practice can't accommodate.
3. "Where it's underdetermined" -- short, just the parts several different theories would all license equally well.
4. "What would have to change" -- about a paragraph, what the practice would look like if we replaced the presupposed view with, say, the idea that authorship comes in degrees or that the relevant fact is about process rather than product.
