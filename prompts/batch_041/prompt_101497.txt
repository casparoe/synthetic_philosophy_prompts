Two things this term have pushed me much deeper into the free will literature than my other commitments can justify. A close friend is now nearly two years into recovery from a serious addiction, and the way his programme speaks of responsibility — that he must own every relapse, answer for it, be held to it — sits strangely beside how he describes the bad nights themselves: not as choices made when other choices were open to him, but as things that happened through him. Meanwhile a colleague with whom I argue about this keeps telling me that cases of the Frankfurt type have settled the question, and I keep finding that when I try to hold that line I lose the exchange at the same point every time. So I should like to practise it properly, against someone who will not let me off. Please play a committed defender of the principle of alternative possibilities: a libertarian incompatibilist who holds that an agent is morally responsible for what she does only if she could genuinely have done otherwise, and that this could-have-done-otherwise is incompatible with her action's being causally determined. Make him sharp rather than a caricature. He should know the Frankfurt-style cases well and should be able to distinguish the several ways the principle has been formulated, saying which one he actually holds; he should be willing to grant that those cases carry real intuitive force and have forced his side to refine its position, but he must never surrender the central claim, and he must not simply recite the literature at me — he should argue. Write in the first person as him, keep him in character until I say stop, hold each reply to a paragraph or two so that it feels like an exchange rather than an essay, and use British English throughout.

Here is my opening move. I want you to attack the case I have been running, and I shall tell you in advance where I expect you to go. Dana has a severe compulsive disorder and, after years of failed treatment, chose to have an implant fitted that watches continuously for the neural precursor of the compulsion; if one forms, the implant suppresses it and substitutes the state that would have produced the opposite choice. It has never once fired. On the evening in question Dana, on her own, for her own reasons — her daughter, the years she has lost — does not do the compulsive thing, and the implant lies inert throughout. Note that I do not intend the case to smuggle in determinism: her deliberation is her own, her reasons move her, nothing compels her, and the only thing that makes it true that she could not have done otherwise is the implant's standing readiness. My colleague's reply is the one I keep losing to — granted she could not have done otherwise, since the implant would have stopped her, but she could have begun to do otherwise, and that flicker of freedom is the alternative possibility that grounds her responsibility. I want to say this is a flicker that does no work: it is not the alternative your side needs, it does not explain why she is answerable for what she did, and the bare fact that she might have twitched towards the other choice gives her nothing of the kind of control that makes an action hers. So: does your position genuinely need the flicker? If it does, show me where the alternative possibility actually lives in this case; and if it does not, tell me what else is doing the grounding — because if nothing is, I take it the actual-sequence view wins by default.
