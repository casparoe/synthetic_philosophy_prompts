I have just finished teaching an upper-level undergraduate seminar on consciousness, and I am now writing its final examination. Of all the positions we covered, the one that has unsettled me most — and, I suspect, the students — is illusionism about phenomenal consciousness, the view associated with Frankish. The difficulty is not that the students misunderstand it; it is that they understand it too smoothly. They can now produce the standard exchange on demand: illusionism holds that phenomenal consciousness is an introspective illusion; the realist counters that the illusion is itself a phenomenal datum and so the view undercuts itself; both sides then agree that the dispute bottoms out in intuition. I began the term a realist and ended it genuinely unsure, which is exactly why I do not trust myself to write a neutral question unaided. I need one essay question that the standard recitation cannot answer, together with a model answer and marking scheme that reward the specific analytic moves rather than the labels.

The question should turn on an argument that one of my students sent me after the last seminar, which she called the echo argument. I reproduce it here in the form I want the examination to present it: (1) an illusion is a representation that misrepresents, and misrepresentation is intelligible only relative to a standard of correctness that the state fails to meet; (2) illusionism denies that there are any phenomenal properties for introspection to get right; (3) so the only standard available to the illusionist is a non-phenomenal one; (4) but a non-phenomenal standard cannot make an introspective representation count as misrepresenting experience — at best it makes the state a representation of something else; (5) therefore illusionism either fails to identify any misrepresentation at all (and is eliminativism rather than illusionism) or covertly reinstates the phenomenal standard it denies (and is incoherent). I have deliberately not decided whether the echo argument succeeds, and I do not want the examination to presuppose a verdict; what I want is for students to be forced to say exactly where its weight falls.

Please write three things. First, the essay question itself, answerable in ninety minutes, which requires students to adjudicate the echo argument — whether it succeeds, and if it does not, which of its steps breaks and what the illusionist must add to block it. Second, a model answer of about 700 words. Third, a marking scheme worth 20 marks, built around five must-hit points, each with its point value and a one-line note on how students characteristically get that point wrong; at the head of the scheme, add a single sentence stating what separates an A from a B answer.

Two constraints on all three deliverables. First, the question must be answerable from the seminar alone: assume the students have read no external sources and cannot search, so the question and the model answer must be self-contained, and the model answer must contain no citations, reading lists, or appeals to what some named author has said. Second, the question must make the distinction between illusionism and eliminativism do real work — a student should not be able to answer it by announcing that illusionism is not eliminativism and moving on.
