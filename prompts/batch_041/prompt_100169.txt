I'm a second-year PhD student in epistemology and I'm the TA for our upper-level 'Knowledge and Testimony' seminar, which means the final has landed on my desk and I've got alot of latitude over one of the essay questions. Last year's scripts were wall-to-wall Hume-versus-Reid recitation and I could have marked the lot with a rubber stamp, so this year I want a question that actually does some work. The thing I keep coming back to (I was chewing it over with a mate in the pub and neither of us could settle it) is that the reductionism/non-reductionism fight looks beautifully clean in the textbooks and then goes genuinely messy the moment you take away the hearer's inductive base.

So I've cooked up a case to hang the question on and I'd like you to build the question, a model answer and a marking scheme around it. The case: a research institute launches a system — call it Delphi — that answers factual questions in fluent prose. Every answer of Delphi's that has ever been checked against the world has turned out correct, but the only public record of those checks is the institute's own published reports, and the thing's innards are opaque even to the people who built it. A postgrad, Nadia, has no other source for the question she cares about; she reads a Delphi answer, finds nothing she can independently check it against, and is minded to believe it. Is she justified? I want the question to force students to seperate out what local reductionism, global reductionism and anti-reductionism each have to say here, and — this is the crux I actually care about — to notice that the reductionist's positive-reasons requirement is in trouble precisely because the only available evidence of Delphi's reliability is itself testimony (so the hearer's supposed reasons look circular), whereas the anti-reductionist gets the verdict almost for free and then has to say whether a default entitlement was ever meant to cover an opaque non-human source, or whether quietly restricting it to human testifiers is just a fudge. Bonus if they can say what the case reveals about what the disagreement is really about — the order of a hearer's reasons versus the source of their entitlement, and whose contribution (the hearer's reasons or the speaker's reliability) is doing the justifying.

Could you write me the exam question itself (a paragraph or so, self-contained enough to hand out), a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme. For the scheme I want it built round five must-hit points with marks totalling 20, a short note on how each point separates an A answer from a B one, and a one-line 'typical mistake' for each. Everything in British English, please — the students write in it and it would be a bit odd if the model answer didn't match. And please don't have the model answer name-drop a load of papers or dates; I want the moves, not a bibliography, and the students can't look anything up in the hall anyway.
