Ok, I could use a second opinion on something. My office neighbor is a semanticist -- old-school externalist, thinks internalism about content is basically dead -- and for the last year or so I've been keeping a running list of her verdicts on cases, because I want to figure out what rule she's actually running rather than the one she says she's running. Now I have a new case I want to spring on her, and I'd like to know what she's going to say before I do it.

Her verdicts so far:

- The standard Twin Earth thing. Oscar and his twin say the same-sounding things; she says they mean different things, and each of them is right about his own stuff. So internalism about content is out.
- The Gödel/Schmidt business. The speaker's only grip on the name is a description that the wrong guy happens to satisfy. She says the name still refers to Gödel. Descriptions aren't doing the work.
- Elm/beech. A guy who couldn't tell an elm from a beech to save his life still means elms by 'elm'. She says he defers, and deference counts.
- The arthritis-in-the-thigh case. The patient's usage is idiosyncratic -- he thinks you can get arthritis in the thigh -- and she says his word means what the medical community's word means, so what he says is false. Individual usage doesn't fix reference.
- One I made up for a seminar, which she went along with: a fur-trading town where the trappers who coined the word for the animal had never seen a whole one, only pelts, and their beliefs about it were mostly wrong (they thought it was a kind of aquatic cat). She says the word refers to the marten -- not to any aquatic cat, not to nothing. So the thing, not the beliefs of whoever introduced the term, is doing the work.

Notice the problem: at least two rules fit all five of those. (i) The causal-historical chain rule: reference is fixed by whatever the term was originally grounded in and rides down the chain of use. (ii) The community/environment rule: reference is fixed by whatever the linguistic community's practice and the local environment make the term out to be. Both fit every case above, because in every case the original grounding and current community practice point at the same thing. Which is annoying, and is exactly why I need the new case.

Here it is. On an island, 'mora' has been used for centuries for the fruit of one particular tree. Long ago traders brought a different tree with fruit that's indistinguishable in taste and use; the original tree died out on the island; nobody remembers any of this; there are no experts and no written record; today the islanders just call the new fruit 'mora'. An islander says mora are sweeter this year. What does she say 'mora' refers to? Chain rule: the original fruit, now extinct -- so either the word names a kind that no longer exists or it names nothing and the islanders' claims are false. Community rule: the new fruit, and the islanders are right.

What I want from you: which rule you think she's running, your best guess at her verdict on mora and your reasons, and which further case would actually settle it. My own inclination is that the discriminator is to keep the original kind around -- same setup, but the old tree is still growing there, and when you show the islanders one they say 'that's not mora, that's the old tree.' If she says 'mora' still means the old fruit, she's a chain person; if she says it means the new one, she's a community person. Tell me if there's a better discriminator, and whether you think she'd wriggle out of the mora case by saying the chain got severed or that the term goes empty when the kind dies out. Also, is there a third rule that fits all five of her verdicts and predicts something different again? If so, what does it say about mora, and what case kills it?
