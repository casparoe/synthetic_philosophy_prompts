My metaphysics reading group has spent the term on counterpart theory and transworld identity, and a friend has produced an argument that she believes refutes counterpart theory outright. It runs as follows.

(1) On counterpart theory, a de re modal claim about an individual — that it could have been F, or that it must have been F — is true just in case some, or every, counterpart of that individual is F in some, or every, accessible world.
(2) A counterpart of an individual in another possible world is never identical with that individual; such counterparts are distinct individuals that resemble their originals.
(3) Take the claim that an individual is necessarily identical with itself — that it could not have failed to be the very individual it is.
(4) By (1), that claim is true just in case every counterpart of the individual is identical with the individual in the world where the counterpart occurs.
(5) By (2), no counterpart of an individual in another world is identical with it.
(6) So it is not the case that every counterpart of the individual is identical with it.
(7) So the claim that the individual is necessarily identical with itself comes out false.
(8) But it is a principle of any modal logic worth accepting that everything is necessarily identical with itself.
(9) Therefore counterpart theory is false.
(10) Transworld identity is the remaining option, so transworld identity is true.

She regards this as decisive, and the group is split: some accept (10), some feel that (4) is doing something illegitimate but cannot articulate what, and some think the argument is simply valid and we must choose. For my part, I would rather keep both counterpart theory and the necessity of self-identity than surrender either; I would give up almost anything else first. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked plausible, and what rejecting it costs. If you think the argument is valid instead, tell me what giving up the necessity of self-identity would cost, and what giving up counterpart theory would cost. Finally, say whether the argument proves too much — whether the same pattern of reasoning would embarrass any account of de re modality, and not counterpart theory alone.
