Before my graduate seminar takes up any particular account of probability in the Everettian interpretation, I want the specification of the problem itself worked out, so that we are not evaluating contenders against a moving target. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of probability in a no-collapse, branching universe must deliver. Include the cases it has to get right, with the consideration behind each — equal-amplitude branchings, unequal amplitudes where no finite counting of branches can reproduce the ratio, a measurement with a continuum of outcomes, and a branching that is later undone by recoherence, where we need to know whether the probability is refunded. Include the distinctions it may not collapse — objective branch weight versus an agent's credence, the probability of an outcome versus its relative frequency in any single world, and the many-worlds reading versus a hidden-variable reading that merely looks branching. Include the formal results it must respect: that the squared-amplitude measure is not negotiable, that any measure over outcomes is constrained by Gleason-type considerations, and that the preferred-basis problem undercuts any naive counting of branches. For each requirement, mark whether it is non-negotiable or merely a commitment of one camp — decision-theoretic derivations, envariance arguments, and self-locating-uncertainty accounts each load the list differently. Then tell me which single requirement, if enforced, would eliminate the most of the live positions, and whether any pair in your list looks jointly unsatisfiable. I care far more about the criteria than the scoring. Above all, I want the requirement that probability not be quietly redefined as an agent's ignorance about which branch he occupies, since that is the move on which several otherwise attractive accounts depend.
