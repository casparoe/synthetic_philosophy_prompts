I sit on the board of a small neighbourhood association — roughly forty households, a shared tool shed, an annual street fair, a modest budget. We have a printed set of standing procedures that has grown by accretion since the association was founded in the mid-1990s, and at our last meeting two newer members proposed a clean-up. Most of the rules are plainly sensible, but three look arbitrary: draft minutes must be circulated to every household before they can be formally approved; the tool shed key must be signed out even though every household holds its own copy; and the street fair budget must be approved twice, once in March and again in April. I found myself defending all three, mainly on the ground that somebody at some point must have had a reason, and that we ought not to discard arrangements whose point we no longer understand. A friend objected that this argument proves far too much: by that logic no institution could ever be reformed, since the reasons behind inherited arrangements are usually invisible to the people who inherit them. I suspect he is half right, and that I need a sharper version of the caution I was reaching for — one that tells me when the caution is warranted and when it is merely an excuse.

Some further facts that may bear on it. Our archive is complete, so for any rule we can usually recover when it was introduced and by whom. The double budget approval dates from 2007; the minutes show it was a workaround for a treasurer who travelled every April, and that treasurer moved away in 2012 with no recurrence of the problem. The circulation requirement dates from the founding meeting, with no reason recorded, and the member who proposed it has since died. The key-sign-out rule also dates from the founding; the one person who remembers it believes it had something to do with insurance, though she is unsure, and our policy has been rewritten twice since. I am less interested in a verdict on each rule than in the reasoning that should produce one: what precisely the argument for caution consists in, what makes it strong or weak in a given case, and how the recoverability of the original reason — and the fact that the reason is sometimes recoverable and sometimes not — should adjust the weight it carries. I would also like to know how to recognize when the caution is being abused, that is, when someone is invoking the wisdom of the past not as a genuine epistemic warning but as a way of shutting down a decision the group is entitled to revisit.

Please lead with your advice in a few paragraphs of prose, addressed to me and my board. Then give me a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "general_principle" (a string, your central piece of reasoning in two or three sentences); "rule_assessments" (an array of three objects, one per rule, each with the fields "rule" (a short string identifying it), "verdict" (one of "keep", "scrap", or "investigate_further"), and "reason" (a string)); "conditions_for_caution" (an array of strings, each naming a condition that makes the conservative presumption strong); "conditions_against_caution" (an array of strings, each naming a condition that weakens or defeats it); "abuse_test" (a string, a practical test for distinguishing a genuine appeal from a pretextual one); "information_to_gather" (an array of strings, listing what we should establish before deciding); and "what_would_change_the_verdict" (a string, describing the evidence that would flip your recommendation on any of the three rules).
