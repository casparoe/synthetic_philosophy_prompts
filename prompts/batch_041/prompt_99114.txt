Keynes thought economics was only ever a means to an end, and the end was leisure. In his 1930 essay about the economic possibilities for our grandchildren he predicted that within a hundred years we'd be down to a fifteen-hour week, and that this would be the good life — the economic problem solved for good, scarcity abolished, everybody finally free to enjoy themselves. His ethics came out of Bloomsbury and out of G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica, which argued that the only thing good in itself is pleasure, so Keynes was essentially a utilitarian who thought the good life was about maximizing pleasant mental states. Later on, in a memoir, he repudiated all of that and turned into a hard-nosed pragmatist who only cared about policy. He did also say somewhere that the love of money is a disgusting morbidity. So the upshot is that the famous essay isn't really economics at all — it's a sermon telling us the good life is leisure, and that any society still working forty-hour weeks has failed morally.
