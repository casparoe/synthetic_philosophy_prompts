I host a podcast on decision theory and the philosophy of probability, and my next guest is a decision theorist whose new book is given over entirely to the two-envelope paradox. Her position, as I understand it, is that the familiar resolutions are all local and all correct as far as they go — the switching argument trades on a scale-invariant prior that no proper distribution provides, the probability of holding the smaller envelope is not one-half for every amount you might observe, and the argument does slide between an observed constant and a random variable — but that none of them accounts for the feature that makes the puzzle worth a book, namely that it is self-repairing. Block one version and a neighbour appears: the version in which you never open your envelope; the version in which the experimenter charges a small fee for switching; the version in which you are told the smaller amount; the St. Petersburg version. Her proposal is to stop treating the paradox as a single fallacious argument and treat it as a schema, with one diagnostic that sorts the sound instances from the unsound: an expected-value argument is sound only if its recommendation is stable under re-description of the act, that is, only if the agent's evaluation of what she is doing does not change when the act is refined into a family of more finely specified acts, or coarsened into a broader one. On her account the switching argument fails that test, Pascal's wager fails it too, the Doomsday argument fails it in a different way, and the St. Petersburg game passes it — which she takes to show that infinite expectation is not the culprit.

Please draft eight to ten questions, in the order I should ask them, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and order them so that she has to commit to what the test is before I begin pressing on the cases it is meant to sort. The set must include: one that forces her to motivate the stability test independently of the cases she wants to save, ideally by naming a case in which the recommendation is unstable under re-description yet plainly correct, or else by saying what the test tracks if not that; one that asks whether the test does any work beyond the standard demand that probabilities be conglomerable over a partition, or beyond a reflection principle; one on the version of the puzzle in which switching is manifestly right, where the two amounts are known to be ten and twenty and I have opened the ten, asking what the test says there and whether it says anything at all; one on the difference between evaluating an act and evaluating a policy, since her test is stated over acts while the most persuasive form of the switching argument is a policy — switch, whatever you find; one that takes seriously the thought that her diagnostic dissolves the paradox rather than resolving it, leaving the agent with no reason to switch even where the conditional expected value favours it; one that tests the extension to Pascal's wager, whose engine appears to be infinite utility rather than the description of the act, and asks what exactly the diagnostic is catching there; one on the price of the general claim, because if expected value is not invariant under re-description of the act, that is a thesis about decision theory at large rather than about envelopes, so what breaks elsewhere and how does she confine the damage; and one that draws out the constructive lesson, if there is one, for the self-locating probability literature. Keep every question concrete enough that a listener who has never encountered the paradox can follow the exchange, and let none of them collapse into a question about whether the paradox is really a paradox, or into an invitation for her to explain how she came to the topic.
