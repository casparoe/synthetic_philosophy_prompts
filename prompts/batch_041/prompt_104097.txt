Our reading group has been stuck on Scanlon's contractualism for about four months, specifically the aggregation problem, and I'm starting to think we're not even having the same arguement. Here's the shape of it. The contractualist story is that an act is wrong when it's ruled out by principles no one could reasonably reject, and the objections doing the rejecting are supposed to be made from the standpoint of each person separately — you can't just add up the mild gripes of the many to outweigh the serious complaint of the one. Fine. Then someone says: so contractualism can't explain why we should save five people instead of one when saving the one is easy; the one's complaint is enormous but the five's complaints, taken one at a time, are what, exactly? And the reply is always, no, it handles numbers, because — and then some move about generic reasons, or about how the principle that lets you save the one is itself rejectable, or about not treating anyone merely as a means. And around we go. Nobody's moved and I don't think anyone's being stupid.

So what I want isn't a survey and isn't a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement. Is the fight really about the numbers, or about what kind of thing a moral reason is — whether reasonable rejection names a threshold you clear or fail, versus a comparative weight you can trade off? Is it that the two sides are answering different questions under one name — one asking what makes an act wrong (a justificatory question), the other asking which act we have most reason to do (a deliberative one)? Or is it that they share a picture of morality as addressed to persons one at a time and differ only over whether that picture can be made to yield the answers everyone already accepts? My own hypothesis, which I'd like you to test rather than assume, is that this is a disagreement about the unit of justification — whether the justificatory burden gets discharged person-by-person or can be discharged collectively — and that once you see that, the two sides aren't so much contradicting each other as talking past each other about what a principle is for. Tell me where that's right and where it's wrong.

Be concrete about what each side would actually say in the one-versus-five case; don't just slap a label on the positions. And finish by telling me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or nothing at all, with a reason. Also theres one more thing: if you think the impasse is a sign the question is ill-posed rather than just hard, say so and say why, because half the group suspects that and the other half thinks it's a cop-out.
