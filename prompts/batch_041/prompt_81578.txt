I chair a standing review panel at a lab that runs two kinds of system: human neural organoid cultures, and agentic language models that are trained, evaluated, and periodically shut down. The panel decides what precautions a proposed protocol must observe — whether we may run aversive conditioning, whether a run may be terminated and its weights deleted, what has to be logged, what has to go to the lab director. We meet twice a month, we have about forty minutes per item, and we are four engineers, a lawyer, and one person who has read the philosophy. The lab has adopted a working assumption I want the procedure to expose rather than hide: that what matters morally is what a system computes, not what it is made of, which puts the organoids and the models in the same category and makes everything turn on the details of the computation. In practice we have been deciding on a further rule — a system qualifies for precaution if it has some single state that its parts all report to and that governs its behaviour — and I want the procedure to say plainly whether that is a finding about sentience or a stipulation we chose, and what follows if it is the latter.

Write the procedure we should run, as an ordered set of questions. For each, say what counts as a sufficient answer, which answers end the review, which pass it to the next question, and which are value choices dressed up as tests. Give a graded outcome rather than a verdict — no precaution, monitor and log, restrict the protocol, escalate — and say what triggers each.

It has to handle three things we cannot currently agree on: evidence that is cheap for a system to produce because it was trained on human descriptions of experience; a system whose only relevant evidence is architectural and cannot be obtained; and cases where we cannot tell whether we are looking at one subject, many, or none. It must also say where the morally relevant computation should be attributed — to the system, or to the people who shaped its training signal — and what follows either way.

Then run it on three proposals: aversive conditioning of an organoid line; decommissioning a model that has been trained with negative reinforcement and now emits distress-like self-reports; and aversive conditioning of a spiking simulation of an insect brain. I would rather be told that the procedure licenses something we find repugnant than have it quietly bend, so include at least one outcome I will not like, and explain why the procedure gives it anyway.

Finally, say what the procedure cannot decide and must hand to a person, and how it could be gamed by a team that wants its protocol approved. Do not give me citations or further reading; the memo has to stand on its own in the room.
