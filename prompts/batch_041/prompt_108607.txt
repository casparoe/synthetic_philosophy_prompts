Simpson's paradox is usually introduced as an arithmetic curiosity: an association observed in a whole dataset can reverse when the dataset is partitioned into subgroups. Introductory discussions often leave students with one of two oversimplified takeaways — either "always trust the subgroups, because the aggregate is an artifact of aggregation" or "always trust the aggregate, because subgrouping is a form of cherry-picking." I want you to explain why neither blanket prescription is defensible, and why adjudicating any particular case requires a substantive assumption about the causal structure that generated the data.

To make this concrete, consider a dataset in which a treatment T is positively associated with recovery R overall, but negatively associated with recovery within each level of some third variable S. Explain how this exact pattern of reversal could arise in two very different ways:

1. a case in which the aggregate association is the misleading one, because S is a common cause of both T and R, so that the subgroup analysis is the one that reflects the genuine causal effect of T on R; and
2. a case in which the subgroup association is the misleading one, because S is a common effect of T and R, so that conditioning on S opens a spurious path that was absent in the aggregate.

For each case, describe a plausible substantive scenario and walk through the causal structure that produces the reversal, making clear why conditioning on the variable has the effect it does. Then state, as precisely as you can, the general principle governing when it is legitimate to condition on a variable rather than analyze the pooled data — and say plainly what that principle implies about the popular idea that one should simply "control for everything you can measure."

Before writing your answer, brainstorm the distinct causal structures that could generate the reversal, and note which of them are distinguishable from the data alone and which are not. Aim for an explanation that is rigorous but intuitive, and avoid merely reciting a textbook account of the paradox.
