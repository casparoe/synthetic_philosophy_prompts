**The Unilateralist's Curse Is a Claim About Decision Rules, Not About Acting Alone**

Suppose that a number of agents are each in a position to take some action that affects everyone, and that each of them can act without the agreement of the others. Each agent is a decent but fallible judge of whether the action is worth taking. The action goes ahead if any one of them judges it worth taking. As the number of agents rises, the probability that the action is taken tends to one, and it does so whether or not the action is worth taking: the rule becomes uninformative about the very question it was meant to answer. This is usually called the unilateralist's curse, and the usual remedy is some conformity principle, under which agents in such cases should defer to a collective decision procedure rather than act on their own judgement.

I want to argue that both the diagnosis and the remedy are framed in the wrong terms. The diagnosis is put as a claim about acting alone, as though the mischief lay in the fact that one agent's judgement can bind the rest. But aloneness is doing no work. What generates the curse is the shape of the decision rule: it is a disjunction over agents. The action goes ahead if agent one is right, or agent two, or agent three. Any rule of that form has the same property, whether it is triggered by one agent or by a committee that acts on any single member's say-so. Equally, a rule that demands a large supermajority can fail in the opposite direction: where the agents' errors are correlated, requiring agreement screens out nothing, because agreement is precisely what correlated error produces.

That last point deserves a name. Call diffuse unilateralism the case in which agents reach their judgements independently, so that their errors are largely uncorrelated; call clustered unilateralism the case in which they share a training, an incentive, an institution or a body of evidence, so that their errors move together. The classic curse is a claim about the diffuse case. But the cases that ought to worry us are clustered, and there the conformity remedy is not merely weak but counterproductive: it converts many small independent chances of being wrong into one large shared chance, and then launders that chance as legitimacy. A unanimous committee with a blind spot is not a correction to a thousand individuals with the same blind spot; it is the same blind spot with better paperwork.

If the curse is a property of the trigger rather than of the actor, the remedy should change the trigger rather than the actor. Conformity asks whether enough others agreed. The alternative I want to propose asks whether the others could have checked. Call it the evidentiary trigger: a unilateral action is permissible only if the agent can state, before acting and in a form others could audit, the evidence that would have made them refrain. This does not require that anyone agree, and it does not require that the agent be right. It requires that the agent's private judgement be made public in the one respect that matters, namely its falsifiability, so that the errors that do occur are errors someone could have caught, and so that the agent has a standing reason to go looking for the evidence that would stop them.

Two riders. The first concerns scope. The curse is often applied to actions that can be undone at little cost, where a mistake is a lesson rather than a loss; confining the remedy to irreversible actions would spare a great deal of needless deference. The second concerns symmetry. Conformity is a remedy for errors that are symmetric across agents and for a collective procedure that is unbiased. Where the collective is captured, timorous, or simply slower than the harm, the ability of one agent to act alone is a corrective rather than a curse, a unilateralist's blessing, and a conformity principle would license the very failure it was meant to prevent.

I am unsure whether the diffuse/clustered distinction is doing real work here, or whether it is a relabelling of the familiar point that independence assumptions are fragile, and I suspect the evidentiary trigger may collapse into an ordinary demand for accountability. With that in mind, I would be grateful for comments of the following kinds, roughly in order of importance:

1. Does the distinction between diffuse and clustered unilateralism earn its keep, or is it a restatement of something simpler?
2. Is the evidentiary trigger a genuine alternative to a conformity principle, or is it the same proposal under a different name? If the latter, which version of the conformity principle is it, and does the essay's rhetoric obscure the identity?
