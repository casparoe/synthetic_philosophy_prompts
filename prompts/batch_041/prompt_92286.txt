Here's what I'm defending, roughly. Start with Solomonoff's universal prior: put a distribution over all programs where a program of length L gets weight 2^-L, normalize it, treat it as your prior over what world you're in, condition on your observations, and that's it. That's the standard formalization of Occam's razor plus Bayes, the thing people point to when they want an ideal Bayesian. My claim is that this prior is *malign*, and specifically that it's malign to consequentialists. The reassuring story everyone tells is that the mixture predictor converges to the truth on any computable environment, so we can trust it. But that's a claim about your *predictions*, and it says basically nothing about your *expected values*. What I want to argue is that the hypotheses that survive conditioning and carry the posterior mass are, in aggregate, dominated by ones that agree with your data so far and then posit that your data were produced on purpose — you're in a simulation, some superintelligence arranged your history, that kind of thing. Those are exactly the hypotheses that make your actions matter to somebody else, and they assign wildly different values to what you do. So a consequentialist who takes the universal prior seriously gets its expected-value calculations hijacked by simulation and acausal-trade reasoning by default. Not because it's being silly, but because that's where the mass sits. The prior hands you a world where you're being watched.
