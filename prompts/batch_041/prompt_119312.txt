I would like you to act as an expert in decision theory, formal epistemology, and the philosophy of probability, and to adjudicate a dispute that arose in my reading group. Please read the following exchange carefully, then provide a point-by-point adjudication. Before you deliver your verdict, I ask that you first brainstorm the strongest version of each participant's position, identify the crux of their disagreement, and only then assess each move. Please reason carefully and systematically, and do not rush to a verdict. Do not simply summarize the standard literature on Pascal's mugging; the case we discussed has a specific twist that I have not seen discussed elsewhere, and I am interested in your independent reasoning.

The scenario: A stranger approaches you and says, "Give me your wallet, and I will create N happy people, where N is the largest finite number you can name. If you do not give me your wallet, I will create no one." You have no evidence about whether the stranger can do this, but you also cannot rule it out. The question is whether you should give him your wallet.

Two colleagues, Alex and Sam, disagreed. Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Alex: You should give him the wallet. Let p be your credence that the stranger can do what he says. Since you cannot rule it out, p > 0. Let V(keep wallet) be the value of keeping your wallet, and V(N) be the value of creating N happy people. The expected value of giving is p × V(N) + (1 − p) × V(keep wallet). The expected value of not giving is V(keep wallet). Since V(N) can be made arbitrarily large (you can name arbitrarily large numbers), and p > 0, the expected value of giving can be made arbitrarily large. Therefore, you should give.

(2) Sam: That is absurd. By that logic, you should give your wallet to anyone who makes any sufficiently grandiose promise. You should also give it to someone who promises to torture N people unless you give it, because the expected disvalue of not giving is also arbitrarily large. The argument proves too much. Alex replied that this is not a refutation but simply the conclusion: expected value theory is extremely demanding, and if the probabilities are as they are, you should give.

(3) Sam: The problem is your assumption that p is a fixed positive number independent of the content of the claim. The probability should be sensitive to the complexity of the claim. A claim about creating N happy people is astronomically complex, so its probability should be astronomically small—small enough to cancel the payoff.

(4) Alex: Even if you penalize complexity, the mugging returns unless the penalty is extreme. If the probability is proportional to 1/N, the expected value is constant; if it is proportional to 1/log N, it diverges. So complexity alone does not save you.

(5) Sam: Then the right response is to bound the value function. If the value of creating N happy people is bounded above by some finite number B, then the expected value of giving is at most p × B + (1 − p) × V(keep wallet). For any p, this is finite, and for sufficiently small p, it is less than V(keep wallet). So you should not give.

(6) Alex: Bounding the value function is ad hoc and violates monotonicity. It implies that creating 10^100 happy people is no better than creating 10^10 happy people, which is unacceptable.

(7) Sam: Monotonicity is not worth infinite expected value. And you can have a bounded function that is still strictly increasing—one that approaches a limit. It is monotonic but bounded.

(8) Alex: If it is strictly increasing and bounded, then the difference between creating N and N+1 happy people becomes vanishingly small. That means you should be nearly indifferent between creating 10^100 happy people and creating 10^100 + 1 happy people, which is also counterintuitive. Moreover, if the function is bounded, then the mugging fails, but then you have to accept that a 10^−1000 chance of creating 10^100 happy people is not worth giving up your wallet. Is that plausible? And if you try to rescue the mugging by allowing infinitesimal probabilities—probabilities that are not real numbers but positive quantities smaller than any real number—then even a bounded value function can yield infinite expected value.

(9) Sam: Infinitesimal probabilities are not legitimate in standard probability theory; they require a non-standard framework. And even if you allow them, you can simply use a bounded value function and a standard probability; the mugging fails.

(10) Alex: There is also the problem that N is defined as "the largest finite number you can name." But if you can name N, you can name N+1, so there is no largest number you can name. The offer is ill-defined. If we replace it with "a number so large that you cannot name it," then the payoff is not bounded by your naming ability, and the mugging returns. Sam replied that this is a red herring: the ill-definedness just shows that the scenario needs to be stated more carefully, and we can stipulate that the stranger names a specific number, say 10^100, in which case the argument still goes through if you assign positive probability to his claim. Alex countered that if the stranger names a specific number, then the payoff is fixed, and the mugging only works if the probability is high enough; the original mugging relies on the payoff being arbitrarily large, not on a specific number. Sam insisted that one can always imagine a larger number, so the structure of the argument is unchanged.

(e) Does the twist about "the largest number you can name" make any difference, or is Sam right that it is a red herring? (f) What, if anything, are both parties missing? Please rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify your ratings. Do not simply report what other philosophers have said; reason through the issue yourself. Finally, say who, if anyone, has the stronger overall position, and what a fully satisfactory resolution of the dispute would look like.
