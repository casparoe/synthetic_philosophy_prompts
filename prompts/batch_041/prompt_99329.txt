I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, and for most of that time I took it as settled that physics had closed the question of time: the block universe is real, the present moment is a matter of perspective, and anyone still insisting on a single objective now is simply behind on the physics. I have come to think that this attitude fuses something the physics genuinely establishes with something philosophers of physics actively dispute, and now I have to run a session on it.

Here is the setup. Once a month I lead a discussion group of adults who read the same blogs I do: comfortable with arguments, no physics beyond high school, no tensor math, fine with a spacetime diagram once it is drawn for them. Next session is sixty minutes at a whiteboard, and the topic is the Rietdijk–Putnam argument — the case that special relativity, through the relativity of simultaneity, rules out a single objective present and thereby supports four-dimensionalism. The room will arrive already convinced of the conclusion and ignorant of the argument for it, which is the worst combination for actually learning anything.

Do not explain the argument to me. Plan the explanation. I want the following, and I want them kept distinct rather than blended into a lecture outline:

The order in which the pieces must be introduced, and the dependency between them. I take the relativity of simultaneity to be load-bearing, but tell me what has to be in place before it — the difference between what an observer sees and what is simultaneous in that observer's frame, what a frame is, the fact that a simultaneity slice is fixed by convention rather than measured — and, crucially, where the step from frame-relative simultaneity to a frame-independent four-dimensional reality must be isolated so the group can inspect it instead of absorbing it. If a later idea can be dropped without loss at this level, say so and say where.

The one example that should carry the whole session, and what it must have and must lack. I want a concrete scenario a non-physicist can hold in their head for the full hour, with two observers in relative motion who assign different simultaneity slices to a distant event. Tell me the features it needs — symmetry between the observers, a spacelike-separated pair, no signals and no observation in the setup — and tell me whether to use the Andromeda version, and if not, why not. My guess is that its light-year scale invites the very confusion I am trying to prevent and that its alien-fleet framing drags in fatalism, but I would rather you make that call on the merits.

The misconceptions the group will bring, in the order they will actually surface, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. I expect at least: simultaneity as an observation effect rather than a coordinate assignment; the slide from simultaneity being frame-relative to there being no fact of the matter about the present; determinism or fatalism, given that the argument was originally presented as a proof of determinism; the appeal to the cosmic microwave background rest frame, or to cosmic time, as a rescue of the present; and the reflexive sense that the argument is either a knockdown or an obvious fallacy. For each, tell me not just that it exists but where it must be intercepted, because I think some of these have to be pre-empted before the example is even drawn.

The check for understanding at the end: one question that reveals whether they have grasped the shape of the argument — specifically, whether they can state what follows from the relativity of simultaneity alone and what further premise is required to reach four-dimensionalism — rather than whether they can recite the conclusion they already believed.

What to leave out at this level, with instructions to say so out loud when I do: the algebra of the Lorentz transformation, the twin paradox, anything from general relativity, and the phenomenology of passage, unless you can convince me one of these is doing work I cannot get elsewhere.

Finally, the group will treat the conclusion as dogma, so the plan has to keep the physics and the metaphysics visibly separate. Where the answer reports something that is settled among people who know the topic, it should mark it as settled; where it reports something genuinely contested — above all whether relativity rules out a privileged present, which is exactly where the real disagreement lies — it must present it as contested and not as the consensus, and it must not let the four-dimensional conclusion read as a consequence of the physics as a matter of established fact.
