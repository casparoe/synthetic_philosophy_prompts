I am presenting a paper at our lab's alignment reading group next month, and I need a question-period prep sheet. The thesis I will defend is that AI safety via debate works, to the extent that it works, not by making the honest debater more persuasive but by making dishonesty self-incriminating. On my account, the judge does not need to understand the domain at issue; the judge needs only to recognize that the two debaters cannot both be right. Debate is therefore best redescribed as an inconsistency-amplification mechanism, and the quantity that actually scales oversight is the judge's competence at detecting mutual inconsistency — a consistency-checking competence — rather than the judge's domain knowledge. The practical upshot is that we should design debate protocols to maximize the surface area for contradiction: obliging each debater to commit to narrow, individually checkable claims, and rewarding the debater who catches the other in a contradiction, rather than maximizing the informativeness or rhetorical force of the arguments. I will illustrate the point with a toy case in which the judge knows nothing about the subject matter and has exactly one tool available — the knowledge that the two debaters cannot both be right.

The audience is a mixed reading group. Most of them are RLHF and reward-modeling engineers; they treat oversight as a reward-modeling problem and are allergic to anything that sounds like smuggling logic into it. Two are learning theorists who know the equilibrium and soundness arguments for debate inside out, and they will press on whether any of my claims survive adversarial play. A couple of philosophers of science also attend, and they care about whether inconsistency is even the right unit of analysis when the claims at issue are statistical or probabilistic rather than propositional.

Write me the prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get from this room — phrased in their vocabulary, not in mine — ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Flag which objections I should concede outright, and which I should hold. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody asks. I do not want the generic objections a nameless alignment skeptic would raise; I want the ones these particular people will raise.
