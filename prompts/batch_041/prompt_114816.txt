Since retiring from a career in experimental geophysics, I have been attending a philosophy of physics reading group, and I have now reached the edge of what I can work out on my own. Last week a visiting speaker used a line I have seen on posters for thirty years -- that spacetime tells matter how to move and matter tells spacetime how to curve -- to announce that the long quarrel between substantivalists and relationists is settled, and settled in the relationist's favour, because the geometry of spacetime is nothing over and above a codification of how matter is arranged and how it moves. He added, as though it were the same remark, that all motion is relative. When I asked afterwards what work the slogan was doing that Newton's bucket had not already done, I received a look suggesting that the question was naive.

Please act as a philosopher of physics with a specialist's command both of general relativity and of the substantivalism--relationism literature. Think carefully, and before you write anything, brainstorm for your own use a list of the distinct precise claims the slogan could be taken to make. Then give me that list as a menu, ordered from the nearly empty to the strong, with a verdict on each: which readings are platitudes no one denies, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which are too vague to assess until they are sharpened. I would like to know, in particular:

- What the slogan says about the bucket, and whether any reading of it makes the bucket argument obsolete rather than merely unfashionable; whether the two halves of the line are separate theses, and whether either half can hold while the other fails -- in a region, say, where there is no matter to speak of.
- What kind of priority the word tells is meant to express: causation, supervenience, determination, or nothing more than the fact that one writes down the field equations and solves for one quantity in terms of the other.
- Whether all motion is relative is a weaker claim than the slogan, an equivalent one, or a different claim altogether, and how each fares against rotation.
- Which reading the speaker must have had in mind for his conclusion to follow, whether that reading is one a careful relationist would accept, and whether it is the reading most people who repeat the line would recognise.

Where the slogan is being made to settle a question it cannot settle, say so plainly and say why. I would prefer your own verdicts to a reading list; I am no longer near a university library and citations would be wasted on me. Finish with a short paragraph, in the plain register of a seminar comment, that I could read aloud to the group without embarrassing myself.
