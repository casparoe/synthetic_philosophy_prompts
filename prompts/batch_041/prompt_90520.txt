I sit on the selection panel of a research foundation that funds work in the historical social sciences, and last night I spent most of an evening arguing with an old friend — a historian of the early modern period — in a way that I now realise our panel has been arguing implicitly for two years without ever admitting it. On Thursday the panel meets to choose between two finalists for our one large five-year grant, and I am expected to circulate the decision memo beforehand. I would like you to write it.

Both proposals are strong, and both are, in their own way, about explanation.

The first comes from a team of environmental and economic historians and is a long-run study of a mountainous region across roughly five centuries. It assembles tax rolls, parish registers, harvest records and climate reconstructions, and its central claim is that the region's recurring pattern of settlement, flight and revolt is explained by slow, durable conditions — soils, altitude, the timing of rainfall, the endemic disease environment — the sort of thing the longue durée tradition took as its proper object. On the team's account these conditions fixed the outer bounds of what anyone living there could do, whatever they chose. Over five centuries almost nothing changes at the level they care about, and they present that very durability as the explanatory point.

The second comes from a single historian and is a tight study of a few decisive months in one valley: a dispute over a mill, a magistrate's decision, a rumour, a crowd, and an outcome nobody intended and that could easily have gone otherwise. The claim is that what happened there and then set the region's trajectory for a century, and that the outcome was contingent — that a slightly different decision by one official would have produced a different world. The slow conditions cannot explain the divergence, on this view, because they were the same on both sides of the fork.

My friend, over the third bottle, took both horns at once, and that is the part I need the memo to answer. Against the first proposal he said that a condition which does not vary cannot explain a variation: the soil is the same in the years the harvest fails and the years it does not, so the soil cannot be why one year went one way. Whatever explains must make a difference, and constants make none. Against the second he said the mirror image: one magistrate's decision is too small, too local and too quick to carry the weight of a century, and calling it decisive is only reading the story backwards from an outcome we already know.

I do not think he can be right about both, though I cannot yet say which of the two I would give up, and the panel will want a reason rather than a preference.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should reach a clear bottom line — which of the two proposals we fund in full and which we decline; the budget covers exactly one, and there is no possibility of funding both in part. The recommendation should rest on an explicit view of what it takes for something, whether a slow condition or a fast decision, to explain historical change, and it should give my friend's two objections the strongest hearing they can get, including the best case for the proposal I end up rejecting. I would also like it to say what we should tell the applicant we turn down, since both of them are people I will have to sit opposite at conferences for the next decade. Write it as a formal memo from the panel chair, at a length I could read aloud in ten minutes.
