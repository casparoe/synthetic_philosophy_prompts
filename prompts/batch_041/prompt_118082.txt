I am a second-year law student, and a note I am trying to write on the fraud-on-the-market presumption in securities class actions has collided with a seminar on the philosophy of economics that I have been sitting in on, so I am carrying two problems at once. The practice I want you to examine is this. In a securities-fraud class action, plaintiffs may proceed on the theory that a company's false or misleading statement was absorbed by the market, so that anyone who bought at the inflated price was defrauded; the central evidence is usually an event study showing that the price fell when the truth came out, and the size of the price drop is also how damages get measured. Defendants try to rebut the presumption by showing that the stock was thinly traded, that the market was inefficient, or that the price move was caused by something else, all of which treats the price as a noisy, contingent, manipulable artifact rather than as a reliable reading of value. The same legal system elsewhere insists that prices detach easily from fundamentals: it mandates disclosure on the assumption that prices would otherwise fail to reflect value, it forbids manipulation, and it requires insiders to abstain from trading on private information on the assumption that such trading moves prices and thereby harms the people on the other side of it. I would like you, acting as an expert in the epistemology of markets and the economics of information, to reconstruct the theory of the market's epistemic authority that this body of practice presupposes — what view of prices as evidence, and of the market as a knower, the practice only makes sense under, which rival views it quietly rules out, whether the parts of the practice presuppose consistent things, and where the practice is merely compatible with a theory rather than committed to it.

Here is the difficulty I keep running into and cannot resolve on my own, and I should say at the outset that I am out of my depth on the formal results, so please explain them in words rather than symbols. There is a family of results in the economics of information that appear to deny that a market can be informative in the way the practice requires. One, associated with Milgrom and Stokey, says roughly that if the market is in efficient equilibrium, if there are no noise traders or other non-rational participants, and if the structure by which traders acquire their information is itself common knowledge, then traders who hold private information cannot profit from it: any bid or offer reveals the private information before anyone accepts it, so no trade occurs. A companion result, Aumann's agreement theorem, says that two agents who share a prior and whose posterior beliefs are common knowledge must have the same posterior, so common knowledge of disagreement is impossible among Bayesians. But a price that moves on news requires trade, and trade seems to require disagreement. The practice therefore appears to presuppose either heterogeneous priors or outright error, exactly the things a Bayesian picture calls irrational — or else it presupposes that prices move for reasons unrelated to information, in which case the price is not evidence of anything. Please begin by laying out the candidate epistemologies of the market that could license the practice — a Hayekian story about the aggregation of dispersed knowledge, a heterogeneous-priors story on which the price is an average of opinions, a noise-and-liquidity story, a story on which the price is a social fact rather than a truth-tracker — before you commit to a reading. Then tell me which of them the practice actually requires and which it merely fits, where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and how the two halves of the regime could be reconciled, if they can be.

Finally, suppose we replaced the implicit epistemology with one that is honest about the no-trade result: prices as the residue of disagreement plus risk premia, not as a truth-tracker. What would have to change? What would a plaintiff have to prove, what evidence would become relevant or irrelevant, and would the practice of measuring damages by price movement survive? One of my classmates has proposed that a plaintiff should have to show not merely that the price moved but that opinion in the market genuinely diverged, so that there was something for the price to aggregate; tell me whether that requirement is coherent or self-undermining, and what it would prove even if it were satisfied. Think carefully and be explicit about your reasoning as you go; do not worry about citing the literature, since I am after the structure of the commitments rather than a bibliography.
