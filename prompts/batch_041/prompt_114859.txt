For the past year I have been the enforcer on a friend's self-binding arrangement. She placed a sum in escrow with me; the terms provide that she may revoke the arrangement only with my agreement; my judgment of whether a request to revoke is genuine is written in as final; and, because she is thorough, a further clause names a third person who can overrule me. Last month the same structure turned up in the other half of my life. I sit on the board of a small housing cooperative whose bylaws contain a clause that no one can amend, protecting a rule that every living member now wants gone; our lawyer says the clause holds. In both cases I am told the device exists to protect a person from herself. I have stopped being able to see where the protection ends and someone else's authority begins, and I would rather think this through in writing than in conversation with my friend.

Write a satirical op-ed of 700 to 800 words, deadpan and never winking, in the voice of a professional commitment counsel — a consultant who drafts self-binding agreements for clients and, in the trade's idiom, calls them Ulysses contracts. The venue is a trade newsletter read by people who draft such agreements. His thesis: the defect in these arrangements is the messy middle — the escape clause, the cooling-off period, the procedure by which a client pleads her case to her own enforcer. His proposal: a serious precommitment should be strictly irrevocable, with no standing for the bound party to petition and no procedure for renegotiation; and entrenched constitutional clauses should be drafted on the same principle, since a document that a later generation may amend has not bound anyone to anything. Let him follow his own logic without flinching — through the point at which the enforcer's judgment must be final, through the question of who enforces against the enforcer, and out the far side — and let him never notice where he has arrived. He should be competent and even sympathetic; give the argument its strongest form rather than a strawman, so that the recoil belongs to the reader.

Three things the piece must get right, so that someone who works on this laughs rather than winces. It must not confuse weakness of will with a considered change of values, and it should make plain that no drafting can distinguish them — which is precisely why the device has to be deaf to both. It should register that once a third party's judgment is final, what was one person's relation to her own future has become a relation between two people. And it should treat the constitutional case as the same device rather than a loose analogy: the amendment clause is a revocation clause, so the speaker needs a position on an amending power that is itself part of the thing it amends. Leave unresolved whether the bound person is the same person.
