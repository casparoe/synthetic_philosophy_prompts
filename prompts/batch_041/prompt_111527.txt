I was in a graduate seminar last week on early modern moral philosophy, working through the justice-and-force material in Pascal, and one of my students — trying to be provocative — boiled the whole thing down to "might makes right" and then sat back like he'd said something. Another student shot back that that's Thrasymachus, not Pascal. And I realised I couldn't cleanly say what the difference is, at least not on my feet, which is embarrassing when I'm the one who assigned the reading. So I want to work it out properly.

The annoying thing is that "might makes right" gets made to do at least five different jobs in my department. Sometimes it's a descriptive claim about how the world actually runs — whoever holds the power ends up setting the rules. Sometimes it's a definitional claim about what the word justice means. Sometimes it's a debunking claim about where our moral concepts came from. Sometimes it's a naked normative endorsement, which is the one everyone pretends nobody holds. And sometimes it's a fatalism about what to do next: if might makes right, then resisting is just losing slowly. Pascal seems to be doing something closer to: justice and force need each other, justice without force is impotent, force without justice is tyrannical, and because we couldn't make the just strong we settled for making the strong just — which sounds like the debunking reading, but I suspect it isn't quite, since he also wants us to obey established law and doesn't treat that as sheer con artistry. There's also a related fragment, or I think there is, about how complaints that force is unjust tend to come from people who don't have any — if I'm misremembering that, tell me and move on.

What I want from you: lay out the distinct precise theses "might makes right" could be expressing, as a menu, running from the ones that are nearly tautological, through the ones that are substantive and defensible, down to the ones that are false or outright incoherent. For each one, say what kind of claim it is (descriptive, definitional, metaethical, normative, predictive), what it would take to falsify it, and whether it's the sort of thing a person could actually endorse in good faith rather than just sneer. Then, separately, tell me which of those readings Pascal himself is committed to, and where exactly he parts company with Thrasymachus and Callicles on one side and Hobbes on the other, since that collapse is what my student performed. Finally, say which single reading my student most likely had in mind when he used the slogan to shut the discussion down, and whether any reading on the menu would actually license the move he made — using it to end a question about whether a particular law was just. If none of them does, say so plainly. Skip the etymology and the history of the phrase. Keep it under 900 words, and organise it as a numbered menu with a one-line verdict at the head of each item, so I can photocopy it for the seminar.
