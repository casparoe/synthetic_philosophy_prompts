Bit of background: I spent thirty-odd years as a physiologist and retired a few years back, and since then I've been reading a lot of philosophy — ethics mostly, some of it way over my head. Anyway I've ended up on the ethics committee at the rehab hospital where I used to do some consulting, and there's a case coming up that I have to write the committee's opinion on, and I'm stuck.

Here's the deal. The hospital gets a fixed pot every year for a new intensive neuro-rehab program — think a set number of therapy hours, way fewer than the patients who'd recieve them. Our charter says we allocate so as to produce the most total patient benefit, and for years we've scored applicants with a well-being-years metric (a QALY sort of thing but for subjective well-being, whatever that means). Standard, boring.

Then this patient turns up — call him Mr. A. He has a rare neurological condition, and on our own metric his projected gain from the program is enormous: not a bit more than the others, but more than all of them combined, by maybe an order of magnitude. His neurologist swears it's real, it's the condition, he simply has an unusually large capacity to benefit. So taken literally, the charter says give him the whole pot. Everyone else gets nothing.

Half the committee says we'd be monsters to do that — starving a dozen people to fatten one. The other half says we wrote the rule, we dont get to ignore it because the answer is ugly; if the metric is broken, fix the metric, don't cheat on one case. And then there's the wrinkle that our metric was calibrated on ordinary patients and Mr. A's score is a wild extrapolation far outside that range — so some of us (me included, I've spent my life measuring things) suspect the number might just be meaningless. A couple of people want to strike his condition from the metric altogether.

I keep thinking about that old thought experiment, the utility monster — Nozick I think, in the libertarian book — where a being enjoys eating people so much that the utilitarian has to feed everyone to it. I always thought it was a cheap gotcha. Now I'm not so sure.

What I need from you is a short written opinion I can hand round before the meeting. It should recommend one concrete course of action (give him the whole pot, split it evenly, cap his share, or send the metric back for revision — pick one and argue for it) with a bottom line we can actually vote on. It has to take the losing side seriously, since half the room will feel steamrolled either way. And please tell me straight whether the monster is really the same problem as our case or whether I'm conflating two different things. No citations or reading lists, please — I can't check anything and neither can the rest of them. Plain language. And say something about whether capacity to benefit is even the kind of thing to measure this way, or whether that's a mistake from the start.
