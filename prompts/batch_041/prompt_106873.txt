Write a poem of twenty to twenty-eight lines in ring composition, on the thermodynamic arrow of time. The middle stanza should be the pivot: a maximally ordered, minimally describable state, with the opening and closing stanzas mirroring each other around it, so that disorder accumulates toward both ends and the verse can be read from either direction without absurdity. The physics the poem should embody is this: the laws of motion do not distinguish past from future, and from any low-entropy macrostate the statistical drift is toward higher entropy in both temporal directions, so nothing in the dynamics by itself picks out the direction we live in; what does the work is the stipulation that the universe began in an extraordinarily improbable low-entropy corner. Keep the body of the poem symmetric so that only the final lines break the symmetry, and let that break come from the human side — memory, records, and traces all point one way, and that is the whole of our reason for calling one direction the past. A philosopher of physics should find it accurate, and a reader who has never thought about entropy should still find it a good poem.
