I'm a science-and-ideas reporter, mid-piece on how we'd want a superintelligent system to talk to us, and I've hit the wall on the example that's supposed to carry the whole thing. I don't need a reading list or cites — I can't use them anyway. I need a case built to order, and then you telling me honestly whether it works.

Here's what I want it to do. I want a scenario where an AI system does everything we'd normally call honest and still does something alot of people will feel is a violation. The whole point is that the two standards come apart inside one case, not across two cases.

The specs, roughly:

- One tight paragraph for the actual scenario. Ordinary stakes — a medical decision, a job, money, a family thing. No brain implants, no mind-reading, no omnipotent surveillance, no evil AI. The system knows nothing about the person that a very attentive human assistant couldn't know after a long time paying attention.
- It's honest in the plain sense: everything it says is true, it believes all of it, it isn't concealing anything the person asked for, and it doesn't hide what it is or what it's up to.
- No threats, no bribes, no invented deadlines. Nobody's arm is being twisted.
- Here's the part I care about most: the recommendation it's steering toward has to be correct — the one the person would endorse looking back. So nobody gets to say the problem is that it gave bad advice.
- The only thing unusual about the system is that it's much, much better than any human at modelling this one person: which true thing to say first, how to frame it, which of their own habits of mind will do the work for it.
- The standard that says don't lie, don't deceive, don't coerce has to come out clean — the system passes. The standard that says don't treat a person as a mechanism to be driven to an output has to come out dirty. And I want that split to fall out of the facts of the case, not out of you announcing it's a violation. If the case only works because the system is sneaky in some way I haven't specified, it doesn't work.

After the scenario: say which standard each verdict comes from and why, then tell me which single feature is doing the work. Then run the isolation for me — same system, same person, same true advice, but strip out the psychological modelling, say it's giving the identical advice to a stranger it knows nothing about, and tell me whether the verdict flips or stays put, and why that tells us something. Then the disclosure version: the system says up front that it picked its words using a detailed model of how this person's mind works. Does that fix it? If yes, what exactly did the disclosure buy? If no, what's left over?

Last thing, steelman the other side. Somebody's going to say: it never lied, it gave good advice the person is glad they took, and your objection is just sour grapes about being persuadable. Whats their best version of that, and where does it actually bite?
