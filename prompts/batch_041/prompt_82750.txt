Ok so something that's been bugging me. In the same hospital, on the same afternoon, you can get two completely different kinds of death, and everybody acts like theyre the same thing. Kind A: the patient has no brainstem reflexes, no drive to breathe on their own, they fail the apnea test, and the doctor says they're dead -- while their heart is still beating and a ventilator is still moving air in and out of them, and the body is warm and pink and digesting. Kind B: the heart stops, everyone stands around for two or three minutes to see if it starts up again on its own, and if it doesn't, that person is dead too, even though a few minutes earlier their brain was fine and in principle somebody could have restarted it. And then in both cases the body can hang around for hours or even a day or two after death, still ventilated, still being perfused, still making urine, until the organs are taken out. I've also heard that in some cases teams will keep running anesthetic into a donor they've already declared dead, just in case there's anybody in there -- if that's really happening, it seems to say something about how much the people doing it believe their own declaration. And lately some protocols apparently re-start circulation to the organs after the person is declared dead, which makes my head hurt.

So: what theory of life and death does this whole apparatus presuppose? Try to state it as cleanly as you can, then tell me which rival views it quietly rules out. Is it committed to death being the irreversible loss of the functioning of the organism as a whole -- and if so, does 'irreversible' mean the thing can't be brought back, or just that we've decided not to bring it back? Because the kind B person looks like a case where the second reading is doing all the work. Does the practice need one concept of death, or is it really running two incompatible ones side by side and hoping nobody notices? Could you keep the practice exactly as it is and swap in a totally different theory underneath -- say, that what dies is the person rather than the organism, or that death is a process with no sharp line at all, or that 'dead' is a legal status we assign when further treatment is futile or when organs are needed? I want to know where the practice is genuinely committed and where it's just underdetermined, so that several theories would license the same behavior.

And then the small version of the same puzzle, because I think it's the same puzzle: the same biology that tells me viruses arent alive sells me a can of spray that says it kills them, and I put viruses in the freezer and say they're dead, and the lab autoclaves them to make sure they're dead. Does the disinfecting/autoclaving practice presuppose the same life-and-death line as the hospital does, or a different one? Is a virus in a freezer dead, dormant, or neither?

Finally, what would actually have to change in the hospital if we took any one of those rival theories seriously -- the personhood view, the process view, or a strict irreversibility view that admitted the kind B patient's brain is still salvageable at the moment we call them dead? Concretely, what stops being legal or stops making sense, and what would the transplant people have to do instead?
