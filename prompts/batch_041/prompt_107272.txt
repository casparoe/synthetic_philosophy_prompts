I'm in a reading group on dispositions, and we keep circling the same cluster of problems — finks, masks, and the suspicion that the simple conditional analysis of dispositions is hopeless. What I want, though, is not another parade of one-off counterexamples but a single designed case in which the distinctions earn their keep, so I'd like you to construct a thought experiment to specification.

Here is what the case must do. It should feature one ordinary physical object — nothing exotic, no science fiction, and no stipulated "special" conditions that simply announce their own philosophical role — observed on a single occasion, and in that one occasion the simple conditional analysis of dispositions (roughly: an object is disposed to M when S because, were it S, it would M) must be shown to fail twice over and in opposite directions. First, there must be a disposition that is genuinely present, whose stimulus condition is genuinely satisfied, and whose manifestation nevertheless does not occur, and the reason must be a masker — an interfering condition that blocks the manifestation while leaving the disposition itself intact — rather than a fink that would have destroyed or altered the disposition had the stimulus arrived. Second, in the same case, some manifestation must occur even though the corresponding disposition is absent — a mimic — so that the analysis is wrong in the other direction as well. The masker and the mimic should issue from the same underlying mechanism, or from two mechanisms a reader can see to be of the same kind, so that the case reads as one situation rather than two glued together.

Once the case is on the table, do the following. For each half, state the disposition and the manifestation, say what the simple conditional analysis predicts, and say why that prediction is wrong. Then identify exactly which feature of the case is doing the work — the counterfactual about whether the disposition would survive the stimulus, the difference between blocking a manifestation and removing a power, or something else — and say what becomes of the case if that feature is stripped out. Finally, put on the hat of a defender of the conditional analysis: give the most plausible repair (a ceteris paribus clause, an "if the disposition were retained" rider, a restriction to intrinsic or categorical bases) and say whether it rescues the analysis in your case or merely relocates the problem. Tell me how confident you are in the verdict you reach, and single out the one step in your reasoning you are least sure of.
