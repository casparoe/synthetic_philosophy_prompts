I am a retired physical chemist — some thirty years spent on statistical mechanics and reaction kinetics — who has in retirement taken up philosophy of science in earnest. I have constructed the following argument in order to make precise the sense in which the higher-level quantities I once worked with (temperature, entropy, reaction rate) were supposed to do genuine explanatory work, and I find that I cannot accept where it lands. The steps are:

(1) Every macroscopic fact about a physical system is fixed by the microphysical facts: given the complete micro-story of the constituents and their evolution, no different macroscopic state was possible.
(2) Whatever is fixed by other facts is nothing over and above those facts.
(3) Whatever is nothing over and above other facts performs no causal work of its own.
(4) Therefore no macroscopic fact — temperature, entropy, the presence of a gene, a reaction rate — ever causes anything that the microphysical facts do not already cause.
(5) Therefore the causal claims of the special sciences, strictly speaking, are false.

The conclusion repels me, and not merely for sentimental reasons: a renormalization-group analysis shows that systems with quite different microscopic constituents share the same critical exponents, which suggests the macroscopic description has an autonomy I cannot see how to earn back from (1)–(4); and interventions on macroscopic variables — a mean kinetic energy, a gene — reliably move macroscopic outcomes in a way that looks for all the world like causation. What I would most like to keep is the reality of higher-level causation; what I am prepared to lose, if I must, is nearly anything else.

Adopt the role of a philosopher of science who works on reduction, emergence, and the metaphysics of causation and who is not frightened by the technical physics. Think carefully, and work the argument through step by step for yourself before you commit to anything — but give me only the following, with no introduction and no conclusion: a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, in which item 1 is your verdict (the single step you would reject, or, if you judge the argument sound, the premise you take to be least costly to abandon); item 2 explains why that step looked innocent; item 3 states what rejecting it costs me elsewhere, that is, which of my remaining commitments I would have to surrender; item 4 says which of the standard positions here (reductive physicalism, non-reductive physicalism, and the several varieties of emergence) has to pay the least, and why; and item 5 is the bottom line — what someone who wants to keep higher-level causation should actually believe.
