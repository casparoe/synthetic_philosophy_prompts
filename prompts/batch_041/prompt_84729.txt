Ok so I'm taking this seminar on philosophy of law and we just spent three weeks on sex work, and I came out more confused than I went in, mostly because I think all three regimes we looked at are quietly doing philosophy and refusing to admit it. Can you help me dig out what each one is actually committed to? Roughly: (1) the Nordic model, where selling is legal but buying is a crime, plus the state funds exit programs and in some cities first-time buyers get diverted into "john school" sessions where they hear from women who've left; (2) the New Zealand approach, where it's just decriminalized and treated as ordinary work, so labor law applies, she can sue a client or a boss, occupational safety rules kick in, and there's no register of who does it; (3) the German/Dutch style, where it's legal but licensed, brothels need permits, workers register with the state and I think in some places have to show up for health checks, and inspectors can walk into the premises.

What I really want is the presuppositional anatomy of (1), since it's the one with the weird asymmetry. Criminalizing the buyer while decriminalizing the seller seems to commit you to something specific about consent: either the money somehow voids her consent (so she's a victim and he isn't a counterparty, he's a perpetrator), or her consent is perfectly fine and the wrong is somewhere else entirely, in the commodification of sex, or in the fact that his demand props up an industry that would shrink if he stopped. Those are pretty different theories and I suspect the practice doesn't actually need to choose between them, which is interesting by itself. Also note we don't criminalize the person who buys a shirt made by someone in a sweatshop, or the employer who hires someone desperate, so whatever (1) presupposes has to be special to sex, or to something sex has in common with surrogacy but not with garment work. Say what that special thing is, if anything. And the john school detail seems to presuppose something else again, namely that he's a corrigible rational agent who can be educated into desisting, while the exit programs presuppose she needs to be rescued. Are those consistent, or is the practice running two incompatible pictures of agency at once?

Then, more generally: distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it's underdetermined, i.e. how many different theories would license exactly this policy. And flip it at the end: if we swapped out the implicit view for the sex-work-is-work one that (2) seems to presuppose, what concretely has to change, does john school become an HR training, do exit programs become retraining funded by the industry, does the health check move from her to him, and does the whole demand-reduction rationale survive at all?
