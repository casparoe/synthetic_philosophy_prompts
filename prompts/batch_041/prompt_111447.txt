I am giving a single guest session in a colleague's undergraduate typography course, and I would like you to plan the session rather than deliver it. The students can recognize a dozen typefaces on sight and will argue about kerning, but they have never been asked what makes an 'a' an 'a'; my aim is to leave them with the question properly posed and the beginnings of an answer, not with a doctrine.

The frame I want to use is the Letter Spirit project (Douglas Hofstadter and Gary McGraw, in Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies, 1995): the idea of a program that, given a few letters drawn in an unfamiliar style, must design the remaining letters of the alphabet so that the whole set hangs together — which forces the designer, human or machine, to decide what belongs to the identity of a letter and what belongs to its style. Do not assume the students have heard of it; the plan should let them arrive at the project's question on their own before I name it.

Two pieces of raw material I want woven in, because they are the sort of thing the students will not have met. First, the lowercase 'a' has two widely used canonical constructions: the double-storey 'a' of most printed text, with its bowl and upper hook, and the single-storey 'a' of handwriting and of many sans-serif typefaces, which is simply a circle and a stem — so a single letter, within one alphabet, comes in two forms neither of which much resembles the other, yet both are read as 'a' without hesitation. Second, a letterform's identity is in part a matter of the contrasts its alphabet affords — an 'a' is what it is partly because it is not the 'o', the 'e', the 'd', or the 'g' beside it — which makes what makes an 'a' an 'a' and what makes a set of letters a style a single question rather than two. Keep in mind also that 'the letter a' can mean a glyph, a grapheme, or a code point in a character encoding; if the students collapse these, I want the plan to pull them apart at the right moment.

Now plan the session. Specifically:

1. The order in which the ideas must come, and why each depends on the one before it: what has to be in place before the double-storey/single-storey fact can do any work, and what has to be in place before style can be introduced without sounding like mere decoration.
2. The one example that should carry the whole session. I am inclined to let the double-storey/single-storey 'a' carry it, but tell me whether it can bear that weight or whether something else should instead — a mirrored or rotated letter, an 'a' and an 'o' a hair's breadth apart, a letter in a style that seems to fight its own identity — and what the carrying example must have, and must lack, to do the job.
3. The misconceptions the students will arrive with or form — I predict they will treat a letter as a fixed shape matched against a template, and treat style as a skin laid over an unchanging skeleton, and I suspect some will swing to the view that any mark is a letter if a community agrees to call it one — and precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off, and by what.
4. The single question or task I can set at the end that would show me whether the question has landed, rather than whether they can repeat my phrasing.
5. What I should deliberately leave out at this level, and how I should say so out loud when I do.

Give me the plan for the explanation, not the explanation itself, and no readings.
