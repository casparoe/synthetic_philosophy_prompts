OK so background: I'm a novelist, three historical novels, and I keep bangin into a craft problem that I'm now pretty sure is a philosophy-of-history problem wearing a disguise. Wondering if you can help me think it through, because I want to use it in the next book and I don't want to embarass myself.

In a novel, characters come in two kinds. Load-bearing ones and decoration. Kill a load-bearing character and the whole plot collapses, you rewrite from page one. Kill a decoration and the plot just... reroutes. It finds another path to roughly the same ending, maybe a chapter later. I've started applying that test to real history, and the case I can't get out of my head is Nazi Germany. So: was Hitler load-bearing? Or would the story of the 1930s have rerouted around his absence?

Here's what I actually want, and it's not a both-sides book report. Walk the real sequence and tell me, beat by beat, which beats are Hitler-beats and which are structure-beats. The putsch in '23 and the party's near-death after it. The long slog through the late twenties when the party was still a fringe joke. The breakthrough once the depression hit and the coalition politics of the chancellorship started eating itself. The backroom deal that actually put him in the chancellery in January '33, with the old conservative elites thinking they could use him. The fire, the emergency decree, the enabling act. For each of those: would it have happened without him, would it have happened with someone else, and who's the someone else -- Strasser, Göring, a military junta, a restored Papen, whoever.

Then the part I really care about, which is where I think the whole debate gets lazy. The structuralist move is to say there was a vacant slot -- some authoritarian strongman was going to fill it -- so the individual doesn't matter. Fine, but does that argument prove too much? If every individual is replaceable, then the great-man view is unfalsifiable and so is the structural one, and we've learned nothing. Push on that. And push on the distinction between explaining some authoritarian regime plus some war, versus explaining that specific genocide. My hunch is the slot explains the first and the second needs the man, but I might be fooling myself because that's the version that makes a better novel.

Last thing: as a fiction writer I'm allowed to make a character load-bearing when a historian isn't. Tell me where that move is legitimate historical method and where it's just narrative convenience, and what the great-man vs structural debate looks like once you admit that both sides are partly telling a story. Where is each side silent, and which one actually survives this case better?
