My friend and I argued late into the night about whether anything can be better or worse when the future is infinite, and I came away holding a view I would like you to try to demolish. The view is this: when a population is infinite or the future unbounded, there is no fact of the matter about whether one outcome is better than another, because the relevant comparisons simply fail; the correct practical response is therefore to stop maximizing across such worlds and to reason lexically and locally instead — never to trade a finite good for a merely infinite one, and never to treat an unbounded sum as a reason for anything.

What would count against that? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to mark which of them I would have to concede myself, as opposed to which only a hostile critic would press. Specifically: is there a defensible way of comparing some infinite worlds that I am quietly ignoring — one that is better for infinitely many people and worse for none, or one that improves at every finite stage while never settling? Does giving up comparisons make the view self-undermining in practice, since I still have to choose and to act under uncertainty? And does the position collapse into the much stronger claim that no axiology can ever be correct, which is not what I mean to assert? My friend's parting shot was that a view on which nothing is better or worse for an infinite future cannot tell us to care about that future at all; tell me whether that is fatal, or whether I can answer it without reinstating the very comparisons I gave up. Include any defeater I have not anticipated, and finish by naming the single one whose truth would do the view the most damage.
