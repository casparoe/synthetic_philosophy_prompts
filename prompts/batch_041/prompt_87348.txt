Here's the paper, more or less as it stands. Title: Anekāntavāda without many-valued truth. The recieved picture is that the Jains held reality to be many-sided (anekānta) and concluded that every judgement is true only in a qualified way, which they then expressed through the seven conditional predications of syādvāda: of a given subject you may say, qualifiedly, that it is, that it is not, that it both is and is not, that it is inexpressible, and then the rest of the permutations of those. The standard gloss is that this makes Jainism a tolerant pluralism about truth — everyone is a bit right, so nobody should be too sure — and people either celebrate it as a proto-liberalism or roll their eyes at it as a refusal to take sides. I want to argue that both reactions miss what the doctrine is actually doing. My claim is that syādvāda isn't a theory of truth at all but a theory of predication under an indexed standpoint: fix the subject, fix the predicate, and the respect in which the predicate applies is doing all the work. On this reading the seven modes are not seven truth-values; they're the cells of a table you get by crossing one predicate with the standpoints (nayas) from which it can be asserted, and is and is not never conflict because they're never indexed to the same standpoint. So it comes out as a context-sensitivity thesis, closer to an indexical semantics than to dialetheism. Then the second half draws what I think is the uncomfortable consequence: if it's just indexing, it can't underwrite the ecumenical conclusions its fans want, because once the standpoint is fixed there's exactly one right answer, and disagreement between traditions turns into a disagreement about which standpoint to occupy — which is a substantive disagreement you can lose. I call this the deflationary reading and I claim it's a feature rather than a bug. (I've cut the blind-men-and-the-elephant bit, it felt like filler.)
