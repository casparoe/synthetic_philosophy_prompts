I have been reading rationalist blogs and physics-adjacent forums for the better part of a decade, and there is one analogy that surfaces whenever the direction of time comes up: entropy is a shuffled deck. The cosmos began in a neatly ordered state the way a new pack comes out of the box; the second law says only that shuffles vastly outnumber orders; nothing forbids the cards from falling back into sequence, it is merely fantastically improbable; and so the arrow of time is a fact about our ignorance and our bookkeeping rather than a feature of the world. The people who deploy this are usually out to license a specific conclusion — that there is nothing deep about temporal asymmetry, that the past hypothesis does all the explanatory work, and that irreversibility is just probability.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than another exposition of the second law. Assume I am comfortable with the standard machinery — ensembles, macrostates, the Gibbs/Boltzmann distinction, coarse-graining — and give me the philosophical reckoning instead.

First, lay out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the deck, the shuffle, the "ordered" starting arrangement, the counting of arrangements, the observer, and the initial condition. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and be specific about which breaks are load-bearing for the conclusion above and which are merely decorative.

I am especially interested in four pressure points. Whether the analogy can absorb the reversibility and recurrence objections without collapsing into a restatement of them. Whether "order" in the card sense is the same kind of thing as low entropy — a shuffled deck is still a perfectly determinate arrangement, and an arrangement we can describe in a single sentence, the ace on top say, is not thereby low-entropy, so the analogy seems to run together "specified" and "statistically rare." Whether the fact that we re-sort decks routinely makes reversal look far more accessible than reversing molecular momenta, and what echo-type experiments do to that impression. And whether the shuffle itself, being a stochastic process with an intrinsic direction, quietly presupposes the very arrow it is meant to explain.

Finally, adjudicate: separate the defensible statistical claim from the over-claim that the arrow is subjective or that nothing is genuinely irreversible, and say whether the conclusion the analogy is used to support survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If it does not, propose a better analogy — records, mixing, boundary conditions, or something of your own — and say what it captures that the deck cannot.
