I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, mostly decision theory and the epistemology of evidence, and I've got the 90-minute slot for our probability and uncertainty reading group next week -- about 14 people, mixed philosophy and econ, and I'm abit worried the economists will be the ones who've seen everything before. Here's what I want to do with it.

Keynes thought there were two seperate things going on when some body of evidence makes a hypothesis probable: how probable it makes it, and how much evidence it's actually resting on. He called the second one the weight of the argument (it's in the Treatise on Probability, ch. 6, if anyone asks), and the bit I care about is that they come apart -- the same probability can ride on a mountain of evidence or on almost nothing. My canonical case is two urns: one you've sampled a hundred times and it keeps coming out 50/50, one where you know how many balls are in it and nothing at all about the mix. Probability of black is a half in both, everyone still reaches for the first urn, and I want them to feel that pull with their hands rather than nod at it.

The catch is I don't want this to turn into another Ellsberg / ambiguity-aversion demo. Half the room will have seen that a dozen times and will immediately say oh that's just second-order uncertainty, or that's just variance aversion, and honestly that's the move I want the activity to make *harder* rather than easier -- so the design has to make it costly for them to redescribe what they're doing that way. So: something where they act, in rounds, with stakes, and end up committed to a preference at equal probability that they then have to defend out loud. No real money, points or chocolate is fine.

Give me the rules and materials (printable in five minutes), a round-by-round timeline, the debrief that maps what actually happened onto probability vs weight, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the activity can't do -- my guess is it can't show that caring about weight is *rational*, that's an argument I'll have to give at the end myself. And if there's a version where the weight goes up while the probability goes down (Keynes's odd claim that fresh evidence always raises the weight but can lower the probability), that'd be a nice button to end on.
