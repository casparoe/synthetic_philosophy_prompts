I'm writing a pantoum for a talk on the hedonistic imperative -- the view that pain and malaise are just machinery we inherited because it helped our genes in the ancestral environment, and that biotechnology could abolish suffering across all sentient life, replacing the hedonic treadmill with a motivational system that runs on steadily climbing gradients of bliss instead of resetting to the same baseline after every high. The form has to do the work here: each stanza's second and fourth lines come back as the next stanza's first and third, so the poem keeps handing you back the lines you thought you'd put down, which is what the treadmill does to anyone who's ever gotten what they wanted. Four or five quatrains. Then break it -- a real pantoum folds shut at the end, last line doubling as the first, and I want you to refuse that closing, because the refusal is the whole argument. Accurate enough that someone who actually holds this view wouldn't wince, and please don't let it turn into a hymn; the obvious objection has to stay audible in it, that a life with nothing left to escape from might be a life with nothing in it, and I dont want that resolved.
