I teach a seminar on religion and the liberal state and I keep having the same fight with a colleague from the law faculty -- she's German, grew up paying Kirchensteuer, and she insists the German settlement is the most neutral church-state arrangement anyone has ever built, which drives me up the wall because I teach the American separation story as the baseline. I'd like to actually work it through instead of trading anecdotes. So here's the case as I understand it (tell me if I've got any of this wrong): Germany has no established church, but it has a church tax. Religious communities recognized as public-law corporations can ask the tax office to levy a surcharge on their members' income tax -- 8 or 9 percent of the income tax bill depending on the state -- and the state keeps a collection fee. It supplies something like 70 percent of church revenue, so it is not small change. Membership is fixed by baptism and stored in a state database that employers see, which means the state is effectively the registrar of who belongs to which church; if you want out you have to go to a government office and make a declaration, and some churches have said that people who do that can't get a church funeral or a religious marriage. Non-members pay nothing, and nothing comparable exists for the nonreligious. (For what it's worth, France's 1905 seperation law still doesn't apply in Alsace-Moselle, where the state pays clergy salaries, so this isn't only a German oddity, and there's been a record wave of formal departures in Germany over the last few years.)

What I want is to run this through the neutrality literature and then run the literature back through the case. So distinguish at least two readings of what liberal neutrality actually demands here -- a hands-off reading, on which the objectionable thing is the state using religious affiliation as a legal category at all (collecting the money, keeping the register, defining who is a member), and an even-handedness reading, on which the state may support religion as such so long as it supports all recognized religions equally and doesn't pick winners. Tell me what each reading says about the German case and about the American one -- tax exemption for churches, legislative chaplains, the motto on the coins, all the things a strict separationist hates -- because I suspect the US fails the even-handedness test at least as badly, and maybe fails the hands-off test too once you count tax exemption as a subsidy. Then flip it around: does the German case show that neutrality of effect is an impossible standard and we should just settle for neutrality of intent, or does it show something else -- that the real problem was never the money but the state's role in fixing who counts as a member, so that a church treating a registry declaration as a genuine defection is tacitly conceding the state's authority over its own boundaries? And finish by telling me where the theory simply goes silent. I don't think the neutrality framework has anything useful to say about the infant-baptism default -- about people who were enrolled in a church before they could consent and now owe it money -- and I'd like to know whether that's a gap in the theory or a sign I'm asking the wrong question.
