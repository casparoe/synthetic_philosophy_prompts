I just finished one of those pop-psychology books about character (can't remember the author, some journalist) and the chapter on virtue ethics and social psychology has been bugging me. Here's the gist of what it claims, and I want you to audit it.

The book says virtue ethics is built on the idea that people have stable character traits — honesty, courage, compassion — that reliably show up as honest, courageous, compassionate behaviour across different situations. Then social psychology supposedly killed this. In the famous Good Samaritan study, seminary students on their way to give a talk walked past a man slumped in a doorway; whether they stopped to help was predicted almost entirely by whether they'd been told they were running late, and the students about to lecture on the Good Samaritan parable were no better than the others. In the Milgram experiments, ordinary people kept shocking a screaming stranger to what they believed was near-death, just because a man in a lab coat told them the experiment required them to keep going. And Hartshorne and May tested thousands of schoolkids for honesty and cheating and found that a kid who cheated on one test was basically no more likely to cheat on the next — the correlation between honest behaviour in different situations was near zero. The book's conclusion is that there are no character traits, only situations, so virtue ethics is empirically refuted. It adds that the only move left to virtue ethicists is to say virtue is an ideal to aspire to rather than a description of how anyone actually is, which gives up the game.

Does the virtue ethics being attacked look like any version an actual virtue ethicist holds? Are those experiments as clean as the book makes them sound, and do they even measure the things virtue ethics is about?
