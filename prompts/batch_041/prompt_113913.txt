I chair the ethics committee at a hospital that has just approved a first-in-human trial of a gradual-replacement neural prosthesis. Over roughly a year of scheduled sessions, implanted modules take over the function of small regions of a patient's cortex, and if the process runs to completion the patient's mental life would in principle continue on entirely synthetic hardware. Our consent forms, our advance-directive policy, and our rules about who may authorize withdrawal all presuppose a single continuous person whose wishes track across the whole trial, and the protocol as written makes that presupposition difficult even to state precisely; two committee members have already begun arguing about it. Please write a decision procedure the committee can actually run on individual cases: the questions to ask, in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a decision, and where it must hand the matter to the committee as a policy choice rather than a finding about persons. Ground the steps in whatever the philosophical work on personal identity and on what matters in survival genuinely supports, keep them usable by the clinicians and lawyers who will be voting, and mark plainly where that work underdetermines the answer. Then run the procedure on three cases: a patient two thirds of the way through who insists that the person who signed the consent form was someone else and asks to stop, knowing that stopping now is probably fatal; a patient whose completed successor requests life support that her own advance directive, written beforehand, refused; and a proposed amendment that would run the process on two separated hemispheres in parallel, yielding two candidates who each remember signing the form, under trial rules that let each participant count once. Tell me where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and what it cannot settle.
