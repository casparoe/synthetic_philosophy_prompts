Two colleagues of mine — a decision theorist I will call the Exchange theorist and a moral philosopher I will call the Integrity theorist — have spent a year arguing about moral compromise and have agreed to close out the exchange by writing a joint statement, and I would like you to draft that statement for them.

The Exchange theorist's view is that when two parties with different value systems settle on a joint course of action, the settlement can be a genuine improvement for both by each party's own lights, and that the analogy with gains from trade is not decorative. A choice among options requires only that each party be able to rank them, not that the parties share a measure of value; so two parties can move from a default outcome to one that each ranks higher, and a series of such moves can carry them to a point from which no further move is mutually preferred. On this view, the complaint that one betrays one's values by conceding on a lower-ranked item at a favourable rate is a confusion: one has simply acted on one's own ranking.

The Integrity theorist's view is that this borrows a result about preferences and applies it where it does not hold. Values do not merely rank; they make claims, and a claim is not discharged by being outweighed by another claim within one's own ranking. Conceding on a value is not like selling a commodity at a good price: it leaves a residue for which one remains answerable, and the sum of the two parties' gains does not cancel it. At best a compromise is a lesser evil that both may be permitted to choose, never a gain; and calling it a gain obscures what has been given up.

Produce the document they should both be able to sign. It should contain, in order: (1) a numbered list of claims both can accept, covering how the problem is to be set up, what a settlement is, what counts as a gain by a party's own lights, and which of the usual objections are live; (2) the disagreements that survive, each stated in wording its own side would accept as fair to it, and made as precise as possible; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party; and (4) for each crux, the case, argument, or thought experiment that both parties agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about the outcome recorded in advance. Close with a note saying whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, which part, and why.

Use one running example throughout: two campaigners in a coalition who must jointly endorse one of several policy packages before a vote; each ranks the packages by her own values; if they cannot agree the coalition endorses nothing and the vote is lost, an outcome both rank below every package. Include in the example one package that each campaigner would have to regard as violating something she treats as a constraint rather than as a cost.

I expect that at least two of the cruxes are these, and I want the statement to settle whether I am right: first, that the two parties may have been talking past each other because the trade analogy is naturally interpersonal (two parties exchanging) while the corruption objection is naturally intra-personal (one agent weighing her own values), so that part of the dispute may not survive the separation of the two cases; and second, that the trade analogy may need only rankings while the integrity objection insists on something more, and it is unclear whether that something more is a genuine requirement or an artefact of the analogy's vocabulary. The parties should also decide whether any values are the kind that cannot be ranked against others at all, since if some are, the frontier picture fails precisely where it is most needed.

Do not include citations, a bibliography, or attributions to named authors: both parties want the statement to rest on its own arguments and I will add references myself. Where a position someone else has defended is relevant, describe it rather than naming it.

I want a committed draft, not a menu. Where the record leaves a choice of formulation open, make the choice and defend it, and put your own verdict — with reasons — in a short drafter's note appended to the statement rather than hedging inside the joint text. In that note, take a definite position on the question the collaboration was convened to settle; do not merely report that both sides have a case.
