I am an amateur type designer, and I have run into a question I cannot answer cleanly: when is a mark an 'a'? I can report my verdicts on a spread of cases, but I do not know what principle, if any, they jointly express, and I suspect that not all of them can be right at once.

1. The familiar double-story 'a' of an old-style serif face: an 'a', unambiguously.
2. A geometric single-story 'a', a circle with a straight stem on its right: an 'a', equally and without qualification.
3. The same single-story 'a' as written by a six-year-old — wobbly, asymmetric, the stem not quite meeting the bowl: still an 'a'.
4. That 'a' condensed to half its width, or stretched to twice its height: still an 'a'.
5. The same 'a' set down carelessly, so that it leans ten degrees off the baseline: still an 'a'.
6. The single-story 'a' rotated a full 180 degrees: not an 'a'. It is a turned 'a', and I would not read it as the letter.
7. The single-story 'a' mirrored left to right: not an 'a'.
8. The Greek lowercase alpha: not an 'a', though I grant it is the ancestor and I often draw it identically by hand.
9. The Cyrillic 'а', which is to my eye geometrically indistinguishable from a Latin 'a': I want to say it is not an 'a' — it is the Russian letter — yet I cannot point to any difference in the mark itself, and this verdict makes me uneasy.
10. A Latin font whose 'a' position has been filled, by a production error, with a shape identical to that font's 'o': I would say the font contains no 'a' at that position; the mark is an 'o', whatever the character map claims.
11. A drop of ink that dries into a shape indistinguishable from a one-story 'a': I want to say it is not an 'a' — nothing was meant by it — even though I admit I would read it as one if I came across it on a page.
12. The same shape, drawn deliberately by someone intending the letter 'a': an 'a'.
13. A sign-painter who sets out to paint an 'a' but produces a mark that every reader takes for an 'o': I would say he failed to paint an 'a'; the mark is an 'o', not a botched 'a'.
14. A corporate logo in which the 'a' of the company's name has been abstracted until no one, seeing the mark alone, would recognize a letter at all: I am inclined to say it is still the 'a' of that name.
15. A substitution cipher I used as a child, in which I wrote 'o' wherever an 'a' belonged and 'a' wherever an 'o' belonged: I would say those 'o'-shaped marks are not 'a's; they are 'o's I misused.

My tentative principle was that a mark is an 'a' just in case a competent reader of the script would spontaneously read it as 'a'. It seems to dispose of the clear cases, the wobbly hand, the stretching, and the slight lean, but it comes apart on cases 9, 11, and 14, and cases 14 and 15 appear to contradict one another: both are marks that serve as 'a' without resembling one, yet I want to call one an 'a' and deny it of the other.

What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, that does the greatest justice to these verdicts; a precise statement of which verdict each candidate forces me to abandon; and an account of what the survivor predicts for cases I have not listed — for instance, a font in which the 'a' and the 'e' differ only by a single hairline, or a script in which 'a' and 'o' are the very same mark, told apart only by position. If it should turn out that no principle fits, tell me what that shows: whether letter identity is a matter of essence at all, or only of family resemblance and the uses to which we put marks.
