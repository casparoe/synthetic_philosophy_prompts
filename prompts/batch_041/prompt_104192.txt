I am designing a session for an advanced seminar on secession and self-determination, and I want a dialogue that avoids the usual set pieces. Please write a sustained exchange between two political philosophers, both of whom are formidable and neither of whom is a caricature. The first defends the remedial-right view: a group has a moral right to secede only if it has suffered serious and persistent injustice at the hands of the state, and secession is a remedy of last resort. The second defends a primary-right view: a territorially concentrated group with a distinct political identity may secede even absent injustice, provided the decision is made through a fair internal process and the resulting state would respect basic rights. Set the dialogue in an invented but realistic case: a democratic federation called the Union, and a prosperous, linguistically distinct region called Cordelia that was incorporated two centuries ago after a war, now wants to leave. Cordelia is not oppressed; indeed, it is richer than the rest of the Union and resents fiscal transfers to poorer regions. A substantial minority within Cordelia, however, identifies with the Union and fears becoming a permanent minority in a new state. Neither philosopher may cite actual authors, statutes, treaties, or historical cases; the argument must be self-contained.

The dialogue should move through at least these pressure points, with each speaker responding to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them: whether the absence of injustice is fatal to Cordelia's claim; whether the remedial view can avoid a secession cascade without simply entrenching the status quo; whether the primary-right view can solve the boundary problem, since the right to secede must belong to some determinate unit and any proposed boundary will cut through people's identities and interests; whether the internal minority in Cordelia has a veto, a claim to special protections, or only the right to exit; and whether the rump Union's interest in territorial integrity and in the welfare of its remaining citizens can ever outweigh a secession claim. Let the remedial theorist press the hostage objection and the fiscal-externality objection, and let the primary-right theorist press the complaint that the remedial view makes self-determination a prize for the already oppressed and offers nothing to peoples whose grievance is not injustice but distinctness. Both should be willing to refine their positions under pressure, and each should land at least one genuinely uncomfortable point. End not with a forced synthesis but with the disagreement sharpened, perhaps by one speaker identifying a remaining disagreement about what political legitimacy is for. Aim for roughly 1500 to 2000 words, and give the speakers names and a minimal setting so the exchange reads like a real conversation.
