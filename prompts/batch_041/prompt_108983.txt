Last night I had an argument with a friend that lasted until nearly two in the morning, and I have not been able to stop thinking about it since. She works in philanthropy and is a committed adherent of the cause-prioritization framework: when deciding where to give, one scores a cause on importance (how many people it affects, and how badly), tractability (how much progress a dollar can buy), and neglectedness (how few other resources are already devoted to it). I said the framework was a useful heuristic rather than a master key; she replied that this was precisely the kind of defeatism that leaves money on the table. Then she made an argument I cannot shake: if you apply the three criteria to the question of where to conduct cause-prioritization research, the answer is that such research is itself the most important, tractable, and neglected cause—and therefore the correct move is for a foundation to spend its entire budget on research into how to spend its budget. I told her that this was a reductio ad absurdum; she replied that it was a portfolio. I would like to commission a satirical piece that takes her position seriously enough to see where it actually leads.

Please write a deadpan internal memo, about 750–850 words, from the Director of Portfolio Allocation at a fictional grantmaker called the Aster Foundation to its board of trustees, recommending that the foundation allocate its entire 2026 grantmaking budget to a new program of cause-prioritization research—that is, research on how to prioritize causes—rather than to any object-level cause such as global health, animal welfare, or existential risk. The Director should be entirely sincere, slightly pompous, and fluent in the vocabulary of strategy consulting; the humor should come from the framework being applied with perfect consistency and no self-awareness. The memo should include a scoring table in which the meta-program beats every object-level cause on importance, tractability, and neglectedness; a section titled “Anticipated Objections” in which the Director addresses the worry that the argument is circular, the worry that the foundation would be grading its own homework, and the worry that the whole exercise is unfalsifiable, with each answer making the problem worse; and a closing recommendation that the board adopt the proposal unanimously and schedule a follow-up meeting to discuss whether the follow-up meeting itself should be funded.

To sharpen the satire, have the Director introduce a fourth criterion he has invented, which he calls “counterfactual elasticity”: the degree to which a dollar’s impact depends on the foundation’s own decision to give it. The Director should argue that cause-prioritization research scores infinitely high on this criterion, because without the foundation’s decision to fund it, the research would not happen at all—whereas a dollar to, say, bed nets might have been given by someone else. The memo should not have the Director notice that this criterion would justify funding literally anything the foundation is considering, that it collapses the distinction between a cause and an intervention, or that it creates an infinite regress of meta-prioritization. Let the reader notice those things. The target is the practice of cause prioritization, not any named living person; an invented foundation and an invented Director are fine. No citations, no reading list, no appeals to authority—just the memo.
