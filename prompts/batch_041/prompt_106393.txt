My friend and I had a five-hour argument about consent last night and I'm still annoyed about it. She's finishing a PhD in philosophy; I'm the consent-and-compliance analyst at the research ethics office of a mid-size teaching hospital, which means I'm the person who reads every consent form and sits in on the consent conversations for the studies we run, maybe fifteen or twenty protocols a month. Her claim was that most of what I approve isn't real consent — the cancer patients who think the trial is their last chance, the employees in the study their own company is funding, the undergrads doing it for the money, the biobank form where people sign away tissue for research nobody's thought of yet. She kept saying "they can't say no" as if that settled it, and I kept saying "but they did sign" which even I could hear was weak.

The thing that actually stuck with me: she said the difference between coercion and a plain offer is whether the person still has the option they had before, and I said fine, so what did they have before, and she said that's the whole problem, that's the contested part, and then we just yelled. Because she's right that "what they'd have without the study" isn't a fact lying around — it depends on what you take the baseline to be, the world as it is or the world as it should be. And now I have to go back to work and approve or not approve actual studies and I don't have a procedure.

So: give me one. A decision procedure I can actually run, in maybe twenty minutes per protocol, without a philosophy degree, for the question "is this consent valid" — and seperate, if it isn't the same question, "is this consent autonomous," because she kept insisting those come apart and I half belive her. I want the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it hands the thing to a person — our chair, or the participant's own clinician, or the patient themselves — because the call is about values and not facts. And mark the steps that are judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothing.

Three cases to run it on, and one of them should come out the way I won't like:

1. An early-phase trial for a cancer with basically no good options left, enrolling patients who've been told this is what's available. Nobody's lying to them, but the consent conversation is happening on the worst week of their life and everyone in the room knows the drug probably won't work.
2. A wellness study where the sponsor is the participants' own employer and participation is "voluntary" but the sign-up sheet goes around the team meeting.
3. A broad biobank consent — tissue and data now, for studies not yet designed, some of which the participant would object to if they knew.

And fold the deception case in somewhere: a study where the real purpose isn't disclosed because disclosing it would ruin the result.

Don't give me citations or a reading list, I can't use them at work and half the time I can't get the papers anyway — just tell me what the thinking is and make it usable. And please tell me honestly which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely fought over by people who actually know the area, because my friend said some of what she said like it was obvious and I have a feeling some of it isn't.
