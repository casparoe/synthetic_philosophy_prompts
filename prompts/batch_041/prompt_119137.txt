I have been arguing about this with two friends since our decision-theory reading group dissolved, and I have landed on the side I did not expect. I now think acausal trade is real — that two agents with no causal channel between them, separated by distance, by time, or merely by the fact that neither is aware the other exists, can each do better by working out what the other's decision procedure will output and acting so as to satisfy the other's preferences, with the mutual benefit sustained by the correlation between their algorithms rather than by anything passing between them. I do not mean the tame version in which the agents are near-identical and one reasons from its own source code to the other's behaviour; I mean the strong version, where the parties have different utility functions, unequal power, no shared history, and no evidence of each other's existence beyond a probability estimate that something of the relevant kind is out there, including the extreme cases where they lie outside each other's light cones or occupy disjoint branches of an Everettian multiverse. I take the apparatus to hang together — the Newcomb-flavoured predictor setups, the thought that one's own decision is evidence about what other instances of the same algorithm will do, the shift from causal to updateless or functional reasoning — and I take the practical upshot seriously enough to have started asking what I, an ordinary agent of middling intelligence, am supposed to do about it. So I would like you to play prosecutor, and to do it well enough that a sympathetic reader would wince.

What would count against this? Set aside the cheap objection that it sounds like magic, and the equally cheap reply that causality was never the point. I want the objections that should actually move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want to know which of them are the sort of thing a defender would concede on the spot and which they would wave off. I am already aware of a few candidate weak points and would like them handled rather than restated: that once the space of possible trading partners is large enough, it is unclear which bargains bind, so that the view seems to underdetermine what I should do rather than tell me; that the notion of logical correlation may be smuggling causal intuitions back in under a new name; that the whole thing may be precommitment reasoning in a costume and thus not a new phenomenon at all; that the power-asymmetry requirement quietly rules out the cases that made the idea exciting; and that if the framework is generous enough, it threatens to license something like a Pascalian mugging, where the expected-value ledger is dominated by fantastically improbable partners demanding something monstrous. Beyond the objections, tell me what the position predicts or forbids that its rivals do not, and what observation, formal result, or decision-theoretic development would genuinely have to occur for a committed acausal trader to abandon it rather than patch it.

Finally, the question I actually care about: has this view been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind or just the ordinary price of doing speculative metaphysics? The version I have described is deliberately strong, but I notice that the more one adds conditions — the partners must be similar enough, or share a decision theory, or be able to predict each other — the harder it becomes to say what is left to disagree about. I would like an honest verdict on whether the position risks anything at all, whether the risk is borne by acausal trade itself or offloaded onto the decision theory underneath it, and whether a reasonable person should treat that arrangement as a virtue, a vice, or simply the shape that any sufficiently abstract view must take. For what it is worth, the thing I would personally find most persuasive against it is a demonstration that the machinery is idle — that in every case where it seems to add a reason for action, ordinary causal reasoning plus a clear-eyed view of one's own precommitments already recommends the same thing — so weigh your ranking with that in mind, and tell me if it is the wrong thing to be persuaded by.
