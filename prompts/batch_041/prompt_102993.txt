I teach at a big public high school and I write somewhere around seventy college rec letters a year, which means I spend every October making character claims I'm not sure I'm untitled to make -- X is honest, X is a natural leader, X is kind -- on the basis of having watched a kid for maybe ninety minutes a day in one room, most of it while they were doing algebra. Last spring I read a pile of stuff on the situationist critique of virtue ethics for a book group and it wrecked me a little: the seminarians who hurried past a man slumped in a doorway because they'd been told they were late for their talk, the people who stopped to help a stranger pick up spilled papers just because they'd found a dime in the payphone a minute before. If stuff like that moves behavior around that much, what exactly am I asserting when I say a seventeen-year-old is honest? I've also seen the counterpoint that the critique doesn't kill character talk, it just forces you to shrink the unit of assessment -- from global traits to traits indexed to a kind of situation, and that personality predicts behavior much better in weak situations where nobody's telling you what to do than in strong ones where the script is obvious.

What I want is a procedure I can actually run in the twenty minutes I get per letter, something my department head could look at and sign off on and that an admissions reader would find fair. Please turn the situationist critique into a checklist: the questions to ask in order before I'm allowed to put a character claim on paper, what counts as enough evidence for each, when the honest move is to just describe the behavior and let the reader infer the trait, and where the procedure has to stop and hand it back to my judgment because it's a values question about how much risk of overclaiming is acceptable. Constraints: no psychological testing, I can't poll other teachers, I only ever see these kids in my own classroom plus whatever club I advise, and I can't just write vague mush -- the letters have to actually say something.

Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one to three sentences, no intro and no wrap-up. Items one through three are the procedure's stages, in the order I'd run them. Item four runs it on two cases: a kid who cheated once in a class that was obviously too hard for them and has been spotless since, and a kid who is lovely in my room but who I have only ever seen in my room. Item five is the case where the procedure tells me something I won't want to hear, plus an honest statement of what the procedure can't do.
