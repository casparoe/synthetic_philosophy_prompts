I'm a software engineer on the clinical policy team at a mid-sized health plan, and I have no formal background in philosophy — what I know about the metaphysics of disease I've picked up from arguing with our medical directors in hallway conversations. We're rebuilding our prior-authorization workflow, and the step that keeps blowing up is the one where a nurse reviewer has to decide whether the thing in front of them counts as a disease, or as a medical necessity, at all. Right now that call is made case by case and mostly by seniority, and the inconsistency shows up in our appeals data: the same presentation gets classified one way for one member and the opposite way for the next, and nobody can tell me which reviewer was right. I've been told to write the spec for that step, and I'd rather not just hard-code whatever our current medical director happens to believe. The debate I keep being pointed at is the naturalism-versus-normativism fight in philosophy of medicine — roughly, Boorse's line that a disease is a statistically abnormal reduction in functional efficiency and is a value-free matter of biology, against the normativist line that "disease" is at bottom a value judgment about what is unwanted or harmful, with Wakefield's hybrid (a genuine dysfunction of some natural function, plus harm) sitting between them. I need that made operational. I don't want citations, a reading list, or a summary of who said what — I need a procedure.

Concretely: give me a decision procedure that a reviewer with no philosophy training can run in about ten minutes, laid out in the order the questions would actually be asked, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates with "not a disease," where it terminates with "disease," and — this is the part I care about most — where it hands the decision back to a person because the question remaining is a values call rather than a factual one. Tell me which distinctions have to survive the compression and which I can safely drop; in particular, keep separate (a) statistically unusual in some reference population, (b) a failure of some biological or psychological mechanism to do what it was selected or designed to do, and (c) unwanted, harmful, or distressing — and tell me what to do when those three come apart. Say something about how to choose the reference class, since I suspect a large share of our inconsistency is that different reviewers are quietly comparing members against different baselines: age, sex, ancestry, the population the member actually lives in versus some idealized healthy norm. And be explicit about which steps are thresholds dressed up as facts. If "clinically significant distress" or "interferes with daily functioning" is doing the real work, say so, so I can log it as a value judgment and route it to a medical director for sign-off instead of letting the software pretend it decided.

Then run the procedure end to end on three cases, and please don't sandbag them. First: a member from a small founder population in which a particular enzyme variant is present in roughly forty percent of adults and produces mild joint pain starting around age fifty; the community treats this as ordinary aging and mostly doesn't seek care for it. Second: a member with chronically elevated blood pressure that the specialist attributes to years of night-shift work and unstable scheduling, where the underlying mechanism is the same one we would call a disease in someone with a different job. Third: a member with a harmless anatomical variant — no pain, no loss of function, normal lifespan — who is nonetheless screened out of their trade by an employer's physical exam and is now depressed about it. For each case, show the answers to each question, where the procedure stops, and what the reviewer would actually see on screen. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer my company won't like — either "not a disease, deny" or "disease, cover it" — and say why I shouldn't just overrule it. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do, where it will misfire, and whether the decision to encode any version of it in software is itself one of the values calls rather than the neutral engineering choice I'd like to think it is.
