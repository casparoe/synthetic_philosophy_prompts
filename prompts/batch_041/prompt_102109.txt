I'm a philosophy PhD student (ethics/decision theory adjacent) and I've been sitting in on the AI alignment reading group in the CS department, and there's this whole research agenda that's been quietly bugging me. The idea, roughly: you can't specify everything you care about, so instead of writing down a full objective you build agents that are penalized for making big changes to the world — "low-impact" agents — and the hope is that this stops the thing from bulldozing the biosphere on its way to making paperclips. Concretely, a lot of these schemes score an action by how far it moves the world from some default or reference trajectory, often "what would have happened if the agent had done nothing," and then the agent maximizes its actual objective while staying close to that reference. My roommate, who does RL, thinks this is obviously fine and I keep failing to explain why I think something is being assumed.

So here's what I actually want from you. What does this practice presuppose philosophically? Does treating inaction (or the default trajectory) as the thing to hug smuggle in the view that the status quo is the neutral baseline, that value lives in the deviation rather than in the resulting state? Does it require a principled distinction between an action's intended effects and its side effects, and is that distinction even coherent if value is holistic — if what matters is the whole state of the world and you can't cleanly carve it into "the goal part" and "the collateral part"? And the thing I keep tripping on: these same people are paranoid about the agent gaming the impact penalty, but if the measure is just distance-from-doing-nothing, isn't the agent incentivized to arrange the world in advance so that doing nothing is already awful, or to fiddle with what counts as the reference? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of value here, tell me which rival views it genuinely rules out versus which ones it merely happens to fit, and say where the practice is underdetermined so that several theories would license it.

And then the part I really care about: is there some weaker structure than a full value specification that could make "side effect" well-defined — a preferred direction, a partial order, a set of invariances the agent is supposed to preserve — or is the whole project doomed to smuggle in a specification it claims not to need? As a test case, if we swapped "do nothing" for a reference like "what a competent, well-meaning human would have done," what breaks and what gets fixed? I don't need citations or a reading list, I just want the reconstruction and the residue, and please don't just tell me it's all "value-laden" — I know that much, I want to know specifically which commitments do the work.
