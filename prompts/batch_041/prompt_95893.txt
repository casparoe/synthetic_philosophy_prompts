I'm a software engineer with no formal training in game theory, and I'm about to design a multi-agent simulation. Before I do, I want to check a summary that a colleague wrote for our team's internal wiki, because I'm not qualified to tell where it goes wrong and I'd rather not build on it if it does. Here is his summary in full:

"The prisoner's dilemma shows that two rational agents who each pursue their own interest both end up worse off than if they had cooperated, which is why cooperating is irrational when the game is played only once. Repeat the game, though, and cooperation becomes rational: in the round-robin computer tournaments run by Robert Axelrod, a simple strategy called tit-for-tat — open by cooperating, then copy whatever the opponent did last round — proved to be the optimal strategy for the iterated game, and the folk theorem generalizes the result, since any cooperative outcome can be sustained as an equilibrium provided the game is repeated indefinitely. This is the whole basis of the evolution of cooperation in nature: nice strategies that reciprocate and punish defection outcompete selfish ones. The practical takeaway is that if you want cooperation, make the interaction repeated, start friendly, and punish defection."

Two features of my actual setting may matter. First, my game is noisy: agents occasionally fail to deliver a message, so an intended cooperative move can arrive looking like a defection, and I have no way to tell from the outside which one happened. Second, my colleague's recommended configuration is to set the continuation probability very high — he suggests 0.99 — and to treat cooperation as essentially guaranteed once that is in place.
