I write the physical-sciences desk at a mid-sized general-interest magazine, and I keep having the same fight with my freelancers and my copy desk. Our house style has no rule for the hardest verb in our business: the verb to be, applied to things nobody has ever seen. One week a writer files that dark matter is a particle that has so far escaped detection; the next week a different writer files that the inflaton field would be the cause of the primordial fluctuations; a third treats the wave function as pure bookkeeping and gets an angry letter from a reader accusing us of philosophical cowardice; and a fourth, covering the Casimir effect, tells readers that virtual particles popping in and out of the vacuum push two plates together, which my most physics-literate contributor insists is close to a category error. I have come to think this is not a style question but an epistemological one, and that the sharpest available frame for it is the standoff between the no-miracles argument for scientific realism and the pessimistic meta-induction, together with the structural-realist compromise that tries to keep the mathematics while letting go of the furniture. What I want from you is not an essay on that debate but something my desk can actually run at deadline.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure — a set of questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a flat no, and where it must hand the sentence to a human because the remaining question is one of editorial values rather than of evidence. It needs to be executable in about ten minutes by a writer who has no philosophy training, it has to end in one of four house verdicts that I can put in the margin of a draft — assert, attribute, hedge, or flag as model — and it should be honest about which of its steps are real discriminations and which are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. I do not want a reading list or citations; assume the writer knows the words but not the arguments. Then run the procedure, showing the marginal verdict at each step, on three cases: the dark-matter particle; the inflaton field, where the entity is defined almost entirely by its role in a theory we cannot yet test directly; and the virtual particles invoked to explain the Casimir effect, where I suspect the honest verdict is one my readers and several of my writers will hate. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I personally dislike — I have my own prejudices about which popular entities are respectable — and say plainly what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which kinds of entity it has no grip on, and why it should not be allowed to turn every sentence in the magazine into a hedge.
