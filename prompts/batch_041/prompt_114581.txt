I write a column on philosophy and the human sciences for a general-reader ideas magazine, and I am building an interactive quiz to run alongside a long feature on the paradox of deontology. The paradox, as I will present it to readers, is this: a morality of constraints tells you not to kill one person even when doing so is the only way to save five, which means it tells you to let the world go worse by its own lights, and a morality that instructs you to make things worse by its own lights looks as though it has sawed off the branch it sits on. My readers are educated nonspecialists. They can follow a carefully described case, but they will not know the literature, and I do not want the quiz to be answerable by anyone who has merely skimmed an encyclopedia entry. Please write the quiz itself and its scoring key, not an essay about the debate.

Build the instrument so that it sorts readers along two independent axes, though the quiz should never name either axis to the reader.

The first axis is the reader's diagnosis of the paradox itself. At one end, the paradox is real and shows that constraints must be abandoned or reduced to something with a consequentialist foundation. In the middle, the paradox is real but tolerable: constraints genuinely do make the world worse by some measure, and that is a price worth paying, or at least a tension one lives with rather than solves. At the other end, the paradox is a pseudo-problem, generated by an error about the standpoint from which value is assessed, about the metric in which the comparison is made, or about which alternatives are being compared in the first place.

The second axis is the ground on which the reader's constraint rests. There are four, and they should be kept distinct: the agent's own integrity and agency, so that the constraint protects the person who would have to act; the status or inviolability of the person who would be used, so that the constraint protects the one acted upon; a requirement of agreement, so that the constraint marks what no one could reasonably reject; and the consequences of the practice or rule rather than of the act, so that the constraint is justified at the level of a general policy.

The two axes give a grid of twelve cells. Some of them are empty, and I want that shown rather than hidden. After the scoring key, include a short map of the grid, marking which combinations are occupied by a view a reader might actually hold, which are empty, and — for each empty cell — a sentence on whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it.

Write nine questions. Each must be a concrete case with two or three answer options, and each option should be a claim a reader could endorse without knowing where it leads. No question may ask whether the reader believes in constraints, values consequences, or is a deontologist, and no question may use the vocabulary of the two axes. The cases should do the sorting. Please make sure the following situations appear somewhere among the nine: the classic choice between killing one healthy person and letting five die for want of organs; a case in which the only way to stop someone else from killing five is to kill that person, or an innocent third party; a case in which a minor violation, such as breaking a promise or a confidence, would prevent a graver one; a case in which the forbidden deed could be performed by a machine the reader switches on, or by someone the reader hires, so that the reader must say whether the constraint attaches to the deed or to the reader's intention; a threshold case in which the numbers on the other side are enormous; a case in which the harm prevented is of the same kind as the harm done and a case in which it is of a different kind; a case in which the reader may let someone else do the killing in order to save more; a case in which the reader's own deepest commitment or life's work is what would have to be sacrificed; and a case in which the reader must choose between acting and being acted upon, so that the question is whether constraints bind even in self-defense.

For the scoring key, map answer patterns onto the positions that occupy the grid. Give each position a descriptive name rather than a school's name, and where no one plausibly holds a combination, mark the leaf as unnamed and say what would have to be true of someone who landed there. At the end, flag any two positions the quiz cannot reliably separate, and say plainly why — whether the questions fail to reach the difference or the difference is not real. Do not include a reading list or citations; my readers cannot use them, and I do not want the answer to collapse into a bibliography. Write in American English.
