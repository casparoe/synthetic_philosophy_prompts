alright, decision theory question, I've been chewing on this for like a week and cant find the leak.

Background: I'm a card-carrying pay-in-counterfactual-mugging guy. You know the setup. Omega flips a coin; heads, she hands you $10k, tails, she shows up and asks you for $100. Either way you only ever learn about any of it in the tails branch, so by the time she's asking, the coin has already landed and nothing you say now can change whether you got the money. I pay. My reason is that what you're really choosing isn't an action, it's a disposition, and "pay when she asks" is the disposition that would've collected the $10k in the heads branch, so ex ante it's a great trade even though the $100 is a dead loss once you're actually sitting in tails.

A friend has an argument that this reasoning proves way too much and I can't tell whether he's right. Here it is:

(1) What you should evaluate is the choice scored from a standpoint before you know which branch you're in — you average over the branches weighted by their ex ante probabilities, rather than scoring the act by what you know at the moment you act. Call this the Disposition Principle.
(2) By (1), you pay in counterfactual mugging. "Pay" scores +$10k in heads and −$100 in tails, and ex ante that's 50/50, so it wins.
(3) Now the termite thing (XOR blackmail, whatever you want to call it). A predictor — greedy, absolutely honest, and very good at modelling you — sends you a letter saying that exactly one of two things is true: that you will pay him $1000, or that your building already has a terrible termite infestation (he didn't give you the termites). An infestation would cost you about $1M to deal with. By the same reasoning as (2), you pay: not paying is the branch where the letter's other disjunct has to be doing the work, i.e. the branch where you're infested, whereas paying is compatible with being fine. Ex ante, pay.
(4) But paying the termite guy is a total sucker move. The predictor only bothers sending that letter to people he's already checked don't have termites — that's the entire grift — so you fork over $1000 for nothing, and he farms everyone who thinks the way you do.
(5) So the Disposition Principle that makes you pay in counterfactual mugging also makes you pay in the termite case, where paying is plainly wrong.
(6) So the Disposition Principle is too strong, and the counterfactul-mugging intuition that rests on it goes down with it.

I'd really like to keep (1) and (2). Is this argument valid? If it isn't, which step is doing the dirty work, and why does it look so innocent? And if I do have to give something up, which piece is cheapest to lose, and what does losing it cost me on the counterfactual mugging side?
