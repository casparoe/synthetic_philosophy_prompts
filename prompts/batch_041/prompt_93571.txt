Having read rationalist blogs and forums for some years, I have noticed that the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax are usually invoked together and rarely distinguished, and I recently encountered a comment that packaged them as follows: the alignment tax is the extra cost a lab pays to make its AI safe; the competitiveness constraint says that in a race, any lab that pays that tax will be outcompeted by a lab that does not; therefore safety is impossible unless every major lab is forced to pay the tax simultaneously; since a binding treaty is unlikely, the only real solution is a singleton (a single global decision-maker or AI monopoly), which is why technical alignment work matters less than ensuring that a single actor controls the transition. My suspicion is that this mixes a definition, a strategic claim, and a policy conclusion, and that the slide from one to the next is doing more work than the argument admits. Which parts would people who work on this accept, which are common simplifications, and which are mistaken? Where would a defender of the comment push back? In particular, does the conclusion follow from the two premises, or does it depend on extra assumptions about how labs discount the future, whether safety work can be a competitive advantage, how races end, and whether the tax is a fixed per-unit cost rather than an equilibrium price that shifts with expectations and coordination? Finally, if the tax is better modeled as an equilibrium phenomenon, what are the most important implications for what a lab, a regulator, or an individual researcher should do?
