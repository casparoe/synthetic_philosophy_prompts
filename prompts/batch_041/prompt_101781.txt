I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been trying to understand the family of decision problems that includes the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery. I would like you to build me a decision tree that sorts the positions on these problems, and I want the tree to be usable by someone who knows the game but none of the surrounding literature.

Here is the game as I will use it, so that the tree does not depend on any particular published statement of it. A population of N people, all strangers to one another and unable to communicate, each independently decides whether to submit an entry. In the Platonia version, a fixed prize goes to the sole entrant if exactly one entry is submitted, and nothing is paid otherwise; submitting an entry costs nothing. In the Luring Lottery version, the prize is divided equally among all who submit, and if nobody submits, the prize is discarded; again, submission is free. N is common knowledge and large. The question, for each person, is whether to submit.

I want the tree to start from commitments a person could state without knowing any position names, and to end at named positions. The positions I need as leaves are causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, superrationality in the style Douglas Hofstadter introduced, Kantian universalizability, team reasoning, functional decision theory together with its timeless and updateless relatives, and plain Nash-equilibrium reasoning. Add any further position you think earns a leaf. Every branch node must be a concrete case or a claim on which the positions genuinely diverge, phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. Do not use a node that amounts to asking which label the person prefers.

Aim for eight to twelve questions and no more than twelve leaves. Include at least these cases as nodes: a variant in which the population is divided into two groups of different sizes and the prize is paid only when exactly one entry arrives from each group, so that two entries are needed in total and the symmetry between players is broken; a variant in which you learn that a large number of others have already submitted; and a variant in which submitting costs a small amount. I want the two-group variant in particular to serve as the node that separates positions which depend on symmetry from those which do not.

After the tree, list the combinations of answers that no named position occupies, and give a one-sentence diagnosis of each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two positions give identical answers on every question in the tree, flag them as a pair the tree cannot separate, rather than adding a question whose only purpose is to force them apart.

Finally, add a short scoring key: for a reader who answers the questions, the key should map their answer pattern to the position it most nearly matches, and should say which single question is the strongest discriminator for each position.
