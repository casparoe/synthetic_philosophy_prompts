In many democracies the method of election is not uniform across bodies. The national legislature is filled in single-member districts by plurality: the candidate with the most votes takes the seat however thin the margin, and the votes cast for everyone else elect no one. A regional assembly, a second chamber, or a delegation to a supranational parliament is filled instead by party-list proportional representation, commonly with a legal threshold below which a party receives nothing at all, so that those votes too are discarded. On the same day the same voter may be handed a district ballot and a party ballot. Campaigns tell her to vote tactically in the district race, for the lesser evil among those who can actually win, and sincerely for the list. The district member is expected to spend much of the week on casework for individual constituents; no list member owes a service of that kind to anyone in particular. Some systems let a candidate stand in a district and on a list at once, so that one rejected by the voters of the district may enter the chamber all the same; some reserve list seats expressly to correct the distortion the district results have produced.

I want the implicit theory of each practice. Take the district rule first: does awarding the seat on a mere plurality, rather than a majority, commit it to a view about what a vote is — the designation of a person to act for a place, or the expression of a preference that a counting rule aggregates — and does the territorial basis of the district presuppose that interests are represented by locality rather than by opinion, and if so, what must be true of interests for that to hold? Then the list rule: does it presuppose that a legislature ought to be a mirror or sample of the electorate's preferences? Is that a different conception of representation from the district rule's, or the same conception with a different unit? What does the threshold presuppose — that some preferences are too slight or too eccentric to deserve a seat, which is a claim about the significance of opinions and not merely their number? What does dual candidacy presuppose about whether a seat belongs to a party or to a person, and what does it presuppose about proportionality that corrective seats are defined as corrections of the district result rather than the other way round?

Which of these commitments does each practice strictly require, and which does it merely sit well with — could a defender of single-member districts accept the mirror conception and argue only about the unit, or a defender of lists accept that representation is of persons and places? Then say whether the two practices, side by side in one polity, presuppose consistent things, or whether the arrangement is held together by a division of labour between territorial accountability in one chamber and ideological proportionality in another, and identify what is philosophically underdetermined, in the sense that several theories of representation would license the very same arrangements. Finally, if we replaced the presupposed view with its rival — if we decided that a legislature is properly a proportional image of opinion rather than a body of local agents — tell me what would have to change: how districts are drawn, what the member's week is for, what counts as a vote that elects no one, and whether the threshold survives.
