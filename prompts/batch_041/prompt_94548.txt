I've been arguing about this with a friend who's an amateur archaeology buff, and we keep talking past each other about what it even means to say that a society collapsed, so I want to set out where I actually land on the cases and see whether there's a principle underneath. The Late Bronze Age in the eastern Mediterranean: a collapse, no question — the palaces go, the Hittite empire goes, the trade networks go, and writing itself is lost for centuries, which for me is the clincher. The Classic Maya: I want to say this is not a collapse, even though the cities are abandoned and the political order ends, because the Maya are still there, still speaking the languages, still holding much of the cosmology — the thing changed, it didn't die. Norse Greenland: a collapse, and maybe the clearest one, even though it's tiny, because the settlements simply stop and there's no successor. The western Roman Empire: not a collapse of a society at all, only of a state, since the eastern half continues and the language, the church, and most of daily life carry on — my friend says that's a cheat, and I half agree. Easter Island: I used to take the ecocide story as the textbook collapse, and now I've read enough to think the population crash is largely post-contact and the whole narrative is contested, so I don't trust my verdict here. And the Soviet Union in 1991: I keep wanting to call it a collapse, since that's what everyone says, but by any measure I'd actually defend, the society kept its cities, its literacy, its industry, and its capacity to run a state — life got much worse without the machinery breaking. Tokugawa Japan becoming Meiji Japan: not a collapse, plainly, even though an entire hereditary order is abolished within a generation, which tells me that being drastic is not the same as collapsing.

Here's the principle I've been trying out, and I'd like it tested and repaired. A society collapses, I think, not when it loses a lot, but when it loses the capacity to repair itself — the losses don't have to be large, they have to exceed what the society's own institutions can absorb and rebuild. On that view the Norse colony collapses because nothing survives that could have recovered it; the Soviet Union doesn't collapse because the recovery machinery is intact even when the damage is enormous; Bronze Age Greece collapses because recovery takes centuries and comes from outside; and the Maya don't collapse because new cities get built, in the same tradition, by the same people. I also remember reading somewhere that collapse is really just rapid simplification, and that it's often the rational move rather than a failure, when the returns on more complexity have gone negative — I don't know whether that helps or just relabels the problem.

What I can't tell is whether my principle actually delivers my verdicts or merely sounds good. It seems to make the Bronze Age and the Maya cases turn on things I haven't specified — how long a gap counts as a failure to recover, and whether help or reinvention from outside disqualifies the recovery — and I suspect a magnitude-based principle, where collapse is just a sharp and sustained drop in population or complexity, would handle Easter Island and the Soviet Union better while forcing me to give up one of the others. So: is there one principle, or a small set of competing ones, that gets all or most of my verdicts? For each clause, tell me which case forces it. Then say what it predicts for cases I haven't listed — pick two real ones, not invented ones — and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best principle cannot keep. I'd rather lose a verdict than paper over the inconsistency, but I do want to know which one I'm losing and why.
