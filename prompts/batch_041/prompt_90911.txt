I've been doing the records for our local history group for about six years now — transcribing gravestones, scanning the parish books, chasing up old ledgers people bring in — and I keep having strong feelings about what isn't in them, and I can't work out what the rule is that I'm actually going by. Maybe you can.

Here's what I think, case by case.

My nan's diaries, 1961 to 2003. Weather, prices, who came round. She never once mentions her brother who died at nine, or the baby she lost between my mum and my aunt. I don't think she did anything wrong. It's a diary.

The poorhouse register, 1880s. Forty-odd inmates listed as 'M. Ryan (idiot), 40' and nothing else — no birthplace, no family, no death date, though the book has columns for all of it. Nobody alive did that. I still think something wrong happened there.

The factory accident book, 1912 to 1934. Every cut finger and crushed hand recorded beautifully: name, department, date. The women in the packing room aren't in it at all, because they were paid out of a different book that nobody ever handed over. No one decided anything. That one feels like the worst of the lot to me, worse then the poorhouse.

My great-aunt's burial book. Babies who died before they were christened get a dash, no name, because that's what the form was for. She'd have wept for every one of them. Still wrong, I think.

The hospice volunteer who wrote down the old men's stories — one of them asked her to leave out the prison years and she did, and now her notebook is the only record of him that exists anywhere. I feel worse about that omission than about the factory book. Which makes no sense, because he asked and she listened.

My uncle tapeing the elders in 1986 — he only taped the men. He didn't think anything of it. My mum's lot are furious now. I honestly can't tell whether the wrong happened in 1986 or in 2010.

And the cemetery website: I skipped the stones I couldn't read. People now cite my transcriptions as though they're the whole churchyard. That one's on me and I never even noticed doing it.

So: is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? Intent doesn't seem to do it, since the ones with the worst intent (my great-aunt, following the form) bother me less than the ones with no intent at all. Nor does 'only surviving record', because the diary is the only surviving record of my nan and I let her off. What I want to know is what makes a silence in a record a wrong, as opposed to just how records are — and who it's a wrong against. Please also give me one case I haven't thought of where your principle says the opposite of what my gut would say, and tell me straight which of my verdicts above you'd have to throw out to keep the rest.
