I'm in a fortnightly reading group on moral uncertainty and we've hit a wall. Two of us have been having the same fight for about six sessions now and it's starting to feel less like disagreement and more like the two of us talking past each other. She's what I'd call a formalist: she thinks that once you grant that a sane, well-informed agent can be genuinely torn between two moral theories -- not torn about the facts, torn about which theory is right -- you need some machinery for deciding what to do in that state, and that the natural machinery is maximising expected choiceworthiness (or a refinement of it that builds in some caution about moral risk), with the awkwardness of comparing amounts of choiceworthiness across theories being a hard technical problem rather than a reductio of the whole project. He's the sceptic: he thinks the project is either empty or built on a fiction. His line is that without intertheoretic comparisons you end up with something that just tells you to follow whichever theory you find most probable, and that if you supply the comparisons by hand you can get any verdict you like out of the same case by fiddling with the normalisation, so the decision theory is never what's really doing the work. He thinks the honest response to not knowing which theory is right is a lot less formal -- get clearer about your own moral beliefs, be humble about your track record, be careful with the irreversible stuff, and let practical wisdom mop up. For what it's worth I'm somewhere between them, which is how I got nominated to write this up.

I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher who works on normative uncertainty and is unusually good at forcing precision out of people who are gesturing at each other, and draft the joint statement the two of them could actually both put their names to. Start with a numbered list of claims both sides accept: about what moral uncertainty is and isn't, about what any adequate response to it would have to do, about which objections are live and which are cheap shots, and about the cases where they agree the formalist's machinery gets the right answer and the sceptic's misgivings don't bite. Then a disagreements table. For each remaining dispute, give the precise statement of the disagreement in wording that each side would accept as fair to its own view -- this matters more than anything else in the document, I want formulations that are neither vacuous nor quietly loaded toward one side -- then the crux underneath it, then the case, argument or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on that crux, and then, recorded seperately, what each side predicts about it. If some part of the fight turns out to be merely verbal, say which part and why, and don't be diplomatic if you think one of them is really making two claims and sliding between them.

Three things I'd like the document to confront head-on, because they're what keep derailing us. First, a case where one of the theories you're uncertain between is itself a theory of how to act under moral uncertainty, so whatever rule you adopt has to be applied to itself -- if the sceptic's view gets non-trivial credence, what does the formalist's rule say to do then? Second, a case where one candidate theory assigns infinite or lexically prior value to something, so that even a small credence in it swamps every other consideration and the expected-choiceworthiness verdict comes out absurd. Third, a case where the theories agree completely on how to rank the options but disagree about what the units on the scale mean, so that the only thing left to fight over is the normalisation. These three are our own inventions, so don't worry about whether they're realistic -- just say what each side's machinery does with them. Take your time and brainstorm the candidate cruxes before you start drafting; the whole value here is in the formulations. Don't pad it out with literature -- I know the literature, I want the document, and no reading lists or citations please, they're no use to me here. British English throughout, our department is pedantic about it.
