I am applying to a small interdisciplinary foundation for a six-month independent research grant on cause prioritization. I have a half-formed idea that I want you to turn into a short research proposal. The standard importance–tractability–neglectedness heuristic treats neglectedness as a property of a cause, usually measured by how few people or dollars are currently devoted to it. My suspicion is that this conflates at least two things: neglectedness as a stock (how crowded the cause is now) and neglectedness as a marginal-return claim (what the next unit of effort would achieve). These can come apart systematically, and the gap is not just measurement error. It is generated by the heuristic's own use: identifying a cause as neglected and tractable attracts attention to it, which changes the property that made it a priority. I have been thinking of this as a neglectedness feedback loop, and I want to know whether it makes static priority rankings unstable in a way that matters for real allocation decisions.

Please write the proposal. It should state the central question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer. For example: under what conditions does ranking causes by a static neglectedness score converge with ranking them by the marginal value of additional effort, and how should the ranking change when the act of ranking itself shifts effort toward the top-ranked cause? Explain why existing treatments of neglectedness—those that treat it as a proxy for room for more funding, those that treat it as an information problem, and those that treat it as a simple attention measure—do not already settle the question. Then set out the method. What conceptual distinctions would I need to draw (for instance, resource neglectedness vs. attention neglectedness vs. institutional neglectedness; absolute vs. marginal neglectedness; exogenous vs. endogenous tractability)? What simple formal apparatus would make the feedback tractable—a two-cause model with endogenous attention, a fixed-point condition, or a small simulation? What kinds of cases, historical or hypothetical, would test the framework, and what would disconfirm it? Be concrete about what I would actually do month by month over six months, what a successful deliverable looks like (a paper, a decision procedure, a taxonomy with counterexamples, or something else), and what would count as the project failing or collapsing into stipulation. Also give an honest assessment of the biggest risk: that the reflexivity is either trivial or so general that it applies to any resource-allocation problem and therefore yields no special guidance for cause prioritization. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I will add those myself. The proposal itself should be about 1,500 words.
