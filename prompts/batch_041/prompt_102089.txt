You are a philosopher-poet with a firm grasp of decision theory and a good ear for verse. Before you write, think carefully about what the poem has to accomplish and where its form should carry the philosophical weight, then give me the poem alone.

Lately I have been preoccupied with an idea I would call evidential cooperation in large worlds, and I want a poem that embodies it. The idea, as I understand it, is this: two agents who never interact and never learn of each other's choices — who may stand in no causal relation at all — can still be described as cooperating, because each one's decision to cooperate is evidence about what the other decides. My choosing to cooperate tells me something about what agents like me choose, and in a large world there are many of them; the cooperation is constituted by the correlation in the choosing, not by any exchange between the parties. What I cannot settle is whether this deserves the name of cooperation at all, or whether it is only two dispositions that happen to rhyme. It has become personal: someone close to me and I keep arriving at the same choices without ever discussing them, and I no longer know how to describe what we are doing.

Write me a poem of roughly twenty-four to thirty lines, in two voices, that enacts the problem rather than explaining it. Use alternating stanzas, each spoken by one party; the voices must never address each other, never share a stanza, and the poem must never show any contact, message, or meeting between them. Let both voices speak one and the same refrain, word for word — a line that can be read either as a statement of fact or as a promise. Keep their imagery parallel enough that a reader cannot tell whether they inhabit one world or two, and let that ambiguity stand as the crux rather than a riddle to be solved. Close with the refrain alone on its own line, unassigned, so the reader has to decide whose it is. Do not use the words cooperation, evidence, correlation, decision, or world anywhere in the poem; make the idea concrete instead. Someone who knows the concept should be able to point to the line where the poem earns the evidential reading and to the place where it withholds the causal one.
