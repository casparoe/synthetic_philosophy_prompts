The question was (10 points, ~250 words):

"In the lecture I split the work money does into two jobs. The conversion job: it lets me swap what I have right now for something I can hold on to until I need what you have. The commensuration job: it's supposed to put two unlike goods on a single scale. (a) Which of these jobs does Aristotle think money does, and what does his answer tell us about where he thinks the commensurability of goods comes from? (b) Then connect that to his account of justice in exchange (proportional reciprocity) and to what he says about wealth-getting and interest."

And this is what the student handed in:

"Aristotle treats money as something that exists by convention rather than by nature. It isn't like a house or a shoe, which is what it is whether or not anyone agrees to it; a coin is money only because a community treats it as money, and he even points out that its name tracks this, since nomisma comes from nomos. Money's point is to stand in for need: instead of having to find the one person who both has what I want and wants what I have, I can sell my surplus for coin now and buy what I want later.

So money does both of the jobs from the lecture. The conversion job is obvious — it lets what I have now sit in a form I can carry to market next month. But it does the commensuration job too. Aristotle says that before anything can be exchanged the goods have to be comparable, and he holds that money is the thing that makes them comparable: it is the measure that puts the shoemaker's shoes and the builder's house on one scale. And since money is by convention, the comparability it supplies is by convention as well — there is no natural measure for it to track, and the ratio at which goods trade is whatever the currency convention settles on. That is why the exchange ratio isn't something you could read off nature.

What that gives him is justice in exchange as proportional reciprocity rather than equality: what's required is that the ratio be preserved, not that each side gives the same amount. His point about the doctor and the farmer is that exchange relates parties who are unlike — two doctors don't trade with each other — and the exchange is what ties them together.

The same ideas run through the discussion of wealth-getting. Acquisition that serves the household has a natural limit, whereas chrematistics, which aims at money for its own sake, has none. He treats interest as the worst case, because the lender's gain doesn't come from any work of his own — nobody has laboured for the return, the money just breeds. So the city is held together by proportional reciprocity, and money is the device that makes it possible."
