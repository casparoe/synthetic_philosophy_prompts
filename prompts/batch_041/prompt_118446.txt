I have been arguing with a friend who used to work in intelligence analysis about the collapse of the Soviet Union, and I would like a proper analysis rather than a talking point. His claim is that the episode vindicates the outside view: the Kremlinologists and Soviet specialists, for all their inside knowledge, were caught out, while anyone who simply asked how long polities of that kind tend to last would have seen the end coming. My suspicion is that this is hindsight doing the work, and that the base rate is impressive only because we now know which reference class to pick.

So please treat the collapse of the Soviet Union -- roughly from Gorbachev's accession through the revolutions in Eastern Europe in 1989, the failed coup in August 1991, and the dissolution of the Union at the end of that year, with the awkward fact that the spring 1991 referendum produced a majority for preserving a renewed Union while several republics refused to take part at all -- as a case study in reference-class forecasting and the outside view, and run the analysis in both directions. First, the mapping: enumerate the candidate reference classes (great powers that have lost an empire; twentieth-century land empires that dissolved, Ottoman and Austro-Hungarian and Russian among them; communist regimes; multi-ethnic federations; regimes that undertake partial liberalization), say what base rate each supplies, and defend how one would have chosen among them ex ante rather than after the fact. Be explicit about whether any of these base rates is well defined, given how few cases there are and how non-independent they look -- 1848, 1918 and 1989 read like cascades rather than draws from an urn -- and say whether the Soviet Union's status as both a member of the class and a successor to another member contaminates the sample. Second, what the outside view actually delivers in 1980 or 1985: a date, a direction, a hazard rate, or nothing usable? Is a claim of the form that empires last about so many years a statistical statement or a metaphor wearing numbers, and what would it mean for the hazard to be roughly constant with age rather than rising as the decline narratives imply? Third, where the theory goes quiet or misdescribes the case: the timing, the mechanism (elite defection and dissolution from within rather than conquest or revolution from below), the comparatively peaceful form the end took, and the speed of the unravelling after 1989.

Fourth, and this is what I actually want adjudicated: what does the episode show about the outside view itself? Is it a confirmation of the method, an anomaly, or an illustration that the method's apparent success depends on choosing the reference class once the answer is already known -- and if the last, is there any version of the outside view that escapes the circularity, for instance one that fixes the class in advance and accepts that it can only tell you the odds and never the occasion? I do not want a bibliography or a list of who said what; I want the argument, a disciplined account of which features of the case the theory explains and which it cannot reach, and a clear signal at each point where you are making a judgment call rather than reporting a result.
