I am a graduate student in statistics who has been sitting in on a reading group about the foundations of probability, and I keep meeting a pattern that I now recognise immediately but can neither name nor adjudicate. Two people agree on every fact about a set-up, and yet they assign different probabilities, because each has silently decided that a different quantity is the one that is spread evenly. Last term two friends of mine argued for an hour about a machine that turns out cubes: one said that the side lengths are the things distributed uniformly between zero and one metre, the other that the volumes are, and they gave different answers to the innocent question of how likely a cube is to come out smaller than half a metre on a side, each appealing to the thought that the machine has no preferences. I have since met the same disagreement in an older and prettier form, about how likely a randomly drawn chord of a circle is to be longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle; the answers one third, one half and one quarter are all defended, in earnest, by people who agree about every geometrical fact. I know that the second of these examples has a famous name attached to it, and I have been told more than once that the general moral is that no prior is ever genuinely uninformative. Neither of those facts is what I am after.

Two further things I have noticed make me think that general moral is too quick. The first is that these disputes do not all end the same way. Sometimes somebody produces a further consideration — that the answer ought not to change when the whole set-up is rescaled, or when the object is rotated, or when two descriptions of the same situation are exchanged for one another — and everyone, including the people who had given the other answers, accepts that the matter is now settled and that the discarded answers were artefacts of a poor choice of variable. Other times the same style of consideration is produced on both sides, the demands cannot all be met at once, and the discussion ends with a shrug and the remark that the question was ill-posed. I cannot tell in advance which kind of case I am in. The second is that in the chord case the obvious symmetry seems to be useless: as far as I can see, all three of the standard answers are unaffected by rotating the circle, so whatever is doing the real work in the arguments that people accept must be something other than that symmetry, and I do not know how to tell a symmetry argument that genuinely constrains the answer from one that merely ratifies a convention that happens to feel natural to us.

What I would like is to know whether there is an established concept, or a body of work, for this general situation — a probability that is only defined relative to a choice of which variable is to be uniform, where invariance considerations sometimes rescue the question and sometimes do not — and how it stands to the nearest things I already know of, such as the puzzle about the chords, and the older worry about which class of cases one should count when assigning a probability to a single case. Please do not stop at a label. What I want is a genuine carving: what exactly the phenomenon is, what distinguishes the disputes that a consideration of this kind can settle from those it cannot, where the boundary lies between this and the perfectly respectable fact that a probability may depend on one's background information, and, if there is no established concept for it, a proposed term with a definition and clear cases on either side of its edge.
