I am convening a session of a graduate seminar in political theory on the theme of idealised consent, and I want to stage a confrontation between John Stuart Mill and a proposal from the contemporary AI alignment literature. The proposal, introduced by Eliezer Yudkowsky and usually called coherent extrapolated volition, holds roughly this: if we ever build a machine of very great power, we should not build it to satisfy our present preferences, since those are ignorant and parochial; we should build it to satisfy the preferences we would have if we knew more, reasoned much faster, were more the people we wished we were, and had grown up farther together — the last clause gesturing at something like a single human community rather than a plurality of separate ones. The extrapolated preference set is then to serve as the machine's target, with the important qualification that where extrapolation yields several stable attractors rather than one convergent answer, the machine is to hold satisficing options open rather than pick a winner on its own. A colleague in the department insists that this is simply Mill's competent judge with a computer attached: the same move of deferring to a purified version of what people actually want, and the same confidence that the purified wants would vindicate individuality and self-development. I am not persuaded, and since the students will want a worked answer, I would like you to reconstruct what Mill, transported to the present and given a fair and neutral summary of the proposal, would have made of it as a specification of what a very powerful institution should aim at.

Please proceed in three steps, and trace the reasoning rather than summarising the literature. First, the strongest case that can be made for the proposal in Mill's own vocabulary: his account of the higher pleasures and of the competent judge who has experience of both; his conviction that liberty serves the interests of human beings regarded as progressive creatures capable of development; his view that what a person now wants may diverge from what serves her development, and that the divergence is often something she would herself acknowledge on reflection. Second, the strongest case against, and here I want you to be explicit about whether Mill's fallibilism about received opinion, and about any authority's claim to decide for others, extends from coercion to the design of an agent's target, or whether the fallibility worry loses its force once the target is extrapolated rather than imposed. You should also say what work the grown-up-together clause does: whether a single convergent volition for all humanity is compatible with Mill's insistence that experiments in living, and hence persisting disagreement, are the engine of improvement, or whether the satisficing qualification is enough to save it.

Third, a verdict. Which way would Mill come down, which of his commitments would have to be stretched or abandoned to reach that verdict, and which elements of the question are so anachronistic that the reconstruction tells us more about our own commitments than about his? I should add that I am not interested in whether the alignment literature got Mill right, only in what a coherent Millian position on this question would look like, so please do not hedge by declaring the matter underdetermined; if it is, say which way the balance of his commitments tips, and how far.
