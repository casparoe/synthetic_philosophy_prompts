Last month I was appointed to the board of the foundation that runs our town's former detention site — the place with the small museum, the wall of names, and roughly six hundred recorded testimonies gathered in the mid-1990s from survivors and from elderly local residents who saw what happened. My own family is in that collection twice over, which is part of why I'm asking for help rather than trusting my gut. At Thursday's meeting we have to decide a proposal from the site's director: seal the entire testimony collection for forty years, release instead a single redacted narrative summary prepared by a contract historian, and redesign the memorial so that it erodes rather than being periodically restored — grass over the foundation, the names cut into soft stone that will blur within a generation. The director's argument is the Rieff line: in a town where the two communities still live on the same three streets, an open archive of accusations against named people whose grandchildren are our neighbors has kept the wound suppurating, and a generation of enforced quiet is the only realistic path to something like coexistence. The opposing faction says this is institutional forgetting dressed up as therapy, that the descendants of victims hold the strongest claim to the material, and that a foundation created to remember has no business designing its own oblivion.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. I need a clear bottom line — approve the director's proposal, reject it, or approve it with specified conditions — with a concrete time frame: what is sealed, until when, who can lift the seal and by what procedure, and what happens at the review date if the political situation has changed or has not. The reasoning has to be something both the director and the dissenting trustees can actually engage with, not a restatement of the principle that memory matters. Two things I want the memo to work through rather than assume. First, the collection does at least two different jobs: it is evidence, useful for establishing what happened and for answering denial, and it is also a relational thing — a place where a particular community keeps faith with its own dead and gives its children somewhere to stand. Does the case for sealing it survive if you separate those jobs, or does the argument only look strong when they are run together? Second, if there is a duty to remember, it looks as though it is owed by a thick community to its own members and its own dead, and much weaker when it is owed to humanity at large; so I need the memo to say who is supposed to hold the duty here, whether a foundation and a ministry can discharge it on behalf of people who never asked them to, and whether the descendants of the victims can waive or transfer a duty that may be owed to the dead rather than to the living.

I should also flag that my mother's testimony is item 214, and that one of the men named in the collection is the grandfather of a colleague on the board — tell me plainly whether I should recuse myself and let the vice-chair sign this, and if not, why not. I don't need a literature review or citations; I need the reasoning, an honest statement of the strongest case against whatever you recommend, and an explicit list of the questions the memo cannot settle on the merits because they are political rather than philosophical.
