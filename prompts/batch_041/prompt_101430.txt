You are a philosopher of economics and social choice theorist with a firm command of the formal apparatus of welfare economics — expected-utility theory and the von Neumann–Morgenstern representation, the Pareto conditions, the role of interpersonal comparability, and the difference between ex ante and ex post evaluation. Before you write anything, think carefully and sketch the argumentative arc, so that the dialogue builds rather than circles.

Context: a friend and I have just spent a long evening arguing about Harsanyi's social aggregation theorem — the result that if individual preferences and a social preference ordering both satisfy the expected-utility axioms over lotteries, and the social ordering satisfies a Pareto condition, then the social ordering can be represented as a weighted sum of individual utilities. She treats the theorem as a vindication of utilitarianism, inherited from the veil-of-ignorance thought that a rational chooser behind such a veil would maximize expected utility over the prospect of being anyone. I think it establishes less than she claims, and that the live question is not whether the derivation is valid but which of its premises is quietly doing work it is not entitled to do. I want to see the dispute staged at full strength.

Write a dialogue between the two of us. Call her the Aggregator and me the Skeptic.

Constraints:

- Both characters must be fully competent; neither is a strawman. The Aggregator should be able to state the theorem and reconstruct its derivation cleanly, and to meet the obvious objections on their merits. The Skeptic should grant whatever is genuinely correct in the Aggregator's position and then locate the disagreement at one specific premise rather than rejecting the whole framework.

- The exchange must be genuine back-and-forth: each speaker should answer the other's actual last point, not restate a standing position, and neither should concede merely to keep the peace.

- Have them work through at least two objections with their replies, at least one of which a textbook summary of the Harsanyi-versus-Rawls exchange would not anticipate. Among the candidates you might consider: the gap between a weighted-sum conclusion and the equal weighting the utilitarian actually needs, and what extra premise is required to close it; whether the theorem aggregates expected utilities (ex ante) or realized ones (ex post), and why that matters for a case in which each person faces a separate lottery of her own; and whether the argument silently presupposes cardinal, interpersonally comparable utilities of exactly the kind the premises were meant to avoid. Choose whichever you judge strongest, and have a character say why it is the strongest.

- End with the two of them agreeing on precisely what the crux is, even though they remain on opposite sides of it. Do not force convergence, and do not let it trail off into a shrug.

After the dialogue, add a brief closing note — a few sentences — stating how confident you are in the technical claims your characters make, and identifying the single point in the exchange you are least sure of, whether because it rests on a contested reading of the theorem or because you had to choose among competing formulations. Be candid about where the dialogue takes liberties with the ideas.
