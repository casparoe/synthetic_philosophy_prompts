I am trying to understand an apparent tension in A.J. Ayer's philosophy. As I understand it, the verification principle that Ayer defends in Language, Truth and Logic implies that any statement about a future life is literally meaningless, because no possible experience could verify it; this is why he treats religious and metaphysical claims as nonsense. Yet I have read that Ayer devoted part of that same book to the question of survival after death, and that late in life, after a near-death experience, he conceded that the experience had somewhat weakened his confidence that death is the end. How can these things be reconciled? If the verification principle really entails that there is life after death is cognitively meaningless, then Ayer should have had nothing to discuss, and no experience should have been able to shake a conviction that was not even a genuine belief in the first place. And if he did think the question was meaningful, does that not show that his positivism was much less radical than it is usually taken to be?
