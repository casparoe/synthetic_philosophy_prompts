For a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind that I am running as a teaching assistant, I want to stage the disagreement about multiple realizability as a short dialogue rather than as a lecture, so that the students can see where the real pressure points are. The two speakers should be a functionalist who treats multiple realizability as the central empirical consideration in favor of her view, and a critic who is happy to grant that the same mental state may occur in creatures with very different physical constitutions, but who insists that this fact does not establish functionalism — because the functional role is either specified so thinly that it collapses into a sophisticated behaviorism, or so thickly that the supposedly alien case no longer counts as realizing it. Neither character should be a strawman: each should be able to state the other's position fairly, concede whatever is correct in it, and then explain why it does not go through; and each reply should engage with what the other has just actually said rather than pivoting to a prepared point.

I would like the exchange to be driven by a concrete case rather than by slogans, so please build it around the following invented example and have both characters reason about it out loud. On a distant planet there is a eusocial species whose members, when damaged, withdraw from activity, avoid the damaging stimulus, and learn to avoid similar stimuli in the future; but their internal economy is a distributed chemical signaling network, with no neuron-like cells and no internal states that map onto anything resembling our nociceptors or our affective states. The functionalist will want to say that such a creature is in pain; the critic will want to say that this verdict is either trivially true, because pain has been defined as whatever plays the causal role, or else covertly dependent on a human-shaped conception of what the role must contain. Let them press each other on whether their disagreement is empirical or merely verbal, on what sort of discovery could settle it one way or the other, and on whether the critic's alternative — that pain requires a particular kind of realization — can avoid the charge that it makes pain an arbitrary biological parochialism. I would prefer the dialogue to end in a genuine standoff or a partial and hard-won agreement rather than a tidy victory for either side, and I would like it to be long enough for each position to be developed through at least two or three exchanges.
