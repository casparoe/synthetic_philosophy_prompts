Im a science-and-ideas reporter and I'm putting the last touches on a feature about G. E. Moore — the Cambridge philosopher who thought the two best things a person can be doing are enjoying beautiful things and being close to the people they love, and who had this strange doctrine he called organic unities, roughly that a whole can be worth more than the sum of its parts, so that things worth next to nothing on their own can, joined up, be worth a great deal. (He also had a test where you imagine a thing entirely by itself, nothing and no one around, to see what it's worth alone — I want the poem to make that move and then undo it.) I want to close the piece with a poem. Not a poem about that idea. A poem that is that idea.

So: a sestina. Six end-words, permuted the usual way, and pick them so three come from the beauty side and three from the friendship side, so the envoi has to braid the two into one thing. Here's the trick I want the form to pull off: every one of the six words should be a word you'd never build a poem around on its own — small, dull, almost nothing — and by the end the reader should feel that in this company they've come to be worth something none of them had alone, so that one of them, late in the poem, reads like a different word than the one in the first stanza. Put one genuinely cold or unlovely word among the six and let where it sits decide what it's worth. It has to work as a poem for someone who's never heard of Moore, and it shouldn't make a philosopher of value wince — none of that greeting-card business about how love and beauty are lovely.

Two things before you start. If I've got Moore wrong — if the doctrine is really only that a whole's value isn't the arithmetic sum and doesn't mean a part's value can shift with its neighbours, or if a sestina is the wrong machine for this — say so plainly instead of writing me the poem I asked for. And when it's done, I dont want a bibliography, just a short note on where it takes liberties with him or leans on a reading that's contested.
