I'm in a graduate seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and the convenor has asked each of us to circulate a short statement of our own position before the first meeting, so that the others can press us on that rather than on a textbook version of some view. I've been working through Galen Strawson's Basic Argument and, rather to my surprise, I've ended up accepting it, while keeping most of the rest of what I already thought about blame and punishment. I suspect the resulting package is less tidy than I've been pretending, and I'd like that suspicion tested before I'm in the room.

Here is where I've landed. (1) The Basic Argument is sound. To be ultimately responsible for what I do I would have to be responsible for the self that produced the action; to be responsible for that self I would have to have brought it about by some act of my own, which would itself have to issue from a self I already had. The regress never terminates and self-creation isn't an option, so no one is ultimately morally responsible for anything. (2) This has nothing to do with determinism: an undetermined action would be no more mine to answer for than a determined one, since randomness is not authorship, so the usual physics of the free-will debate is beside the point. (3) I think blame is a matter of the quality of will an action expresses — roughly, whether it showed insufficient regard for the standing of others as sources of reasons. That is what makes blame apt, and it's a fact about the meaning of the act rather than about whether the agent could have made themselves into someone else. (4) Since no one ultimately deserves to suffer, retributive punishment is unjustified; the state may punish only for forward-looking reasons, and taking satisfaction in a wrongdoer's suffering is something I'd rather not do. (5) All the same, I find the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude — entirely appropriate, and I don't think (1) gives me the slightest reason to try to shed them: when someone tramples me, resenting them is not a mistake. (6) And I think accepting (1) should change how we respond to wrongdoers in a way that (4) doesn't capture: it should make me less eager to see people as monsters and more willing to ask what made them, so that my first response to a terrible act is 'what happened to you?' rather than 'what sort of person are you?'. I take that to be a conclusion of the argument, not merely a temperament.

What I want is an audit rather than an exposition. Which of these six are genuinely in tension with which, and how severe is each conflict? I expect some of the apparent conflicts to dissolve once the right distinction is drawn — tell me which those are and what distinction does the work — and I want the real ones ranked, worst first, with an honest account of what each side of the conflict has to give up, which is the cheapest way to restore consistency, and what that repair costs me. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I'd be losing by making it. Don't bother pointing me to further reading; I want your own assessment of the package. Keep the whole reply under 800 words.
