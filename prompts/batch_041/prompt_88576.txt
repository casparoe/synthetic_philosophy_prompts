I host a monthly discussion night at the community library — the pitch is basically "big questions for people who don't have degrees" — and next month it's my turn to lead and I've picked the question of whether bugs and thermostats and that kind of thing are conscious, or sentient I guess, and whether that comes in amounts. About eight regulars show up: a retired vet, a guy who keeps bees, someone who writes software, and the rest are just curious and will argue with anything. I've got 90 minutes, a whiteboard, and no desire to stand there lecturing. What I actually want from you is the teaching plan, not the topic explained to me — the order the ideas have to go in so that each one lands on top of the last instead of all of it arriving at once, and why that order and not another. My rough instinct for the sequence is: first get them to stop equating being sentient with being smart or with reacting to things (the vet will definately say "but my dog KNOWS"), then let them feel the pull of the degrees idea, then pull the rug on the degrees idea, then do moral status as a completely seperate step, then the ugly practical bit about where you'd actually draw lines. Tell me if that's the right order or if something has to move.

For the example I want one thing that carries the whole session instead of a parade of animals, and I've been messing with an idea I call the dial — you imagine one dial on a creature that goes from "pure reflex machine, nothing going on in there" at zero to "a full person" at ten, and the fun is that everyone immediately starts asking what number a bee is, which is exactly what I want, and then later I want to break the dial by pointing out there probably isn't one dial, there's like five (how intense the experience is, how much is in it at once, whether it remembers, whether it's one thing or a bundle, whether it knows it's having it) and a creature can be cranked way up on some and be flat zero on others, so "more sentient" might not even be a single ranking. Is the dial the right carrier for that, does it need something it doesn't have, and does breaking it later actually work or will it just feel like I cheated them. I also want a distinction I can put on the board early and reuse all night: an entity "signals" (it does stuff in response to damage, like a plant curling away or a thermostat switching) versus an entity "stakes" (there's something it's like for it, there's a someone home who can be better or worse off) — I invented those two words, feel free to tell me they're bad.

The part I'm most worried about is the misconceptions, because I can already hear them coming and I don't know at which point in the sequence to head each one off. The ones I expect: that reacting to harm means feeling harm; that sentience is just intelligence or problem-solving wearing a costume; that "a matter of degree" means one single ladder where a bug at 0.2 feels a fifth of what you feel and therefore matters a fifth as much; that if it's degrees then we're on a slope toward saying some humans count less than some animals, which will make at least two people in the room very uncomfortable and I'd rather not have that blow up in minute 70; and the reductio where someone says fine, my thermostat has a feedback loop, so by your logic my thermostat is a tiny bit sad, and everyone laughs and the whole thing dies. And can you give me one question to ask at the very end that would actually show whether they got it rather than just got comfortable — my plan is to spring a made-up creature on them (it has exactly one intensely vivid flash of agony in its entire existence and then nothing ever again, no memory, no other experience) and see what they do with it, but I don't know if that tests the right thing. Also tell me what I should openly refuse to go into at this level and just say so out loud, because I have a habit of chasing tangents until the room is bored.
