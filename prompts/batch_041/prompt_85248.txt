For the past year a colleague and I have been arguing about longtermism, and we have agreed to put in writing exactly where we stand. He is a strong longtermist: he holds that improving the very long-term future — the world thousands and millions of years from now — is the top moral priority by a wide margin, so that even a very small probability of a very large effect on that future outweighs spending the same resources on present-day problems. I take the opposing view: that we should direct most of our effort to near- and medium-term welfare, because our estimates of far-future effects are too unreliable and rest on too many contested assumptions to bear that much weight. We both work as software engineers; neither of us has a philosophy background, and we would rather the document stand on its own than lean on names and papers, so please do not cite literature or attribute views to authors — keep it to claims, arguments, and cases.

Draft the joint statement we could both sign. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims we both accept — about the structure of our disagreement, about which premises are empirical and which are moral, and about what would count as relevant evidence. Nothing here should be a concession in disguise for either of us, and nothing should be so vague that it does no work.

2. Our remaining disagreements, each stated in the precise form that the side holding it would accept, rather than a paraphrase the other side would use to score a point. Where a disagreement is really about how a word is being used — 'important', 'priority', 'negligible' — say so, and give the formulation that removes the verbal element.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would genuinely move at least one of us. For each, say which of us would move and in which direction.

4. For each crux, a test, case, or argument we would both agree bears on it, with both our predictions recorded in advance. Make at least these two concrete. First, a decision-theoretic one: consider an intervention that, on its best estimate, has a one-in-a-million chance of preventing a permanent collapse of civilization and otherwise does nothing; we should agree on how to compare one dollar given to it against one dollar given to a proven near-term health intervention, and then each write down the probability at which we would stop funding it, with our reasoning. Second, an epistemic one: what a strong longtermist would have to learn — about our capacity to forecast far-future consequences, or about how sensitive the far future is to present actions — before the position collapses.

5. A closing section on what each of us would need to be shown in order to change our mind, and whether there is anything we both agree would actually settle the matter.

Write it as a document we would both put our names to, in neutral prose, with the agreed claims numbered and the disagreements set out in a table. Begin directly with the document itself — no preface and no restating of this request.
