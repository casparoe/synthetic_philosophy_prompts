Retired biologist here — forty years of putting organisms into boxes, and for the last two I've been reading philosophy of psychiatry at night. I've got an argument that's been gnawing at me and I cant figure out where it breaks. Maybe I built it myself, maybe I read it somewhere:

(1) A classification is objective — latches onto something really there rather than onto our convenience — only if the things classified would have been the same whether or not we'd drawn the distinction.
(2) Psychiatric categories are interactive in Hacking's sense. Call someone depressed, or autistic, or an addict, and it changes what they do, what they notice in themselves, what they turn into. The label loops back into the labelled.
(3) So those people would not have been the same had we never drawn the line.
(4) So the categories don't latch onto anything really there; they're artifacts of our classifying.
(5) But the same move works for gifted, adolescent, criminal, woman, introvert — anything a person can know they're in and take up.
(6) So no classification of people latches onto anything real, and objectivity in the human sciences is a lost cause.
(7) That's plainly too much. Whatever depression is, it isn't only a word we agreed to use.

Which line do I cut? Give me a definite answer, not a survey of options — name the premise or step you'd drop, then be honest about what else falls with it, because I'd rather give up something small than something load-bearing. And whatever you cut, tell me what should stand in its place — I'll be wanting a criterion of objectivity either way.
