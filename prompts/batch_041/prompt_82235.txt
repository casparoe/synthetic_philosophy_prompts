I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book whose protagonist is a professional forecaster — thinks in percentages, keeps a spreadsheet of every prediction she's ever made, gets genuinely upset about being underconfident — and to keep her from sounding like a Wikipedia article I've spent the last year reading philosophy of probability stuff, mostly blogs and seminar recordings and the odd paper a friend sends me. Side effect: I've absorbed a position. Now I'm worried it's a mess. Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) Accuracy, meaning how close my credences sit to the truth, is the only epistemic good there is; whatever else we praise as epistemic virtue is good because it tends to get us closer to the truth. (2) Belief comes in degrees, and the right way to grade a set of credences is a proper scoring rule, Brier score being the obvious one. (3) To be rational just is to maximize expected accuracy from your own point of view — that's what's wrong with incoherent credences, since they're accuracy-dominated by their own cleaned-up counterpart, and with immodest ones. (4) But the thing I actually admire, the gold standard, is calibration: a forecaster whose 70% calls come true 70% of the time. That's what doing this well looks like, and it's what I want my character to be. (5) Coherence obviously isnt enough — a man with a perfectly self-consistent conspiracy theory, or someone who is 0.99 sure of something on no evidence whatsoever, is being irrational, and I'd say that even though every one of his credences is internally tidy. (6) I also think there's a fact of the matter about what the evidence supports, and that rationality is a matter of proportioning your credence to it. And (7) — this one I think is fine, but check me — a credence can be rational even when it turns out to be badly off; being reasonable and being right are seperate things.

I can feel a couple of these grinding against each other but I can never pin down which, and every time I try to lay it out I end up concluding the whole package is fine, which is itself suspicious. So: audit it for me. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and how severe is each one. For the real ones, tell me what the cheapest fix is and what it costs me — I want to know what I'd be giving up, not just which sentence to delete. Then, and this is the part I actually care about: if I had to drop or revise one thing to get the rest to hang together, which one, and what's the best remaining version of my view? Rank the tensions by severity rather than telling me everything is in tension to some degree.
