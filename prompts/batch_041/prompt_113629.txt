I am an undergraduate in a second-year course on rational choice and value theory, and I am trying to determine how much of a popular treatment of the spectrum argument I can trust. The trade book assigned as background reading — a general book on rationality, not a specialist monograph — presents the argument more or less as follows.

"Transitivity is the most basic requirement on any ranking of options. If you prefer A to B and you prefer B to C, you must prefer A to C, on pain of incoherence: an agent with a cyclic ranking can be milked by a money pump, and the whole apparatus of rational choice theory — utility functions, expected utility maximization, cost-benefit analysis — presupposes that the ranking is transitive. Now take a spectrum of possible worlds, beginning with a world containing a single person who lives an extraordinarily good life and ending with a world containing an astronomically large number of people whose lives are just barely worth living, where each intermediate world differs from its neighbours by a step so small that the earlier world is at least as good as the later. By transitivity, the first world must be at least as good as the last. But the last is obviously worse. So transitivity fails: 'better than' is not a transitive relation. Note that this leaves 'at least as good as' untouched; the weak relation can remain perfectly transitive even when the strict relation cycles. The upshot is that our ordinary thinking about value is simply inconsistent, and any theory that tries to systematize it — rational choice theory included — is a useful fiction at best."

In seminar we have been working with a concrete version of the chain: worlds W1 through W12, where each W(k+1) contains ten times as many people as W(k) and every life is lowered by one small notch, W1 being a single person with an enormously rich life and W12 being a hundred billion people whose lives contain just enough good to be worth living. Our instructor's working position is that no step in the chain makes things worse, and that W12 is plainly much worse than W1; she also uses the conventions that "at least as good as" is complete, so that of any two worlds one is at least as good as the other, and that if X is better than Y and Y is at least as good as Z then X is better than Z.

I would like a claim-by-claim audit that I can use to decide how much of the paragraph to keep: what someone working on this problem would accept as stated, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong, with the premise each verdict turns on made explicit. Three points I would particularly like settled. First, the aside that "at least as good as" survives intact even if "better than" cycles: given the conventions above, is that correct, and if it is not, what does that mean for the shape of the conclusion the argument can support? Second, the money-pump sentence: does exploitability establish that a cyclic ranking is incoherent, or something weaker, and does anything about the spectrum argument follow from it either way? Third, the closing moral: even granting that the twelve judgments about the chain cannot all be true together, does it follow that our ordinary thinking is inconsistent, or that rational choice theory is a fiction? My own suspicion is that the last sentence claims more than the argument can deliver, but I cannot tell whether the overreach lies in the moral or in the conclusion itself. Finish by telling me what is left standing once the paragraph is corrected: the strongest version of the conclusion the argument actually supports, stated so that it does not depend on any of the book's overstatements.
