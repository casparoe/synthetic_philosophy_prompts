I am a doctoral student in public policy who has been sitting in on a philosophy department reading group on welfare and social choice, and I have become fixated on an argumentative move that I keep encountering and cannot name. It is easiest to see in two contrasting instances. In the first, someone objects to a cost-benefit analysis by insisting that you simply cannot compare one person's suffering with another's, because there is no common yardstick; the room nods along, and yet the same speaker, ten minutes later, will say without hesitation that a person in agony is worse off than a person who is mildly annoyed, and no one treats this as a retraction. In the second instance, a different speaker defends interpersonal comparison by pointing out that we make such judgments constantly and usually agree about them, and then, without any further argument, treats this as having established that there is a single scale on which all these amounts can be placed and summed. What strikes me is that in both cases a modest claim is quietly exchanged for a much stronger one, and that the exchange runs in opposite directions: in the first, an inability to locate a common unit is treated as settling that there is no fact of the matter about who is worse off; in the second, an ability to rank people in easy cases is treated as settling that there is a common unit. I suspect these are in fact several distinct claims — that there is no fact about the ranking, that we cannot reliably know the ranking, that there is no shared unit, that there is no shared zero point, that the resulting numbers cannot be meaningfully added — and that much of the heat in these debates comes from parties who have collapsed them into one another. I also suspect the collapse is not innocent, because the practical question (may we weigh one person's gain against another's loss, and by how much?) seems to require only some of these claims and not others.

I would like you to act as a philosopher of economics and social choice and help me carve this up. If there is, name it and state precisely which of the distinct claims above it actually covers; if there is not, coin a term, define it, and give me boundary cases showing what falls inside and outside it. In either case, I want the carving more than the label: lay out the distinct claims explicitly, tell me which ones entail which and which are independent, and identify which one (or ones) the practical aggregation question really turns on. Compare your answer to the nearest established concepts you can think of — they may come from measurement theory, welfare economics, or elsewhere — and for each say exactly how it differs from the phenomenon I have described. Do not bother with a reading list or citations; I cannot look anything up right now and want the conceptual work done in front of me.
