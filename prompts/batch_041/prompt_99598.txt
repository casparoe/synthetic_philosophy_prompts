Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about Marx on technology and automation, and he left me with a chain of reasoning that I can neither refute nor accept. He says it is Marx's own view made sharp, and that it shows the labor theory of value is either inconsistent or politically empty. Here it is, numbered as he wrote it out:

(1) For Marx, the value of a commodity is the socially necessary labor time required to produce it.
(2) Machinery, unlike living labor, creates no new value; it transfers to the product only the value it loses through wear.
(3) Capitalist automation replaces living labor with machinery, so the share of output value created by living labor falls as automation advances.
(4) Profit is surplus value, and surplus value is produced only by living labor.
(5) So as automation advances, surplus value falls relative to the mass of output, and at full automation it is zero.
(6) Capital accumulates only by converting surplus value into new capital.
(7) Therefore full automation is impossible under capitalism: capital wants to automate, but complete automation would abolish its own source of profit.
(8) What we actually observe is that capital automates only enough to cheapen labor power and intensify the labor that remains, so workers bear the costs while the machine never delivers the emancipation Marx promised.
(9) His additional step: if a machine creates no value, then the value of a machine is just the labor that made it, and the value of the goods it produces is that labor divided by the machine's output. The more durable and productive the machine, the less value per good. So the most productive economies should have the least total value and the least profit. Since they have the most profit, either value is not labor time or profit does not come from surplus value.
(10) Hence Marx's theory either predicts that automation cannot happen, which is false, or that automation must happen and free everyone, which is also false.
(11) So we should reject the labor theory of value, or at least the claim that machines create no value.

I tried to block the argument with a distinction between the labor embodied in the machine as a stock and the labor displaced by the machine as a flow: the machine is dead labor, but dead labor was once living labor, so automation may only move labor backward along the production chain rather than abolish it. He dismissed that as a shell game, because the whole point of a machine is that it does more work than the labor that made it; if it did not, no capitalist would buy it. I also said value is a social relation rather than an engineering quantity, so the limit case of a fully automated economy may be incoherent rather than a prediction. He said that is just refusing to let the theory be tested.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep (1), (2), and (4); I am less attached to (7) and (8). Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked plausible, and what rejecting it costs. If the argument is valid and the real question is which plausible premise is least costly to give up, say that too. Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
