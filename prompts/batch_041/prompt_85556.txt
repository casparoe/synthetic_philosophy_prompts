I spent thirty-five years as a field ornithologist — mist nets, nest boxes, a long-term study plot I have since handed to a former student — and in all that time I never once had to decide what a unit of selection was; the birds simply went on being birds. Retirement has given me the leisure to catch up on the theory I skipped, and I have fallen in with a philosophy of biology reading group that meets above a bookshop on Thursday evenings. The argument I cannot leave alone is the old quarrel over the units of selection — gene against organism against group, each camp periodically announcing that it has quietly annexed the others. What exasperates me is not the science, which I take to be largely settled, but the rhetoric: the habit, in the popular literature and in the textbooks alike, of crowning one level as the genuine agent and demoting the rest to façades, so that an entire organism becomes a mere vehicle for something smaller and an entire population becomes a mere statistic. I should like a piece of sustained satire that takes that habit literally and follows it wherever it goes.

Please write it as the opening counts of an in-universe legal complaint: a defamation action brought in a fictional court by an organism against its own genes, with their publicists named as co-defendants, over the persistent characterisation of the plaintiff as a vehicle, a survival machine, a temporary coalition, and worse. The register should be deadpan, the way a real pleading is deadpan, and the comedy must come out of the biology and the metaphysics rather than out of whimsy — each count should turn on a genuine move in the debate (the replicator/interactor distinction; the charge that gene-level accounting is merely a change of bookkeeping; the puzzle of genetic elements that cheat their own genome; the question of what kin selection and group selection actually partition) — so that a specialist recognises an author who has done the reading, while a reader who has never heard of the debate can still follow the joke and tell that it is one. No real scientists are to appear as parties, and the plaintiff should come across as sympathetically aggrieved: the reader ought to feel, absurdly, that the complaint is just. Around 250 to 350 words in all.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered counts, each one a sentence or two, with no preamble, no caption, no prayer for relief, and no closing paragraph.
