I am grading the final exam for an undergraduate course in the philosophy of mind and would like your help assessing one student's answer.

The exam question read:

"Some philosophers hold that emotions are feelings, others that they are judgments, and others that they are perceptions. Consider the following case. After an injury leaves her with almost no awareness of her own bodily state, a patient reports that she still sometimes feels afraid and acts accordingly — she recoils and avoids — but describes the fear as faint, 'without its usual bodily charge.' Assess how each of the three families of views accounts for this case. In doing so, distinguish the claim that emotions have representational content from the claim that they are propositional, and say whether the case supports the conclusion that emotions are judgments rather than feelings."

The student's answer was:

"The three families disagree about what an emotion fundamentally is. Feeling theories, following William James, identify an emotion with the perception of bodily changes: we do not tremble because we are afraid so much as we are afraid because we tremble. Judgment theories, such as Solomon's, hold that an emotion is an evaluative judgment about one's situation — fear, for instance, being the judgment that one is in danger. Perceptual theories hold that an emotion is a perception, either of a bodily state or of an evaluative property.

The patient's case bears most directly on the feeling theory. If an emotion just is the awareness of bodily change, then a patient with almost no awareness of her body should have almost no emotions. Yet she still reports fear and still behaves fearfully. The case therefore refutes the feeling theory: the emotion survives the disappearance of the bodily feeling that was supposed to constitute it.

That leaves a judgment theory or a perceptual theory. On the judgment theory, the patient's fear is simply the judgment that she is in danger, and judgments need not be accompanied by any particular bodily state, so the case is unproblematic. On the perceptual theory, the fear is a perception, and perceptions may be faint or degraded, so the pale quality of her fear is exactly what we should expect.

The case thus supports the conclusion that emotions are judgments rather than feelings. It also shows that emotions cannot be assessed for rationality the way beliefs can: if fear is a perception, then it is just a way the world appears to us, and appearances are not the sort of thing that can be right or wrong. This is why the patient's fear remains intelligible even after it loses its usual bodily charge.

The only question left is whether the perceptual or the judgmental reading is correct. Since both treat emotions as having representational content, and representation requires propositional content, both make emotions a species of judgment; the disagreement between them is therefore merely verbal."

Please assign the answer a grade — a letter or a percentage, whichever you prefer — and provide the specific comments that justify it, addressing each of the question's demands in turn rather than offering a general impression.
