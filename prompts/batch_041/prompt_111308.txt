I am an MA student on a course covering the evolution of norms and conventions, and in a fortnight I have to present my end-of-term paper to the weekly reading group at our centre for social and behavioural research. The room is mostly evolutionary game theorists and behavioural economists, who take it that the interesting question about a norm is why following it is stable; alongside them sit a metaethicist and a philosopher of social science who knows the conventions literature far better than I do. There are also two or three students from my own course who will mostly stay quiet.

My thesis, briefly, so that you can aim the objections at something specific. On the account I am working with, a regularity in behaviour counts as a convention when nearly everyone conforms to it, nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to conform, nearly everyone prefers to conform provided the others do, and there is a rival regularity that would have served just as well, all of this being common knowledge. I argue that this buys you predictable behaviour but not demanded behaviour, and that two features of norms outrun it: the standing to criticise, which extends to third parties who were never party to the interaction, and the survival of the demand after the coordination problem that produced it has disappeared.

I make the point with a pair of examples. In a building with one lift, everyone presses the button when they are nearest to it, everyone expects this of everyone else and prefers it, and an alternative arrangement would have worked equally well — yet a visitor who presses it out of turn is not criticised, and nobody supposes they have wronged anyone. By contrast, giving up one's seat to someone who needs it has the same arbitrary coordination structure, but a failure here draws resentment and criticism from bystanders. What the second case adds, I claim, is a distributed readiness to criticise and to treat criticism as warranted — a criticism surplus, if you will — which is not the same thing as a preference for conforming. Evolutionary accounts can model the criticism as costly punishment, but I say they get the direction of explanation backwards: a punisher's payoffs explain why punishing persists, not why the criticism is warranted, and they predict that criticism will fade once punishment becomes cheap to ignore, which it does not.

What I want from you is a question-and-answer preparation sheet, not a defence of my view. Tell me the objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, phrased as these people would phrase them and in their vocabulary; for each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is thinnest. Flag whatever I should concede at the outset rather than fight, and include the question I am most afraid of, with a short account of why I should be. I do not need citations or a reading list — I need to know what will be said to me and what I will say back.
