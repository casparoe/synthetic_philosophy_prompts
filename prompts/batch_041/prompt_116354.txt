Next month I am giving a colloquium paper defending a version of dialetheism that I describe in the paper as non-adjunctive. The thesis: some sentences are both true and false, but truth is not closed under conjunction — an agent can accept A and accept not-A without accepting their conjunction, so adjunction, the rule that lets you infer a conjunction from its two conjuncts, is not valid, and neither is disjunctive syllogism, which is how I block explosion. My motivation is a naive truth predicate, with the T-schema holding without restriction, together with unrestricted comprehension; I want to say that the contradictions these generate are true, and to deny that accepting them commits me to accepting everything. The paper makes two moves: first, a semantics on which a sentence and its negation can each take a designated value, with validity understood as preservation of designatedness; second, a proof-theoretic account on which the two halves of a contradiction are separately assertible even though their conjunction is not.

The audience is a logic and philosophy of language colloquium, and I know roughly who will be there: two classical model theorists who work on set theory, who treat logic as classical first-order logic with a Tarskian semantics; a relevance logician who holds that paraconsistency should come from a relevant conditional rather than from tinkering with conjunction and disjunction, and who regards my conditional as a defect rather than a feature; two philosophers of language who work on negation and the semantic paradoxes and will want to know what my negation means and what the T-schema does for conjunctions; and several graduate students who will ask what the point is. They will be arguing with each other as much as with me.
