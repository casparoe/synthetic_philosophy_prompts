I hold a version of luck egalitarianism and want an honest map of its exposure — not a survey of the literature, but a ranked account of what would actually count against the view as I state it. The version is this. Each person is owed an equal initial share of resources; departures from equality are justified only insofar as they track the parties' own responsible choices; and the claim of those who have chosen badly is neither unlimited nor extinguished but fixed by an insurance device. Behind a veil, each person is imagined to purchase insurance against the consequences of her own future imprudence, priced at her own actuarial risk, the premium deducted from her initial share; the payout on that policy is the floor below which no one is entitled to fall. The imprudent person's claim on the rest of us is the payout, and nothing more. The point of the device is to answer the charge that luck egalitarianism abandons the imprudent: it does not abandon them, it pays out on a policy they are presumed to have wanted.

What I want from you is the view's risk profile. Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of them a committed luck egalitarian would herself concede as a genuine cost, which she would deny, and where the denial is itself a cost. In working through this, I want at least these addressed. First, the premium regress: if actuarial risk tracks unchosen dispositions, upbringing, and deliberative capacity, does pricing the policy at individual risk reintroduce the very brute luck the theory exists to neutralize, so that the device is either unfair or empty? Second, reflexivity: the policy is public and priced against one's own conduct, so the insured person's behavior responds to the coverage — the actuarial price of imprudence is undefined when imprudence is itself a function of being insured. Is that a fixable technicality or a collapse of the device's coherence? Third, the paired cases in which an imprudent person and an unfortunate one end up identically placed; state the view's verdict, then say whether the verdict is a consequence I can defend or one I can only bite. Fourth, leveling down, and whether it is a real objection or a rhetorical one. Fifth, whether the view, once the insurance is made realistic, quietly becomes a different view — prioritarianism, a sufficiency floor, or relational egalitarianism — and what would be lost if it did.

I also want to know what this version predicts that its rivals do not: which empirical findings about the social correlates of imprudence would embarrass it rather than merely complicate it, and what it commits me to that a critic could test. Then assess the insulation question squarely. The veil and the premium are free parameters; does that let the view absorb any counterexample by redrawing the policy, and if so, is that a defect or simply the ordinary fate of an ideal-theoretic device? The consideration I would personally find hardest to answer is any argument showing that the premium itself cannot avoid tracking brute luck. Close with your own verdict: which single defeater, if it survived scrutiny, should make me abandon the view, and whether the version I have described risks anything at all.
