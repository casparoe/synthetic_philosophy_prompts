I teach the graduate decision-theory seminar in our department, and I am assembling the take-home final. I want to include a single essay question on the money-pump tradition and diachronic rationality, and I need it to separate students who actually understand what a pump is doing from students who can only reproduce the textbook three-good cycle and intone that intransitive preferences are irrational. The standard material invites recitation, so I would like the question built around a move that is not part of the standard telling. Here is the hook: a student in last year's class claimed that every money pump can be "collapsed" into a single one-shot choice — rather than offering the agent a sequence of trades, one simply offers her the package "hand me three dollars and I will cycle your holdings back to where they started," which no rational agent would accept; and since the sequential version is just that package chopped into stages, the pump allegedly shows nothing about diachronic rationality and reduces to a claim about the coherence of the agent's preferences at a single time. I want the question to force students to adjudicate this collapse claim, and in doing so to keep three things apart: the bare fact that an agent can be made to pay for nothing; the claim that her preferences are incoherent; and the claim that she violates a genuinely diachronic requirement governing how her attitudes must hang together across time. They should also have to say what work is done by the two familiar replies — the "sophisticated" chooser, who predicts her own future choices and picks now what is best given that prediction, and the "resolute" chooser, who adopts a plan and refuses to be deflected from it — and whether either reply rescues intransitive preferences or merely shows that exploitability and irrationality can come apart.

Please draft one essay question for the final — it may be technically demanding, since the students will already have done the reading on Dutch books and diachronic consistency — together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme. Keep the model answer self-contained: it should carry its argument on the strength of the reasoning alone, without leaning on names, dates, or particular publications, because I cannot count on students to recall those and do not want the answer to hinge on them. The marking scheme should total 20 marks and be built around five must-hit points, each with the marks it is worth and a one-line note on the specific error that typically costs students those marks; I would rather each note name the actual mistake (for instance, conflating "this agent can be exploited" with "this agent's preferences are irrational") than gesture vaguely at insufficient depth. The question should end by requiring a defended verdict rather than a survey: students should come down on whether diachronic money-pump arguments establish a requirement that is genuinely diachronic or whether they collapse into a synchronic coherence requirement, and defend that verdict against the strongest version of the opposing view.
