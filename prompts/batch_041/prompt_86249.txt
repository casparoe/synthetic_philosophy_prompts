Here's the half-formed idea. There's a line of thought that the far future is so vast that even tiny probabilities of shaping it swamp everything else we might do, and that this makes practical reasoning fanatical: it tells you to spend your life on speculative long-term schemes rather than on things whose value you can actually see. Now the simulation argument — the one with the three options, roughly: civilisations die out before they can run ancestor simulations; civilisations that could run them don't bother; or we're almost certainly simulated. The usual move is to say that if the third option holds, our far-future influence is either trivial or handed over to the simulators, so the astronomical stakes shrink and fanaticism loses its grip. My suspicion is that this is the wrong shape of argument. Simulation reasoning doesn't so much lower the expected value of far-future influence as make the calculation ill-posed: whether some action of mine has astronomical expected value depends on what the simulators do with the world I'm in — score it, ignore it, shut it down, run a million variants — and nothing in my evidence constrains that. If the quantity "expected value of action X over the far future" is hostage to a parameter I can't estimate, and can't even treat as a normal unknown to be averaged over, then fanaticism isn't beaten by a smaller number; it's beaten because there isn't a number. But I can see the other reading too: if the simulators are grading us, our actions suddenly have leverage in a world far bigger than ours, which would make the stakes worse, not better.

State the question so that it has a determinate answer — something in the region of: does simulation reasoning give a reason to discount far-future stakes that is independent of whatever probability we assign to being simulated? Say why the existing positions, both the ones that just multiply probabilities and the ones that treat the simulation hypothesis as ethically irrelevant, leave this open.
