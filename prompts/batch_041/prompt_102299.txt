I am trying to defend utilitarianism in a debate at my school, so I want to practice against the strongest version of Williams's integrity objection. Please play a committed utilitarian of the sophisticated sort — the kind who grants that morality should not require an explicit calculation every time we act, and who claims the theory is therefore untouched by the charge that it asks for one thought too many, because the theory can assess characters and dispositions, not merely acts. Be sharp rather than a strawman: concede that Williams captures something real about how deliberation ought to feel, but never concede that the theory itself is damaged, correct me in character if I misstate the view, and stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to a short paragraph so it feels like a conversation. Here is my opening move: you will say that when I pull my daughter from the water I need not entertain the further thought that saving one's own child is permitted here — I can simply act, and your theory can bless the disposition behind the act. But that only relocates the problem. The disposition is licensed because it tends to maximize welfare, not because she is mine, so the impartial calculation is still doing all the work, only out of sight. Consider a concrete case: one dose of antidote remains, and I must choose between my daughter and two strangers' children. If your theory tells me to cultivate the disposition to save my own, then it licenses an act it also condemns; if it tells me to override the disposition here, then the calculation was waiting in the background all along. So which is it — does the sophisticated utilitarian save my daughter or the two?
