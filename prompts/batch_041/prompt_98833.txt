There is a habit in financial journalism and in the reporting of prediction and betting markets of treating a market price as if it were a probability: when a contract that pays a dollar if some event happens trades at thirty cents, the headline says the chance of the event is thirty percent. Running alongside this is a norm among the people who trade and forecast: if you hold a strong view that differs from the price and you have no private information the market lacks, then you are the one who is wrong, and the sensible thing is to move your own estimate toward the price. The same thought turns up outside markets whenever someone insists that two well-informed people who have looked at the same evidence simply cannot go on disagreeing in good faith — that at least one of them must have slipped somewhere in their reasoning.

I have a feeling that this entire way of talking rests on an assumption that almost nobody states out loud, and I want to know what it is. My guess is that it assumes the traders and forecasters all began from the same starting beliefs, so that the only thing that can separate them is the information each happens to be holding, and that once that information is shared their opinions have to meet. Is that the commitment the practice actually needs? Or could the practice get by on something weaker — say, the mere expectation that most disagreements trace back to information rather than to different starting points — without ever claiming that everyone's initial beliefs are alike?

I would like you to do three things. First, reconstruct the assumption that is really doing the work and say plainly what it is and what it is not. Second, separate the parts of the practice that genuinely need it from the parts that would stand without it, and tell me in particular whether the claim that people who share evidence cannot keep disagreeing follows from the assumption on its own or requires something further. Third, tell me what changes if we set the assumption aside: if people can hold starting beliefs that no amount of evidence settles, is a market price still anybody's probability at all, or is it something else — a summary of the distribution of opinions, the view of whichever trader happens to be setting the margin — and is the norm that the dissenter must be wrong still defensible?

There is a puzzle here that I cannot resolve by myself. I have read that under this assumption two people who start from the same beliefs, who are both rational, and who each know the other's opinion cannot end up disagreeing; and, more oddly, that they cannot even have a reason to bet against one another over a difference of opinion alone, because each would read the other's willingness to bet as evidence against his own view. Yet a market is nothing but people betting against each other, and well-informed people disagree all the time. So either the assumption is false, or it is being used loosely in the practice, or something else is going on that I am not seeing. Tell me which it is, and tell me what this practice is genuinely committed to once we are honest about it.
