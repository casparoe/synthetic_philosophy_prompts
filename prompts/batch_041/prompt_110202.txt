I'm a PhD student working on metaphysics and the philosophy of language, and a colleague in the linguistics department has asked me to take a single seventy-five-minute session of her graduate seminar on compositionality — the last slot of the day, so they will be tired. The students are capable: they are at home with Frege's distinction between concepts and objects, with typed semantics, and with the thought that the meaning of a complex is determined by its parts together with their mode of combination. What they have never met is the metaphysical problem that the composition principle is usually taken to answer, and my brief is to give them the regress that goes by Bradley's name and to leave them with a clear sense of what that regress is a problem for. (I know the territory is usually mapped under the heading of the unity of the proposition and that there is a contemporary literature of positions on it, but I do not want the session to become a tour of positions; the point is the problem.) What I want from you is not an explanation of the regress but a plan for the explanation. Give me the order in which the pieces have to come and why each depends on the one before it. My own suspicion is that nothing works until they have been made to feel the difference between a heap and a fact, and that the regress is inert for them until they have been made to want a unifier — so that it must arrive as the failure of a demand they have already accepted, rather than as a puzzle about relations. Tell me whether that is the right spine or whether I have the dependency backwards, and where in the sequence the demand itself should be made explicit.

Second, I need the one example that should carry the whole session, and the conditions it has to satisfy. My candidates are these. First, "a is to the left of b," which I distrust, because it makes the regress look like a trick about spatial relations and invites them to think the remedy is to find a better relation. Second, the letters c-a-t and the word "cat," which has the advantage that they already believe composition happens there, but the disadvantage that they will cheerfully say the unity is conventional and thereby deflate the very tension I need them to feel. Third, since it is their seminar, something from their own shop — a functor and its argument, where combination is an explicit, rule-governed operation — which may help them feel the pull of the question but may equally hand them a syntactic answer to what is meant to be a metaphysical question. Tell me which of these to build on, or whether I need a fourth kind of case altogether, and state what an example has to have and to lack: I take it that the relata must be genuinely independent of one another and the unity must not be merely stipulated, but I want to know what else is required, and what disqualifies a case.

Third, the misconceptions, and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. I expect "so relations do not exist" — the regress read as an argument for a sparse ontology rather than as an argument against a particular account of what it is for a relation to be real. I expect a constant slide between the semantic version of the problem (why is a sentence more than a list of its words?) and the ontological version (why is a's standing in R to b more than a, b, and R?), and I want to know precisely where I can pin the difference down, since I suspect that most of their confusion will live in that slide rather than in the regress itself. I expect "infinite regress, so nothing is ever related," and its cousin, the student who thinks the objection is that the steps would take forever — treating viciousness as a matter of how many steps there are rather than of what each step presupposes. And I expect the confident one who says this is only the third man, or only Zeno, or who has heard that Russell tidied it all up and wants to move on. Then give me one question to put to them at the end that would show whether they have the shape of the problem rather than the steps — something that would reveal whether they can say what would have to be true for the regress to be benign. Finally, what should I refuse to explain at this level, and how should I say so out loud: I mean to keep Bradley's own monist conclusion and the idealist machinery off the table, and I would rather not open the identity theory of truth, but tell me if the plan is dishonest without either, and what I should tell them I am deferring.
