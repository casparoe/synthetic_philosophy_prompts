I teach a second-year epistemology course, and each year I devote a session to cases in which a subject appears to earn a substantive epistemic achievement — knowledge that one of her own faculties is reliable — by an inference that costs her nothing. This year a student in the seminar, working from a case she devised herself, produced an argument that our assigned reading had only gestured toward, and I find that after a week of staring at it I cannot decide whether to grade it as a genuine discovery or as an ingenious fallacy. I have set it out below as she wrote it, keeping her example, a bathroom scale.

(1) A true belief formed by a reliable process is knowledge even if the subject has no independent reason to think the process reliable. (2) On each of thirty consecutive mornings I step on my bathroom scale and form the belief that I weigh such-and-such a weight, and on each of the thirty occasions the belief is true. (3) So on each of the thirty mornings I know what I weigh. (4) So, for each of the thirty readings, I know that the reading was accurate. (5) The thirty readings are independent of one another: nothing about the accuracy of the first bears on the accuracy of the second, since each is a fresh measurement. (6) So I know thirty independent facts, each of which is evidence that the scale is accurate. (7) When one knows a body of evidence, and that evidence makes a hypothesis overwhelmingly likely, one knows the hypothesis. (8) So I know that my scale is reliable. (9) But no one can come to know that a device is reliable merely by trusting it; if that were possible, a man could confirm the reliability of his horoscope by rereading it, and there would be no difference between the person who has checked her scale against a standard weight and the person who has only ever consulted it. (10) So (1) is false: knowing that p requires knowing that one's source for p is reliable.

She reads the argument as showing that the externalist premise (1) refutes itself, and several of her classmates agree. I am reluctant to give up (1) — I do not want to tell my students that they must first certify a faculty before they may use it — but I do not want to keep it by fiat, and I suspect she has smuggled in a step that does work she has not paid for. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent, and what rejecting it costs; I would like the accounting done separately for closure, for the independence of evidence, and for the principle that a known body of evidence transmits knowledge to what it makes likely. If the fault lies not in a premise but in the inference from one step to the next, identify the inference and say what rule it violates. And if the honest answer is that (1) can be kept only by paying with (9) — that we can simply bootstrap our way to knowledge of our sources — then say so, and tell me what becomes of the horoscope.
