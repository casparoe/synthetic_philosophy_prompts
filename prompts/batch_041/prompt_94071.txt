I am a high school student writing a long paper for a political theory course, and I would like you to act as a political philosopher who specializes in constitutional design and in the philosophy of collective decision-making. Please think carefully, and begin with a short brainstorming list of candidate correspondences and candidate disanalogies before you commit to the audit itself, so that I can see how you got there.

The thing I want to examine is an analogy I keep meeting in debates about entrenched constitutions and judicial review: that a constitution is a Ulysses contract. The story being borrowed is the Sirens episode. Ulysses wants to hear the Sirens but knows that once he hears them he will steer for the rocks, so he has himself lashed to the mast, and he has the crew's ears stopped with wax so that they cannot hear the song at all; in the translation I read, Circe tells him to "stop your men's ears with wax that none of them may hear," and she adds that if he begs to be let loose, "then they must bind you faster." The moral people draw is that a founding generation may legitimately bind later generations — make rights hard to repeal, make amendment costly, hand the last word to judges rather than to majorities — because a people is clear-headed at the founding and weak-willed afterwards, and being tied down is what saves it from itself.

What I would like from you is an audit of that analogy rather than a defense or a dismissal of it. Please give me:

- a table of correspondences, saying what plays the part of Ulysses, the crew, the ropes and the mast, the wax, the Sirens and their song, the ship, and Ithaca;
- the points where the mapping breaks, sorted into the breaks that matter for the conclusion people want to draw from the analogy and the ones that are merely decorative;
- an account of what the analogy quietly imports from the story that a political community does not have. Three things in particular seem to me to be doing work without being noticed: that Ulysses is one person with one continuing will, whereas a polity is many people whose wills conflict, so that binding yourself and binding your descendants and your opponents may not be the same act at all; that the crew is not restrained by the ropes but instructed to ignore Ulysses's own later pleas, which sounds less like self-restraint and more like one generation telling the enforcers to disregard a later generation's expressed wishes; and that the wax removes the temptation from the crew altogether instead of merely restraining them, which raises the question of whether the political analogue is a state that keeps its citizens from hearing the case for change in the first place;
- your verdict on whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, what the analogy can still legitimately be used to say;
- and, if you think there is a better analogy for entrenchment and judicial review, what it is and why it does the job better.

I would rather be told plainly that I have set this up badly — that the analogy is doing no real argumentative work, or that the audit cannot be done well unless the question is recast — than be handed a tidy answer that politely assumes my framing is fine. If you think I have mixed up the roles in the story, or that one of my three pressure points is not actually a disanalogy, say so and explain why.

End with a final line in exactly this form: \answer{...} containing a short verdict, no more than fifteen words, on whether the analogy can carry the conclusion it is used to support.
