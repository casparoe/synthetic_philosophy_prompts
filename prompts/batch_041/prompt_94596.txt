I'm a second-year PhD student in microbial ecology and I have my upgrade viva in three weeks. The thing I need to settle is, embarrassingly, a single sentence in my first-year report, on which my two supervisors have taken opposite sides. The sentence asserts that an outer-membrane protein we have been characterising in our soil isolate — call the strain SC-4, recovered from a former orchard site in Kent — has the function of exporting a class of synthetic antimicrobials, and thereby confers resistance. What makes this awkward is the history we have reconstructed. The gene looks like a duplication product that diverged under effectively neutral conditions in a small, largely clonal population; there is no evidence of any ancestral exposure to this drug class, which is in any case a twentieth-century synthetic. So the export capacity is real, it is measurable, and it demonstrably contributes to survival in the presence of the drug — but there is no selection history for that effect. Worse, the substrate on which the ancestral protein most plausibly acted is absent from the soil we sampled, so whatever effect an etiological story would nominate as 'the' function is an effect the protein never performs in the wild. My first supervisor says the capacity is an accident, at best a pre-adaptation, and that calling it a function is a category error; my second says that any capacity which contributes to the organism's survival just is a function, and that my first supervisor is smuggling in a selectionist theory of function as though it were the ordinary meaning of the word. I have to write a short section defending a line, and what I want from you is a number to anchor it rather than a survey of the literature.

Please give me your credence that the sentence is correct — that is, that the protein has the function of exporting these antimicrobials, or equivalently that 'the function of the protein is to export these antimicrobials' is a true function ascription. I want the number decomposed rather than gestured at. In particular: (a) your probability that some version of the selected-effects or etiological account is the correct account of biological function ascriptions in general, as against a Cummins-style causal-role account, as against a pluralist view on which both are legitimate and the question of which is 'right' is itself confused; (b) conditional on each of those being correct, your probability that the ascription is true in this case; (c) how much weight you give to the malfunction criterion — the thought that we attribute functions only to things that can malfunction, so that any adequate account must say what going wrong would amount to here; (d) your probability that the argument from biological practice — that knockout-and-phenotype methodology only ever reveals capacities, so that working microbiologists are causal-role theorists in practice — should count as evidence in the philosophical dispute rather than as a mere sociological observation; (e) your probability that the ascription is true relative to one explanatory project (say, predicting the evolution of resistance) and false relative to another (say, reconstructing the lineage's history), and if so, whether the question as I have posed it is thereby defective; and (f) your probability that my supervisors' disagreement is merely verbal, with no fact of the matter, so that the correct thing for my section to say is simply that.

Then combine the terms, and tell me which one the final number is most sensitive to, and what single piece of evidence or argument would move it most. A short table of terms with one line of justification each, followed by the combination and the sensitivity ranking, would suit me better than a tidy essay — I would rather have the weak points marked than the prose smoothed over. If you think the target proposition is still too loose to have a truth value, tighten it yourself, say what you changed, and give me a credence in your repaired version.
