Next month I have to give a 20-minute talk at a stratigraphy workshop and then survive 40 minutes of questions, and I need a Q&A prep sheet.

The room is basically Quaternary stratigraphers -- people who've spent whole careers on GSSPs and correlation, who think the golden-spike procedure is the only thing standing between geochronology and storytelling. There's also two archaeologists who work on the spread of farming, and one historian of science. Nobody in there is a posthumanist and nobody cares about the cultural theory stuff. The position I'm defending is this: the Anthropocene should never have been given a single golden spike, and it shouldn't be a chronostratigraphic unit at all. The human transformation of the sedimentary record is diachronous -- it shows up in different places at wildly different times, a coal district in Britian by 1800, a drained wetland in California by 1900, an Amazonian catchment in the 1970s -- so picking one globally synchronous marker (a plutonium spike, say) is a procedural fiction that gets the geology wrong. I'd rather see the whole thing recognised as a geological event with a diachronous lower boundary, dated regionally, with the rule being something like: the point after which locally deposited sediment is dominated by human-sourced material. And since the boundary would then be drawn region by region, the body that draws it should include archaeologists and historians, not just stratigraphers -- which also means the 2024 vote settling the epoch question didn't settle anything, it just moved the argument.

What I want from you is the Q&A prep: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how likely they are to come and how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way a stratigrapher would put it -- they'll be talking about isochroneity, correlatability, the precedent this sets for the base of the Holocene and the base of the Cambrian, what "dominated" is supposed to mean quantitatively, and whether an event is even the kind of thing that goes on a time scale. For each objection: the two-minute reply, and a note on where that reply is soft, hand-wavy, or likely to get me in worse trouble. Tell me what I should concede before anyone even asks, because I'd rather give the procedural points away early than have them dragged out of me. And give me the one question I'm hoping nobody in that room asks.

No citations and no reading list -- I know the literature, and I'm not going to name anybody's proposal in the talk anyway. I need the room, not the bibliography.
