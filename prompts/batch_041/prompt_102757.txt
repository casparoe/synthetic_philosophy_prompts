So I've been reading about this Fitch thing, the paradox of knowability, and I can't tell if I'm missing something or if everyone else is making alot of fuss over nothing.

Here's how I understand it: suppose every truth could in principle be known by somebody. Now take some truth like "it's raining in Cleveland right now and nobody knows it." If that whole sentence is true, then it can't be known — because to know the second half you'd have to know that nobody knows it, but if you know it, then somebody (you) does know it, so it's not true anymore. So there's a truth that can't be known, and that breaks the "every truth is knowable" idea. OK, I follow that much.

But here's my problem. The sentence I used only breaks the rule because it's got the word "know" baked into it. That feels like a cheap trick, sort of like the liar paradox — you're smuggling the conclusion into the premise by making the truth itself be about knowledge. For any normal truth that's just about the world, like "there are mountains on the moon" or "my neighbor drives a red car," there's no issue at all; in principle somebody could go find out. So isn't the paradox just a quirk about these weird self-referential-ish sentences that happen to mention knowledge? Why can't we just say the knowability rule applies to regular world-truths and not to these strange knowledge-about-knowledge ones, and then everything's fine and the paradox just goes away?

I don't get why philosophers treat this as some deep problem, especially for people who think truth is basically mind-dependent. What am I missing here?
