There is a pattern in the town where I live that I have never been able to reconcile, and I would like someone to tell me what it must be assuming. Crossing the road wherever one likes is unremarkable here: the old rule against jaywalking was abandoned some years ago, on the argument that nobody obeyed it and that it had only ever been used to move along the sort of people the authorities did not much care for. No one feels that those who cross mid-block are doing anything wrong, and no one misses the rule. At the bus stop, by contrast, there is a rule that exists nowhere in writing and is nevertheless obeyed almost perfectly: you join the back of the queue, and if you push in, strangers will say so, and everyone present, including the one who pushed in, treats the rebuke as deserved. And at the hairdresser's and in restaurants there is a convention about tipping that runs in two directions at once: it is a small offence to tip the person behind the bar, and it is a small offence not to tip the person who brings the food, so that the very same act — handing over a little money — is an insult in one place and its omission is an insult in the other.

I have watched visitors get all three wrong. The locals were indulgent when a visitor crossed against the lights, cold when a visitor pushed to the front of the bus queue, and genuinely wounded when a visitor left a coin on the bar. The visitor could not see the difference; to them the crossing looked like the dangerous one, and the coin looked like a kindness.

What I want to know is what all of this presupposes. I do not mean what the locals would say if you asked them — they would say that it is simply how things are done here — but what view of conventions, of fairness, and of wrongdoing the pattern only makes sense under. Where does the bindingness of the bus queue come from, if not from any law? What does it presuppose about the difference between a rule that has merely been allowed to lapse and one that was never written down at all? Do the tipping conventions presuppose something about the meaning of a gift, or about the standing of the person who serves, that would explain why the same gesture cuts both ways? And are the three parts of the pattern committed to a single view of things, or is the town quietly holding two incompatible ones — for instance, treating the queue as a matter of fairness that any reasonable person must respect, while treating the tipping rules as mere local custom that only the initiated can be blamed for breaking? Finally, tell me what the town is assuming about the visitor: is a visitor bound by conventions they could not have known, and if so, on what view of obligation does that rest? I would like the implicit theory reconstructed and then tested — which parts of the pattern it genuinely needs, which parts it merely happens to fit, and what would have to change in the town if it adopted a rival account of why conventions bind at all.
