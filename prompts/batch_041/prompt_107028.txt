I am writing a novel, not a dissertation, and what I need from you is a specification rather than a verdict: what would a theory of historical time, one that says which changes are slow and which are fast and how the two act on one another, have to deliver before I could let it govern the shape of a book? The book follows four generations of a family in a valley above a port, whose lives are decided by things none of them can alter: transhumance routes, the silting of the harbour, the price of grain, the sea's own moods. I will never cite anything, so give me no bibliography; a name is welcome only where it earns its place in the argument.

Set out the desiderata. What must a theory of the relation between long-run structures and events be able to do — which phenomena it must explain, which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must not collapse, which findings it must not contradict — with the case or consideration standing behind each requirement, and with your judgement as to which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one school. Fernand Braudel's three tempos (the événement, the conjoncture, the longue durée) and the Annales tradition around them are the obvious reference point; use them where they help, but the list is the deliverable.

I want the pressure points argued rather than gestured at. At a minimum I expect the list to have to say something about each of these: how a pattern can outlive the people who carry it; whether an event, say an assassination or a wreck or a decision to cut a canal, can alter a trajectory without the deep structure becoming mere scenery; whether structures are themselves the sediment of earlier events, and what that does to the very idea of explanation by structure; how climate, soil, disease and currents are to figure without becoming fate, given that slow drivers are perfectly capable of breaking abruptly; what such a theory can be tested against when the record is written by the eventful and the propertied and the slow and the poor leave no register; how several tempos can coexist in a single present; what the unit of analysis is (a sea, a world-economy, a mountain system, a household) and what licenses treating it as one; and whether any of this can be told at all, since my characters cannot perceive the very causes that govern them and I need to know whether a theory survives that, or whether narration and explanation come apart here.

Then say which pair of requirements you suspect cannot be satisfied together — my own worry is the pair concerning contingency and concerning structural explanation — and which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates. Close with a brief scoring of Braudel's layered scheme, the microhistorians' reduction of scale, and the nested-cycle models ecologists use, in which slow accumulation is periodically released by fast disturbance that cascades across scales.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, namely whether the list can be satisfied as a whole and which requirement does the most work, then give the list and the reasoning; no preamble, and no restating of my question.
