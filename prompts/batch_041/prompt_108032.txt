I have no training in logic or philosophy, but I have been reading Gödel's 1931 paper on undecidable propositions in an English translation, and there is a step I cannot get past. I am going to paraphrase the passage rather than quote it, because I do not have the book in front of me and do not want to garble the words. After he constructs the sentence that is neither provable nor refutable in the system, he says that this sentence is nevertheless true, and the reason he gives seems to be that the sentence asserts its own unprovability, so that if it were provable the system would be inconsistent, and therefore if the system is consistent the sentence must be true. I understand the words but not the move. Truth seems to be doing something here that provability is not, and I cannot tell whether that is a mathematical fact about the system or a fact about the world outside it. I have also read popular expositions of the argument that because a human can see the truth of this sentence while the machine cannot, the human mind cannot be any machine. That argument has always struck me as too quick, but I cannot say exactly where it goes wrong, and I suspect the answer is hidden in the passage I am stuck on.

To make my confusion sharper, I invented a machine for myself, which I call the climber. The climber starts with some standard axioms for arithmetic. Whenever it proves that the system it is currently using is consistent, it adds the Gödel sentence of that system as a new axiom and continues. It can do this over and over, and I can even imagine it doing it into the transfinite, always climbing to a stronger system and always adding the sentence that the previous system could not prove. It seems to me that the climber can prove everything a human can prove, and more, and that the Lucas–Penrose argument would have to say the climber is not a machine either, which cannot be right. But I also suspect that the whole sequence of systems the climber passes through is itself something a single machine could enumerate, in which case the climber is just one more formal system with its own unprovable sentence, and the human's supposed advantage simply reappears at the next level.

What I want to know is whether the passage in Gödel that I am stuck on already contains the distinction between a single system and an open-ended sequence of systems, and whether the truth of the Gödel sentence is the kind of thing that can be seen from outside any system at all, or only from outside each particular system in turn. If you can, please explain the passage itself first, in plain terms, and then tell me what it does and does not license in the anti-mechanist argument.
