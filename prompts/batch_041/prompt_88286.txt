You are a philosopher who works on the metaphysics of ordinary objects, with a specialist's feel for the special composition question — the question of when some things add up to a further thing — and for the neighbouring puzzles about material constitution and identity over time. I want you to treat the verdicts below as data and to work carefully and slowly from them. Begin by restating the cases in your own words and sorting them into groups, noting which verdicts pull together and which pull apart; then look for the principle, or small set of rival principles, that best fits; then test each candidate against fresh cases. Do not simply rehearse the standard positions from the literature. I want the principle that fits my verdicts, and I neither need nor want citations, reading lists, or attributions.

My verdicts. In each case I am asking one question: is there, here, a single thing — one object — in addition to the smaller things it is made of?

1. The wooden chair I am sitting on: yes, one thing.
2. The loose pile of firewood beside the stove: no, just logs.
3. The same logs, nailed and glued into a bench: yes, one thing — a bench.
4. A handful of dry sand: no.
5. The sandstone boulder on the beach, sand cemented into stone: yes, one thing.
6. The desk I assembled from a slab and two filing cabinets, nothing fastened, currently set up and in use: yes, one thing.
7. The cloud over the ridge: yes, one thing, though I grant it is diffuse and short-lived.
8. The drifting patch of plastic debris in the North Pacific that oceanographers map and name: no — that is a region of ocean, not a thing in it.
9. The tornado that came through town last spring: yes — it was one thing while it lasted, and it had powers of its own.
10. The flock of starlings that wheels over the field at dusk, moving as one: no — many birds, and no further thing.
11. The football club I have supported since childhood: yes, one thing. It can be bought, sued, relegated, and it can win, and none of that is true of any player or of the players taken together.
12. My dog: yes, one thing.
13. My dog's leg, on the table after the amputation: no — a leg-shaped piece of meat, not a leg.
14. The blue, tentacled creature that floats on the sea surface and stings swimmers, which I am told is not a single animal but a raft of many specialized individual animals fused together, none of which could live alone: yes, one thing. I cannot bring myself to say otherwise, and the biology does not move me.
15. A lump of clay that has been moulded into the shape of a man: I go back and forth. Some days I say there is one thing there, the clay, and that statue-talk is only a way of describing it; other days I am sure there are two things in the same place at once, a statue and a lump, one of which would survive being squashed and the other of which would not.

What I notice about my own verdicts. My yeses tend to be things caught up in a single goings-on, so that something can happen to the whole that is not merely things happening to the parts — 1, 3, 5, 6, 7, 9, 11, 12, 14. My nos tend to be heaps and crowds — 2, 4, 8, 10. But three collisions will not sit still: 9 against 10, since a tornado is one thing and a murmuration is not, and the only difference I can put my finger on is that the tornado has a boundary you can point at; 11 against both, since a club has no boundary at all and its members are mostly not doing anything together, and yet I am sure the club exists; and 7 against 8, since the cloud and the plastic patch are both diffuse and both tracked and named by scientists, and I say yes to one and no to the other.

The tentative principle I would offer you to test and repair: some things compose a further thing just when they are jointly caught up in one ongoing process or activity that has effects of its own, effects not attributable to the parts taken separately.

What I want from you.

(a) The best principle, or small set of rival principles, that captures my verdicts, stated precisely — necessary and sufficient conditions if you can manage it. For each clause, tell me which of my cases forces it.

(b) Verdicts on cases I have not listed: at least four new cases of your own choosing, including at least one whose candidate parts are not spatially contiguous at all (a language, a market, a corporation, a rumour, a conversation — your choice), at least one process or event (a river, a flame, a wave, a hole, a shadow, a smile), and at least one from the biological borderlands (a virus, a slime mould, a coral reef, an ecosystem, a transplanted organ, the microbes in my gut). Say what the principle delivers in each case, and whether the verdict is credible.

(c) An honest accounting of which of the verdicts above your principle cannot keep, and why I should give them up — or, if you prefer to keep them all, what complexity that costs and why the complexity is worth it.

(d) A verdict on 15, the case I cannot settle, together with an explanation of why my other verdicts leave it undecided.

(e) If a simpler principle fits nearly everything but forces me to abandon one verdict, say which verdict, and defend that trade against the more complicated alternative that saves every verdict.
