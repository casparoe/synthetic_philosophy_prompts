Question 2. Kripke argued that some truths are necessary but knowable only a posteriori, and that this breaks the old equation of necessity with apriority. Two-dimensionalists say the appearance of a conflict comes from running two different intensions together. Taking 'water is H2O' as your case: say what the two intensions are and how each is fixed, say which of them is knowable a priori and which isn't, and explain what the framework buys you that the bare distinction between epistemic and metaphysical modality doesn't -- in particular, what it does for the thesis that conceivability is a guide to possibility. (~500 words.)

Student's answer:

The two-dimensionalist starts from Kripke's separation of the epistemic from the metaphysical, and the aim is to keep the intuition that water could have turned out to be something other than H2O while still holding that water is H2O in every world.

The apparatus is basically Kaplan's character/content distinction, applied to the necessary a posteriori. Every expression gets two intensions. The primary intension is the rule that takes you from a context to a referent: it tells you what the term would pick out if the scenario in question turned out to be the actual one, so the primary intension of 'water' delivers whatever plays the watery role there. The secondary intension is the ordinary content, fixed once we know how the actual world turned out: since the watery stuff here is H2O, the secondary intension of 'water' is H2O at every world. So 'water is H2O' has a contingent primary intension -- in a scenario where the watery stuff is XYZ the sentence comes out false -- and a necessary secondary intension. That's the trick: the a posteriori side of the statement is a fact about the primary intension, the necessity is a fact about the secondary intension, and the apparent conflict disappears.

The primary intension is the a priori element. We don't need any chemistry to know what 'water' would pick out if a given scenario were actual; that follows from the role the term plays for us. The secondary intension is the a posteriori element: it took empirical work to find out that the watery stuff is H2O. So the framework accommodates the necessary a posteriori without giving up the thought that necessity is a metaphysical matter rather than a verbal one.

This is more than the Kripkean distinction by itself gives you. Kripke tells us that conceivability is a poor guide to possibility, since we can conceive of water being XYZ even though water is necessarily H2O. The two-dimensionalist can say something stronger: because the primary intension just is the space of what we can conceive, whatever scenario we can conceive of is possible. Conceive of a world where water is XYZ and you have a real possibility, not an epistemic illusion. That's what the framework is for in the mind-body debate: if we can conceive of a world physically just like ours with nobody home, the framework hands us a possible world, which is what the argument against physicalism needs. The same machinery explains why the physical facts a priori entail the facts about water but not the facts about experience.
