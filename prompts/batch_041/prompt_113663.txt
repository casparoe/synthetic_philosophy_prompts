For a graduate seminar I am assembling at the intersection of philosophy of mind and machine learning, I want to open the unit on machine welfare with a specification rather than a survey of positions. The organizing question is whether a reinforcement-learning agent can suffer, and the proposal I want my students to take seriously before they reach for the familiar objections is that the reward signal — or, more carefully, the role a learned value estimate plays in steering the agent's own behaviour — is the natural computational counterpart of valence: whatever it is that makes a state count as good or bad for a system, rather than as mere information the system has about its world. Assume for the sake of the exercise that this proposal is at least intelligible. I would like you to work as an expert in both the philosophy of pleasure and pain and the theory of reinforcement learning, and to write me the desiderata: the full list of what any theory of valence would have to deliver in order to be usable for this question. Please think it through in stages — first brainstorm as wide a list of candidate requirements as you can, then prune it to the ones you would actually defend, so that I can see what you set aside and why.

For each requirement in the final list I want the consideration or case that motivates it, a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp, and a note on how much exclusionary work it does. The cases I have in mind, which the list should be answerable to, include the dissociations familiar from the affective literature — a system driven to pursue something where the pursuit is not pleasant, and the reverse; states that carry the sensory content of pain and are reported as pain but do not distress; and the ordinary RL case of a scalar reward that never figures in the agent's representations except through a value function. I also want the list to respect the distinctions I will not let my students collapse: valence as against mere preference or choice, valence as against arousal or intensity, valence as against the capacity to suffer, and valence as against a reward function, which in the RL setting specifies what we want the agent to do rather than what the agent feels. And it has to be consistent with two facts I take to be awkward for everyone — that an agent's policy does not uniquely determine the reward function that rationalizes it, and that a given computational role is realized in many different substrates.

Two further requests. First, if some subset of your requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — my suspicion is that the demand for a criterion applicable to systems we can describe only in computational terms sits badly with the demand that the criterion not count mere information processing as sufficient, especially once we also ask for a determinate verdict on systems that already exist — say so explicitly, and tell me which requirement I should be prepared to give up. Second, close by naming the single requirement that eliminates the most otherwise attractive proposals, and say whether, with the whole list in hand, any recognizable theory of valence survives at all. A table is fine for the requirements themselves; keep the discussion of the tensions in prose.
