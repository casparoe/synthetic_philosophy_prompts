I'm a retired lake ecologist — thirty-eight years on eutrophication and regime shifts in shallow lakes, if that's relevant — and since I retired I've been reading my way into philosophy. Philosophy of science mostly, some philosophy of history, a bit on causation. I've ended up with a set of views about societal collapse that I built from the ecology side, and I'd like someone with real philosophical training to audit them for me. Before you answer, please brainstorm a little — lay out the commitments and sketch how they might bear on each other — and then give me the audit proper. Take the role of someone who works on explanation and evidence in the historical sciences (paleoecology, archaeology, historical geology) and who has also read a fair bit of philosophy of the social sciences.

Here's where I've landed. Fair warning: I havent sorted these out, that's the whole point of asking.

1. Collapse is real. I don't mean it's a handy metaphor. In a shallow lake you get a genuine shift from clear water to turbid, and it has hysteresis — you can't just undo it by putting the nutrient loading back where it was. I think human societies can undergo that same kind of shift, and that "collapse" names something that happens in the world, not just in the eye of the historian.

2. Easter Island is our cleanest case of a society wrecking itself through its own resource use. If I had to teach one example, that's definately the one.

3. Explanations of these events should be material. Soils, water, climate, energy, timber, calories. When an account starts leaning on what a society "believed" or what its "values" were, I get itchy — that's usually where the rigor goes to die.

4. But I also think a lot of collapse talk is the view from the top. When the palace burns and the scribes stop writing, we declare the civilization over, while the farmers in the next valley go on farming and never notice. So "collapse" is often just the perspective of the people whose particular institutions fell apart.

5. And I think the resilience / adaptive-cycle picture I used for lakes is basically right for human societies too — growth, conservation, release, reorganization. A society that "collapses" is a system that has gone through release into reorganization.

Go through these and tell me which ones actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and how bad each real one is. I'd much rather have the tensions ranked by severity than a list where everything is equally bad. For each real tension, tell me the cheapest way out and what that fix costs me. Then tell me which single one of the five I'd be best off giving up.

Two things about the answer itself. First, say how confident you are in your main claims — I'd rather have "fairly sure about X, genuinely unsure about Y" than uniform hedging all the way down. Second, single out the one point in your answer you're least sure of, and say what would change your mind about it.
