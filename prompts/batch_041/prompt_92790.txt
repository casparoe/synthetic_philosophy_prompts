I am three drafts into a novel about a stratigrapher who spent a decade on the working group that tried to give the Anthropocene a formal place on the geological time scale, and who was in the room when the proposal was finally refused. The ninth chapter turns on a scene in which she explains to a doctoral student why she voted against her own proposal, and what she says is not a confession but an argument, delivered flatly, the way one reads out a proof. I have written it out below in her voice. My difficulty is that I cannot tell whether it is a proof or a trap, and the last third of the book depends on her holding a position rather than a mood.

She begins with the mechanics. A unit of the geological time scale is made official by choosing one point in one outcrop — a "golden spike" — that everyone agrees to read as the boundary, and by showing that the same signal appears in the same order everywhere on Earth, so that the boundary is the same instant everywhere; her word for this is "isochronous." Then she lays it out:

1. A boundary is scientific only if it is isochronous: one instant, everywhere, legible in rock.
2. Human influence is not isochronous. It arrives in different places at different times, from the first farmers who burned forest to the last cities to be electrified.
3. The only signal that is genuinely synchronous and global is the plutonium laid into the mud of every continent by a few decades of atmospheric weapons testing by a handful of states.
4. So the Anthropocene can be given a real boundary only if it is defined as the nuclear age — which is not the phenomenon the word was coined to name.
5. If instead we put the boundary where the phenomenon actually is — at agriculture, or at the industrial revolution, or at the 1610 dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide that followed the collapse of American populations — then the boundary is honest but not isochronous, and therefore not a boundary at all.
6. So the Anthropocene is either a unit that does not mean what it says or a meaning that cannot be a unit.
7. And in any case the decision belongs to a commission. The spike is chosen; the proposal is voted on; the term is ratified or refused. A period that is voted into existence is not discovered. It is legislated.

It is the last step I have stopped trusting. If a period can be legislated, then so can the end of the Cretaceous, whose boundary was fixed by exactly the same procedure, and I do not believe — nor does she — that a committee could have voted the dinosaurs out of extinction. So something in the chain is doing quiet work I have not caught, and I would rather find it now than in copy edits.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each no longer than two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary: which step I should cut; why it looked so convincing in her mouth; what cutting it costs her; which alternative cut is nearly as good, and what that one costs; and what the argument gets right that survives either repair. I would rather keep (1) and lose (7) than the reverse — the rocks being real matters to me more than the commission being right — but tell me if that preference is the mistake.
