I am advising a small foundation on how to spend a three-year program: three researchers, conceptual work on the foundations of generational analysis — metaphysics and social theory rather than survey design or econometrics, though the group will be expected to read the quantitative literature closely. Before I recommend a portfolio I want the agenda defended, and I want two competing organizing principles evaluated rather than assumed.

The first trustee proposes subordinating everything to a predictive test: an account of generations earns its keep only if it licenses inferences from the claim that cohort C was formed by event E to predictions about how members of C will behave. The second proposes a stability test: an account succeeds to the extent that its boundaries survive re-description, that is, survive being redrawn by a different but equally defensible convention. Tell me whether either test should organize the program, what each would rule out that we might want to keep, and which you would adopt if forced to choose.

Then give me the ranking itself. Treat the following as the candidate pool, not as a complete list, and add what is missing: (a) whether age, period, and cohort effects can be separated at all, given that they are linearly dependent and that a single cross-section equally supports "each cohort is more liberal than the last" and "people grow more conservative as they age"; (b) the Mannheimian distinction between a generation as a shared location in historical time and a generation as an actuality — a group that has worked a common situation into a common consciousness — and whether the second notion does work the first cannot; (c) whether the familiar labels name anything causally efficacious, or whether they are interactive kinds, classifications that feed back into the people classified, so that the boundaries are partly constituted by the drawing of them; (d) whether social change is better explained by cohort replacement — old cohorts dying, new ones arriving — than by the conversion of individuals, and what that implies for how the kinds should be individuated; (e) whether disputes over where one generation ends are disagreements about the world, about the reference of a term fixed by social practice, or merely verbal.

For each item, specify what depends on its resolution and what it in turn depends on; how tractable it is for philosophically trained researchers rather than demographers; which parts of the area are settled enough that work there would be wasted; and what argument or result within two years would move it up or down the list. I want the ranking, the criteria behind it, an account of where those criteria conflict, and the case for the top two. A survey of the field will not help me.
