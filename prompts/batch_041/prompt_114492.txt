I've been roped into refereeing a long-running departmental fight and I need help writing the document that should have ended it, so here goes.

The fight: one colleague is a full-on Jeffrey conditionalizer. When evidence comes in and they aren't certain of it, they don't condition on the evidence itself; they shift credences by probability kinematics, holding the conditional probabilities on the relevant partition rigid and just swapping in the new weights over that partition. The other colleague thinks this is either unnecessary or straight-up incoherent. Their line: any genuine evidence is something you end up certain of, so plain conditionalization covers all of it, and when you aren't certain of something you should condition on the fact that you had the experience or that you hold the degree of belief -- not on the uncertain thing itself. They've read all the same material and have been talking past each other for years. I want the joint statement they could actually both sign.

Two things I want worked in, because we kept circling them and I think that's where the real action is.

First, a case. A doctor has a patient and two imperfect tests. Test A is a cheap screening test; test B is a lab assay whose interpretation depends on how much she trusts the A result, and running B also tells her something about A's reliability, not just about the patient. She can run them in either order. Fill in plausible numbers yourself and tell me whether the two orders have to agree. My Jeffrey colleague says order can legitimately matter and it's no reductio; the other says a theory of learning that makes your final credence depend on which test you happened to run first is broken. I want the document to say exactly what each of them is committed to here and what would settle it.

Second, the partition thing. Everyone agrees you rigidify the conditionals on some partition, but nobody in the room could tell me what fixes which partition, or what happens when two people with the same evidence pick different ones. I suspect a crux lives there but I dont trust my own read.

Give it to me as:

1. "Claims we both sign" -- numbered, 10-12 claims, roughly 600 words. What uncertain evidence is, what the two procedures have in common, what each side will grant about the other's motivations and about the standard objections to its own view. Nothing vacuous -- if a claim would be signed by any Bayesian whatsoever, cut it.

2. "Where we part" -- a table. For each remaining disagreement: the neutral statement of the issue, then the wording each side would accept as its own position, phrased so that the other side agrees it's a fair statement of the view even while rejecting it.

3. "Cruxes" -- 3 or 4, each about 150 words. Why settling it would move at least one side, and which side.

4. "Tests and predictions" -- about 500 words. For each crux, the case, argument, or formal result that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Put the two-test case here.

5. "Verbal or genuine" -- about 150 words. Which parts of this fight are just different words for the same commitment, and which are real.

Please dont give me a bibliography or point me at papers -- I can't check them and neither can the person I'm handing this to, so every argument has to stand on its own. And dont hedge it all into mush: where one side definately has the better of it, the document should say so in the crux section without pretending it's a tie.
