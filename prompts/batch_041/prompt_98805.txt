I'm assembling the essay section of the final for an advanced seminar I teach on accuracy-first epistemology and epistemic utility theory — a mixed room of strong upper-level undergraduates and first-year graduate students. Over the term we worked through proper and strictly proper scoring rules, the accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism, and the expected-accuracy argument for conditionalization, and a failure keeps surfacing in the students' term papers: they present the dominance argument as if it were a finished vindication of probabilism — incoherent credences lose on accuracy, so coherence is required — without noticing what kind of dominance is actually on offer, what the argument quietly presupposes, and what it conspicuously leaves untouched. I want a single essay question, worth about 90 minutes of writing, that forces them to reconstruct the dialectic instead of reciting it. It should make them state precisely the form of dominance the argument establishes and explain why the difference between a single rival credence function that is at least as accurate in every world and one that is strictly more accurate in every world matters for how compelling the conclusion is; say what role the partition of states and the choice of scoring rule play in the result, and whether the conclusion comes out scoring-rule-relative; bring out why the argument leaves the problem of the priors alone — that it constrains coherence without telling an agent which coherent credence function to adopt — and say what a defender of the approach might reasonably reply; and finally engage the objection that dominance reasoning only carries weight when one option is at least as good as its rival in every state, so that the accuracy-firster owes us an account of why epistemic rationality should be sensitive to dominance in the first place.

Please write the question itself, then a model answer of roughly 600 words, then a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth (totaling 20) and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each point wrong. Keep the whole thing answerable from the concepts the course covered: no citations, no reading lists, no author names in either the question or the model answer. Since I'll be using this to train junior TAs as much as to grade students, I'd also like a short note after the marking scheme stating how confident you are in the philosophical claims your model answer makes and singling out the one point in it you are least sure of.
