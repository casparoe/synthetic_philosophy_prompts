I teach a final-year undergraduate seminar on the strategy and ethics of nuclear weapons — sixteen students, a single 90-minute session — and I want to spend the meeting on an idea that students can state perfectly and still not feel. The idea is this: a deterrent threat is hard to believe when carrying it out would be suicidal, and it becomes believable the moment the threatening state can credibly claim that the terrible outcome no longer rests on its own deliberate choice — that it has been handed over, at least partly, to a process it does not fully control: a tripwire, a delegation to subordinates, an automatic mechanism, the risk of an accident, plain chance. The purchase of credibility is paid for in real risk. You make the threat believable by making the disaster genuinely more likely, and if both sides do this, the outcome slips out of anyone's hands. This is the standard deterrence-theory treatment of credibility through risk — Schelling is the name usually attached to it — and what I care about is the paradox at its center, not the label.

Design an in-class game that stages this.

Constraints on the design:

- Setting and scale: sixteen students, one 90-minute session, a seminar room with a whiteboard. Teams of four.
- Materials must be ordinary: index cards, dice, tokens, a ladder or track drawn on the board.
- The rules must contain a chance device whose use is a genuine choice. It is not enough that the game include randomness; players must be able to decide whether to move part of the outcome into chance's hands, and it must be visible to the other side that they have done so.
- Include private information about how much each side values the disputed object, so that resolve is not common knowledge.
- Include at least one commitment mechanism — a way to make retreat costly or impossible — that is public and irreversible.
- Do not let players write enforceable agreements; if they try, the rules should make any deal unenforceable. The point is that cooperation is tempting but cannot be guaranteed.
- Payoffs must be workable without real money.
- The idea must live in what happens, not in what I say. No framing lecture up front; the game should be playable from a one-page rules sheet. Name the idea only in the debrief.

Then give me the following, in this order and with these headings:

1. "The idea and the setting" — one paragraph, under 120 words, restating what the game is for, so I can check the design against it.
2. "Materials and setup" — a list, about 150 words, including how teams are formed and what is public on the board.
3. "The rules" — step by step, about 400 words, precise enough that I can run the session from the sheet: how a turn ends, how the chance device is invoked, how the game ends.
4. "Payoffs" — a table, plus a short paragraph explaining the numbers and why the incentive to escalate exists.
5. "Session timeline" — minute by minute, about 150 words, splitting setup, play, and debrief.
6. "Debrief" — about 400 words, mapping specific moments that will have occurred to parts of the idea: where credibility came from, what the chance device bought, why the safe outcome was hard to reach, and what each side could and could not control.
7. "What will go wrong the first time" — about 250 words on the predictable failure modes (students playing it as ordinary chicken, refusing to escalate, cooperating too easily, one side exploiting the chance mechanism so the game ends early or feels arbitrary), and how I should handle each.
8. "What the game cannot reproduce" — about 200 words, being frank about which feature of the real idea the activity cannot stage and that I will have to supply in speech — for instance, that real probabilities of catastrophe are not a known die roll but an unquantifiable fog, and that the danger in the real case grows out of organizational complexity and misperception rather than a clean randomizer.
