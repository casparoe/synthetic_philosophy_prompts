I hold a strong version of the multiple-discovery thesis: for any discovery whose time has come — where the relevant concepts, instruments, and background knowledge are all in place — it will be made independently by more than one person, and the fact that history usually attaches a single name is an artifact of who published first, not a fact about the discovery itself. On this view the classic priority disputes (Newton and Leibniz over the calculus, Darwin and Wallace over natural selection) are sociologically interesting but epistemically idle: the discovery was inevitable, and the discoverer is close to interchangeable.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, including: genuine 'singletons' — discoveries that were fully ripe yet made only once, with no near-miss rivals — and how much weight they carry; the selection bias in how we notice simultaneity at all (we remember the doubles and forget the discoveries that simply never happened); the asymmetry between multiple *attempters* and multiple *succeeders*, since a result can be palpably in the air while only one person can actually crack it; and whether the thesis can be stated so that no conceivable historical record could falsify it. Which of these would a committed defender concede as counting against them, and which would they wave away as not really being about inevitability? I would be most moved by evidence that the 'ripeness' condition can be specified independently of the outcome, so tell me whether it can, or whether it is quietly defined after the fact by looking at who succeeded. End by saying honestly whether the strong thesis risks anything at all — and if it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, whether that is a fatal problem or merely the price of a thesis of this kind.
