I am a final-year undergraduate taking a course on science, technology, and public policy, and I am writing a seminar paper on the precautionary principle. Before I evaluate any particular formulation of it, I want a clear specification of what an adequate formulation would have to deliver. Please act as a philosopher of science and decision theorist with expertise in risk regulation and regulatory science, and think carefully before you commit to the list.

Begin with a short brainstorming section: set out the various things people demand of the principle — that it be action-guiding under incomplete knowledge, that it shift the burden of proof onto those who propose an activity, that it not license paralysis, that it be usable by a regulator with finite resources, and anything else you judge relevant — and say which of these look to you like genuine desiderata and which look like the commitments of one camp dressed up as criteria.

Then give me the specification itself: the requirements an adequate formulation must meet, each with the case or consideration behind it, ordered by how much work each one does in narrowing the field.

To give the specification something to bite on, treat the following as the materials it must accommodate. Assume a four-way distinction among decision situations, which I want you to take as a stipulation rather than as something to source: risk, where the relevant outcomes and their probabilities are both known; uncertainty, where the outcomes are known but their probabilities are not; ambiguity, where even the framing is contested and the list of outcomes is in dispute; and ignorance, where we do not know what we do not know. Any formulation will have to say which of these it is a rule for, and whether one rule can serve for all four.

It should get cases like these right: a substance where early evidence of a serious mechanism appeared well before the mechanism was established, and where the producer of the substance funded the research that was supposed to settle the question; a restriction whose near-term effect is to delay a technology that, on the best available evidence, would later have removed a larger and better-established harm; a ban that displaces the risk rather than removing it, so that the substitute carries a hazard at least as serious; two technologies with evidentiary records of identical quality, one long established and one new, where the principle directs us to the established one — say whether the principle is getting that case right or merely encoding a bias toward the status quo; a harm of a type no one had framed, so that no amount of evidence-gathering of the kind then available could have registered it; a decision that becomes harder the longer it is deferred, because the option to act lapses, so that doing nothing is not the neutral baseline; and a harm with a small probability but a loss large enough to have no finite bound, set against a remedy whose costs are certain, large, and borne by identifiable people now.

It should also respect these distinctions: prevention, acting against a risk we can specify, versus precaution, acting where we cannot yet specify it; the trigger of precaution, that is, what state of knowledge activates it, versus its strength, how much protection it demands, versus its burden, who must show what; scientific dissent that reflects unresolved evidence versus dissent that is manufactured or paid for; reversibility versus magnitude of harm; and the level at which the rule operates — individual, firm, regulator, court — since the same words do different work at each level.

And it should be consistent with these facts about decision-making: every measure has opportunity costs, and foregone benefits are themselves harms, often to people worse off than those protected; a subjective expected-utility calculation is always formally available, so the principle must say what it adds to one rather than pretending to replace it; safety factors presuppose some baseline and cannot be conjured out of ignorance; patterns in public risk perception such as dread, novelty, involuntariness, and unequal exposure are not automatically errors to be corrected; and compliance and enforcement are imperfect, so a rule that cannot be implemented is not a rule.

Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a particular camp, and identify any subset you believe is jointly unsatisfiable, saying what would have to give. I am especially interested in whether a principle strong enough to be action-guiding under ignorance can avoid being self-defeating when applied to itself, and can avoid the paralysis that follows from the fact that almost any action, including precautionary regulation, carries some unquantifiable downside. Finish by naming the single requirement that, if I could keep only one, would eliminate the most candidate formulations, and score the main families of formulation against the list — the strong version that treats precaution as a decision rule, the version that shifts the burden of proof, the version that governs only the choice of procedure, and the version that is simply cost-benefit analysis with a margin of safety — though keep the weight on the criteria rather than the scoring.

Please do not give me a reading list, citations, or author names in place of arguments; I want the specification and the reasoning behind each requirement. A table is fine for the scoring, and a clear list is fine for the requirements themselves.
