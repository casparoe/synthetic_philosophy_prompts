I am preparing a graduate seminar on practical reason, and the unit on indifference keeps going badly: my students arrive able to recite the puzzle of the ass between two identical bales of hay and then split immediately into those who think the ass is irrational for starving and those who think starving is precisely what a theory of rational choice requires, without either side having first said what a theory of choice without preference would have to deliver before it could settle anything. Before I turn them loose on the literature, I want to hand them the specification itself. So please write the desiderata for a theory of choice among options the agent does not prefer: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results from the rest of decision theory and from the psychology of deliberation it must not contradict, and the explanatory work it owes — each requirement accompanied by the case or consideration that motivates it. Be warned that I have salted the unit with material of my own, and I want the list to survive it. I ask them to distinguish the ass's predicament, where the two bales really are equally good, from the predicament of someone choosing between two careers that are neither better nor worse than each other nor equal in value (some of my colleagues call this parity, others file it under incomparability), and to say whether those two situations can carry the same normative upshot. I also give them a variant in which the ass is not choosing between bales but must commit in advance to a standing policy for resolving ties, in full view of an adversary who afterwards decides what the two bales are, so that any deterministic tie-breaking rule the ass adopts can be read off and used to steer it; and a variant in which the starvation is caused not by the tie at all but by the ass's insistence on waiting for a discriminating reason, so that the failure, if there is one, is a failure about delay rather than about preference. What I want is the requirements stated so that a student can see which of them are genuine demands on any theory in this area and which are merely the house style of one camp — the expected-utility camp, the incomparability camp, those who hold that an arbitrary choice is a rational choice, and those who hold that it is a rational failure.

Give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no preamble, no summary at the end, no closing scorecard. Each item is one requirement, put in a sentence or two, then the case or consideration that motivates it in another sentence or two, then a verdict on whether you would treat it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Where a requirement collides with another on the list, say so in the item where the collision surfaces and tell me which of the two you would surrender; if two of the five cannot be satisfied together, make that plain in whichever item renders the conflict most visible, and if one requirement does more than any other to eliminate otherwise attractive theories, name it there.
