Two things happened this year that I would not have expected to bear on each other. In the spring I chaired the selection panel for a small fellowship, and the five of us found that we could always say which of two applications was stronger — confidently, usually unanimously — without the judgments ever composing into an order. Whichever pair we began with, we could work our way around the shortlist and arrive back where we started, each intermediate comparison still seeming right; we did eventually send up a ranked list, but only by falling back on a weighted score that all of us privately thought a worse guide than the judgments it replaced. In the autumn my family spent months choosing among a ladder of arrangements for my father's care, each rung a little more supervision and a little less independence than the one below, where every step up was plainly better than the step beneath it and the top of the ladder did not seem better than the bottom. A colleague in the philosophy department told me the general issue is the transitivity of 'better than' and that there is a literature on spectrum arguments, but when I asked what a theory would have to do to be adequate to cases like these she gave me the shape of the problem rather than the standard I wanted.

What I am asking for, then, is a specification rather than a theory. Write the desiderata for an account of comparative betterness: the requirements any adequate theory of 'better than' must meet, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, each marked as either non-negotiable or as the commitment of one camp rather than a genuine requirement. I want the list to cover the phenomena and not only the formal properties — the distinctions it must not collapse, the ordinary judgments it may not simply declare false, the results from decision theory or from measurement theory that it cannot contradict. And I want the places where the requirements pull against one another. In particular, if some subset of them cannot be jointly satisfied — transitivity, together with the principle that a small enough improvement is still an improvement, together with the denial that there is any sharp line at which improving turns into worsening, together with the assumption that any two items are comparable — then say so plainly, show which member of the set has to be surrendered, and tell me which single requirement on the list does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive candidates. That would be the most valuable part of the document.

Three things I would like treated as candidate requirements rather than as settled answers, each argued for or against on its merits. First, that the spectrum cases are a Sorites problem in the comparative itself, so that any theory owes an account of how 'better than' behaves at the margins: whether it has sharp boundaries at all, whether the comparison class is part of its content rather than a feature of the context of utterance, and whether the relation is one thing or a family. Second, that 'better than' may not be a single relation in either of my cases — the care ladder varies along safety, autonomy, cost and proximity at once, and it may be that a respect-relative comparative is transitive while the all-things-considered one is not, in which case the theory must say which of the two the transitivity question is even about. Third, whether parity is a genuine fourth relation alongside the better, the worse, and the equally good — distinct from incomparability — or a relabelling of the problem.

Since I have to act — I chair the panel again in the spring, and my family will have to revisit my father's arrangements — include whatever the list implies for someone who must rank or choose: what a chooser is entitled to do when the ordering fails, and whether the failure is best read as a defect in the options, in the comparative, or in the demand for a total order. Keep the weight on the criteria; a closing paragraph on which of the familiar moves best survives the list — holding transitivity and rejecting one of the spectrum's premises, giving up transitivity, or treating the comparative as vague — is welcome but secondary. No bibliography or citations, please; I can find the literature myself, and I would rather have the positions described than named.
