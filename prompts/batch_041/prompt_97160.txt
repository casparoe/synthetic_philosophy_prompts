I help run a small supported-housing charity — I'm the vice chair of the board, not an academic, I just did a night class in philosophy years ago and it's left me with enough knowledge to be annoying at meetings. Whenever something bad happens to one of our tenants (someone dies of an overdose after we missed a couple of welfare checks, a fella falls down a stairwell we'd been meaning to fix the light in) we have to do one of these "learning reviews," and we always get stuck on the same thing: did we cause this, or did it just happen while we weren't doing anything? Someone says well if we'd done X it wouldn't have happened, and then someone else says you could say that about anything, if we'd done Y or Z instead it also wouldn't have happened, and then the meeting dissolves and we write "lessons learned" and go home. I've realised this is a real topic with a name — causation by omission — so what I want is a procedure my committee can actually run on a case, not a lecture. Something like: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the thing stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question has turned into a question about what we owed the tenant rather than about what happened. It has to be honest about the trap where "it wouldn't have happened without us doing nothing" lets in basically everything we've ever failed to do, and about the way whether skipping something even registers as a cause seems to depend on whether anyone expected us to do it in the first place (and on who those people are, and whether we ever said we'd do it). It should also handle the case where we did do something and the case where two different things we failed to do would each have been enough on their own, and where we acted but the harm still came through something else. I need to be able to run it in an afternoon with four trustees who aren't philosophers, and I need it to say plainly which steps are just judgement calls wearing a lab coat.

Then please run it on two of our actual-ish cases. First: a tenant with a known history dies of an overdose after we missed two scheduled welfare checks. Second, and this is the one I'm worried about: a tenant died during a cold snap and we had never applied for a grant that would have paid for a night warden, which we had said in our annual report we were going to apply for; I think we're basically blameless there and I suspect the procedure will disagree with me, so if it does, say so straight and tell me why, don't soften it.

And at the end, tell me where the whole thing is most likely to be wrong — which consideration, if it turned out to be the real story, would flip the verdict on either case, and what would make you say the procedure itself was the problem rather than the case. I'd rather have a procedure with a visible seam than a tidy one that quietly hides the bit where we're just deciding what kind of charity we want to be.
