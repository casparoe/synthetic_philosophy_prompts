My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about laws of nature, and we ended up further in the weeds than either of us intended. She is a Humean: for her the laws are nothing over and above the axioms of whichever deductive system best balances simplicity, strength, and fit against the total mosaic of local matters of fact, so that talk of "governing" is at best a useful fiction laid over a world that is, at bottom, just a vast pattern of particular occurrences. I lean the other way and think the laws do something — that they govern, or else that they flow from the natures of the fundamental properties themselves, so that which generalizations are laws is settled by what those properties are disposed to do rather than by our taste in summaries. By the end we were too tired to keep going, and I suspect we were talking past each other rather than actually meeting on the points that divide us. I'd like you to write the dialogue we should have had: two philosophers, each as sharp and well-informed as the other, sitting at my kitchen table with the wine gone, taking each other's arguments seriously enough to answer the specific thing just said rather than the thing they wish had been said.

Please have them work through two concrete puzzles instead of trading slogans. The first is a toy universe I'll call the Two-Ledger World: its complete history admits two candidate best systems, one a shade simpler but leaving a small cluster of coincidences unexplained, the other a shade more complex but accounting for them, so that the Humean's balance of theoretical virtues has to break the tie. I want to see whether the anti-Humean can make anything of the fact that a slightly different weighting of simplicity against strength would have issued in a different set of laws, and whether the Humean can show that this is a feature rather than a bug. The second is the case of an uninstantiated regularity — a sweeping prohibition that nothing in the world ever violates only because nothing ever comes close to violating it — and whether such a generalization deserves the name of law, or whether calling it one is a mere artifact of the best system's economy. Neither of them should be allowed to win by caricature: the Humean cannot be a crude regularity theorist who thinks "law" just means "true generalization," and the anti-Humean cannot be a mystic who waves at "necessitation" without saying what work it does. Let the exchange be genuinely dialectical — each reply should pick up what the other just conceded or pressed — and let it end unresolved, with the real disagreement located precisely rather than dissolved. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher of science reading it wouldn't wince at either side's formulation of their own view.
