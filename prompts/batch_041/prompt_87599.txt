I chair the methods review panel of the National Replication Fund, which supports multi-site replications in the social and behavioural sciences. At Thursday's meeting we must dispose of an application from a five-site consortium that proposes to replicate a widely cited priming effect. The design is sequential and Bayesian: the team will recruit to a pre-registered maximum, but intends to stop early as soon as the Bayes factor for the effect exceeds ten, and it proposes to report that Bayes factor as the primary evidence. One panellist, a frequentist, says this is optional stopping by another name and that our standing requirement of calibrated error control is thereby breached; another, a Bayesian, replies that error probabilities are not properties of the evidence and that the fund has no business imposing a frequentist straitjacket on a Bayesian design. The applicant has offered a compromise: an adversarial prior centred on zero and scaled to half the original effect, together with a simulation study showing a false-positive rate no worse than five per cent under that prior. We are all conscious of the replication crisis and of the winner's curse that inflates published effects, and the applicant's stated reason for the Bayesian design is that it will let them stop as soon as the evidence is strong, which they say is more honest than fixed-horizon testing. I should add that I am a psychologist by training, comfortable with both approaches in practice but not a statistician, and I need an opinion I can defend to colleagues who are.

Our fund has just introduced a pilot condition, the dual-reporting condition, which requires every funded replication to report both a Bayes factor and a design-based error guarantee, and to pre-specify any stopping rule in a manner that is transparent under both frameworks. The applicant's compromise is plainly an attempt to satisfy that condition, but the frequentist panellist objects that a guarantee which depends on the applicant's chosen prior is not the kind of guarantee the fund's remit requires, while the Bayesian panellist objects that the dual-reporting condition is itself incoherent because it treats a posterior odds as if it were an error probability. The panel must decide whether the dual-reporting condition can be met by the applicant's proposal, and if not, what conditions would make it meetable.

Please draft the short written opinion I shall circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- fund as proposed, fund with specified conditions, defer for revision, or reject -- with a time frame and reasoning that the applicant could actually respond to. Engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side, whether that is the Bayesian's claim that error probabilities are irrelevant once the data are in or the frequentist's claim that a prior-dependent guarantee cannot satisfy the fund's remit, and do not pretend the disagreement is merely verbal. The opinion should read as a decision memo for an institutional body, not as an essay on the foundations of statistics; it should be no longer than two pages, and it should not ask for citations, bibliographies, or a reading list, since the panel must decide the case in front of it.
