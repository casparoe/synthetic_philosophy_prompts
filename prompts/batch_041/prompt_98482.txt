I write about philosophy and the history of ideas for a general readership, and I have been asked to run a ninety-minute session at a small residential seminar for graduate students in law, political theory, and history — roughly eighteen people, seated around one large table, no laptops, one flip chart, no budget for anything. What I want them to feel is Pascal's account of justice and force, which normally gets reduced to a slogan and which is much stranger and more uncomfortable than the slogan. As I understand it from the Pensées, there are three layers: a rule that is just but unenforced is merely a wish; a rule that is enforced but not believed just is a tyranny; and, since we cannot make what is just strong, we go the other way and make what is strong just, taking whatever distribution of power has already settled into place and dressing it in the costume of custom, precedent, and title until the costume itself starts to function as the reason. That third layer is the one I care about and the one a lecture cannot deliver. Design an activity that delivers it.

Concretely: I want a staged deliberation with real choices and small real stakes — points on a board, reputations in the room, nothing financial — set in a fictional settlement that has to allocate a scarce resource (a drought, a shared aquifer, a fixed grain store, whatever you think works cleanly). Two tallies go on the flip chart and are updated after every round, and they must be visibly independent: one for whether people actually comply, one for whether they think the rule is legitimate, taken by secret ballot so nobody has to say it out loud. Give some participants hidden interest cards so that a fair rule genuinely costs them, and make the enforcement role — whoever holds the sword — personally bear a cost for enforcing against the strong, so that the room discovers that the person able to enforce is usually not the person willing to enforce the just thing. Then introduce a labeling rule: the council may attach a status to any allocation, calling it a longstanding custom of the settlement or calling it this council's new decision, and let them watch the legitimacy tally move even when the content of the rule does not change at all. I would like the sequence to run without any framing lecture, and I do not want Pascal's name mentioned until the debrief, so that the room arrives at the uncomfortable move — arguing that the enforceable allocation is, on reflection, the just one — before they are told that this is the move they were set up to make.

Give me the full runnable package: the setup and materials, the roles, the round structure and timings within the ninety minutes, the payoff and scoring scheme, the wording of the secret ballot, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the three layers above — pointing to the round where the just rule died for lack of an enforcer, the round where compliance held but legitimacy collapsed, and the round where the group talked itself into the strong rule being the right rule. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I expect at least one faction to try to game the ballot, at least one participant to refuse the premise and insist that a genuinely fair rule would have commanded compliance, and a strong pull in the debrief toward the comfortable reading that this is all a design flaw we could fix with better institutions rather than a permanent feature of the situation. Give me handling notes for each. And be honest and specific about what the activity cannot reproduce: I suspect it cannot reproduce Pascal's theological framing, on which human justice is a counterfeit of a justice we have no access to and the whole arrangement is a consequence of our condition rather than a puzzle with a solution, and it probably cannot reproduce his conservatism about custom — the thought that since we can never agree on what is just, the only defensible ground for obedience is the settled fact of what has long been done. Tell me which of those the facilitator has to supply in words, and what the activity will leave the room believing if I do not.
