ok so my reading group has burned two sessions on the utility monster and I want an outside verdict. You know the case — a being who gets so much more welfare out of any given unit of a resource than the rest of us that the welfare-maximizing allocation hands it everything. The standard line is that this is a knock-down objection to utilitarianism. My friend thinks the objection is a dud and built the following argument for it, which I've writen out as steps:

1. Take any two beings and any fixed quantity of a resource — a dose of a medicine, say. If one of them would get more welfare out of the dose than the other, then giving it to that one produces more total welfare.
2. More total welfare is better.
3. So there's nothing wrong in principle with handing a resource to whoever gets the most out of it.
4. Now take a being who gets vastly more out of every resource than anyone else does, and notice that we can dial that difference up as far as we like — there's no ceiling on how much more.
5. By 3, the allocation that maximizes total welfare gives everything to that being.
6. That's supposed to be the reductio: aggregate-welfare maximization is false.
7. But 5 only gets to 6 by repeating a step nobody objects to. We really do give the medicine to the patient it helps more.
8. So whatever is monstrous about 6 has to come from the size of the difference, not from the shape of the reasoning.
9. And size alone can't turn a kind of reasoning from fine into monstrous. If it could, there'd be some size of difference where it flips, and nobody has ever located that line.
10. So the monster isn't an objection to aggregation at all. It's an objection to letting welfare differences of any size decide anything — i.e. to everything short of strict equality.
11. Strict equality is absurd, for the medicine reason.
12. So the monster refutes everything, and therefore refutes nothing. It's no argument against utilitarianism.

I'd rather keep 1 and 3 than 12 — 3 is basically the medicine case and 12 can't be right. My own hunch is that something slips between 4 and 5, but I can't say what. Somebody else in the group said it's just pascal's mugging with welfare instead of money — you know, the one where a stranger offers you an absurd payoff if you hand him your wallet, on a probability small enough that expected value still says yes — and that whatever fixes that case fixes this one. I don't buy it but I can't say why.

So which step do I cut, and what does cutting it cost me? If the honest answer is that I have to give up more than one, say so and price the package. And does the pascal thing actually help, or is it a different problem wearing the same coat?
