For a long feature I am writing on the philosophy of death, I want to end not with a paragraph but with a poem — something a reader can sit with, not an argument rendered as verse. The piece turns on the old Epicurean claim that death is nothing to us and on the industry of objections and repairs that claim has generated, and I want the poem's form, not merely its content, to carry the reasoning.

Write it as a two-part poem of roughly equal halves, the second answering the first line for line, with the end-rhymes of the second half running in reverse order, so that the two halves rhyme across the poem's middle and the last line closes on the rhyme that opened it. The first half should stand on the near side of death and the second on the far side, and the mirroring should be legible: a reader who has taught this argument should see that the halves are meant to be symmetrical, and should also see the precise point at which the poem refuses the symmetry.

Three ideas should be woven through, accurately rather than gestured at. First, the experience requirement behind the Epicurean verdict — that a thing can be bad for you only where there is a you to undergo it — set against the deprivation account, on which death is bad not because it is felt but because it removes goods one would otherwise have had; let the poem feel the cost of each, that the first makes death harmless by emptying it of a subject, and the second seems to need a subject who is not there to do the comparing. Second, the timing puzzle: at no moment is the dead person present to be harmed — before death one is alive, after it one is not, and the middle is not a time in anyone's life. Do not solve this; let the reader feel the gap where the harm was supposed to sit. Third, the symmetry argument — that the eternity before we were born is the same nothing as the eternity after we die, yet we dread only one of them — together with one attempt to break the symmetry, the thought that death thwarts desires that reached past our own lives (that one's children fare well, that some work outlast one), whereas the time before our birth thwarted nothing. Hold that repair up without endorsing it.

The coda, four lines at most and free to fall outside the mirror, should land on the one asymmetry the argument cannot talk away: that the nothing behind us and the nothing ahead of us are the same nothing, and only one of them is ahead. Leave the dread and the argument standing beside each other; do not resolve them into consolation or into a shrug.

No names, titles, or dates should be needed, and nothing should read as if it wants a footnote. Aim for something a philosopher would find accurate and a general reader would find moving.
