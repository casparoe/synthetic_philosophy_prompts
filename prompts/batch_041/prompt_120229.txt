I'm a public history MA student, and last term I did a placement with the volunteer committee that runs the memorial in my grandmother's town — it commemorates a fire at a textile mill in 1911 that the town marks every year — and I came away with a question I can't get past. Here is the practice, as concretely as I can put it. The memorial itself lists forty-seven names. The parish burial register, the coroner's list, and the company's own records between them support forty-four; the other three appear to be one transcription error from a newspaper of the period and two people whose families insisted they had been at the mill that night even though the documents place them elsewhere. The committee's standing rule is that a name is never removed once it is cut into the stone, because, as the chair put it to me, "the memorial belongs to the families, not to the archive." Adding a name, by contrast, is easy: a family need only come forward and ask, and no proof is required. Two names were added in the 1990s on exactly that basis, and one of them is one of the three I mentioned. Corrections, when they come, are published in a scholarly appendix to the committee's occasional papers, which almost nobody reads. And the same committee, out of the same small budget, funds a documentary edition of the mill's records that gets the count right and flags the three disputed names as unsupported, while the committee's own centennial booklet narrates the fire as killing forty-seven and footnotes the discrepancy. So the same body, on the same evening, keeps two incompatible books: one in which a mere claim is enough to put a name into the record and no amount of evidence is enough to take one out, and one in which evidence governs throughout.

What theory of the relation between a community's memory and the historical record does that practice presuppose? A friend who works in heritage policy told me that a memorial is not a claim about the past at all but a promise about the future — that we will go on saying these names — and that you cannot falsify a promise; I have no idea whether that is a real position or just something that sounds good, but it is the kind of answer I'm after. Does the practice presuppose that memory and history are simply different practices answering to different standards, so that they cannot genuinely conflict because they are not in the same business? Or that the community's belief about the fire is itself a historical fact — something that happened, and happened to forty-seven names' worth of people — so that the memorial is accurate as a record of the community even where it is false as a record of the fire? Or does it presuppose something stronger, that where the two conflict the community's memory has a standing the record cannot override, because the record's own authority is derivative from the community's practice of keeping it? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is underdetermined, so that several different theories would license everything the committee does. Tell me whether the two books really are incompatible or whether the division of labour between them is defensible, and — the part I most want — what would have to change if one held that there is a single record and that a community's memory, where it contradicts that record, must give way: which parts of the current practice survive that commitment and which stand exposed as merely sentimental.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question. I'd like you to say how confident you are in your main claims, and to single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
