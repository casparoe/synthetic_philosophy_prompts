I retired two years ago after thirty-odd years as a botanist — herbarium and field work, mostly sedges, which is a thing you can spend a life on — and I've been trying to make up for never having read any philosophy while I had a job. I'm working through a metaphysics book and I keep hitting the argument about relations: a relation can't be what ties two things together, because you'd need another relation to tie that relation to its terms, and another to tie that one, and off it goes forever. Same family of worries as the question of what makes a sentence more than a heap of words in an order. I beleive it's called Bradley's regress and the unity-of-the-proposition problem, but I may be mangling it. I understand the regress as a puzzle. What I can't see is what it's supposed to mean for someone who has to build something, and I suspect the answer isn't "nothing".

Here's the something. The herbarium I used to run is finally digitising its collection, and because I'm cheap and available they've made me the person who signs off on the data model. So every week or so I'm deciding, for some relationship in a specimen record, whether it stays a field on that record or gets promoted to a thing in its own right, with its own fields, its own identifier, its own history. My colleagues treat this as an engineering question with an obvious answer, and it smells to me exactly like the regress: the moment you make a relationship into an object, you need fresh relationships to attach it to its objects, and those could be objects too, and nobody has ever told me where that's supposed to stop or why stopping isn't just laziness. I want a procedure I can actually run, in the order I'd run it, in a Monday meeting with two curators and a database contractor, for deciding when a relationship has to become an entity and when it can stay a field. It needs a stopping rule, and it needs to say whether that rule is a real answer to the regress or a truce, and it needs to mark the steps where a curator has to decide something that isn't a data-modelling question at all. Please ground it in whatever the philosophy actually establishes here, and be blunt about where the analogy to data modelling breaks down — models are finite things made by fiat, so maybe the regress is toothless in my case, in which case say so and give me the procedure anyway.

Then run it on three, and don't skip the awkward parts. First, 'collected by': many of our collectors are also the determiners and sometimes the donors, so the same person shows up attached to the specimen in three different ways. Second, 'growing on': a specimen's substrate might be a host plant, a rock, a strip of bark, or a rotting log, and we've never agreed whether that's one relationship or four. Third, 'annotated by': we keep every determination as a separate annotation with its own author and date, nobody erases anybody, and later annotators are sometimes just wrong — so the record carries a pile of competing claims about what the plant is, and I need to know whether those are relationships, things, or something else entirely. I'd like at least one of the three to come out the way I don't want it to, if that's what the procedure says; I'd rather be annoyed than flattered. And tell me plainly what the procedure can't do, and where it's a costume over a judgement call — I've spent enough years around instruments to know when I'm being sold precision.
