A data-science newsletter I subscribe to recently ran a short explainer on Simpson's paradox, and I would like you to audit it for accuracy. The gist was this: 'Simpson's paradox is the situation in which a trend that holds in every subgroup of a dataset reverses once the subgroups are pooled, so that the same data support two contradictory conclusions. It is caused by a lurking variable — a confounder — and the cure is to control for it by analysing the subgroups separately. This is why the pooled figure is always the artefact and the disaggregated figure is the real effect; the paradox shows that pooled statistics, such as national averages, are meaningless, and the Berkeley admissions case is the standard proof that aggregate figures mislead.' Please go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what a working statistician would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply false. I am especially interested in whether the 'two contradictory conclusions' framing is defensible, whether the confounder diagnosis and the stratify-to-cure prescription hold in general, and whether the closing moral about aggregate statistics follows from anything true in the piece — and, if the prescription is too crude, what should actually govern the decision to pool or to split. I suspect the general direction is sound but that several of the load-bearing claims are not, and I would like to know which of my instincts to keep.
