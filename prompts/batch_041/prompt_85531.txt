So I'm midway through a phil of mind PhD (animal cognition mostly, but my supervisor keeps sending me to the insect neuro lab's reading group so I at least know what a mushroom body is), and last week I ended up refereeing a two-hour dinner fight between my two flatmates and I still can't decide. Maya does decision theory; Ravi does invertebrate neuro. Neither will budge and they've now decided I owe them a ruling.

Maya went first: whatever the exact figure, the numbers people throw around for insects are absurd — somewhere in the quintillions alive at any moment, plus however many trillions get farmed and killed every year — and nobody can rule out sentience in them, so even a tiny per-insect probability of mattering morally multiplies into a welfare total that dwarfs every vertebrate concern on the books. She says the form of the argument is fine and we should just follow it. Ravi said no, you're multiplying across a line you're not entitled to multiply across: there's a difference between a risk, where you know what the bad outcome is and just don't know if it'll happen, and a status question, where you don't know whether the thing even goes in the ledger, and expected value needs a fixed ledger — he called this "ledger-boundary" confusion and said you can't launder a degree of concept-application into a quantity. Maya said that line isn't real, that we cross it constantly (future people, animals, contested cases in medicine), and that even if it were real it would only block the arithmetic and not the decision, since some policy has to get chosen and the scale argument just says don't choose the one that's catastrophic if insects count. Ravi came back that the real problem is the conclusion never changes: any nonzero p times astronomical N beats everything, so the argument says insects win no matter what the evidence turns out to be, and a rule you can't update on in either direction isn't a discovery about insects, it's a decision rule wearing a lab coat — and you can't estimate that p to one significant figure anyway, so the whole thing is rhetoric with numbers attached. Maya's last word was that insensitivity to evidence is precisely what precaution means, and that p isn't un-updatable — nociceptors, learning, analgesia, all of it moves the estimate.

Can you adjudicate this move by move? Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion: points 1 through 4 should rate Maya's and Ravi's four moves in order, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating, and point 5 should say who is more right overall and what both of them are missing.
