I'm teaching a grad seminar that pairs Dennett's two-bitser with Searle's original/derived intentionality distinction, and I want the students to notice that the law is already quietly taking sides in that fight. Here's what bugs me. When a company's customer-service chatbot makes a promise its own policy doesnt allow, courts hold the company to the promise -- the chatbot's words are the company's words, as if the model had no content of its own. But the same company's defense is that the model "misunderstood" or "hallucinated," which sounds like we're treating the model as a genuine content-bearer that got things wrong. And meanwhile, in product-liability and safety regulation, we treat the thing's "perception" as a bare mechanism with no content at all. So: what view of intentionality does this cluster of practices actually presuppose? Is it consistently Searle -- derived content all the way down, the model borrowing whatever meaning it has from the humans around it -- or does the "it misunderstood" move commit us to something closer to original intentionality? Which rival theories does the practice rule out, and which does it merely fit comfortably with? And if you think the practice is inconsistent, dont just lay out the options -- pick the strand we should give up and defend it, and tell me what the doctrine looks like if we go all the way to one side, either full Searle (the model never has content, only its users do) or full Dennett (the intentional stance is the right level of description and the original/derived line was never doing any work). No citations needed, just the argument.
