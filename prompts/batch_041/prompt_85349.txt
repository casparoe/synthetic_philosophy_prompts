I am assembling a small interdisciplinary working group — four to six people for three years, drawn from philosophy, history of science, and Earth-system science — with a conceptual rather than empirical remit: we will not be coring sediments or building models. Our topic is the Anthropocene construed as a historical period, in the sense in which historians and geologists both use that word: a stretch of time with a defensible beginning, a boundary that can in principle be located, and some authority competent to fix it.

The stratigraphic route to settling this has recently stalled. A proposal to formalize the Anthropocene as an epoch, anchored in mid-twentieth-century plutonium fallout from nuclear weapons testing and a candidate reference site at Crawford Lake in Ontario, was voted down by the relevant stratigraphic subcommission in 2024, and the rejection was subsequently confirmed by the international bodies above it — even as the term continues to circulate widely in the humanities, social sciences, and public discourse. Meanwhile the candidate onsets remain in dispute, running from the Neolithic spread of agriculture, through the early-seventeenth-century dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide that some associate with the collapse of American populations after European contact, through eighteenth-century industrialization, to the mid-twentieth-century Great Acceleration.

Which conceptual questions should this group prioritize, and in what order? I want a defended agenda, not a survey, and not a reading list. For each question you rank, tell me why it sits upstream of the others, whether it is answerable by argument and conceptual analysis rather than by new measurements, and what would actually change downstream — in historiography, in Earth science, in law or conservation practice — if it were settled one way rather than another. Also tell me which apparently weighty questions are really disputes about how to use a word rather than about the world, and should therefore be deprioritized or dissolved.

To be clear about the criteria I care about: importance, tractability for people in armchairs and archives rather than laboratories, how many other questions depend on this one, and whether settling it would change anyone's behavior. Where those criteria pull apart, say so and say which you weight most.

Candidates worth considering, though I do not want you merely to reorder my list — add, cut, and reframe as you see fit: the authority question, i.e. whether there is a fact of the matter about when a geological period begins independent of the institutions empowered to declare it, and what follows for the concept if those institutions decline to declare it; the event-versus-epoch question, i.e. whether the human transformation of the Earth system is better modelled as a globally asynchronous, diachronous process than as a synchronous boundary, and what that does to the periodization project as a whole; the natural-kind question, i.e. whether the Anthropocene picks out a joint in nature or an evaluatively loaded category, and what evidence could distinguish those possibilities; the normative-entanglement question, i.e. whether any choice of onset date is inevitably a political act — indicting industrialization, colonialism, or the postwar order — and whether that counts against the category or is simply true of all periodization; and the downstream-utility question, i.e. what would actually change for environmental law, heritage designation, or conservation baselines if the boundary were fixed at one candidate date rather than another.

Do not just rank these five. Tell me which cut across which, where a decision on one question settles or dissolves another, where the group could make genuine progress in three years, and where it would only be re-litigating. Finally, if the funder could support only two of the questions you recommend, say which two you would keep and what you would tell them to cut.
