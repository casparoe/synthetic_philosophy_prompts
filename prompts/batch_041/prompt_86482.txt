I keep meeting a particular analogy in discussions of how strictly a society should enforce its norms. The cultural tightness-and-looseness literature frames a society's rules as its immune system: tight cultures are the ones kept on chronic alert against threat, looseness is what a culture can afford once the threat recedes, and the strictness of high-threat societies therefore comes out as an adaptive response rather than a failure of nerve. I have seen the same picture pressed into service for two further claims -- that loosening is safe wherever threat is genuinely low, and that strict enforcement in high-threat environments deserves deference rather than criticism. I want the analogy audited, not endorsed or waved away. Bear in mind that real immune systems do a great deal the tidy version leaves out: they are tuned to tolerate false alarms rather than miss a true threat, they routinely misclassify harmless things as hostile, and they can turn on the body that hosts them.

Please give me the following, in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. The Analogy as Its Users Deploy It (~100 words). State the mapping and the conclusion it is being made to license, in its strongest form.

2. Correspondence Table (one table, eight to ten rows). What plays the role of the pathogen, the barrier, the antibodies, immune memory, inflammation, the false alarm, autoimmune disease, and the metabolic cost of a standing immune response.

3. Where the Mapping Breaks (~400 words). Separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque. Give weight to the false-alarm and self-attack features of real immunity, and to anything a society has that an immune system lacks -- an internal opposition, a procedure for amending its own rules, a distinction between what it does and what it is entitled to do.

4. What the Analogy Quietly Imports (~250 words). Which features of the source domain are being carried into the target without argument, and what work does that smuggled cargo do?

5. Does the Conclusion Survive? (~250 words). Distinguish the explanatory claim (why tightness arises) from the justificatory claim (whether it ought to be enforced or deferred to). Say which, if either, the analogy can carry, and where the remaining argument has to come from.

6. A Better Analogy, If There Is One (~150 words). If some other comparison does the explanatory job without doing the justificatory work for free, name it and say what it buys and what it costs.
