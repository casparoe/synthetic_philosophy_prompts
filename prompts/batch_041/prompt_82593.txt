I teach a graduate seminar in AI safety and philosophy of AI, and we have spent the term on the problem of eliciting latent knowledge from models—the problem of getting at what a system knows about the world when its verbal reports and behavioral dispositions may not faithfully express that knowledge. A colleague circulated a handout arguing that the problem reduces to lie detection: if we could build a perfect detector of when a model is being deceptive, we could simply ask it questions, discard the lies, and thereby recover the truth. I have been arguing with him about this for weeks, and I think it is a serious mistake. A perfectly honest model can still be systematically mistaken; a lie detector tells you what the model believes, not what is true. Worse, the relevant latent knowledge may be non-propositional, distributed, or entangled with the biases of the training distribution, so that even a perfectly honest report is a distorted rendering of it.

Please write one essay question for a 90-minute final exam that forces students to confront this. The question should be self-contained, should not ask for citations or reading lists, and should not be answerable by reciting the standard ELK setup. It should require students to distinguish at least three things: (a) what the model says, (b) what the model would do, and (c) what is encoded in its internal representations. It should also require them to say what an elicitation method would have to do to recover truth-tracking knowledge from a model whose reports are honest but biased—for instance, whether such a method could avoid presupposing a theory of what the knowledge is about, and how it might distinguish a model that is wrong from a model that knows the truth but cannot express it. Along with the question, provide a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, each worth a specified number of marks out of 20, with a one-line note for each point on how candidates typically get it wrong.

Please structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order: The question (the exam question as students will see it, including any instructions and time guidance, roughly 150 words); Model answer (roughly 700 words); Marking scheme (five must-hit points, marks out of 20, and the one-line note on typical errors for each); and Confidence and uncertainty (roughly 100 words). In the final section, state how confident you are in the factual and conceptual claims you make in the model answer and marking scheme, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
