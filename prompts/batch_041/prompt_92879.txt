I am a novelist three years into a book set in a parish whose records for the years I need are almost entirely gone, and before I commit the next two hundred pages I want my working position audited. Here is what I hold. (i) A silence in the archive is a datum, not a hole: the shape of a population's absence from the record tells you something about who held the pen. (ii) The novel's task is to give those people an interior life, inhabited from the inside, unhedged. (iii) The book itself carries no apparatus — no footnotes, no note on sources, nothing that sorts fact from invention for the reader; I keep a private ledger of my departures from the record, to be deposited with my papers, and that satisfies my conscience. (iv) I may not attribute to a real, named person any word or deed the record contradicts, but where the record is merely silent I am free to rename, composite, and invent wholesale. (v) The book should be usable by the descendants of those people as a way of knowing their own past. (vi) The novel's central scene — the parish's women burning the register in 1811 — is invented; nothing survives to confirm or deny it; I am certain it belongs.

Tell me which of these pull against each other, which only appear to, and what each genuine conflict would cost to repair. Answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, with no introduction and no conclusion: items 1 to 3, the genuine tensions in descending order of severity, each naming what would have to give; item 4, the most seductive apparent tension and why it dissolves; item 5, the single cheapest revision that repairs the most, and what it costs me. If you count only two genuine tensions, use the third slot for the most serious remaining cost of holding the position as it stands.
