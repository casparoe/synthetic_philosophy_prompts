I serve on the steering committee of a 480-unit housing cooperative. We own and maintain six aging buildings, and the big decisions — annual budgets, special assessments, which contractor to hire, whether to replace the boilers or keep patching them, whether to sell a parcel of land to fund the roof — are all put to a vote of the full membership at quarterly meetings. Turnout runs around fifteen percent. Those who come are disproportionately retired members with free evenings; the structural engineers, accountants, and general contractors in the building mostly stay home, and several members have told me they abstain on anything technical because they do not feel competent to judge it.

A fellow committee member has proposed amending the bylaws. Every member would still get one vote, but any member who passes a short online quiz would have their vote counted twice. The quiz would cover the co-op's reserve fund, the difference between a capital and an operating expense, and the basics of building maintenance. It is open-book, drawn from our own documents, and may be retaken as many times as one likes, with answers explained after each attempt. Her argument is that decisions involving millions of dollars should not be settled by whoever happens to be free on a Tuesday evening, and that a member who will not spend twenty minutes learning what a reserve study is has no business choosing between two roofing bids. Two other members have signed on, and the amendment will be voted on at the next general meeting.

A different member has counter-proposed the opposite: select twenty members by lot, pay them a modest stipend, have them sit through two sessions with an independent engineer and an independent accountant, and let them put a recommendation to the full membership, which would then vote it up or down without amendment.

I have to decide what to support, and I want your help thinking it through. Two facts about our situation that I suspect matter. First, the committee that would write and administer the quiz is also the body that recommends contractors, and one of the current front-runners for the roof work employs the brother-in-law of our president. Second, our interests genuinely conflict: the retired members on fixed incomes want assessments held down, the younger families want the roof fixed now, and there is no neutral reading of the reserve study that resolves that disagreement for us.

Tell me whether the weighted-vote amendment is a good idea and what it would actually do to the quality of our decisions, not just to how they look; whether the sortition proposal is better or worse, and on what dimension; and what I should argue for at the meeting if neither is right. Be concrete about the mechanism — how many members would need to pass the quiz before it changed an outcome, what the quiz would end up selecting for once people learn it can be retaken indefinitely, and what happens when the members with extra weight are the same ones with a financial stake in the answer. I am not looking for a general defense of democracy or a general attack on expertise; I want to know what follows for this building, these numbers, and this ballot.
