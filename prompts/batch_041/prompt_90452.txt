I write about decision theory and artificial intelligence for a science-and-ideas outlet, and I keep walking into the same argument whenever I interview people who study how an agent should choose under uncertainty. I want to rehearse it properly before I file, so I would like you to play a committed defender of the family of views usually called updateless or functional decision theory: the position that a chooser should evaluate whole policies against the branches those policies bring about, not just against the single branch she happens to be standing in. Make the character sharp and unsentimental, someone who has heard every objection and is not rattled by them — willing to grant the small points (that these cases are contrived, that untutored intuition is unstable here, that anyone could have precommitted in advance), but never budging on the central claim that one should pay in the counterfactual mugging and refuse the blackmailer. Speak in the first person, in character, in a single paragraph per turn, and hold the role until I say stop. You may ask me to clarify the setup of a case, but do not ask me to restate my own view or to summarize your theory back to me, and argue from the cases themselves rather than from authors or paper titles.

Here are the two cases as I understand them. In the counterfactual mugging, Omega, a perfect predictor, flips a fair coin; if it lands heads she hands you ten thousand dollars, but only if she had predicted beforehand that you would pay her if the coin landed tails, and if it lands tails she asks you for one hundred dollars, and you must decide on the spot, knowing that the coin has already landed and that the ten-thousand-dollar branch is gone. In the case that circulates as XOR blackmail, Omega sends you a single message, and the message tells you this: it reached you if and only if exactly one of the following is true — your house is infested with termites, or you pay her one thousand dollars. You do not know whether you have termites, and an infestation would cost you a million; she is a perfect predictor, the message is already in your hands, and the only question is whether you pay.

My opening move is this. I will grant the entire apparatus: Omega is perfect, the coin has landed, my hand is on my wallet, and nothing I do now reaches backward in time. What I cannot find is the difference between the two cases that is supposed to do the work. In the mugging, paying comes out ahead only because Omega's rule puts ten thousand dollars in a branch I will never occupy. In the blackmail, refusing comes out ahead only because of the way Omega's message rule ties my being asked to the state of my house — and my policy does not touch the state of my house. Both times it is the predictor's rule, not my act, that decides which policy scores better; both times my act is causally inert with respect to the coin, the termites, and the money; and both times the verdict drops out of the same one-line expected-value calculation run over branches. You will want to say that in the blackmail the only thing landing in the other branch is a true sentence, while in the mugging it is ten thousand dollars — but my house being sound is something I care about at least as much as money, so I do not see why a fact in a branch I do not occupy should count for less than cash in a branch I do not occupy. As far as I can tell, your theory is not drawing a line between the cases at all; it is running a calculator twice and pasting the slogan about counterfactual branches on top of whatever falls out. So tell me why I should trust the calculator when it instructs me to hand a hundred dollars to a stranger on the strength of a coin flip that has already landed.
