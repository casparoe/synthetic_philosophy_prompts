The question, which she deliberately set in a modified form — she changed the number of participants and the size of the stake from the version discussed in the course text, so that the class could not reproduce from memory the treatment of the lottery Hofstadter ran in his Scientific American column — is printed as follows. Question 4 (12 marks). Three colleagues — Anne, Ben and Cara — are each invited to drop a sealed envelope into a box. Each acts independently, without communicating with the others, and each chooses a probability with which to drop her envelope; she may choose any probability between 0 and 1, including 0 and 1. If the box contains exactly one envelope when the exercise ends, its sender receives £900. If the box contains no envelopes, or two, or three, nobody receives anything. All three are equally rational, all three know the rules of the exercise, and each knows that the others will reason about it in exactly the way she does. (a) In the sense introduced by Hofstadter, in which a superrational agent asks what would happen if everyone placed in her position reasoned exactly as she does: what probability of dropping an envelope should each colleague choose, and what is each colleague's expected payoff at that probability? Show your reasoning. (6 marks) (b) Is the profile you identified in part (a) a Nash equilibrium of this game? Justify your answer. (3 marks) (c) What does the comparison between your answers to (a) and (b) show about the relation between superrationality and Nash equilibrium? (3 marks) The script I have to mark reads, in full, as follows. (a) The three colleagues occupy exactly the same position, so a superrational agent must assume that all three will settle on the same probability, p. My chance of winning is then the chance that I drop my envelope and that neither of the other two drops hers, which is p(1 − p)², so my expected payoff is 900p(1 − p)². Differentiating with respect to p and setting the derivative to zero gives 900(1 − p)(1 − 3p) = 0, so p = 1/3 (the other root, p = 1, is the case in which all three envelopes are dropped and nobody can win). It is worth noting that the same answer follows from the simpler thought that the group should arrange for the expected number of envelopes to be equal to the number of prizes on offer, that is, 3p = 1. At p = 1/3 the probability that exactly one envelope is dropped is 3 × (1/3) × (2/3)² = 4/9, so the expected total payout is 900 × 4/9 = £400, or £133.33 for each colleague. This is the largest total the three can extract from the exercise, and it is a good deal better than the £0 each of them would get if all three simply dropped their envelopes for certain. (b) Yes. The three colleagues are symmetric, and none of them can do better by choosing a different probability while the other two stay at 1/3 — any deviation would break the symmetry on which the calculation in (a) rests, and would therefore alter what the others do. So p = 1/3 for all three is the symmetric Nash equilibrium of the game, and indeed the only one. (c) The exercise is interesting because it separates two notions that are easily run together. A Nash equilibrium requires only that no colleague could have improved her payoff by changing her own probability on her own; superrationality requires her to ask what would happen if everyone in her position reasoned as she does, which is a stronger demand, because it ties the others' choices to her own. Here, though, the two notions come to the same thing: the colleagues' interests are perfectly aligned, since each of them wants exactly one envelope in the box, and so the probability that is best for each of them if all three reason alike is also the probability from which none of them can profitably depart. That is why the exercise is a good illustration of superrationality — it shows that superrational reasoning and ordinary equilibrium reasoning are not rivals but two routes to the same recommendation. The difference between them appears only where the payoffs are asymmetric, as in Newcomb's problem.
