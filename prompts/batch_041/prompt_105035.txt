At the science-and-society seminar I have been attending since I retired from the bench, two colleagues got into an argument over a talk we had just heard, and I found myself unable to take a side. The speaker, a clinical researcher, had presented a trial whose overall result was null but had led with a striking relative risk reduction from one small subgroup, mentioning the null overall result only in passing. Every sentence was accurate; the subgroup was, as far as I could tell, chosen after the fact. The exchange went roughly as follows. (1) A called it manipulation: the speaker had engineered a false impression by exploiting the audience's known tendency to overweight vivid subgroup findings and to discount how such subgroups get picked. (2) B said that if that is manipulation, then every choice of emphasis is, since a talk must select, and selection without falsehood is not deception — the audience could always read the paper. (3) A answered that selecting for relevance differs from selecting to exploit a bias, and that the speaker chose this subgroup precisely because of how listeners would misread it, so both the intent and the mechanism condemn it. (4) B replied that "manipulation" is then merely a name for persuasion one dislikes, and proposed what he called the audience-endorsement test: if the audience, on reflection, would still prefer to be told the vivid number first because it spurs them to read further, then nothing about their agency was bypassed, and whether it was bypassed depends on the audience, not on the speaker. (5) A granted the point about reflection but added that one can manipulate without intending to — a speaker who sincerely believes the subgroup is the real story may still arrange the facts so as to bypass scrutiny — so intent cannot be the whole of it.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each of the five moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. I would also like to know what, if anything, both parties are missing, and who is more nearly right overall.
