A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about the singleton hypothesis, and I came away still holding the view I went in with, but with no clear sense of what would have to happen for me to give it up. That is what I want from you.

The position, stated as sharply as I can. Take "singleton" in the sense the existential-risk literature gives it: an order in which a single agency sits at the top and is effective enough that no combination of the agents beneath it could defy it. My claims are three. First, some singleton is the likely long-run equilibrium for any civilization that lasts long enough to have the problem, because the highest-level coordination problems carry the largest payoffs, so whatever solves them is selected for. Second, the real content of the hypothesis lies in a distinction my friend and I improvised over the course of the evening: between a coordination singleton, where no agent could act against the combined will of the rest and the order is held together by deterrence and aligned incentives, and a command singleton, where one agent could prevail against all the others together. Third, the attractor sits nearer the command end, at least once a general-purpose capability like AI is in play, because capability advantages compound and a decisive lead can be locked in.

My friend's parting shots were several. He said the distinction is a distinction without a difference, and that the hypothesis as stated cannot lose: any order durable enough will look singleton-like from far enough away, and any order that comes apart will be redescribed as never having been a true singleton. He noted that concentration and fragmentation have alternated for as long as we have records — empires accrete and then shatter — so an attractor claim owes an account of why the ratchet only clicks one way. He added that control has diseconomies of scale, that the largest structures of control are typically the most fragile because the returns on further complexity fall off, and that the same local-knowledge problem that was supposed to sink central planning would apply to any top-level controller, however capable. I don't think he is right, but I could not say precisely why, and I suspect that if I start patching the view I will end up with something unfalsifiable, which I would rather learn now.

So: what would count against the position? Sort the defeaters by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a defender of the view would themselves concede as counting against it, not only which ones critics would press. Say what the view predicts that its rivals do not, where its rivals include durable multipolarity and the flat denial that there is any attractor at all. Tell me whether the coordination/command distinction does real work or merely insulates the claim, and whether the view survives only by retreating to a long enough timescale, or a loose enough notion of agency, that it stops saying anything. It would also help to know whether the hypothesis is even well defined about scope: a singleton over the planet and a singleton over the accessible universe are different claims, and the second does not follow from the first.

What would move me most is a mechanism by which a multipolar order stays stable once a general capability is widely available, or an argument that the selection story is equivocal rather than one-directional. If the honest answer is that the view is well insulated, say so, and tell me whether that is a defect in it or simply a feature of long-run structural claims.
