I am a third-year undergraduate in an upper-level seminar on perception and early modern metaphysics, and in two weeks I present my term paper to the group: twenty-five minutes of presentation, then roughly twenty minutes of questions. The paper defends a qualified version of Berkeley's master argument, and what I want from you is not more argument but a question-period prep sheet.

The thesis, compressed: the argument is valid, but its premise is defensible only if "conceive" is read as "imagine." An idea of a sensible thing is always a perceptual situation with a standpoint built into it, so anything I genuinely conceive, I conceive as perceived or perceivable from somewhere, and I can hold that line under pressure. What I deny is that any of this touches supposition. I can suppose that a tree exists with nobody perceiving it, and supposing delivers a sentence rather than a scene: no standpoint, no imagery, nothing presented to the mind. So the honest conclusion is that an unperceived sensible thing cannot be imagined, not that it cannot be supposed, and the reason the argument has always looked like a trick is that a single word covers both acts and the slide between them is invisible. I then argue that this weakened conclusion is still enough for the argument's purpose, because the opponent it is aimed at is already committed to explaining meaning in terms of ideas in the mind.

The room is about fifteen people, and I know them well enough to say what each faction will do. Four early modern specialists read Berkeley historically, distrust reconstruction, and will want to know what licenses my reading rather than a more familiar one. Three analytic metaphysicians use conceivability as evidence for possibility and will press on whether my distinction marks a real difference or only a redescription. Two people from philosophy of mind who follow the imagery debates will say my key premise is an empirical claim that is probably false. Two Berkeley-friendly graduate students will think I have conceded away the conclusion. I already know the stock objections — equivocation on "conceive," the de dicto/de re point, the charge that the argument proves too much — so do not spend space introducing them; I need them in this room's vocabulary, and I need the objections that are specific to my reconstruction.

Give me the objections in the order they will actually arrive, each phrased the way the group that raises it would phrase it, ranked by how much damage it does to the paper; for each, a two-minute spoken reply and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what to concede in my first two minutes to take the sting out, and which objections I can dispatch in a sentence without seeming evasive. End with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and my best answer to it. Keep every reply self-contained — I will not have the texts in front of me, and I should not be leaning on what commentators are supposed to have said. Write in American English.
