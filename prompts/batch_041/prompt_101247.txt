In my department's reading group on scientific method, a colleague repeatedly invokes Occam's razor — in her mouth, "the simpler explanation is the more likely one" — to close disputes. Last week she used it to dismiss another member's suggestion that a cluster of anomalous measurements reflected a single systematic instrument fault rather than a run of independent rare events, and on earlier occasions she has used it against interpretations of quantum mechanics that posit additional branches or hidden variables. I can never tell what she has actually committed herself to, or whether the appeal is doing any work. Please set out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making, arranged as a menu I can bring to the next meeting, and give a verdict on each: which are near-trivial restatements of what a rational inquirer does anyway, which are substantive and defensible, and which are simply false. I would like the readings to include at least a merely pragmatic rule of theory choice, the claim that complexity enters as a penalty on prior probability in something like a Solomonoff-style universal prior, the claim that the world itself is algorithmically simple, and the claim that compressibility by a short program tracks truth. Along the way I want to know where the Kolmogorov-complexity reading and the Bayesian-prior reading come apart — including whether the complexity of a hypothesis is even well-defined independently of a chosen description language, and what follows for the razor's practical use if it is not — and whether "fewer entities" and "shorter description" and "fewer free parameters" pick out the same or different theses. Finally, say which reading my colleague must be running if her two dismissals are to be licensed by it, and what question I could put to her to find out.
