I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am at work on an essay about free will that uses Groundhog Day as its central exhibit. The film is famous enough that I can assume you know it: Phil Connors, the weatherman, wakes each morning to the same February 2 in Punxsutawney; he repeats the day until, after many iterations, he becomes the person the town needs and the loop releases him. What I want from you is a reading of the film through the idea of sphexishness—the wasp that, when its cricket is moved while it inspects its burrow, simply begins the inspection routine again, as though nothing had happened. The philosophical question is not whether Phil is literally a wasp, but whether the film gives us any principled way to say that his eventual flexibility is not just a longer, more complex version of the wasp's loop.

I would like you to press on a distinction I have been trying to formulate, and to tell me whether it survives contact with the film. Call the first-order sphexish case one in which an agent repeats a fixed sequence because the sequence is triggered by the same cues; the wasp is first-order sphexish. Call the second-order case one in which an agent can vary its first-order routines, learn new ones, and represent the fact that it is doing so, but cannot revise the deeper rules by which it selects and revises routines; such an agent would be flexible in output and rigid in architecture. The film seems to move Phil from the first condition toward something like the second, or perhaps beyond it. Does it? And if it does, is that movement enough for freedom in any interesting sense, or is it just the difference between a short leash and a long one?

Some specific pressure points. Early Phil is plainly sphexish: he tries the same seductions, the same escapes, the same suicides, and the day resets. Later Phil acquires skills—piano, ice sculpting—and begins to use the day to help others. The film treats this as growth. But the loop's exit condition is never stated, and that omission matters. If the day ends only when Phil has become genuinely good, then the loop looks like a training schedule, and his final goodness looks like an operant response shaped by reinforcement. If the day ends when he stops trying to escape and accepts the day, then the film may be making a different claim about freedom, one closer to acceptance than to control. And if the day ends for no reason at all, the film may be cheating, because it has given us a transformation without a criterion. I want you to adjudicate among these readings, and to say which one the film actually supports and which one it merely invites.

I also want the reading to be honest about where the film resists the philosophical lens. Groundhog Day is a romantic comedy, and its resolution is sentimental: Rita's love, or Phil's worthiness of it, coincides with the loop's end. That coincidence may be doing more work than any theory of agency. Does the film confuse being loved with being free? Does it confuse moral improvement with the acquisition of a policy that happens to satisfy an unseen experimenter? And does the sphexishness frame make the film darker than it wants to be, by suggesting that Phil's hard-won character is itself a routine—one that the loop, not Phil, selected?

Finally, I would like the essay to say what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the wasp and the compatibilism literature do not. Does it dramatize something about the phenomenology of repetition that arguments miss—the boredom, the despair, the way a reason can feel like a compulsion from the inside? Does it show that flexibility comes in degrees and that the interesting question is not whether we are sphexish but which loop we are in? And does it offer any criterion, however implicit, for distinguishing a free act from a merely novel one? Please write the reading as criticism, not as a summary, and do not reduce either the film or the idea to an illustration of the other. The piece needs to be rigorous enough for philosophers but readable for the rest of us.
