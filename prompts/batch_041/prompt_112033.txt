I am a student in a third-year course on social choice and democratic theory, and I have been asked to conduct a public interview with a visiting scholar for our departmental student paper. The guest is not a public figure, so please treat him as an unnamed defender of the following position. He argues that the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem is routinely overread. On his reading, the theorem establishes only that no resolute, onto social choice function on an unrestricted domain of ordinal preferences is both strategy-proof and non-dictatorial; it does not establish that every non-dictatorial collective decision procedure is manipulable. He regards the familiar escape routes—restricting the domain to single-peaked preferences, allowing randomization, introducing cardinal utility or transfers, and weakening strategy-proofness to Bayesian incentive compatibility—as changes of subject rather than genuine counterexamples, because each relaxes one of the theorem's background conditions. His positive proposal is to distinguish strategy-proofness as a property of a mechanism from what he calls strategy-proofness for the right reasons, which asks whether a mechanism's incentive properties survive when we vary the informational assumptions we are willing to make about voters.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having him restate the theorem. I want at least one question each on: the distinction between a resolute social choice function and a social choice correspondence, and whether returning a menu or set of outcomes avoids the theorem; the random-mechanism analogue of the theorem and whether random dictatorship is an escape or a reductio; the single-peaked and median-voter case as a domain restriction, and whether multidimensional policy spaces make that escape fragile; the difference between dominant-strategy strategy-proofness and Bayesian incentive compatibility, and whether the latter is a genuine weakening or merely a change of informational assumptions; transfer-based mechanisms such as VCG, and whether adding money changes the subject; the difference between individual and group strategy-proofness, and whether coalitional manipulation is the deeper problem; the revelation principle and which equilibrium concept the theorem really needs; and what he takes the theorem's proper normative upshot to be, given that he rejects both the libertarian reading that markets or experts should decide and the elitist reading that democracy is impossible. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and make the follow-ups press him where the position is weakest. If you have room, add one question on whether the theorem predicts too much or too little about observed strategic voting. Please phrase the questions in the register of a serious but not hostile graduate-student interviewer.
