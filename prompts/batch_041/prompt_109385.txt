I have been working through the White Horse dialogue, the most famous surviving piece of the group later labelled the School of Names, and I have written its argument out in the most general form I can manage. It is the general form that worries me. I take the dialogue to be defending the proposition that a white horse is not a horse, and I take its opening move to be that horse is the name by which one picks things out according to shape, white is the name by which one picks things out according to colour, and what picks out according to colour is not what picks out according to shape. Here is the whole thing:

1. A name is fixed by the work it does: horse picks things out by shape, white picks things out by colour.
2. What picks out by colour is not what picks out by shape.
3. So white and horse are not the same name; they do different work.
4. A compound does the work of the name it contains and more besides: white horse contains horse, but adds a restriction by colour.
5. So white horse does not do the work that horse does. One who is looking for a horse will accept a yellow or a black horse; one who is looking for a white horse will not.
6. So white horse and horse are not the same name, and do not have the same range.
7. So a white horse is not a horse.
8. Nothing here turns on the colour. One who is looking for a dog will accept a white dog; one who is looking for a brown dog will not. So brown dog is not dog, and in general MN is not N, for any modifier M and head N.
9. The same pattern applies to the modifier: white horse is not white either. A white horse is therefore neither white nor a horse, and a hard white stone is neither hard nor white. And since any thing fails to answer to some compound built on the name it bears — some colour, size or place that is not its — nothing is ever what it is called.

I cannot accept 9, and I doubt anyone can. But I also cannot see which earlier step to give up, and 1 and 5 are the two I would least like to lose. I have tried to close off the exit I expected to take, namely that the argument trades on the ambiguity of the word for is not, by keeping every step in terms of the work a name does and what answers to it rather than in terms of identity or membership; if the fault is still an ambiguity, I need to be told exactly where it survives that restatement.

What I want from you is a diagnosis: which step to reject, why it looked acceptable in the first place, and an honest price for the alternatives. I am also unsure what becomes of the Advocate's thesis once the repair is made — whether something substantive survives for him, or whether the dialogue comes out as pure sophistry, and if so which of the two readings of it, as a claim about names or as a claim about animals, is the one that has to go. Finally, does the same diagnosis dispose of the companion thesis of the Hard and White dialogue, on which hardness and whiteness are separate and a hard white stone is, as I read it, two rather than one? If it does, say what the shared error is; if it does not, say what the difference is.
