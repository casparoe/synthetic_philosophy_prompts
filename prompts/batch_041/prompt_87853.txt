Assume the role of a philosopher working at the intersection of the metaphysics of mind and normative ethics, and think carefully before you answer; I want a considered analysis, not a survey of the literature.

I teach a graduate seminar that keeps circling back to a puzzle I have never seen named cleanly, and I would like your help in working out whether it already has a home. Suppose one takes seriously the family of views on which experience, or something very close to it, is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the physical world, so that the constituents of ordinary matter are not merely correlated with experience but are, in some thin sense, subjects. When my students then ask what this implies for suffering, the discussion splits in a way that strikes me as symptomatic rather than substantive. One group reasons that if experiential states are everywhere, then almost any physical transaction -- a rock weathering, a photon striking a retina, the thermal jostling of a gas -- becomes a candidate site of something morally considerable, and the upshot is a moral status that is at once enormous and utterly without joints. The other group reasons that since these micro-subjects have no unified perspective, no interests, and no capacity to be harmed as a whole, none of it matters, and the view therefore has no ethical consequences at all. What unsettles me is that both moves seem to be driven by the same underlying gap: once experience is treated as fundamental, there is no non-arbitrary principle fixing where a subject of experience begins and ends, and hence no non-arbitrary principle fixing the bearer of moral status. The dispute, in other words, is not about whether suffering is bad or about any empirical fact, but about the individuation of the moral subject under a metaphysics that refuses to privilege the organism.

My question is whether this specific configuration has a name. I am aware of the combination problem and of the problem of the many, and I suspect what I am describing is adjacent to both, but neither seems to capture it: the combination problem asks how micro-experiences yield a macro-experience, and the problem of the many asks which of several overlapping candidate objects is the real one; what I am after is the ethical face of the same difficulty -- the absence of a principled boundary that would let us say which physical goings-on have a subject who can be wronged. If there is an established term, give it to me and tell me precisely what it covers and what it leaves out. If there is not, please coin one, define it, and do the hard part: distinguish it from the combination problem, from the problem of the many, from a mere sorites-style boundary problem, and from the general thesis that moral status comes in degrees. I want the carving rather than the label -- what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundary cases lie. Begin by brainstorming the candidate diagnoses before you commit to one.
