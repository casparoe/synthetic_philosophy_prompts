I'm in an ethics seminar on the good life and we keep circling the moral saint question -- whether a life that's maximally morally good is actually a life you'd want, or admire without reservation. I lost an argument about it at a bar afterwards, so now I want to be rigourous about it. Before I read anything else, I want the spec: what would any adequate account of the relation between living morally well and living an admirable/good life have to deliver? Not which account is right -- what the requirements on one are. So: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the features of how people actually live that it can't just explain away as weakness. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just house preferences of one camp (consequentialists, Kantians, virtue people). I'm using this to sort views for a seminar paper, so I don't need it to settle the first-order question for me -- I need the criteria to be good enough to exclude things.

Some raw material so you aren't starting from the usual examples. I keep thinking about two figures. First, the pervasively moral person: everything is oriented toward doing good, they'd never take a holiday they could have donated, and there's no compartment of life that morality doesn't reach into. Second, the one-big-thing person: donates a kidney, or spends five years caring for a parent, or gives away half their income for a decade, and is otherwise pretty ordinary and fairly self-interested. Does an account have to treat those as the same kind of thing, or can it say they differ in kind -- and what does it cost either way? I also want the requirement list to make room for what I'll call uncompensated loss: the sense that a life can be morally flawless and still be a waste of a life. The loss isn't a moral cost, the agent isn't irrational for accepting it, and nobody was wronged -- but it's still a loss, and it feels like a theory that can't register it is missing something. And then the asymmetry: we admire the saint, but most of us don't want to be one or want our kids to be one, and I don't think that's automatically just weakness or hypocrisy.

The part I most care about is whether some of these requirements can't be had together. Specifically whether all three of these can hold at once: (1) morality is impartial and gives no special weight to the agent's own projects, relationships and attachments; (2) there's no principled ceiling on how much morality can ask of you; (3) a life spent meeting those demands can still be a fully good human life rather than a diminished one. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly -- that's the actual result I want out of this. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work, i.e. knocks out the most candidate accounts on its own. You can close with a short note on how demandingness-first views, Kantian duty views, and virtue-ethical views fare against the list, but keep that part brief, I'll do the scoring in class. Also, for what it's worth, I started giving away a chunk of my income this year and it's been weirdly corrosive in a way I did not expect, so this stopped being academic for me around March.
