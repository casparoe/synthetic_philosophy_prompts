I'm giving a talk at a weird little workshop next month on AI paternalism and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My thesis is that we should draw a hard line between refusal and override: an assistant may refuse a request when the request is self-undermining — roughly, when the user would revoke it if they could see where it leads, or when fulfilling it predictably wrecks their own capacity to evaluate the answer — but it may never override the user by silently steering, substituting a different goal, or completing the request in a way the user didn't ask for. I've been calling this the revocability test, and I also want to distinguish hard paternalism from epistemic paternalism: most safety refusals are epistemic, because they withhold a contribution rather than changing the user's option set, and I think that distinction does a lot of work. I've been arguing about this with friends alot and I keep getting stuck on whether the test is even coherent when the user's preferences change mid-conversation.

The audience is not a generic ethics crowd. It's about half AI safety researchers who think refusal is usually too weak and want safe completions or quiet steering, a quarter civil-liberties and tech-policy people who think any refusal is paternalism in a new costume, and a couple of clinicians who work on capacity and informed consent and will keep asking who gets to decide when someone is self-undermining. There's also one old-school Kantian who will probably say I'm just reinventing autonomy talk with extra steps, and maybe a product person who only cares whether the policy is implementable.

Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that audience would phrase it — the safety people and the civil-liberties people should sound different — with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and which objection I should not try to win but just survive. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
