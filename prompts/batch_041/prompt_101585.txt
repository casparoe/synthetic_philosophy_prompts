I am a high-school student, and I run the philosophy club at my school. This term the club has been working through paradoxes of self-reference — the liar, the sorites, the surprise examination — and for the last few weeks we have been playing a self-amending game of the kind I have read about, in which the players do not merely act within the rules but vote to change them, so that the rules of the game are themselves moves in the game. Playing it produced an argument that we could not settle at the table, and I would like to turn that argument into a written problem set, so that members have to reason it out on paper instead of talking over one another. I am asking you to write that problem set for me.

Here is the game as I have written it for the club. Ten players are present. The rules, in the order they were adopted, are these:

Rule 1. Any player may, at the start of their turn, propose a change to any rule, including this one.
Rule 2. A proposed change is adopted if it receives the level of support required by the rule it changes; if the rule it changes states no level of its own, the change requires a simple majority of all players present.
Rule 3. This rule may be changed only with the unanimous support of all players.
Rule 4. If a single proposal changes more than one rule, it is adopted only if it meets the strictest requirement among the rules it changes.
Rule 5. If two rules conflict, the rule with the lower number prevails.

The argument arose over this case. On her turn, a player proposes a single proposal with two parts: first, Rule 3 is repealed; second, Rule 2 is replaced by a rule reading that a proposed change is adopted if it receives the support of more than half of all players present. The vote is seven in favour and three against.

Please write me three things.

First, the question itself. It should have two parts. Part (a) should ask whether the proposal was validly adopted, and should be framed so that a student cannot answer it by picking the answer they like and asserting it — the question should force them to see that the stated rules do not by themselves settle the matter, and to say what would have to be added to settle it. Part (b) should take the same proposal, suppose instead that the vote had been ten to nothing, and then suppose the club immediately passes, by a bare majority, a second proposal restoring Rule 3 and the old Rule 2; it should ask whether the club can bind itself in that way and whether anything in the rules forbids it. The whole question should be answerable in about forty minutes and should be worth twenty marks. It must be answerable from the rules I have printed above: the members will have no internet access, and I do not want the question to turn on knowing the name of any philosopher, book, or real constitution, or on citing anything. A student who merely drops a famous name should earn nothing for it; only the reasoning should count.

Second, a model answer of about six hundred words, written at the level of a strong high-school student who has read a little philosophy but has taken no university course. It should reach a definite position on part (a) while being honest that the rules underdetermine it, and it should deal with the strongest objection to its own position.

Third, a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, four marks each, for a total of twenty. For each point, give one line on the characteristic way a student misses it. Please make the scheme separate an A answer from a B answer by naming the specific moves the A answer must contain, and make sure it flags the moves that look impressive but should cost marks — in particular, I am suspicious of any answer that reaches for Rule 5 as though it decided the case, and I would like to know whether that suspicion is justified.
