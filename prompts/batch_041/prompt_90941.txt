Two members of my school's philosophy club argued at the end of last week's meeting, and I would like a careful adjudication of the exchange rather than a summary of the theory behind it. The theory is David Lewis's account of convention as a solution to a coordination problem. The argument was about a practice in our own cafeteria. I am reporting it as a bystander: I have no stake in either side, and I will not say which of them I find more convincing.

The practice. About twenty of us eat lunch at one long table. Whoever sits at the far end gets up whenever anyone needs something — cutlery, a napkin, the sauce bottle — and nobody at the near end ever does. No one planned this. Two years ago a student sitting at that end fetched the salt because it was nearest to her, and by the end of that term the table simply worked that way. New students pick it up within a week and nobody explains it to them. It is not written down, no teacher enforces it, and the student at the far end has never been asked whether she wanted that seat.

Ravi's case. There is a recurrent coordination problem here: things are needed, somebody has to fetch them, and nobody wants a scene about who goes. The far-end solution is arbitrary in the relevant sense — any of several arrangements would have solved the problem equally well, including taking turns. No one agreed to it and no one has to like it; on Lewis's account all that is needed is that each of us conforms because we expect the others to, and that this is common knowledge. That it grew out of one person's single action rather than a decision changes nothing. This is precisely why it is so durable: each new student conforms because they see everyone else doing it, so the practice reproduces itself without anyone ever endorsing it. And the tell is that it needs no enforcement — no one has to be threatened into it, because going along is already each person's best response to what the others are doing.

Mei's four points. (1) The condition that everyone prefers to conform given that the others do is satisfied by any settled pattern of behaviour whatsoever, even one everybody resents; if that is the test, it cannot separate a convention from a rut, so Ravi's no-enforcement tell proves nothing. (2) The student at the far end does not prefer the arrangement; she prefers it to being the only one who does not fetch — and that alternative is bad only because the others would think less of her. So this is not a coordination solution at all but a norm held up by disapproval, which is a different thing. (3) Arbitrary is doing illicit work: the far-end rule is not arbitrary as between the two ends, since it lands on the same person every time. It looks arbitrary only if you ignore who pays, and once you count that, the alternatives are not equally good and the condition fails. (4) The evolutionary story makes matters worse rather than neutral: nobody chose it, so there is nobody to complain to, and the fact that it arrived by precedent is exactly why it feels like a fact of nature instead of an arrangement.

Ravi's reply. Mei wants the account to certify arrangements, and it was never meant to; it is an analysis of a kind of regularity, not a justification of one. Disapproval disqualifies nothing, since disapproval is one of the things that can keep mutual expectations in place, so her second point describes a mechanism the account absorbs. Her third point is his point: he is not claiming the arrangement is good or that it could not be swapped for a fairer one, only that the problem it solves does not single out this solution, which is why the problem cannot be cited in its defence. If the account is too weak to tell a convention from a rut, that is a defect in the account, not in his description of the table.

Mei's answer to that. His reply concedes what she was after: an account that cannot distinguish a convention from an entrenched unfair arrangement cannot explain why anyone should go along with it, so he cannot move from "it is a convention" to "there is nothing here to object to" — and his no-enforcement line was exactly that move. Ravi says he never made that move. Mei says he made it twice.

Please work through this move by move. For each of Mei's four numbered points, say whether it is right, wrong, or right for the wrong reason, and do the same for Ravi's claims; then say what each of them is missing; then give your own verdict on whether the far-end practice is a convention on Lewis's account, whether the account needs amending if it is, and whether calling it a convention gives anyone at the table a reason to keep doing it. I am not asking for references or for the history of this debate — I want the verdict.
