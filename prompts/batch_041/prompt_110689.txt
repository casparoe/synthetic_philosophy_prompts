Our design team is redoing the winter fuel payment leaflet — it goes out to alot of people with low literacy, plenty of them who's first language isn't English, and legally the exact amount and the exact deadline have to appear on it somewhere. Our designer wants to pull the numbers out of the body text and let a row of pictograms carry it instead: house with a flame, calendar, coin. When I said people still need the actual date, she quoted a line she says comes from a 1920s Vienna museum that did picture statistics — "To remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures" — and moved on. She's also adamant the icons should need no key or legend, on the grounds that if you need a key, the picture has failed.

I don't think she's simply wrong, but I can't tell what that sentence is actually claiming. It might be a claim about memory, or about what a leaflet is for, or about the figures themselves being misleading, or something else entirely. Can you give me the menu — the distinct precise things the line could mean, which of them are trivially true, which are real and defensible, which are false, and for each one whether it would actually get her the two things she's using it for (dropping the numbers, no key)? Where the readings pull against each other, tell me which one her use of it actually requires. I want something I can read out at the next meeting, not an essay.
