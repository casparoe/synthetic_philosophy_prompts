I'm a graduate student who has been volunteered to open the discussion at a moral psychology reading group, and I've realized I don't have a firm enough grip on what we are even asking of a theory of moral worth. Before I defend any particular line, I want the specification written out in full. Take it that we are after an account of when an action is performed for the right reasons in the sense that earns the action a distinctive kind of moral credit — not merely the sense in which the action happens to be right, and not merely the sense in which the agent is a decent person. Give me the desiderata: what any adequate theory of that has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement.

Here is what I want on the list, though I expect you to add to it and to tell me which items are genuine requirements rather than the prejudices of one camp. The cases: the honest shopkeeper who charges fair prices only because honesty is good for business, whom we want to say acts rightly but without moral worth, and where the theory must explain why the motive disqualifies rather than merely fails to add anything; the reluctant agent who does the right thing from a sense of duty while resenting every minute of it; the case of someone who acts against his own settled moral verdict — he believes he is doing wrong by helping a fugitive — but from a compassion that tracks the real moral reasons, and where many of us want to call the act morally worthy even though he is not acting from his conception of what is right; the overdetermined case, where duty and friendship both move the agent, so that the act would have been done for the right reason even had the other motive been absent; the case where the agent does the right thing under a description that is false (he helps because he takes the stranger for his brother, though what makes the help right is the stranger's need); and the moral analogue of a Gettier case, where the right-making feature is something the agent could not have known. The distinctions I take it the theory cannot collapse: between the moral worth of an action, the praiseworthiness of the person, and the rightness of the action; between acting for a reason and merely having one, and between doing so under one description and doing so under another; between credit for getting it right and credit for trying hard; and between the worth of the motive and the worth of the act.

Beyond the cases, tell me which structural constraints the theory has to respect — for instance, that moral worth should attach to something within the agent's control, that it should not be hostage to the very luck the cases above turn on, that it should leave room for morally worthy action done without deliberation or self-conscious reasoning, and that it should not make moral worth unavailable to someone who does the right thing for the right reasons while believing, on balance, that the act is merely permissible rather than required. Then, and this is the part I most want: flag any subset of these requirements that you think cannot be jointly satisfied. My own suspicion is that the fault line runs between the demand that the agent act for the reason that is in fact the right-making one and the demand that moral worth not be a matter of luck, and I would like you to tell me whether that is the real pressure point or a red herring, and what each side of the current debate — the duty-driven Kantians, the motive-centred Humeans, the reasons-responsive accounts, and the knowledge-based accounts — has to give up in order to escape it. You can close with a short verdict on which single requirement eliminates the most candidates, but keep the weight on the criteria; I will do the scoring of particular authors myself.
