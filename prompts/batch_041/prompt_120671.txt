Someone in my department keeps ending arguments with 'only people act — the rest is just a way of talking.' Last week it was aimed at me. I'd said that the hiring norm in our field tightened over the 2010s and that nobody in particular tightened it: it tightened because everyone was adjusting to everyone else. He says thats not a thing. Theres no 'field' that acts, there are just a few hundred people who each made a choice, and since none of them intended the shift, my sentence is a colorful way of describing their choices rather than a real explanation of anything.

I half agree with him and I'm fairly sure he's cheating, but I can't say exactly where. Can you take 'only people act' and spell out the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making, ordered from the boring to the wild — a claim about what exists, a claim about what group-subject sentences mean, a claim about which explanations are legitimate, a claim about whether a group can decide or believe anything, and whatever else is hiding in there. For each one, say whether its trivial, substantive, or false, and what it would need to be true about group predication: in particular whether every true sentence whose subject is a group can be paraphrased without loss into sentences about individuals, what 'loss' is supposed to mean there, and what we do with the cases where nobody did the thing named (nobody raised the rate, nobody tightened the norm, yet the sentence looks true).

Then tell me which of the readings actually gets him my claim and which ones just sound like they do. My suspicion is that the ones doing the work for him are either analytic or false, and that the ones that survive scrutiny leave my explanation alone. Lay it out as a menu I can bring to him, not a verdict.
