I'm in a fortnightly reading group that has spent the better part of a year on the coherence arguments for expected-utility maximisation, and two of our members have been circling each other since about week three. The first holds that the representation theorems, together with the exploitation arguments, establish that an agent who violates the relevant axioms is irrational — not that she is imprudent or exploitable, but that the axioms are not optional ideals at all, being partly constitutive of what it is to have preferences. The second is happy to grant every theorem and every money pump and denies precisely that: on his view the theorems show only that a sufficiently well-behaved preference structure can be represented by a utility function together with a probability function, that representability is not requirement, and that for an agent with vague, multi-dimensional or context-sensitive preferences the axioms look less like rationality than like an aesthetic imposed on the data. His stock example is a friend who cycles between three flats differing only in rent, commuting time and floor space, and who cannot be argued out of the cycle.

What I want from you is the document these two should have produced instead of another year of talking past one another: a joint statement they could both put their names to. Open with a numbered section of claims both can sign — the mathematical results and what they do and do not entail, what each side concedes about the other's examples, anything both agree is simply not in dispute — and keep it non-vacuous, so that neither of them can read it and say that it is not his view. Follow it with the residual disagreements set out as a table, each stated in wording that the side holding it would accept as its own position and the other side would accept as a fair statement of what it is rejecting. Then the cruxes. I would guess that one is whether the axioms are constitutive of preference or substantive normative claims, and that another is whether the argument's force depends on the agent being exploitable — that is, whether a one-shot intransitivity that no one ever gets round to pumping is already a rational failure, or only a liability in the presence of an adversary who can run you in circles. But I would far rather you told me what the real cruxes are than accept my guesses, and where a dispute turns out to be about words rather than substance, say which words.

For each crux, give a case, argument or result that both sides would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance, in a form the other side would accept as a genuine prediction rather than a restatement of the position. Carry the three-flats case through as a running example wherever it earns its keep, including a money pump that could actually be run on it, since I want to see whether the two of them disagree about the case itself or only about what follows from it. Please write this as a formal joint statement rather than a dialogue or a commentary, and resist the temptation to resolve the disagreement in a closing paragraph — the whole value of the exercise lies in the exactness of what remains open. End with a short section on what either side would count as evidence against its own view, because I suspect that at least one of them has never thought about it.
