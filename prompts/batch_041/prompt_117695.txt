I help maintain the standard library of a formal proof assistant, and I am the reviewing editor for its analysis, algebra, and topology sections. A substantial fraction of incoming pull requests depend, usually without the author's awareness, on some form of the axiom of choice, and our current practice is a single boolean field labelled "uses AC". That field is too coarse and too often wrong. It does not distinguish countable choice from dependent choice from the full axiom; it is filled in by authors who have never had to make the distinction; and it misses proofs that invoke a choice principle without noticing — selecting a representative from each equivalence class, silently fixing an enumeration for each member of a countable family, passing to a well-ordering, or appealing to an imported lemma that already depends on choice. Our users are split. Some only want to know that a theorem holds in ordinary mathematics; others extract executable programs from our proofs and cannot tolerate an unmarked non-constructive step. For the second group a mislabelled theorem is worse than a missing one.

Write me a procedure I can put in our contributor guidelines and also run myself during review. It should be a sequence of questions or tests in the order I should ask them, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, an explicit point at which the procedure returns a verdict (accept as choice-free, accept with a particular label, require a rewrite, or reject), and the points at which it must hand the decision to a human because the question is one of library policy rather than of mathematics — for instance, what we are willing to add to our base theory, which our maintainers' committee decides and I cannot. Assume the person running the procedure has a solid undergraduate background and knows the statement of the axiom of choice, but has never had to distinguish countable choice from dependent choice; introduce whatever further distinctions the procedure needs inside the procedure itself, and assume no access to the literature. Budget fifteen minutes per theorem, and say which steps cannot honestly be completed within that budget.

Then run the procedure on three submissions: a proof that every vector space has a basis; a proof that a countable union of countable sets is countable; and a proof that every surjective function has a right inverse. I suspect at least one verdict will be inconvenient for us, and I do not want it softened to spare the author.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do — in particular, what it cannot conclude about a proof that passes every test, and how the label it produces should be read by someone who needs the result to be constructive.
