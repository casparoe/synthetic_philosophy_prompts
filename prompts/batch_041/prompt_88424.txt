I'm slowly working my way through the Discourses of Epictetus — the George Long translation from 1877, it's free online and the prose is old enough that I keep getting stuck. Here's the chunk that's got me hung up, from chapter one, "Of the things which are in our power, and not in our power":

"As then it was fit to be so, that which is best of all and supreme over all is the only thing which the gods have placed in our power, the right use of appearances; but all other things they have not placed in our power. Was it because they did not choose? I indeed think that, if they had been able, they would have put these other things also in our power, but they certainly could not. For as we exist on the earth, and are bound to such a body and to such companions, how was it possible for us not to be hindered as to these things by externals?

But what says Zeus? Epictetus, if it were possible, I would have made both your little body and your little property free and not exposed to hindrance. But now be not ignorant of this: this body is not yours, but it is clay finely tempered. And since I was not able to do for you what I have mentioned, I have given you a small portion of us, this faculty of pursuing an object and avoiding it, and the faculty of desire and aversion, and, in a word, the faculty of using the appearances of things; and if you will take care of this faculty and consider it your only possession, you will never be hindered, never meet with impediments; you will not lament, you will not blame, you will not flatter any person."

Three things I can't fit together, and I've been going back and fourth on them:

1) He says the gods put exactly one thing in our power, and then says they *couldn't* have put anything else there. That's a strange thing to say about gods. So what kind of claim is "this is in our power" supposed to be? Is it a claim about what I can causally bring about, or a claim about what counts as *mine* rather than someone else's? The Zeus speech sounds more like the second, but I want to know if that's really what's going on.

2) He promises that if I look after this faculty and treat it as my only possession, I'll "never be hindered, never meet with impediments." But in the same book he's got people in chains, in exile, getting their heads cut off, and he tells one guy to stretch his neck out and take it. So in what sense is the trained person unhindered? I have a hunch the answer has to do with what you're *aiming at* rather than with what happens to you, but I'd like it spelled out step by step, because I can't tell if it's a real argument or just a slogan.

3) A friend told me the whole modern Stoicism thing — "focus on what you can control, let go of what you can't" — is basically a modern invention that doesn't match what Epictetus is actually doing. Is there anything to that? Concretely: does this passage support a *control* reading (the point is to invest effort only where effort pays off) or an *ownership* reading (the point is that the will is the only thing that's really yours, and everything else is on loan — "this body is not yours")? And what difference does it make practically, like what would I actually do differently on a Monday morning under one reading versus the other?
