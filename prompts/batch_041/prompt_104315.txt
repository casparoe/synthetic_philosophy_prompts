I'm a science-and-ideas journalist finishing a long feature on the socialist calculation debate — Mises and Hayek against Lange — and on what a century of computing has done to it. I need your credence in one proposition, with the arithmetic showing.

The proposition: there exists a procedure by which a single authority, granted unlimited computing power but no access to any information a market economy does not also generate, and facing citizens exactly as self-interested and strategically minded as they are under capitalism, could allocate resources at least as well as a realistically imperfect market economy, measured by the preferences those citizens actually hold. If you think the proposition has no truth value until 'at least as well as' is cashed out, say so, pick the most defensible cashing-out, and give me the number for that.

Two features of the case I want treated as part of the problem rather than as decoration. First, the planning board's data set is the market's own: every price, every transaction, every choice ever made — in effect a complete revealed-preference record that it can train on and simulate forward. Second, the boundary between plan and market is not clean even now; a large firm is a small command economy with administered internal prices, and many of the largest markets in the world are cleared by algorithm rather than by haggling.

Lead with the number in your first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Decompose it into terms, each with a rough probability and one line of justification: (1) computational tractability — is the allocation problem merely hard, or intractable? (2) incentive compatibility — does strategic misreporting survive even with perfect computation? (3) tacit and inarticulable knowledge of particular circumstances; (4) the fact that preferences are partly formed by the institutions that elicit them; (5) whether interpersonal comparison has a determinate enough meaning for the comparison to be made at all; (6) anything else you think belongs, including how much of any residual gap is politics rather than principle. Then combine them explicitly and tell me which term your number is most sensitive to, and what evidence or argument would move it most.

Two of my sources put this at roughly 0.05 and roughly 0.5. Say which term they most likely differ on, and whether the disagreement is substantive or merely a disagreement about what 'possible' means. Finally, give me a second number: how much of your credence survives if the planner is denied the market's data entirely — a closed economy from a standing start — and tell me how much of the drop is a fact about technology and how much a fact about institutions.
