I'm taking a jurisprudence seminar this term and we've spent the last three weeks on statutory interpretation — textualism vs. purposivism, whether a judge follows what the words say or what the statute was trying to do. I said there's no way a court just reads it straight in a case like that, and my professor cut me off with "well, hard cases make bad law," and moved on, and like half the room nodded as if that settled it. I've heard that line a hundred times — from judges, from my uncle who watches alot of true crime, from people online — and I've never once been told what it actually asserts.

So can you lay out for me the different precise claims that one sentence could be making? I want the full menu, from the readings so thin that nobody could disagree with them to the ones that are actually radical and contested, with a verdict on each — platitude, substantive but defensible, or just false. Please pay attention to where the thing is ambiguous in ways people don't notice: what counts as a "hard" case (morally hard for the judge, or just legally unsettled?), what "bad" means and bad for whom, and whether the claim is about this one decision, about the rule the decision lays down for later cases, about the reasoning in the opinion, or about the future litigants who'll come along and cite it. Then tell me which reading my professor was most likely leaning on when he used it to shut me down, and whether that reading actually gets him his conclusion — that the court has to apply the plain text and let the murderer inherit — or whether it only supports something mushier, like "courts should be cautious" or "the legislature should fix this."

Lay it out as a numbered list I can bring to office hours, not one reading explained at length, cause I want to see the whole menu and then argue about which row he was on.
