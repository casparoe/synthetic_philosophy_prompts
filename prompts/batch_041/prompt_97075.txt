My uncle died three weeks ago, at seventy-nine. He taught physics at a small liberal arts college for thirty years and then spent the last two decades of his life reading and annotating Vedanta — not as a convert, exactly, but because he thought the Upanishads had gotten something about consciousness right that physics kept circling without landing, and because he found in Schrödinger the version of it he could actually defend. I'm the one giving the memorial remarks, this Saturday, in a college chapel the department has generously made available to us. The room will be roughly half family and half his old colleagues, with a small contingent from a Vedanta study group he sat with on Tuesday nights for fifteen years and never once mentioned to me. He left a page of instructions, clearly rewritten several times. He asked that nobody say he is watching over us, or that he has gone to a better place, or that he is at peace now; and he added, in a line I keep rereading, that we should not dress the old doctrines up to make them easier to swallow. He also asked that whoever speaks not give a lecture, and that we remember he had a sense of humor about all of this — which is true, and is the hardest part.

What I need from you is the remarks: six or seven minutes spoken aloud, no footnotes, nothing that presupposes a seminar. The difficulty is that what he actually believed is nearly impossible to say to a grieving room without either falsifying it or hurting people. He held, with Schrödinger, that the plurality we perceive is only an appearance and is not real, and that knowledge, feeling and choice are essentially eternal and unchangeable and numerically one in all men, nay in all sentient beings. He insisted this was a claim about numerical identity, not a warm feeling of connection, and he was impatient with both of the consolations people reach for. He thought "his consciousness lives on" was a category error, since on his view consciousness was never plural to begin with — there was only ever one thing, and the counting of minds is something we do to bodies, something no one has ever observed. But he also thought "we are all one" was usually a slogan rather than a thought, and a cruel thing to say to someone in grief, because it quietly retracts the particular person being grieved: if the individual was never real, then my aunt has not lost anyone. Half the room, meanwhile, wants to hear that he is somehow still there, and I cannot honestly give them that either.

So write remarks that hold all of this at once: a eulogy for a specific man — his Tuesday nights, his handwriting crowding the margins of everything he owned, the way he graded problem sets — that does not pretend the individual was an illusion, does not offer a survival he would have denied, and finds whatever consolation is genuinely available on his own view without pretending that view is easy or that it says what people want it to say. It should land as affection, and it should be sayable by someone who is not a philosopher and is not sure he understands it either.
