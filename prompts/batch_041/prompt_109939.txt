I have recently joined the scarce-resource allocation panel at my hospital, and a decision we made last month has split the panel into two camps that no longer hear each other. One camp holds that numbers do not count in themselves: each person who needs the resource has a claim of equal strength, equal claims do not add up, and saving five rather than one is therefore not, as such, better justified — fairness has to come from a lottery or from something other than arithmetic. The other camp holds that numbers count: when we can save more lives at the same cost we should, and the refusal to aggregate is a mistake about what justification is for. Our case was harder than the textbook ones: we held one full course of a curative therapy, enough to restore a single patient completely, or a partial course that would substantially but incompletely benefit five; our reasoning is published; and the same choice will recur for years. I have to get the two of them to produce a document together, and I want what a real adversarial collaboration would yield rather than a truce in vague language.

Draft it. Give the claims both can sign — about how the choice should be described, about what is at stake, and about which familiar objections both agree are not decisive — then the residual disagreement in a formulation each side would accept as its own. Identify the cruxes, among them whether the claim that numbers count is a claim about reasons or about decision procedures, whether recurrence across cases changes anything, and whether the numbers-skeptic's lottery quietly reintroduces aggregation. For each crux, set out a case or argument both sides agree bears on it and record each side's prediction in advance. Say where the dispute turns out to be merely verbal, and format the agreed claims as a numbered list followed by a disagreements table.
