I belong to a small reading group that has spent the past term working through the literature on path dependence and lock-in — the thought that early and possibly arbitrary events can fix an arrangement that later becomes very hard to escape — and we keep colliding with a question that the standard examples never quite settle. Two of us have been at it for weeks over whether a particular institutional arrangement our own department adopted some decades ago (an oddly specific committee structure, if you want a case, though any comparable arrangement will do) was genuinely locked in by its history, or whether, had the clock been reset, something essentially the same would have arrived anyway. I would like to see the disagreement worked out properly rather than traded in slogans, so please write a dialogue between two people who are equally well informed and equally sharp, each trying to bring the other round.

Let one of them be a contingentist: she holds that the arrangement is a frozen accident, the survivor of a run of small, local, individually reversible decisions, none of which was forced, and that a rerun would have thrown up something plainly different. She will appeal to increasing returns, to the way the earliest adopters quietly set the terms for everyone after them, to coordination landing on whatever happened to come first, and to the awkward fact that the people who chose the thing could not have known what they were choosing. Let the other be a convergentist: he holds that, given the tasks that had to be done, the resources on hand, and the sheer number of people who had to be coordinated, any workable solution would have had to look much like this one — that the contingency is skin-deep, and that the same outcome would have arrived anyway, if by another route. He will appeal to functional fit, to the narrowness of the feasible set, and to the tendency of independently invented solutions to the same problem to resemble one another.

What I most want the dialogue to reach is the thing we keep failing to pin down. It seems to me that much of the disagreement is not about the facts at all, but about the grain at which the outcome is described: described coarsely, the convergentist is obviously right; described finely, the contingentist is obviously right; and neither of them will say which description we are owed, or why. I would like the two of them to be made to confront this directly — to press each other on what is to count as the same outcome, on whether there is a principled rather than merely convenient level of description, and on whether the question of lock-in even has a determinate answer once that is settled. If one of them can force a concession from the other, let that happen; if instead the exchange sharpens the question without resolving it, that is entirely acceptable. What I do not want is a tidy compromise that dissolves the disagreement rather than locating it. Write it as a genuine back-and-forth, with each responding to what the other has actually said, and in British English.
