A colleague of mine defends prenatal selection for non-disease traits with an analogy to which I keep returning, and I would like you to audit it. Her argument runs as follows. Choosing which embryo to implant is like choosing which school to send your child to: in both cases a parent is trying to give her child the best start in life on the best information available; in neither case does anyone suppose that the child's consent is required, or that the child is wronged by being placed in the better of two available options; and in neither case does the fact that the child would not have existed had the other option been taken make any difference to whether the choice was a good one. From this she concludes that selection for traits such as height, sex or a temperamental disposition is permissible, that it may even be obligatory where it would plainly improve the child's life, and that the usual anxieties about designing children and about consent are misplaced.

I want to know how far the analogy holds. Set out what corresponds to what — the parent, the child, the school chosen, the school forgone, the moment of decision, the years in which the choice is lived with — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Separate the break points that matter for her three conclusions (permissibility, obligation, the irrelevance of consent) from those that are merely picturesque. I am especially interested in which features of school choice the analogy quietly imports into the embryo case and whether they are really there, and in whether any part of her conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a different comparison would carry more of the argument, say which, and why.

Lead with your verdict in no more than two sentences, give the correspondence table next, then the analysis. No more than 750 words in total.
