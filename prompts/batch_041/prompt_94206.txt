I got into a Thanksgiving argument with my brother-in-law about whether "Gen Z" is a real thing or just a marketing category, and it's been bugging me for weeks becuase my gut keeps contradicting itself and I want to know what rule I'm actually running. Here's my scorecard. "Boomers" taken as a whole: not real, it's an ad agency word and half the people it covers don't recognize themselves in it. But the slice of them that was 18 to 22 in the late 60s and got drafted or spent the years dodging it: that one I'd say is real, you can feel the seam between them and the people five years older. The cutoff years themselves: fake. Somebody born in December 1980 being a Gen Xer and somebody born in January 1981 being a Millennial is silly, there's no difference between those two people at all. But 1965 versus 1985 is real — one of them had no internet until they were grown and the other had it in high school, and you can hear it when they talk. My friend says the exact cutoffs are just conventions and the lumps underneath are real, like "the Midwest" — nobody can say where it starts but everybody knows Ohio is in it and Colorado isn't. I like that but I can't tell if it's doing any work or just restating the problem.

Now the ones that don't fit. Somebody born in 1985 in a village in China and somebody born in 1985 in Ohio: calling them the same generation feels like a joke, there's nothing shared there. But somebody born in 1985 in Ohio who grew up rich and somebody born the same year in the same city who grew up in foster care: same generation, obviously, and I can't say why that's obvious when the China case isn't. A war that only men fight in — are the women the same generation as the men? I say yes, which seems to kill the whole "shared experience" idea. And my cousin, who does stats for a living, says there's a trap buried in all this: you can't tell apart the effect of being a certain age, the effect of what year it currently is, and the effect of when you were born, because any two of them pin down the third, so in principle nobody can ever prove a "generation effect" exists. Also, and this is the thing that finally made me write this down — my family keeps saying my sister and I are "the same generation" and my cousin who's three years younger isn't, and I realized I'm using "generation" in two totally different senses there, one about the family tree and one about culture, and I bet half my confusion is just that one word doing double duty.

So here's the principle I'd offer, and I want it stress-tested and repaired if it's broken: a generation is real to the extent that a bunch of people were the same age when the same thing happened to the place they lived, and it stops being real when you can't point to the thing or the place. Does that survive my own list, or does it force me to drop one of my verdicts? And what does it say about cases I haven't thought about: people who all started at the same company the same year (a work "generation"?), the people who all graduated into the 2008 crash, a pandemic that hit everybody but at wildly different ages, a technology that reached the cities in 1996 and the countryside in 2012? Also, is there one kind of thing I'm after here, or am I lumping together two or three — birth-year cohorts, event-defined cohorts, family descent — that only look like one? And if the honest answer is "real but vague, real in degrees," clustering that's genuinely in the world with edges we draw ourselves, then tell me what that commits me to about the arbitrary cutoffs, since I want to keep saying the 1980/1981 line is nonsense while still saying the 1965/1985 gap is real. Give me the principle, the case that motivates each part of it, and which of my verdicts it can't save.
