I'm a retired geologist — forty years of fieldwork, and a lot of that time spent worrying about sampling: which outcrops you can actually see, how badly your age estimates are skewed by whatever happens to be exposed, that whole business. I've been using my retirement to read philosophy, mostly badly and mostly out of order, and I've gotten stuck on an argument I keep running into. Roughly: you don't know how many people will ever have lived; you should treat your own position in that sequence as an unremarkable draw from it; so you should expect to sit somewhere near the middle of the story rather than right at the end, which means there probably aren't vastly more of us to come than there have already been. I gather it's been around for decades and that clever people have battered at it, but I've come round to thinking there's something right about it, and that worries me, because I know exactly how easy it is to fool yourself with a sampling assumption you never inspected — I've watched colleagues do it for years with radiometric dates. What makes me take it seriously is that I can run the same recipe on quantities I'd get feedback on: how long I'll be retired, how many more papers I would have written, how long this message is going to get. If the recipe is good, fine, it's a general fact about not knowing durations. If it's nonsense, it's nonsense everywhere, and I want to know where it breaks.

So: what would count against it? Not a who's-who of the people who've written against it — I couldn't care less, and I'd only be taking your word for it anyway. I mean the actual considerations: which arguments, which cases, which formal results should move me, sorted from decisive down to merely awkward, and with the sort of thing a sympathetic defender would themselves concede counts against them, as opposed to the stuff only critics say. What does the view predict that rival ways of thinking about our place in the sequence don't? And be straight with me at the end about whether it's been qualified into something no observation could ever touch — and if so, whether that's a real problem or just what a claim like this has to be. I should say what would definately move me: either a case where the recipe gives a demonstrably wrong answer, or a demonstration that it can't give a wrong answer. Those look like the only two options and I'd like to know which one we're in. Also, tell me how confident you are in each of the things you say, and single out the one you're least sure about — I'd much rather have the uncertainty flagged than smoothed over.
