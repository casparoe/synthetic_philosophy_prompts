The Knobe effect is the finding that people's judgments about whether an action was done intentionally are driven by their moral assessment of the outcome. In the original study, a chairman is told that a new program will increase profits but will also harm the environment; he says he doesn't care about the environment, he only wants the profits, and the program goes ahead. Asked whether he harmed the environment intentionally, the large majority of respondents say yes. When the vignette is rewritten so that the program helps the environment, and the chairman says he doesn't care about helping it, only a small minority say he helped it intentionally. The only difference between the two cases is whether the side effect is good or bad. Since the chairman's beliefs and desires are held fixed across the two versions, the result shows that ascriptions of intentionality are not a matter of what the agent believed and wanted, but of whether the assessor morally disapproves of the outcome. This is a counterexample to the Simple View — the traditional claim that one does intentionally only what one wants and expects to do — and it shows that the ordinary concept of intentional action is a moralized one: to call an action intentional is to hold the agent to a norm. The broader lesson is that ordinary speakers are inconsistent in their use of the concept, applying a stricter standard to harmful side effects than to beneficial ones, and that philosophers who rest arguments on their own intuitions about cases like these are relying on an instrument that experimental philosophy has shown to be unreliable.
