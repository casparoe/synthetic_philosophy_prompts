I am presenting a paper next month at a half-day workshop on rules, algorithms, and the limits of formalization, hosted by a law school, and I need a prep sheet for the discussion period. My thesis is that Gödelian incompleteness does not show that legal systems contain gaps, and that the familiar argument to the contrary fails at a specific joint. The case I will make runs roughly as follows.

First, I sort arguments that carry a result from one domain to another into three grades: metaphorical borrowing, where the shape of a proof is used as an image; structural transfer, where one shows that the target literally satisfies the theorem's hypotheses; and limitative transfer, where one concludes that practitioners of the target can never establish certain propositions. Most legal uses of incompleteness are metaphorical borrowing dressed up as structural transfer.

Second, incompleteness is a relation between what a system can prove and what is true in its intended model, so it presupposes a truth predicate independent of provability. On the account of legal validity I favour, validity just is derivability from authoritative sources, and there is no independent domain of legal truths for a system to be incomplete with respect to.

Third, if one insists on supplying such a truth predicate, the theorem reaches only systems rich enough to encode arithmetic, and the only provisions of a legal code that do that are the ones concerning dates, interest, and the computation of damages. A result showing that tax schedules are incomplete while saying nothing about hard cases is a reductio of the analogy.

Fourth, I distinguish three things that are routinely conflated: gaps, where there is no legally correct answer; blind spots, where there is a correct answer that the system's decision procedures cannot certify; and drift, where the answer changes as the sources change. Incompleteness, even if it applied, would deliver blind spots and not gaps.

The audience will be about twenty people: three contracts scholars who regard the importation of mathematics into law as a category mistake and who care about how courts actually fill gaps; a legal philosopher who has written on hard cases and knows enough logic to object that incompleteness is not the same thing as a gap; two computer scientists, one a proof theorist working on verified software, the other a program-synthesis researcher who will want to know which formal system is supposed to be doing the work; and a law-and-economics person who will ask what the Gödelian framing buys over a plain story about incomplete contracts and the cost of writing contingencies.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defence. Order the objections by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, attribute each to the sort of attendee who will raise it, and phrase it the way that person would phrase it, at their level of technicality, with their presuppositions showing. For each objection give me a two-minute reply, mark the point in the reply where I am weakest, and say which objections I should concede before they are raised and which I should hold. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks and tell me how to answer it if it comes anyway. Assume the questions are short and the follow-ups hostile; I have ten minutes. I do not need reading lists or citations.
