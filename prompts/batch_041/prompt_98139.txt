I have come to accept a thoroughly reductionist account of persons, and I want you to press hard on it. On this view, a person is not a further entity over and above a causally interconnected stream of physical and mental events — body, feelings, perceptions, dispositions, consciousness — and the word self names at most a convenient designation for a pattern in that stream, not anything that exists in its own right. Persons are real roughly the way a whirlpool or a heap is real: fully constituted by their parts and their organization, with no remainder left over. What I want is a map of what would count against this — not the tired objection that it makes life meaningless, but the objections that ought to worry someone who already holds the view.

Work through at least these fronts. First, the subject of experience: the intuition that experience is necessarily someone's experience, that there is an irreducible first-personal givenness to it which a stream of impersonal events cannot capture. Second, diachronic unity: what, on the reductionist account, makes an earlier and a later phase one person rather than a mere succession, and whether anything in the account does the work that memory, desert, and promise-keeping seem to require. Third, agency and deliberation: who weighs reasons and who is answerable, if no one is there. And fourth, a rival I want you to take seriously rather than dismiss — the position, held by some Buddhist Personalists, that a person is real but is neither identical with nor simply reducible to the aggregates, something in addition to them yet not a fifth aggregate. Explain what this rival is actually claiming, whether it is a coherent middle path or an unstable compromise, and whether it constitutes a genuine defeater or merely a redescription.

Then do two further things. Tell me which of these the committed reductionist would themselves concede as counting against the view, as opposed to waving away, and rank the whole set from what would be fatal to what is merely awkward. And address squarely the escape hatch I suspect the view relies on: the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth, on which no-self is said to hold ultimately while persons remain perfectly real conventionally. Does that distinction insulate the view from defeat — if an objection lands conventionally it is conceded, if it lands ultimately it is dismissed — and if so, is that a defect in the position or a legitimate feature of a view whose real business is soteriological rather than metaphysical? I would rather be told that the position is well protected, and why that is or is not a problem, than be handed defeaters that no one should accept.
