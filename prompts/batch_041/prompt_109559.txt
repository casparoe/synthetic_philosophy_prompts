Some friends and I got into a long, slightly drunken argument after a podcast about Finland and NATO — one of us kept insisting that a country which won't ask its own citizens to defend it has basically already decided it isn't worth defending, and I've been turning it over ever since. So I'm trying to work out what I actually believe about when a state is allowed to make people fight, and I've got a pile of gut verdicts that I suspect don't all hang together. Here they are, roughly in the order they came out of my mouth. If my country is actually invaded and the government calls up everyone able-bodied, that's legitimate, and someone who dodges is free-riding on the people who go. If the same government conscripts people for a war of choice on the other side of the world, one that isn't about defending anyone here, that's not legitimate — not just dumb, wrong. A straight lottery where everyone's number can come up, no student deferments, no paying a substitute, strikes me as fair; the system where you could stay in school or buy your way out doesn't. But a purely volunteer professional army, which in practice ends up drawing mostly poor and rural kids while the rest of us stay home, I don't think is illegitimate at all, even though I just said the lottery is fairer. A religious pacifist who'll do alternative service should obviously get an exemption. Someone who refuses because he thinks this particular war is unjust — and it turns out I agree it's unjust — I want to let him off too; but a guy who refuses because he thinks a war I think is just is unjust, I want to throw the book at him. Switzerland, where everyone does a stint and keeps a rifle at home and the country stays armed-neutral and doesn't join anybody's war, I find genuinely admirable, and a small country keeping a big conscript reserve because it lives next to a giant seems fine for the same reason. A state hiring foreign contractors or mercenaries to do its fighting so its own citizens don't have to feels worse to me than conscripting those citizens would, even though the mercenaries are there voluntarily. Conscripting people in wartime for hospitals and civil defense and ditch-digging rather than front-line combat seems fine, maybe even obviously fine. And peacetime conscription for civilian "national service" — teaching, eldercare, that sort of thing — I honestly don't know what to think; part of me likes it and part of me thinks it's servitude with a nicer name.

Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets most of these? The one I keep reaching for is something like: coercion is permissible when it's the price of a public good that the coerced person also shares in, and the burden is spread by a fair rule — but that seems to make the lottery come out ahead of the volunteer army, which I don't actually believe, so maybe that's the verdict I have to give up. I also can't tell whether my verdicts are tracking whether the war is just, or whether the burden is fairly shared, or whether the person is being used as a mere means, and I suspect it's a messy mix. Two other things are rattling around: one friend's claim that a draft is good precisely because it stops the public from sleepwalking into wars their own kids won't fight, and the old half-belief that if you can be made to fight you should get the vote and vice versa — military service as the price of citizenship, or citizenship as the reward for it.

So: tell me what I believe, which principle or principles fit best, what they predict for a few cases I haven't listed (conscripting only men, a draft for a UN peacekeeping deployment, conscripting people to defend a country that's plainly going to lose anyway, a state that conscripts but forswears all offensive war), and which of my verdicts I should abandon. I don't want a reading list, just the argument — and if the honest answer is that "the war is just" is doing all the work and the conscription question is just downstream of it, say so, because I half-suspect that's the trick I'm playing on myself.
