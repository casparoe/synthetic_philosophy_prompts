I run a philosophy of biology seminar and the species unit breaks my students every year. We do the rounds — the biological species concept and its insistence on interbreeding and reproductive isolation, the phylogenetic species concepts that want species to be the smallest diagnosable lineages, the view that species are individuals (particulars with a beginning and an end) rather than classes or kinds, the pluralists who say there simply isn't one right answer and we should let a hundred taxonomies bloom, and the eliminativists who think we should stop using the word "species" in biology altogether. By week three people are genuinely cross with each other and I have no idea what to tell them. The strange thing is that nobody seems to disagree about the facts. Everyone agrees about gene flow, about ring species, about asexual lineages, about hybrids, about what the tree of life looks like. And yet the realist, the pluralist and the eliminativist are exactly as far apart as they were at the start of term. One of my students asked whether asking "is this one species or two" is like asking whether the Mississippi is one river or two — I thought it was either very stupid or very good and I still can't decide which.

My colleague in the philosophy of language — of course it's the philosopher of language — says the whole thing is a verbal dispute. His line is that "species" is like "gene": a term whose content is fixed by the theoretical work it's supposed to do, so there's no fact of the matter about the correct definition, only facts about which taxonomy serves which purpose. I think that's too quick but I honestly can't say why, which is embarassing. I also have a nagging suspicion of my own, and I want you to treat it as just a hypothesis: that the two camps are answering different questions under one name — one about what the units of classification are, one about what the units of evolution and lineage-splitting are — and that the impasse is downstream of that conflation rather than of any real metaphysical disagreement.

So: diagnose this for me. Is it substantive or verbal, a hidden empirical question, a difference in what counts as a counterexample (are ring species and asexuals data that a theory has to accommodate, or peripheral noise?), or two questions wearing one word? Engage with what each side actually says — don't just survey the views, tell me what kind of disagreement this is and why the arguments keep sliding past each other. Then tell me what, given your diagnosis, would actually count as progress: a distinction, a decision to stop asking one of the two questions, a formal result about whether any one taxonomy could do all the jobs, or an honest admission that the question is ill-posed and we should move on. And if you think the honest answer is that nothing will move it, say that too and tell me why. Finally — this is the part I'll actually use in class — name the single case or thought experiment that would do the most diagnostic work, and say what each side is committed to saying about it.
