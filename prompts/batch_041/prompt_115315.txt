I am presenting a paper at a small cognitive science workshop next month and I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the paper. My thesis concerns the familiar four-rung ladder of creature kinds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian — as Dennett sets it out. The standard reading treats the ladder as a single scale of increasingly sophisticated hypothesis testing, and I argue that this reading is a mistake. On my account, the first three rungs differ only in where a hypothesis is tested: in the gene pool, in the creature's own emitted behavior within its lifetime, and in a simulation the creature runs before it acts. The fourth rung does not extend that axis but replaces it. What distinguishes a Gregorian creature is not that it tests representations rather than actions, but that the apparatus with which it tests them was built by other minds and handed to it ready-made: language, notation, experimental protocol, statistical habit, the whole toolkit of externalized memory. The transition that matters, I claim, is not the invention of simulation but the invention of the transmission of simulation tools, and cumulative culture is its consequence rather than its cause.

I draw three consequences and expect all three to be contested. First, the ladder is not a scale of intelligence or mental power, and any ranking of animals, children, or machines along it is confused. Second, the fourth rung is a claim about the provenance of the testing equipment and therefore about social organization as much as about cognition, not a higher grade of the same thing. Third, if the criterion is provenance, the rung admits of degrees — a child who inherits a language but invents her own counting method sits partly on two rungs at once — and I do not yet know whether that is a feature or a concession that dissolves the ladder.

The audience will be about twenty people. Four or five are comparative psychologists who work on corvid and ape cognition; they regard ladder-talk as an anthropocentric scala naturae in disguise, and they have findings on animal planning, tool manufacture, and metacognition that they will use to argue that my Popperian rung cannot be separated from the Skinnerian one without an operational test I cannot supply. Three are developmental psychologists who study cumulative cultural learning in children — imitation versus emulation, the ratchet effect — and will say the fourth rung is already theorized and that my provenance criterion merely renames cumulative culture; they will press on whether inheriting a tool and inheriting a norm belong on the same rung. Two are evolutionary biologists: one works on niche construction and will say my fourth rung is extra-genetic inheritance and needs no new rung of its own; the other is skeptical of ladders generally and will want the selection story — what the fitness advantage is of inheriting a testing apparatus rather than building one, and why that would be a discrete transition rather than a slope. One is a philosopher of mind who knows the original taxonomy closely and will defend it, arguing that the distinction between where a hypothesis is tested and who built the tester is unstable, since the Skinnerian creature's environment is already scaffolded by conspecifics. One or two come from a machine learning group and will ask where a large language model sits: it is trained on the products of Gregorian creatures, but does it inherit a culture in the relevant sense?

Mark which objections I should concede up front and which I must hold, and identify the one reply that has to land for the paper to survive the hour.
