I've been arguing for weeks with two friends — one a psychiatry resident, the other a devoted reader of Thomas Szasz — ever since the three of us read The Myth of Mental Illness (1961) together, and our argument keeps circling one fork in the road. The resident insists that whatever you call it, the distress is real, it runs in families, it responds to treatment, and the brain is a body part like any other; the Szaszian insists in reply that an illness, strictly speaking, is a deviation from the normal structure or function of a body, that no one has ever located the lesion for depression, schizophrenia, or what our grandparents called hysteria, and that what we call mental illness is therefore a metaphor doing the work of moral and legal judgment while pretending to be medicine — that the asylum, involuntary commitment, and the insanity defense are instruments of social control wearing a white coat. I want to take the Szaszian side and push it as hard as it will go, in the form of sustained, deadpan satire, so I can find out where the position either earns its keep or quietly embarrasses itself. So please write me the website FAQ page for a fictional institution — imagine a well-funded residential facility that has adopted the myth-of-mental-illness thesis as settled doctrine and organized its entire practice around it — in which the whole thing is played absolutely straight, in the serene, bureaucratic, faintly cheerful voice of its director of admissions, with no winking, no scare quotes, and no narrator signaling that any of it is a joke.

The humor should come from getting the content right rather than from absurdity, so that a reader who has actually read Szasz laughs instead of wincing: the entries should faithfully embody his real commitments — that disease requires a demonstrable bodily lesion, that so-called patients are people with difficulties of everyday life rather than sick people, that coerced treatment is imprisonment whatever its motives, that a society which reclassifies moral problems as medical ones ends up running a therapeutic state, and that the corollary he himself drew, that people are responsible agents, means the delusional and the suicidal are not patients but authors of their own conduct. Let the FAQ walk through the questions a prospective resident or their family would actually ask — what happens here, whether there are diagnoses, whether there are drugs, what the facility does when someone wants to die, what it does when someone is dangerous, who decides that a difficulty is a difficulty of living and not an illness, what it tells an insurance company, and what it says to a court that wants an opinion on criminal responsibility — and let each answer be internally consistent with the doctrine and calm about its own consequences. The standard objections should land through the answers themselves rather than being announced: that the absence of a lesion today is not the absence of a disease, that someone has to decide which troubles count as living and which as sickness and that this decider is doing exactly what the doctrine claims to abolish, and that a world with no insanity defense quietly converts the sick into the guilty. Around 800 words, formatted as numbered questions with answers, in American English, and please keep it legible to a reader who has never heard of Szasz while still rewarding the one who has.
