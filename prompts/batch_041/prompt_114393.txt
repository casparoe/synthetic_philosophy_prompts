I am in the middle of a dissertation chapter on the brain-in-a-vat scenario, and I have reached the stage where I need a case rather than another argument. The difficulty is the semantic-externalist rejoinder, which holds that an envatted subject cannot so much as think the thought that she is a brain in a vat, because her words and concepts — vat, brain, table, tree — bear no causal relation of the right kind to the things they would have to refer to, and so refer to nothing of the sort; on this view the skeptical hypothesis is not false but unstateable. I want to determine whether the rejoinder can be turned by constructing a scenario in which the requisite causal connection is actually supplied. My purpose is to show that the rejoinder, even granting it, does no epistemic work: if reference to real objects can be secured while knowledge of them still fails, the skeptic has lost nothing, and the two lines of response to the vat scenario come apart cleanly.

Please construct such a case to the following specifications, and then demonstrate that it satisfies them.

1. The subject is a brain in a vat in the strict sense: her cognitive life is sustained artificially and she has no ordinary perceptual or motor commerce with the world. The machinery may be stipulated; exotic physics and time travel may not.
2. She holds one ordinary perceptual belief about the objects her apparent surroundings derive from, and that belief is true. Its truth must be generated by the scenario rather than stipulated: the case has to include a real causal-historical route from those objects to her concepts, so that her terms latch onto the very things an embodied counterpart's terms latch onto.
3. Her total experiential evidence is exactly what it would be in the corresponding non-envatted life. Do not rig her evidence to make her more or less reasonable than her embodied twin.
4. Despite 2 and 3, her belief is not knowledge. Name the condition that fails and show that it fails.
5. The scenario itself should be presentable in a single paragraph, and the environment it connects her to should be mundane — a house, a laboratory — not a world with different physics.

After the scenario, walk through the specifications one at a time and say why each holds, giving at least the internalist justification verdict, the reliability verdict, and the safety verdict for the belief in question. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the work for specification 2 and the single feature that does the work for specification 4, and say whether they are the same feature. For each, run the removal test: state what happens to the case when that feature is deleted and everything else is held fixed. Anticipate the two most likely attacks on the case — a defender of the semantic rejoinder who denies that specification 2 has really been met or argues that 2 and 4 cannot be satisfied together, and an internalist who holds that truth plus justification suffices for knowledge and therefore rejects 4 — and say how the case should be defended against each. Finish with a verdict: does the case show that the semantic rejoinder, sound or not, is epistemically idle against the skeptic?
