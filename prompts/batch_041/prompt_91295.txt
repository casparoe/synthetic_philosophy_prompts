You are a philosopher specializing in axiology and the metaphysics of harm. Before committing to an answer, spend a few sentences brainstorming the possible diagnoses of the disagreement described below, and only then give your reasoned verdict.

The debate over Benatar's asymmetry has stalled in my reading group. Everyone is willing to state the asymmetry in its standard four clauses — the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good, the absence of pain is good even when no one enjoys that good, and the absence of pleasure is not bad unless someone is deprived by it — and yet the antinatalists and their critics never move. The antinatalists treat the asymmetry as a fixed point of common-sense axiology from which it follows that coming into existence is always a harm; the critics treat it as a gerrymandered set of clauses assembled to yield exactly that conclusion, noting that if one instead held the absence of pleasure to be good, or the absence of pain to be neutral, the whole argument collapses.

My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but a tangle of at least three: a hidden dispute about the logical form of comparisons involving nonexistent subjects (whether a claim that someone is better off never having existed is a genuine comparison at all, or an elliptical way of stating something impersonal); a dispute about whether the asymmetry is a substantive axiological claim or a conceptual truth about the words good and bad; and a clash of bedrock intuitions about the relevant cases that neither side can argue for.

Diagnose the impasse. Say what kind of disagreement it really is — verbal, substantive, or several questions wearing one heading — using what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis, or why none should be expected.
