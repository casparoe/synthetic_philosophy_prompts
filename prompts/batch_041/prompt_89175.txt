The bits that have me beaten are these. He says that "this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together", and then that "demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will." A little further on he tells us that "money serves us as a guarantee of exchange in the future", which sounds like a quite different job from being a measure of demand. And then, having insisted for a page or two that exchange requires equality and equality requires comensurability, he finishes with: "Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes."

My confusion is roughly this. Earlier he treats exchange as something natural — it grows out of need, and the whole point of the shrine to the Graces is that repaying a good is a natural bond of society — yet money itself he calls a matter of custom, which can be made useless at will. Then, almost in the same breath, the thing that really measures value turns out to be demand, which sounds natural enough after all, with money merely standing in for it. And that last line seems to give the game away: if different things can't be commensurable in the strict sense, then the equality he's been demanding isn't really there, and 'sufficient for practical purposes' starts to look like a confession rather than a solution. A friend of mine who did a classics degree once told me there's a long-running argument about whether this chapter even holds together, and that some people think he's quietly giving money two incompatible roles, but I don't trust my memory of what she said and in any case I don't want to be told what 'the scholars think' — I'd rather have the argument laid out so I can judge it myself. Does that final concession save the account or ruin it? And how, if at all, does any of this fit with the passage in the Politics where he's so scathing about money-making?
