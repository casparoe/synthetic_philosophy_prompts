I am a PhD student in philosophy working on logic and formal epistemology, and I have agreed to run a single ninety-minute session in our department's interdisciplinary seminar on formal systems and computation. There will be fourteen participants: graduate students in philosophy, cognitive science and computer science, three of whom have taken a course in mathematical logic and the rest of whom have not. What I want them to leave with is not a theorem but an experience of one particular step — the difference between failing to find something inside a formal system and proving that it cannot be found, and the discomfort of realising that settling the second question requires leaving the system behind. I want them to take that step themselves, mid-session, and I do not intend to name the move until the debrief.

The material below is fixed. I have checked all of it and would like you to treat it as given: do not alter the rules, do not introduce further results of your own, and do not let the activity depend on anything about this material that is not stated here.

The system. Strings are built from the letters M, I and U. The only axiom is the string MI. There are four rules. (1) A string ending in I may have U appended: xI becomes xIU. (2) A string beginning with M may have everything after the M duplicated: Mx becomes Mxx. (3) Any occurrence of the substring III may be replaced by a single U. (4) Any occurrence of the substring UU may be deleted. A string is a theorem if it can be reached from MI by finitely many applications of the four rules. The task I will give the participants is to derive the string MU.

What they must not be told. MU is not a theorem. The argument is an invariant one: in every theorem the number of I's leaves remainder 1 or 2 on division by 3 — the axiom has one I, rule 1 changes no I's, rule 3 removes three I's, rule 4 changes no I's, and rule 2 doubles the I count, and doubling never turns a remainder of 1 or 2 into 0. MU contains no I's, and 0 is divisible by 3. What matters for my purposes is that this argument is not a derivation but a claim about all derivations at once, and that no amount of failed searching, however long, could have established the same conclusion, since the space of derivations is infinite in both depth and branching.

Two further facts about the system, which I want available for the later stages but not handed to the group as results to be accepted on my authority. First, the converse holds: a string is a theorem exactly when it begins with M, contains exactly one M, and has a number of I's not divisible by 3. The sufficiency direction can be shown constructively: choose a power of two that is at least the required number of I's and congruent to it modulo 3, reach it by repeated doubling, trim the surplus in threes with rule 3, make the number of U's even with rule 1 if necessary, delete the U's in pairs with rule 4, and then convert triples of I's into U's in the right places. Second, the following small derivations are correct and may be used as counterexamples: MI to MII to MIIII to MUI (rules 2, 2, 3); MUI to MUIU (rule 1); MUIU to MUIUUIU (rule 2); and MI to MII to MIIU (rules 2, 1).

Now the design. The session must be arranged so that the pressure to make the metatheoretic step comes from what happens to the participants rather than from anything I say.

1. No lecture at the start, and no framing that reveals what the session is really about. The rules go on a card, the task is announced, and that is all. I do not want the puzzle named, or its source mentioned, until the debrief.

2. The setup must make the participants assume the target is reachable without anyone asserting anything false. I want the incentive structure to carry that presupposition — a prize for the first correct derivation, a time limit, a public scoreboard, whatever you judge best — so that I can keep a straight face and never have to lie. Tell me exactly what to say when the prize goes unclaimed and someone asks whether it is possible at all: I need a move that neither lies nor gives the game away.

3. The group's own output must become visible to them. Design a public record — a ledger, a board, a shared document — of every string anyone has derived and every claimed derivation, together with a participant in the role of referee who checks each claim against the four rules and rejects illegal applications. The structural fact I want them to see should be in the data they generate: the strings they reach fall into visible residue classes.

4. I want three confidence checkpoints, at roughly equal intervals, at which each team records in writing a percentage for the claim that MU is derivable, together with a one-line justification. These are not stakes; they are a record of what they took themselves to have, and they will be revisited in the debrief.

5. The pivot from search to proof must be produced by the group. I am willing to ask the pivot question and nothing more — have we shown that it cannot be done, or only that we have not yet done it? — and I would like a mechanism that makes that question land at the right moment: a census of the ledger, a tally of how many distinct strings have been reached, a participant whose assigned role is to press it. Advise me on the timing, and on what to do if it lands too early or too late.

6. After the pivot, the group should hunt for an invariant rather than be handed one. Have each team commit in writing to a proposed invariant before you let them see anything else. Then give them a gallery of candidates to classify as true of every theorem, false of some theorem, or true but useless because the target satisfies it too. The gallery should contain at least these five, each of which I have verified: the number of I's is never a multiple of 3 (true of every theorem, preserved by all four rules, and false of MU — the decisive one); no two U's are ever adjacent (false: MUIU is a theorem and rule 2 takes it to MUIUUIU); the letter M occurs only as the first symbol (true, and useless, since MU satisfies it as well); the number of U's is always even (false: MUI is a theorem and contains a single U); the number of I's is always odd (false: MII is a theorem). The criterion the group must articulate — an invariant settles the question when it holds of the axiom, is preserved by every rule, and fails of the target — is the moment of the session I care most about. Design this phase so that a participant says, in their own words, that they are now reasoning about all possible derivations rather than producing one, and so that this is the point at which the room changes.

7. After the proof, stage a short objection and let the group handle it: if MU is unreachable, then add MU as an axiom, and now it is derivable. I want them to see what happens to the invariant (it becomes false), and to see that this does not give them a derivation of MU in the original system but changes the question. Do not let me or the group assert that no invariant survives for the augmented system; I do not know that, and the honest handling is more interesting than a false claim.

8. The debrief should be elicited rather than delivered: the group reconstructs the invariant argument from the ledger they produced. It should map specific moments of the session onto parts of the idea — the search, the first sight of structure in their own data, the pivot question, the criterion for a decisive invariant, the asymmetry between a positive result (settled by a finite witness inside the system) and a negative one (settled only by an argument about all derivations), and the fact that the invariant, once found, turns out to yield more than the negative result. It should end by naming the move. It should also say plainly what the session has not done: the metatheory here is ordinary prose with a quantifier over derivations, whereas making the system talk about its own theorems requires encoding strings as numbers and the rules as arithmetic, and that is where self-reference lives. Do not let the debrief pretend the activity reaches that.

9. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each case: teams that will not stop searching; illegal rule applications, including the specific misreadings I should expect; a participant who recognises the puzzle and announces the answer; a participant who proposes a plausible invariant that is false, and what happens to the room's confidence when the referee rejects it; the confusion of not-found with cannot-be-found, which is the whole point and will certainly occur; and the possibility that the group leaves feeling set up rather than let in on something.

10. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. I suspect two things already: that knowing the answer in advance makes my own staging a kind of theatre the participants cannot be kept inside for long, and that the invariant arrives as one item on a handout rather than as something they had to invent under uncertainty. Tell me what else is missing, and what the facilitator has to supply by hand.

Deliver a runnable plan: the materials to prepare, the opening three minutes, the roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, the instructions for each phase phrased as I would say them aloud (I want to be able to read them off), the prize and how to administer it without money, the debrief, the failure modes with remedies, and the closing note on what the activity cannot deliver.
