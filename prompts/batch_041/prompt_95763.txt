I write copy for a small legal-tech outfit and I want to try a parody piece for the portfolio. Draft me a help-center FAQ page for a fake product — a settlement app for divorces, business breakups, fighting roommates, whatever — that resolves every dispute by the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, written by someone who has absorbed it so thoroughly that it reads as plain common sense. The page should never say bargaining theory, never say game theory, never name an economist. 600–700 words, numbered Q&As, deadpan corporate-help-desk voice, no winking, no hedging about how fairness is complicated.

The funny has to come from the actual content, so every answer should be a real consequence of the solution rather than a made-up quirk of the company. So: the equal-proportional-gains rule (each side ends up with the same fraction of what it could have gotten); the disagreement point as "what happens if you both walk away"; the ideal point as the best each side could get if the other side just lay down; and the consequence that nothing else about the available deals matters, so the engine only needs two numbers per person and the whole messy interior of what's actually on the table is irrelevant to your answer — that one should be a straight-faced Q&A. Get some mileage out of scale invariance (enter your payoffs in dollars or in units of personal satisfaction, any consistent linear rescaling gives the same split) and out of symmetry (something smug about a perfectly mirror-image couple getting a perfectly mirror-image outcome). Then use individual monotonicity as a marketing differentiator: an unnamed competitor whose method maximizes the product of the two sides' gains can actually pay you less when your own outside option improves, whereas this product never punishes you for improving your position — sell that hard, since it's true. Work in a testimonial or two, and one deadpan answer about what happens when the set of possible deals isn't a nice convex blob.

A game theorist should read it and think I know what KS is and that the joke is fair; everyone else should find it funny and a little ominous. No citations, no real companies or living people, and don't put the company's lines in scare quotes like you're above them.
