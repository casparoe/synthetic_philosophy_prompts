I run a biweekly reading group on the philosophy and governance of emerging technology: sixteen participants, mostly graduate students in philosophy and computer science with a few working engineers, and a single hundred-minute session to work with. What I want to put in front of them is a distinction that keeps collapsing when we discuss it, between two ways a project can lose its purpose. The first is loud: some agent begins visibly optimizing for its own influence, everyone can see it happening, there is a moment, there is something to object to. The second is quiet: no one ever does anything wrong, each step is a reasonable and defensible response to the previous one, and the enterprise ends up somewhere none of the participants would have chosen — a colleague called these influence-seeking and slow value drift, and I have found the names less useful than the phenomenon. My group has no trouble imagining the first, and is ready with objections the moment it is described; the second they treat as a vague caution about metrics, because when it is described in a single breath it sounds either trivial or paranoid. I want them to have to produce it themselves, one locally justified decision at a time, and only afterwards discover where the sequence went. So please design the session: they should not hear either failure named until the debrief, and the two should be distinguishable in what happens to them rather than in what I say about it.

I would like rules, materials (assume nothing beyond index cards, a whiteboard, and a way to keep two running tallies — no money, no laptops), a timeline with rough minutes, and a payoff scheme that rewards something the group will actually find tempting rather than something they will merely agree is rational. My own instinct, which you should feel free to rework or discard: run the drift as a relay in which each participant receives the previous participant's statement of what the group is trying to do and rewrites it to be more actionable, more measurable, or more persuasive to an imagined funder, scoring points for legibility and being penalized only for an outright break — so that no individual revision is the mistake and the mistake is only visible in the comparison between the first card and the last. Stage the grab, in parallel, as something the rules permit and reward: a participant who accumulates enough of the public tally may simply take over another participant's role, so the room has to decide in the moment whether to object, and can watch itself decide. The mechanism of the drift has to be that every single move is defensible — if any participant can point and say that this was the moment it broke, the activity has failed — and the two modes should be comparable at the end rather than merely run side by side.

The debrief should map particular moments of the session onto the two structures: the moment a proxy became the goal, the moment someone noticed and what happened to them when they said so, the moment the takeover was available and what the room did with it. It should end by making the case, without hedging, that oversight, voting, and vigilance are well shaped for the loud failure and badly shaped for the quiet one, and why. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle each case: I expect at least one participant to game the scoring, at least one to stop playing in order to critique the metric, and a real risk that the whole thing reads as a rigged demonstration whose conclusion was foregone. Finally, be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief — my own suspicion is that a room of people who can inspect one another's stated intentions is an easy case, and that the difficulty lies with systems whose objectives cannot be read off in the first place; if that is right, I would rather be told so plainly than have the debrief paper over it.
