I sit on a small advisory group that is preparing a briefing on the relation between church and state, and one of our members — a defender of the established church — has been pressing an analogy he wants the group to adopt. He argues that the parish church is best understood as a public utility, or more precisely as a national park. Like a park, he says, it is an institution older than the state, upheld by an accumulated endowment and at the margins by public support; it is used intensively by a small minority and hardly at all by the rest of us; and yet its presence is a common good — a shared backdrop that is simply there for everyone at the moments that matter, the christening, the wedding, the funeral, whether or not they believe anything. On this picture, disestablishing the church to save money or to satisfy a principle of neutrality would be like closing the national parks: a false economy that falls hardest on exactly those people who have no private alternative.

I want the argument met on its own terms rather than waved away. Please audit the analogy. Begin with a correspondence table — what plays the role of the park, the visitors, the non-visitors, the upkeep, the rangers, the wilderness, the entrance fee — and then set out the disanalogies, separating those that bear on the conclusion he draws from those that are merely decorative. I am particularly interested in two things. First, whether an analogy built on the properties of a public good can carry the claim about non-users at all, or whether the church is better classified as a merit good, and if so, whose conception of merit is doing the work. Second, how the comparison behaves once we stop treating the English arrangement as the baseline and ask the same question of other settlements — the German church tax withheld by the state from registered members, the public-law status of the churches there, the French separation of church and state, the American bar on establishment — since his picture of a church supported by all and used by few fits some of these far better than others.

Close with a verdict: does the conclusion survive the disanalogies, and if it does not, what analogy would serve his purpose better?

Keep the whole thing under 800 words, the table included.
