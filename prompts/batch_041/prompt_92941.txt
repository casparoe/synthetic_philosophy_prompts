I have been arguing with a friend who defends the old Garrisonian verdict on the Constitution, and he reaches for a comparison that is still common in these arguments: the Union as a bargain, the Constitution as a contract. The slaveholding states, he says, entered a common government on terms that counted three-fifths of the enslaved for representation, obliged the free states to deliver up persons who had fled from labour, and shielded the Atlantic trade for twenty years. On his reading the Constitution is a compact with slavery: the slaveholder is a party to it, those clauses are the consideration he received, and anyone who swears to uphold the document becomes a party to the wrong. He takes this to settle what the abolitionists were arguing about in the 1840s, and to expose Douglass's later claim that the very same document was antislavery as sentimentality dressed up as legal argument.

I would like the contract picture audited rather than merely rejected. Set out the correspondence as explicitly as you can: what plays the role of the parties, the offer and acceptance, the consideration, the terms, the breach, and the remedy? Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which of the breaks matter for the use my friend is making of it. Three places interest me in particular. First, can the enslaved be represented as a party to the bargain rather than as its object, and does the figure leave any room for those who never consented and were never asked? Second, is a constitution properly read as a private agreement whose meaning is fixed once and for all by the intention of those who signed it, or does the fact that it must govern people not yet born, and can be amended by them, mark a difference in kind rather than in degree? Third, if the document really is a contract, whose reading of it counts? Its most authoritative interpreters in the 1850s read it as protecting property in human beings, and a contract ordinarily means what the court says it means, in which case the analogy may cut against the conclusion my friend wants. Consider also what follows if the bargain is one a court would refuse to enforce as illegal or unconscionable: does the whole instrument fall, or only the offending terms?

Weigh against all this Douglass's own counter-move: that the text never once uses the words slave or slavery; that it speaks throughout of persons and never of property in persons; that it opens by constituting one people rather than a race of masters and a race of bondsmen; and that even the clause on the Atlantic trade reads as a grudging postponement rather than an endorsement. Can the contract analogy accommodate that reading, or does it rule it out in advance? And tell me whether the conclusion my friend draws, that the founding was a pact with evil and that fidelity to it is complicity, survives the disanalogies once they are on the table, or whether it was the metaphor, and not the history, that was carrying it all along. If a different figure of comparison would do the work better, name it and say why.
