I run a mid-sized software company, and for the past two years I have been trying to make our internal management less hierarchical. Concretely, we replaced top-down quarterly targets with what we call growth contracts: each employee writes their own goals for the next three months, revises them at the end of every quarter, and everyone's contract sits on a shared dashboard visible to the whole team. Feedback is solicited continuously rather than saved for an annual review, and managers are told to coach rather than to grade. My intention was to dismantle the old apparatus of surveillance and ranking. Nobody is measured against a target set from above, nobody receives a letter grade, and the score a person sees is one they largely wrote for themselves. I have come to think of the whole arrangement as an act of institutional disarmament.

A friend who studied Foucault told me, half-jokingly, that I have built the most perfect disciplinary institution she has ever seen, and I want to know whether she is right and what follows from it. Two things have happened that I cannot square with my original intention. First, people now write contracts they are confident they can hit; the stretch that used to come from an imposed target has quietly drained out, and yet everyone feels more exposed than before. Second, the person whose dashboard has been quietest for the longest has become, without anyone deciding it, the one the team quietly worries about. What are the most important implications of Foucault's account of disciplinary power — of the examination, of normalization, and of the way power operates through the production of knowledge about individuals — for a design like this one? I am not asking for a summary of Foucault's theses; I want the concrete implications for what I have actually built, including any that would be uncomfortable or that would force me to abandon part of the design.

I would like you to press especially on three things. First, does making each person the author of their own norms, and then making those norms visible to everyone, weaken disciplinary power or merely relocate and intensify it — and if it intensifies it, by what mechanism? Second, if discipline works by constituting people as objects of a particular kind of knowledge, what kind of knowledge does my dashboard produce, what does that knowledge make it easy to see, and what does it make it hard to see? Third, if power and resistance are not opposites but coextensive — resistance always arising within the vocabulary of the power it opposes — what does that imply for whether genuine change is possible here, and does it make my original ambition incoherent? Please be specific about which features of the arrangement carry the implications you identify, and say plainly if the honest conclusion is that the design cannot do what I wanted it to do.
