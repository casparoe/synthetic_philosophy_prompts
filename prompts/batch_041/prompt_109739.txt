I'm in a reading group that's slowly working through a book about Gödel and the mind, and one of the regulars — call him Dev — has spent the last three weeks insisting that the incompleteness theorems settle, once and for all, whether a machine could ever be a real mathematician or ever be able to check its own safety. I've written his argument out as cleanly as I can, and I genuinely cant tell whether it's a proof or a magic trick. Here it is:

(1) Anything that reasons by fixed rules — a computer program, an AI, whatever — is at bottom just a formal system: some axioms plus rules for deriving consequences. (2) By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, any consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic has a sentence it can neither prove nor refute, and in fact that sentence is true — the system's own Gödel sentence, which in effect says of itself that it isn't provable in the system. (3) So every such system leaves a truth out of reach. (4) But a human mathematician can see that the Gödel sentence is true; we just reason about it. (5) So for any machine there's a truth the human can see and the machine can't. (6) Therefore the mind isn't a formal system, and no machine, however powerful, will ever do everything a human mathematician can. (7) And the second incompleteness theorem says no consistent system can prove its own consistency — so an AI can never establish, by its own reasoning, that it is consistent or trustworthy, which makes the whole project of building systems that certify their own safety a dead end.

I find (6) and (7) far too strong, and the whole thing feels like it eats itself — if (4) is really available to Dev, it looks like it should be available to everyone, and then the argument stops distinguishing anything. But I don't want to be the guy who just waves his hands and mutters that Gödel doesn't apply to minds; I want to be able to say exactly which move fails and why. So put on your logician / philosopher-of-mathematics hat and think it through slowly, no stock replies: start by spelling out what the theorems actually demand of the systems they apply to, and what it's really supposed to mean to say that a human can see the Gödel sentence is true. Then tell me which numbered step you'd reject — (4) and (5) look shakiest to me, but maybe it's (1), or maybe the leap in (7) — and for the step or steps you give up, explain why it looked so plausible and what it costs me. If I keep (4), what exactly do I owe about (5)? And if I have to drop (4), does that mean I've conceded we're just machines, or is there a third option where the theorems still do real work without proving Dev's conclusion? Take your time with this, and don't just tell me it's a fallacy — price the alternatives.
