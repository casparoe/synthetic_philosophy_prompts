I'm in a political theory seminar and we burned the whole session on whether our university should bar a speaker who's known for saying nasty things about immigrants. One guy kept coming back to "your right to swing your fist ends where my nose begins" -- every time someone raised offense he'd trot it out like it obviously settles that you can restrict any speech that lands on somebody else. I've now heard that same line do duty for everything from the crowded-theater exception to campus speech codes to "don't be a jerk online," and I don't believe one sentence can carry all that. Can you take it apart for me?

What I want is a menu of the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making. For instance: what counts as a "nose" -- actual bodily harm, offense, hurt feelings, being made to feel unsafe? Does the fist have to land, or is a threat or an invitation enough? Is the claim about what the law may do, about what the state may do more generally, or about what ordinary people may do to each other (shunning, boycotting, no-platforming)? Does it say harm to others is sufficient to justify restriction, or only necessary -- and either way, necessary or sufficient for what, exactly? And does "my nose" have to belong to an identifiable person who's worse off, or can diffuse social harm count? For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude everyone would nod at, a real and contestable thesis worth arguing for, or just false; and then tell me which of those readings the guy in my seminar has to be relying on for the line to actually deliver his conclusion. My suspicion is that the honest readings leave him a long way short, but I want the seperate cases laid out rather than a verdict on the slogan as a whole. Lead with that last point in your first sentence -- which reading, if any, does the work he needs -- and then give me the breakdown.
