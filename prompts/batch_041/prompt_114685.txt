I belong to a small reading group that has spent the term on Dennett's 1991 paper "Real Patterns" and the surrounding discussion of the physical, design, and intentional stances, and two of us have now been arguing for weeks over a single word — whether the account earns the word "real." The first holds what I will call pattern realism: a pattern is real to exactly the degree that it supports compression and prediction; realness is thus objective but always relative to a purpose or a description language; the intentional stance is in no worse a position than the physical stance; beliefs, desires, and centers of gravity are all real in the same sense; and there is no further, stance-independent fact about which pattern is genuinely there. The second holds a naturalist line of the sort James Ladyman and Don Ross press: the physical stance alone describes what is fundamentally there; the design and intentional stances are instruments for prediction; and a higher-level pattern counts as real only if it is projectible, supports counterfactuals, and earns its keep in our best science, rather than merely being convenient relative to our interests — so that talk of the thermostat wanting the room at twenty degrees is a façon de parler, however useful. They keep talking past each other, and I want the document they should have written.

Draft the joint statement of their adversarial collaboration. It should contain (a) a numbered list of claims both can sign — about what the three stances are for, what each does and does not claim about the furniture of the world, what work the word "real" is being asked to do in each camp, and where they agree the real pressure lies; (b) a disagreements table giving the precise remaining differences, with each side's position phrased in wording that side would accept as its own; and (c) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I expect one crux to be the criterion of pattern-reality (mere compressibility versus projectibility, counterfactual support, or indispensability to our best science) and another to be whether the design stance is autonomous or parasitic on the intentional stance. For each crux, give the case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, together with what each side predicts about that case, written down in advance.

Make them face the hard cases rather than the easy ones: a pattern that compresses beautifully only under a purpose-built encoding, so that the same string that looks random in one notation is a tidy repetition in another; two equally compressive but mutually incompatible descriptions of the same data, each positing different entities, so that compression alone cannot choose between them; a pattern that predicts well within the observed range but fails to support counterfactuals outside it; and the thermostat and the chess engine, where design-stance talk seems irresistible yet no one wants to say the thermostat literally wants anything. If you conclude that part of their dispute is verbal — that they mean different things by "real" or are answering different questions — say exactly which part and why; and if you think one camp's criterion collapses into the other's, say so and show the collapse.
