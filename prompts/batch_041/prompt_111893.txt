Ok so I've been rereading Turing's 1950 Mind paper on the imitation game and I keep getting stuck on the bit near the end where he brings up telepathy. His move, as I understand it, is basically: if telepathy is real — and he doesn't seem willing to just dismiss it, he treats the statistical evidence as a serious problem rather than a crank thing — then the interrogator might be picking up the machine's answers directly, without any of it going through the teleprinter, and the whole setup collapses. So he says the experiment should be run inside a room that blocks whatever it is that carries telepathy. What I want is a comparison between that worry and the demand-characteristics / experimenter-expectancy problem in psychology — Orne, Rosenthal, double-blind designs, the whole apparatus for stopping the experimenter from accidentally tipping the subject off. My hunch is there's a real structural parallel: in both cases you have a setup thats supposed to isolate one channel of influence, and then someone notices there might be a second channel nobody is monitoring, so the clean result is maybe an artifact of the unmonitored channel rather than the thing you were trying to measure. But I also think they come apart in ways that matter. In psychology the confound is mostly something you can control for, blind away, or at least estimate; Turing's telepathy worry feels more all-or-nothing, where if the phenomenon exists at all the test just fails and no amount of blinding saves it. And I'm not sure the analogy even holds in the right place, because Turing's worry is about a channel between the judge and the machine, not about the experimenter leaking cues to the subject — those are different directions of leakage. So: how far does the analogy actually go, where does it break, and are these really the same kind of methodological worry or two different ones that happen to rhyme? I dont need a history of either one, just the comparison itself.
