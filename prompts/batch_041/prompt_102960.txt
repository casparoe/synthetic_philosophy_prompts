Since retiring from the bench I have been teaching myself philosophy of science, and I have built an argument that I now distrust because it seems to prove far more than I am willing to accept. I would like you to locate the step that fails, explain why it looked convincing, and tell me what it costs to give it up.

I had been reading about the view — associated with Milton Friedman, as I understand it — that a theory's assumptions need not be realistic, and that the only thing that counts in appraising it is how accurately it predicts. I tried to work out what that commits one to, and got this:

(1) A scientific theory is to be assessed solely by the accuracy of the predictions it yields; whether its assumptions are realistic is irrelevant to that assessment.
(2) The ideal gas law assumes that molecules are point masses exerting no forces on one another. That assumption is false, yet the law predicts the behaviour of dilute gases accurately. So by (1) it is a good theory.
(3) Let T be any theory whose assumptions are false but whose predictions in its domain are accurate, and let A be any further falsehood that plays no role whatever in deriving those predictions — say, that the gas molecules are guided by benevolent spirits.
(4) The theory T+A, formed by adding A to T, yields exactly the same predictions as T.
(5) Therefore, by (1), T+A is exactly as good a theory as T.
(6) Since this can be repeated with any idle falsehood at all, no amount of added falsehood can lower a theory's scientific merit, so long as the predictions are left untouched.
(7) It follows that there is no scientific difference between a fruitful idealization, which is false, and a superstition, which is false and idle; wherever the predictions agree, the two are on a par.
(8) But that cannot be right — it would make the whole practice of model-building, in which we argue over which idealizations are legitimate and which distort, an empty exercise.
(9) Hence (1) is false.

I would sooner keep (2), which I believe, than (7), which I find absurd. Tell me which premise or step to reject, why it looked so reasonable, and what I am paying if I reject it — and, if you think the chain is sound and I must simply choose which plausible claim to surrender, say so and price the options.
