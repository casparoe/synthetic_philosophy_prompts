I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training past one intro ethics elective, and my roommate is a philosophy grad student who likes to ambush me with puzzles over coffee. This one has been stuck in my head for two weeks and I can't find the loose thread, so I'm outsourcing it to you.

Here's how he sets it up. Imagine 500 possible lives, L1 through L500. They're identical in every respect except one: each life contains a chronic pain, and the pain gets a hair more intense as you climb the list. L1 is a twinge so faint you'd only notice it if you went looking. L500 is agony — the kind where the person can't hold a thought together and would beg for it to stop. And crucially, between any two neighbors on the list, say L17 and L18, the difference is genuinely imperceptible. Nobody could feel the difference, and if you were picking one for yourself you'd say it doesn't matter either way. Then he gives me this:

(1) If one life is worse than a second, and the second is worse than a third, then the first is worse than the third. That's just what "worse than" means — any ranking worth the name has to work like this.
(2) For every pair of neighbors, the higher-numbered life is worse, because it has strictly more pain and everything else is held equal.
(3) A difference that's imperceptible in a single step can't, just by being repeated, turn into a difference anyone can perceive or that matters. Stacking up a bunch of nothing still gives you nothing.
(4) So L500 is worse than L1 — he gets this straight from (1) and (2), chaining all the way up.
(5) But if L500 is worse than L1, then a difference nobody could ever feel, applied step by step, has somehow produced the gulf between a twinge and torture, which (3) says is impossible.
(6) He says the only exit is to give up (1): "worse than" isn't transitive, and if that's true then there's no coherent fact of the matter about which of two lives is worse, and every comparison of lives, or policies, or anything else in terms of more and less suffering is a fiction.

I'd rather keep (1) then (6) — I think a relation that isn't transitive just isn't a comparison, and I'm not willing to say it's a coin flip whether agony is worse than a twinge. But I also can't bring myself to deny (3), it looks obviously true, and I can't find a step where he's cheated. So: which step do I reject, and what am I actually paying to reject it? If the fix is to drop (3), I want to know what that does to my grip on small harms being small — does it commit me to saying enough trivial annoyances add up to something as bad as torture, or is there a way to have accumulation without that? Tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis and flag the one point you're least sure about. Write it in British English, please, and lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — no preamble, no restating my puzzle, just the verdict and then the argument for it.
