I am a philosophy professor who works mainly in epistemology but also teaches a graduate course on formal methods in philosophy, and I have been trying to get clear on the debate over the norm of assertion by translating it into a framework I find easier to reason in: sender-receiver signaling games. I know the basic model — nature selects a state, a sender observes it with some reliability and sends a message, a receiver hears the message and chooses an action — and I am comfortable with the notions of separating and pooling equilibria, but I am not a game theorist. My rough translation is that assertion is the message, the knowledge account becomes a constraint that the sender's information set be contained in the true state, the truth account becomes a constraint that the proposition expressed by the message be true, and the belief account becomes a constraint on the sender's credence. I would like you to restate the three accounts in that vocabulary more carefully and faithfully than I have, and then give me the residue report. Do not smuggle in the receiver's payoffs or the equilibrium concept as if they were part of the speaker's norm; keep the translation honest, and treat what fails to translate as the interesting part.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Items 1 through 3 should restate the knowledge, truth, and belief accounts respectively, saying in each case what the norm becomes as a condition on the sender's strategy and what the receiver's inference looks like. Item 4 should say what the three accounts share and where they diverge once they are stated in signaling-game terms. Item 5 should be the residue report: which parts of the philosophical debate have no counterpart in the signaling-game vocabulary, and whether that residue is a defect of the signaling framework or a sign that the original debate is less unified than it appears.
