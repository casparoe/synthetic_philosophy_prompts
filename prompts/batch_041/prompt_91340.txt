I am organizing a session of a graduate epistemology reading group around the puzzle usually called the dogmatism paradox, and I want to bring my group a ranked assessment of the available ways out rather than yet another survey. I will state the puzzle in my own words so that the handout is self-contained: suppose you know some proposition P, and suppose you also accept the innocuous-looking claim that if P is true, then any future evidence that appears to count against P must be misleading. From those two things it seems to follow that once you know P you are entitled to discount in advance any evidence that later shows up against it — which is precisely the dogmatic stance that knowledge was supposed to be incompatible with. When I floated this at dinner, a friend who works on online learning kept insisting it was just a fact about updating on noisy data and that philosophers were making a meal of a tautology, which is part of why I want the ranking argued rather than merely asserted.

Please rank the six candidate responses below from the one that most nearly earns its keep to the one that most nearly collapses into restating the puzzle. By "earns its keep" I mean: it preserves a robust role for knowledge — knowledge that is not instantly hostage to any apparent counter-evidence — while conceding as little as it can to the dogmatist, and without simply denying a premise that the dogmatist is plainly entitled to. The candidates, in deliberately flat language: (1) deny the closure step, on the grounds that knowing P does not put you in a position to know, of every counter-consideration in advance, that it is misleading; (2) confine the paradox to belief revision rather than knowledge, so that it constrains how a rational agent updates without making knowledge itself hostage to counter-evidence; (3) reject the fixed-stock picture of evidence, since whether something counts as evidence against P depends on background knowledge and on whether P is true, so there is no neutral standpoint from which to identify "evidence against P" ahead of time; (4) adopt contextualism about "knows," so that the arrival of counter-evidence shifts the context and raises the standard, meaning the knowledge that licensed the dismissal is not the knowledge you have afterward; (5) separate the epistemic from the deliberative, holding that knowledge may license treating P as settled for action while still obliging you to keep gathering and weighing evidence, the paradox having misread a permission about action as a permission about inquiry; and (6) accept the argument as valid and diagnose a hidden premise, namely the demand that a justified believer be able to answer every counter-argument without begging the question — that is, the assumption that justification must be defense-available rather than merely reliable.
