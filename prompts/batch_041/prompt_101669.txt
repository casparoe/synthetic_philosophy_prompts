We've been reading about Moore's paradox in my little philosophy group and the discussion has completely stalled. One camp says a sentence like "it's raining out but I don't believe it is" is absurd because the second half contradicts what the first half is doing -- saying it commits you to the very thing you then deny -- so the weirdness is really a fact about assertion, about what you can coherently put forward, and the sentence itself is perfectly consistent. The other camp says no, the real impossibility is at the level of belief: you literally can't hold the belief that it's raining and that you don't believe it's raining, the state cancels itself out, and the unassertability is just a downstream symptom. Neither side has budged in months and they keep reusing the same two examples.

My hunch is that they're not even answering the same question -- one side is asking what the sentence means or does, the other is asking what a mind can contain -- and that if that's right, the "paradox" dissolves in different ways depending on which question you were really asking. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement underneath? And what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a decisive case, a formal result, or nothing at all?

Also, I want you to test the diagnosis on a case I think breaks one side: self-deception. If someone can sincerely believe it's raining while also being genuinely convinced that they don't believe it's raining, then the Moorean conjunction can be true and even believed, which seems to undercut the "you can't believe it" side -- unless self-deception doesn't give you the right kind of belief in the first place. Does it actually discriminate between the two camps, or can each side just absorb it? Don't give me a survey or a reading list, I want the diagnosis with reasons and an honest answer about whether anything would move this.
