I spent most of last night arguing with a friend — a doctoral student who works on the norms governing assertion — about the sentence "It's raining, but I don't believe it." He kept wielding it as though it settled something. The sentence, he said, is absurd to assert, yet the speaker need not be wrong about the weather and the two clauses plainly do not contradict each other; so the absurdity cannot be located in what the sentence says, and must instead come from the act of saying it — from the fact that to assert something is to commit yourself to believing it. I could not get him to say what he thought the sentence was actually claiming, and every reading I offered he waved away as not the one he meant; at one point he added that the absurdity shows content and force can come apart, and at another that it shows a person cannot be a stranger to her own beliefs. Then he produced two cousins and insisted they were absurd in precisely the same way: "It might be raining, but I don't believe it is," and "I believe it's raining, but I don't believe that I believe it." The second strikes me as no more than a confession of poor self-knowledge, and the first as not absurd at all, but I could not say why with any precision, and we ended the evening talking past each other.

What I want is a menu I can bring to the next round: the distinct precise claims that "It's raining, but I don't believe it" could be expressing, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and say what kind of thing it locates the trouble in — the content of the sentence, the state of the speaker, or the act of assertion — since I suspect that is where he and I keep missing each other. Then tell me which of those readings would actually support his belief-norm-of-assertion argument, which (if any) would support the stronger claim that a believer cannot be mistaken or ignorant about her own beliefs, and which are doing no work in the argument at all. Finally, take the two cousins one at a time: say whether each is Moore-paradoxical in the same sense as the original, and if not, exactly what the difference reveals — I want to know whether the first is saved by the fact that "might" leaves room for disbelief, and whether the second is a genuine paradox of belief or merely a report of failed introspection. If it turns out that part of our dispute is verbal, say which part and why.
