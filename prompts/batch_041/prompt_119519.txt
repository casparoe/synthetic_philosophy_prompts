A couple of friends and I have been argueing for weeks about how to think about risking your bankroll, and before we go any further I want the spec on the table. One of them runs a small betting syndicate -- five of us, pooled money, mostly small edges on football and weather markets, a few hundred bets a year. The other is doing a PhD and keeps saying the whole 'geometric rationality' thing is a cult, that maximising expected wealth is the only thing that's actually rational and everything else is just risk aversion dressed up as maths. Neither of them can tell me what a theory in this area would even have to do to count as right, so that's what I'm after: the desiderata. What does any adequate account of what a rational agent should do when offered a series of bets have to deliver -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results from probability and decision theory it can't contradict -- with the reason each one is a requirement, and which you reckon are negotiable versus non-negotiable. Purpose, if it helps you fix it: I want something good enough to write a house policy for the syndicate that I could also defend to my PhD friend without hand-waving.

Some cases I'd want it to handle, roughly in order of how much they annoy me. (i) A long run of bets each with a small genuine edge, where the growth-optimal answer is a small fraction of bankroll and the expected-wealth answer is 'as much as you can get hold of'; (ii) a one-off bet with a fat edge that you'd obviously take once, where the same rule says stake almost nothing; (iii) an absorbing barrier -- if the pot drops below the amount needed to place a bet at all we're out of the game, so ruin is a threshold and not just a big loss; (iv) several positions open at once with correlated outcomes, where I suspect you can't just run the single-bet rule on each one separately and add up the fractions, and where it's the correlation rather than any individual bet that actually kills you; (v) the edge is estimated, not known -- we're working off a few hundred past results, so a theory that assumes the true probability is handed to you is no use to us; (vi) the pot is a shared pool, so 'maximise my wealth' and 'maximise the pool's wealth' come apart, and it isn't obvious a group should bet like one person; (vii) the payoff isn't always money in the straightforward sense -- sometimes it's money we can only spend on the thing we're betting on, or the win is a reputation thing we'd rather not lose. And three distinctions I want kept rather than collapsed: between a bet you'd take once and the same bet taken a thousand times, between the growth rate of the pot and its expected value, and between the chance of going broke and the size of the loss when you do. Also, do I need a requirement about scale -- that someone with a million and someone with a hundred quid, same information, should risk different absolute amounts but the same fraction -- or is that an artefact of putting everything in money in the first place?

The bit I most want is where the list turns on itself. If some pair or triple of these definately can't be met together, say which and give me the case that shows it -- if 'agrees with expected-utility maximisation on the one-off bet' and 'never risks ruin' and 'is scale-invariant' can't all hold at once, I'd much rather know that than get a tidy list. Tell me too which single requirement does the most work at eliminating candidates, and whether the ones people state most confidently (coherence / no Dutch book, 'must not turn down a +EV one-off bet', 'the rule should be the same for a group as for an individual') are genuine requirements or just a camp's prejudice. Then a short verdict, a couple of sentences each, on how the growth-optimal (Kelly / geometric mean) rule, plain expected-wealth maximisation, fractional Kelly, a flat 'never risk more than 2%' bankroll rule, and the time-average-versus-ensemble-average way of putting things do against the list. I'll do the arguing myself, I just need the spec first.
