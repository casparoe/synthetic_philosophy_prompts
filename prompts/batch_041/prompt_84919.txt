Please explain a conceptual issue in the ethics of panpsychism. Take the view seriously for the sake of argument — experience is fundamental and ubiquitous rather than an emergent trick of nervous systems — and take seriously too the possibility that valence, the pleasant-or-unpleasant character of an experience, is among the basic features of the world, so that there is no level of physical description at which the question of whether things are going well or badly simply drops out. What I want unpacked is what that does to moral status. On one side, the universe now contains vastly more candidate subjects of welfare than we ordinarily suppose, at levels where there is no organism, no memory, no persisting self, and nothing that can care what happens to it; on the other, almost none of that population is reachable by anything we might do, and we have no grip at all on what the inner life of a field or a particle could be like. Explain what a panpsychist should say about whether valenced micro-experience is even the kind of thing that can be summed, and what the unit of such a sum would be; about whether an amount of suffering can be morally considerable without belonging to anyone who suffers; and about whether moral status divorced from moral tractability leaves the position idle rather than action-guiding. Give the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
