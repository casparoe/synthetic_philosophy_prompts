I'm a third-year PhD student working mostly on philosophy of biology and mind, and I've ended up defending something I used to write off as a hand-wave. The idea is that you can have genuine representation and genuine normativity without any representer: a selection process — natural selection, but also reinforcement learning, maybe market competition or immune-system learning — can leave a structure sitting there with a rationale attached, in the sense that the structure is there because it did a particular job, it's for doing that job, and it can be broken or fail at that job, even though nobody, not even the system itself, has ever represented the job. So the frog's fly-detector, the beaver's dam-trigger, the weights that gradient descent happened to settle on — each of them has reasons attached to it that float free of any reasoner. I'm fairly sure this is roughly what Dennett means by a free-floating rationale, though I don't much care about the provenance.

What I actually want from you is a map of what would count against this. Not the generic teleosemantics literature review — I know it exists — but concretely: which observations, cases, or arguments should move someone who holds this, sorted by how decisive they'd be, from fatal down to merely awkward. I'm especially interested in the ones the view's own defenders would concede count against them, as opposed to the ones critics like to rehearse and friends just shrug at, and I want you to say when the shrugging is legitimate and when it's a dodge. Two things I keep circling: (a) if any structure with a selection history automatically gets a rationale, what would it even look like for a structure to have none — and if nothing would, is that a fatal problem or just the price of a stance-ish view? (b) the difference between a structure that reliably does X and a structure that is for doing X seems to be doing all the work here, and I can't tell whether the view earns that distinction or just stipulates it. Also tell me what the position predicts or explains that a rival doesn't — say, flat eliminativism about aboutness, or the view that only states some subject actually entertains can be about anything.

Please split the position if you think it's really two claims bundled together (aboutness vs. normativity), since they may be exposed quite differently. Rank the defeaters, say which single one does the most damage, and if you think the view has been insulated by its own qualifications, just say that plainly instead of pretending there's a live empirical test somewhere. I'd rather you go deep on two or three than list fifteen. End with a last line starting Verdict: naming the most damaging defeater and labeling the whole position insulated, partly exposed, or badly exposed.
