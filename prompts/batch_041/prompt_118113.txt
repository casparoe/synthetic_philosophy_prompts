I am a professor of philosophy and I chair the Governance Committee of my university's Faculty Senate. Our bylaws say only that a selection committee shall rank its candidates by majority preference, which in practice has meant that the chair reads the room and announces a list. After a search last year in which the committee's list was challenged from the floor, the Senate instructed my committee to propose a standing rule specifying in advance how the individual rankings of committee members are to be aggregated into the single ranked list the Dean's office receives. I must bring a written opinion to the Senate on Tuesday, and it has to do two things at once: recommend bylaw language, and settle the case that is actually pending.

Here is the pending case. A nine-member search committee has ranked four finalists, A, B, C and D. The Dean will make offers down the list until the two funded positions are filled, so the first two entries are what decide the matter. The members' rankings are:

- four members: A, then B, then C, then D
- three members: B, then C, then D, then A
- two members: C, then D, then A, then B

The pairwise majority results are these. A is preferred to B by six members to three; B is preferred to C by seven to two; C is preferred to A by five to four; B is preferred to D by seven to two; C is preferred to D by nine to none; and D is preferred to A by five to four. There is therefore no candidate whom a majority prefers to each of the others: the three strongest candidates form a majority cycle, and the fourth beats one of them while losing to the other two. First preferences give A four, B three, C two, D none. On a Borda count, scoring three points for a first place, two for a second and one for a third, the totals are B seventeen, C sixteen, A fourteen, D seven. If each member approves the three candidates they rank above last, the approval totals are C nine, B seven, A six, D five.

Three positions are being argued on my committee, and I expect all three to be argued on the floor. The first holds that only majority rule is legitimate, so that any rule which can hand a place to someone a majority ranks below a rival is unacceptable; its partisans want the bylaws to retain the phrase majority preference, want us to report the cycle honestly rather than paper over it, and have floated the familiar alternative of eliminating the weakest candidate round by round until someone commands a majority. The second holds that what a selection committee should be looking for is the candidate nobody objects to, and reads the approval figures as settling the matter. The third holds that we already have a de facto rule, whatever the chair announces, which in the last three searches has tracked first preferences, and that changing the rule now, with the rankings already in hand, is itself a manipulation of the outcome. I take that last objection seriously, since we are in the position of choosing the rule with the ballots in front of us.

One member has also circulated a note claiming that the majority camp offers no safe harbour: the note says that even a rule which always selects a majority-preferred candidate when one exists can, on some patterns of preference, leave a group worse off for having voted than it would have been had its members stayed home. The note concludes that voting procedure is a shell game. I would like the opinion to say what is right and what is wrong in it.

I do not need the literature surveyed and I do not need citations; I need an argument that stands on its own. If you invoke a general impossibility result, state it in your own words, say precisely which desirable properties it shows cannot be had together, and say what it leaves open. Do not use it as a slogan in either direction. I have taught this material long enough to be immune to the idea that a correct rule is waiting to be discovered; what I want is a defensible one.

Please draft the opinion, roughly a thousand to fourteen hundred words, in the form I will circulate, with a heading block and numbered sections:

1. A recommendation, stated first and without hedging: the aggregation rule you propose, in language that could go into the bylaws, including how ties are to be broken and what is to be done when, as here, the pairwise results cycle. Say whether the same rule should govern every Senate-chartered committee or only committees of this size.
2. The ranked list your rule yields for the pending case, with the arithmetic shown briefly enough that a sceptical colleague can check it.
3. The reasoning: which of the properties one would want in an aggregation rule you are giving up, and why that loss is tolerable for a body that must produce a defensible list on a deadline. I would rather be told plainly which defect I am buying than be told that the recommended rule is fair. Say also how sensitive your recommendation is to small changes in the rankings above.
4. The strongest case for the position you reject, stated in its best form rather than its weakest, followed by your reply.
5. The procedural objection that we are choosing the rule after seeing the results, together with a transitional provision that answers it, including what I should tell the Dean's office this week if the Senate has not yet adopted the rule when the offers are due.
6. A review clause: when, and on what evidence, the Senate should revisit the rule.

Keep it in memo form, with no bibliography and no survey of the literature.
