I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set in a seventeenth-century port city, and I have spent the last several months arguing with a friend who teaches history about what it would even mean for either of us to get the past right. In the course of those arguments I assembled the following chain, and it now worries me because it seems to prove far too much. (1) Many historical descriptions are retrospective: they characterize an earlier event by its place in a later sequence, as when we say that a skirmish was the opening of a long war, though no one at the skirmish could have known that. (2) A description that depends on later events cannot be a description of the past as it was for those who lived it. (3) Historians also group events under concepts such as the Reformation or the Industrial Revolution, concepts that are not found in the events themselves but are imposed by the historian to make a narrative cohere; call these colligatory concepts if you like. (4) If history depends on retrospective descriptions and imposed colligatory concepts, then the past does not come to us already narrated; the narrative is a construction. (5) If every historical narrative is a construction, then no narrative corresponds to the past as it actually was. (6) If no narrative corresponds to the past as it actually was, then a scrupulous history and a nationalist myth are equally constructions; they differ in perspective and rhetorical purpose, not in objectivity. (7) That cannot be right, since it would make the difference between a historian who documents a massacre and a propagandist who denies it a difference of style. (8) So one of the steps must be wrong.

I would rather keep (1) through (4) than (7), but I need to know what it costs to keep them. Which step should I reject, and what conception of historical objectivity can survive the rejection? Please be specific about where the argument equivocates or overreaches, and what the alternatives -- the Rankean aspiration to show the past as it actually was, a Carr-style dialogue between present and past, a White-style emphasis on emplotment -- each have to give up. I am not asking for a reading list; I want the diagnosis.
