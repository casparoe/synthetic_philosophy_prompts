Last month a consignment of ours was held at the port, and the argument that followed turned entirely on whether the goods should be classified as a fruit preparation or a vegetable preparation. That dispute sent me back to Nix v. Hedden, the 1893 decision in which the Supreme Court unanimously held that a tomato is a vegetable for the purposes of the Tariff Act of 1883 even though botany classifies it as a fruit, on the ground that the statute used the words in their ordinary rather than their technical sense. I know what the Court decided. What I cannot get straight is what kind of question it was answering, and I want to use the School of Names to find out.

Gongsun Long is that school's best-known figure, and the White Horse Dialogue, in which he defends the claim that white horses are not horses, is the best-known thing he wrote. Read one way it is a trick; read another, it exploits the fact that "X is not Y" can deny either identity or class membership, so that "white horses are not horses" comes out true on the reading "not all horses are white horses." I would like you to bring that argument, together with the rest of the school's apparatus — the essay on hardness and whiteness, where hardness and whiteness are treated as separable, the essay on pointing at things, which worries about reference and the referent, and the school's practical ambition of rectifying names — to bear on the tomato case, and then to bring the tomato case back to bear on the school.

Concretely. First, say what the Court's opinion presupposes about how the extension of a kind term is fixed, and what, by the school's lights, the Court was really being asked to decide. Second, lay the white horse argument against it: which reading of the paradox — the ambiguity of the negative copula, the difference between the criteria by which "horse" and "white horse" are applied, or the suggestion that a term like "horse" names a stuff rather than a countable kind — actually fits what the Court did, and what each of the other readings would have predicted instead. Third, take the separation of hardness from whiteness seriously and ask whether the Court's move, which prises the botanical property apart from the culinary use, is an instance of that separation or a denial of it, and what the separation thesis implies about a single thing belonging to two kinds at once. Fourth, say plainly where the theory runs out: the school can diagnose the ambiguity in "is," but it appears to offer no rule for choosing between ordinary and technical usage, no account of who has the authority to fix a name, and an interest in social order rather than in statutes. Tell me whether that is a gap in the theory or evidence that it was answering a different question.

Then reverse the direction. Does Nix v. Hedden vindicate the school's view that kind membership is settled by use and convention rather than by nature, or does it show that the paradoxes, being about the identity of terms, are the wrong instrument for the class-membership questions that actually get litigated? And since the Court could decide only by appealing to common understanding, does that tell for or against the charge that the white horse puzzle is merely verbal? Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of horse, of white horse, of the stone that is both hard and white — then the places where the mapping breaks, and end with a verdict: whether the School of Names supplies anything the ordinary-meaning canon does not already supply, and if it does not, what would.
