I am a novelist, and for the past year I have served as the independent lay member of the panel overseeing a mid-sized English county's 'contribution account' pilot. Under the scheme, an out-of-work resident's benefit is paid at a higher rate while their account carries a positive balance of contribution credits, and steps down to a lower rate when the balance runs out. Credits accrue from verified paid work, registered volunteering and approved training, and, since a revision adopted last spring, from the unpaid care of a disabled or elderly household member, though at half the rate of the other categories. Caseworkers have no discretion to waive a shortfall; the balance is computed automatically from a monthly data return, and the claimant learns of the step-down in the same letter that informs them of it.

The pilot now comes up for decision: extend it county-wide, modify it, or wind it down. The panel has heard from claimants who say the scheme restored a sense of usefulness to their week, and from claimants who say it has made them feel they are serving a sentence; from employers grateful for a stream of applicants already habituated to paperwork, and from care charities who argue that paying half rate for care is a moral judgment wearing actuarial costume. I am the member who must write the recommendation, and I want a document my colleagues can actually vote on, not a restatement of my own politics. What strikes me, as a novelist, is that both the scheme's defenders and its critics are trading on a story — the striver and the wastrel, the shirker and the carer — that my profession has been retailing for two hundred years, and that a points ledger is only that story with an arithmetic face.

Please draft the panel's decision memo. It should recommend one of the three courses, with a time frame and any modifications you would attach; it should engage the strongest case for the option you reject, especially the claim that a scheme counting care at half rate is not measuring contribution at all but ratifying the assumption that some people's hours are worth less; and it should say plainly what a memo of this kind cannot settle. Write it for the panel and the council's cabinet rather than for me. Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introductory or closing matter; the first item must be the recommendation itself, with its time frame.
