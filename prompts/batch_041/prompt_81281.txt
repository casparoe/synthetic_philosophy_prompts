I am taking a graduate seminar on agency, mechanism, and free will, and for the past two weeks we have been circling an example that the instructor calls sphexishness, after a solitary digger wasp of the genus Sphex. When the female has paralyzed a cricket and hauled it back toward her burrow, she leaves it on the threshold and goes inside to inspect the burrow before dragging it in. If, while she is inside, you move the cricket a few inches away, she will emerge, find it, drag it back to the threshold — and then, as though the sequence had been reset, go inside to inspect again before bringing it in. Keep moving the cricket and she will keep repeating the cycle, by some accounts indefinitely, without ever noticing that nothing about the burrow has changed. The point of the example is to give us a vivid picture of a creature that is not acting but merely running: the behavior looks purposive from the outside, yet there is no place in it where the wasp is responsive to what the situation actually calls for. Our instructor used it to press the question of whether anything we do is different in kind, or only different in the length and complexity of the program.

A classmate then produced the following argument, which has been bothering me all week because I cannot find the bad step and suspect it proves far too much. (1) A piece of behavior is free only if it is not the output of a routine that would run the same way no matter what the agent thinks, notices, or cares about. (2) Take any piece of behavior whatsoever — including the most reflective, spontaneous, and apparently flexible thing a person ever does. (3) That behavior can be described after the fact as the output of a rule: if not a first-order rule, then a second-order rule governing when the first-order rules apply, and if not that, a third-order rule governing the second, and so on without end. (4) Any such rule is a routine in the sense of (1). (5) Therefore every piece of behavior, however flexible it looks, is the output of a routine, and by (1) nothing anyone does is free. (6) So the wasp and I are not different in kind after all, only in the length and complexity of the routine. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? I would most like to keep (3): I do not see how anyone could deny that the behavior of a finite creature is in some sense capturable by rules, and giving it up seems to commit me to something mysterious. If something has to go, I would rather it be (1) than (3), but I want to know exactly what I would be buying — what (1) was supposed to be doing for the wasp case, and what becomes of the distinction between running and acting once it is gone.
