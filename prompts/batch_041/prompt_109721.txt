I am a junior at a large public high school and the student representative on our school's science fair ethics review committee. The committee meets once a month, usually with five to eight student research proposals on the agenda, and I have about twenty minutes to review each one before we vote. Most proposals involve human participants, and almost all of them are designed and run by students on their classmates. My role is not to make final legal rulings; the faculty advisor and the district research review office have to sign off on anything we approve. But the committee's recommendation carries weight, and I am the only member who has not taken a research methods course. I need a decision procedure I can actually run in a meeting, grounded in the philosophical conditions that make consent valid, rather than in a list of rules I would have to look up.

Please write that procedure for me. I want it as a checklist or a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because the question is about values rather than facts. It should be built around the conditions that philosophers usually treat as necessary for valid consent: that the person is competent to consent, that they are adequately informed, and that their agreement is voluntary rather than coerced or unduly influenced. But I need those conditions translated into something a high school student can apply to peer research, where the participants and the researchers are often friends, classmates, or students of the same teachers, and where the line between a favor and pressure is not obvious.

The procedure has to handle at least these hard cases: a study that uses deception, where participants are told the study is about one thing but it is actually about another; a study where a teacher offers extra credit or some other benefit for participating; and a study where a participant may not fully understand what they are agreeing to, either because of age, disability, or language. It should also say what to do when more than one problem is present at once, and it should distinguish between consent that is invalid and consent that is valid but should still be refused for other reasons. I do not need citations, a reading list, or a survey of the literature. I need the procedure itself, in a form I can defend to teachers and parents who will ask why we approved or blocked a project.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. First, a student wants to test whether peer pressure affects snack choices. Participants will be told the study is about taste preferences, but the real purpose is to see whether they change their answers after hearing a confederate. The student plans to debrief them afterward. Second, a student wants to survey classmates about homework habits, and a teacher has agreed to offer five points of extra credit to anyone who participates. Third, a student wants to survey classmates about school belonging, including a sixteen-year-old classmate with an individualized education program and a mild intellectual disability. The researcher is a close friend of that classmate and plans to read the questions aloud. For each proposal, give me the verdict the procedure produces, the steps that produce it, and the strongest objection someone on the committee would raise. I expect to dislike at least one verdict, so please do not soften the procedure to make it come out the way I would prefer.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. Where is it likely to misfire? Which judgments are really about values rather than facts? What should I do when the procedure gives no clear answer, and what information would a human decision-maker need in those cases? I would rather have a procedure with honest limits than one that pretends to settle everything.
