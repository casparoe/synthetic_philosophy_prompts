I am a fourth-year PhD student in decision theory, and I need help adjudicating a disagreement that has been running in my department's reading group for two weeks. I was present for the whole exchange and took notes, but I genuinely cannot tell who has the better of it, so I am reporting the dispute as neutrally as I can and asking you to settle it move by move.

The background. We were discussing a chooser who faces two pairs of lotteries. In the first pair she must choose between (A) one million dollars for certain and (B) a lottery paying five million with probability 0.10, one million with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. In the second pair she must choose between (C) one million with probability 0.11 and nothing otherwise, and (D) five million with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise. She takes A in the first pair and D in the second. Dana and Marco disagree about whether this pattern is irrational, and about why.

(1) Dana: The two pairs differ only by a common 0.89 chance of one million, which she gets in both A and B and does not get in either C or D. By the independence axiom, and by the sure-thing principle behind it, a branch that is identical across the options should cancel out of the comparison. Since it does not cancel for her, one of her two choices must be a mistake, and the natural diagnosis is that she is overvaluing certainty relative to the small increments of chance that separate the options.

(2) Marco: That is too quick. The 0.89 branch is not experienced as "the same" in the two pairs, because it interacts with what sits next to it. In the first pair, gambling risks converting a sure million into nothing, which is a qualitatively different prospect from risking nothing when nothing was already the default. She is not being inconsistent; she is refusing to end up with zero when a certain positive amount was available. The independence axiom is a substantive claim about which considerations may count as reasons, and she is rejecting it for a reason — the discontinuity between "certain" and "almost certain."

(3) Dana: "Discontinuity at certainty" is a redescription, not a justification. Any choice pattern can be fit by adding a parameter — a kink here, a probability weight there. If the only evidence for the kink is the very pattern it is invoked to explain, and it makes no independent predictions, then it is not doing normative work; it is relabeling a mistake as a preference. Worse, her pattern flips if you nudge the numbers slightly, and a rational preference should not turn on a rounding error.

(4) Marco: Two replies. First, the value at stake is not ad hoc: it is the same value that makes insurance and lottery tickets jointly sensible, namely that we do not treat increments of money linearly and we care about the difference between a possibility and a certainty in a stable, predictable way rather than case by case. Second, and more important, Dana is treating the axiom as the standard and the choice as the defendant. But the axiom can be decomposed into two more basic claims — that a two-stage lottery should be valued by its reduced one-stage form, and that a common branch may be cancelled — and it is not obvious that the chooser has to surrender the more plausible of the two. If the only route to the axiom runs through indifference to how uncertainty is resolved, then the axiom is what needs defending, not her.

(5) Dana: Then let us separate them. I will grant that reduction of compound lotteries is contestable. But this pair does not require it: as presented, it is already one-stage, and the shared item is a plain 0.89 branch, not a stage. So the sure-thing principle is doing the work, and that is the principle I would defend hardest. If two options give you the same thing in every state in which they differ from a third, your ranking of them should not depend on that third. Marco: that is fine as a slogan, but "the same thing in every state" already assumes we have fixed the relevant state space, and her reasons are about the combination of branches — she cares that zero was ever on the table. Dana: the state space is fixed by the lotteries, and "zero was on the table" is a fact about the menu, not about any state, and the value of an option should not depend on the menu.

(6) Marco: But we accept menu-dependence everywhere else — we value having options, we value commitment devices, we pay to keep doors open. And notice what she is actually avoiding: the state of affairs "I gambled and got nothing when I could have had a million for certain." That reason is about a counterfactual, about what would have happened had she chosen otherwise, and it is therefore not capturable state by state. Dana calls that irrational on the grounds that the value of what happens should not depend on what would have happened under a different choice. Marco says that principle is exactly what is in dispute, and that regret and relief are real attitudes, not errors.

Please adjudicate this point by point. For each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating, being explicit about which specific proposition the speaker gets right or wrong. I would especially like a verdict on three things: whether the chooser's pattern genuinely requires abandoning the independence axiom or can be accommodated without it; whether Dana's charge that Marco's "discontinuity at certainty" merely redescribes the pattern is fair, or whether independent motivation and coherence are enough to make a departure from the axiom rational; and whether the sure-thing principle can be used against the chooser without begging the question, given that it presupposes that only state-by-state outcomes matter. Then say who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing — including any technical claim either of them makes that is simply false and should be corrected rather than weighed.
