I have been asked to circulate a short position statement for a workshop on panpsychism and its practical consequences, and I want a consistency check before it goes out. Here, roughly, is where I have landed.

(1) Phenomenal properties are fundamental: every fundamental physical entity has some phenomenal character. Consciousness is not an emergent add-on that appears only at the level of brains. (2) But there are no subjects at the fundamental level. What is fundamental is unowned phenomenal quality — qualities with no one to whom they are given — and a point of view, a way things are like for someone, arises only in sufficiently organized organisms. I take the combination problem to be unsolved and possibly unsolvable. (3) Moral status is sentiocentric: only subjects can be harmed, since suffering presupposes someone for whom things go badly. Whatever lacks a point of view is not a candidate for moral concern. (4) Nevertheless I hold that physics is not morally neutral. Because fundamental phenomenal quality is the material out of which every experience, including every pain, is composed, the sheer amount and intensity of that quality in a system is itself a moral quantity, and there is a standing reason to prefer physical arrangements containing less of it. On this view cosmology, computation and thermodynamics are ethically loaded in a way that ordinary sentiocentrism misses. (5) I am a moral realist: value facts are mind-independent. (6) I am a physicalist about the human mind: nothing about us transcends the physical facts, and consciousness confers no causal powers over and above the physical ones. (7) Fundamental entities and their intrinsic properties are neither created nor destroyed; physical change is rearrangement, so the total quantity of fundamental phenomenal quality is a constant.

My own suspicion is that (1) and (3) sit badly together, since together they seem to make me a pan-sentientist for whom electrons are possible victims, which I find absurd. But I would like to know whether that is the real problem or whether I am looking in the wrong place.

Please order your reply as follows. First, "Genuine tensions": the incompatibilities that are real, most severe first, each naming exactly which commitments collide, why the collision is more than verbal, and what would have to be given up (roughly 120 to 180 words each). Second, "Tensions that only appear": the pairings that look bad but are not, with the reason each dissolves (two to four items, about 60 words each). Third, "Cheapest repairs and their costs": for each genuine tension, the least costly fix and what it costs elsewhere in the package. Fourth, "The single most economical revision": one paragraph naming the one change that would clear up the most, saying what it buys and what it loses. Fifth, "Verdict": three or four sentences on whether the package can survive as it stands.
