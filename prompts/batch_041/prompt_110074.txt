I have been working on a paper about the problem of other minds and have become convinced that the standard formulation—how can I know that another being has experiences like mine?—takes the wrong shape. In particular, I have been drawing a distinction between what I call the inferential stance and the participatory stance. On the inferential stance, the other's mind is a theoretical posit: I observe behavior, I infer an inner cause, and skepticism about other minds is the worry that the inference is underdetermined by the evidence. On the participatory stance, the other's mental life is not inferred but encountered in the second person: I address them, they respond, and their expressions are not evidence for a hidden state but the medium in which that state is realized for me. I want you to make the strongest case you can for the participatory stance over the inferential one. Argue that it is not a version of behaviorism, that it can accommodate the possibility of error and deception, and that it does not simply presuppose what it sets out to explain.

Please present your argument as exactly five numbered claims, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Do not cite or name any philosophers; I want the argument to stand on its own. Assume the reader already knows the standard skeptical problem, so do not spend a step summarizing it. The fifth claim should state what, if anything, the participatory stance still cannot account for.
