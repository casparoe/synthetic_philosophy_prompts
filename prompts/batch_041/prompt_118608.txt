I'm a freelance writer working up a long feature on the early days of kidney dialysis in Seattle -- the artificial kidney center that in the early sixties had one machine and far more patients than it could treat, and the committee the doctors stood up to decide who got it. You probably know the outline: a small committee, seven people I think, mostly not physicians, patient files with the names stripped out, and a scoring process that ended up weighing things like how many dependents you had, whether you seemed emotionally stable, what you'd already contributed and might contribute later. It got nicknamed the God committee and became the founding horror story of medical ethics; the 1962 Life article by Shana Alexander, They Decide Who Lives Who Dies, is what blew it open. I don't need the history from you, I have that. What I want is for you to run the case through Taurek's argument about whether the numbers count -- the David versus five strangers setup, the claim that you aren't required to save the five and can toss a coin instead, the separateness-of-persons reasoning underneath it. Map the episode onto the theory and then tell me where the mapping fails.

Here's where I keep getting stuck. Taurek's argument is built for a one-shot rescue with a fixed set of claimants: five people here, one person there, you can only get to one group. The dialysis committee never had that shape. It had a rolling queue -- patients arriving over months and years, each one dying without treatment, so the set of people who might be saved was never closed. And the scarce thing wasn't a single rescue, it was machine time, which means the real currency was life-years, not lives. Does Taurek's non-aggregation claim even get a grip on a case like that, or is he just silent about it? I'd also like you to split the committee's criteria into two piles and tell me whether his view sorts them the way I assume it does. Prognosis-type criteria -- this person's body will likely tolerate it, that one's won't -- seem to concern only the patient's own chances and maybe are permitted; social-worth criteria -- mother of three versus bachelor, wage earner versus retired -- rank persons against each other and look like exactly the aggregation he denies. Then the part I really can't sort: the files were anonymized, so the members were choosing without knowing who these people were. Does that anonymity help a Taurekian, hurt one, or is it irrelevant to him? And would he want a lottery over the medically eligible, and if so how do you run a lottery over a set that keeps growing while you're running it? My suspicion is that his argument is act-level while the committee's real question was rule-level, meaning what policy to adopt in advance for a queue -- tell me whether that's a dodge or the right reading. Finish with what the episode does to the theory: confirmation, anomaly, or a case he never imagined, and does the fact that the eventual fix was roughly first-come-first-served plus medical critera count for him or against him.
