There's a pattern I keep running into and I don't have a word for it. I decide to do something because it's the right thing — help a friend move, say, or bite my tongue with my kid. Fine so far. But the moment I check my own reason ("am I doing this because it's right, or because I want to look good?"), the checking itself seems to convert the motive: now I'm doing it partly so I can certify that my reason was the right one, and the deed curdles. If I never check, I never know whether the reason was right at all. And it's lopsided — I can usually tell when someone else acted for the right reason, and I trust them *less* if they announce that they did. So there seems to be something about the right reason that you can't aim at or audit without losing it.

Is there an established name for this? I know it's near the paradox of hedonism (aiming at pleasure loses it), near the worry that a person who acts from duty comes off cold or self-satisfied, and near the idea that someone who deliberates about whether to save their spouse has already gone wrong. But none of those is quite it, because those are about having the *wrong* motive or one thought *too many*, whereas mine is about auditing the right one.

If there's no name, coin one and define it: what exactly is the thing, what is it not, and does it apply to motives like love or friendship too (can you aim at being a good friend?), or only to duty? Keep it under 300 words.
