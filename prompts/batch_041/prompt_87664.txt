I'm in a reading group on animal minds and we keep going round in circles — someone says fish must feel pain because they've got nociceptors and opioid receptors, someone else says of course they don't because there's no neocortex, and then we all go home none the wiser. Before we read another paper I want the actual specification. Write me the desiderata that any theory of the distribution of consciousness across animals has to meet: the phenomena it has to account for, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, and the results from biology, evolution and neuroscience it can't contradict — each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and with a view on which of them are genuine requirements and which are just prejudices of one camp. I mean things like: it has to return a verdict for creatures that can never report anything (a bee, a crab, a hatchling, a patient in a vegetative state) and do that without defining consciousness in terms of the very capacities only the report-giving creatures have; it has to cope with the fact that the machinery is wildly different across birds, cephalopods and mammals while the behaviour comes out similar; it has to say whether the distribution is a matter of degree or an all-or-nothing line, and where the line falls; it can't make consciousness turn on features that look like accidents of our own lineage; and it has to be usable, because the whole point of the exercise is decisions about welfare, farming and research, so a theory that shrugs at every hard case isn't doing the job. A friend in the group also keeps pressing a constraint I can't decide about: take anything your theory calls conscious and swap its biology for something functionally identical — if the verdict changes, she says, you were tracking the substrate and not the experience. She's got a second one too, aimed at anyone who says it comes in degrees: any recurrent circuit can be unrolled into a giant look-up table with the same inputs and outputs, so either the table is conscious as well or the theory can't be checked by behaviour at all. Put both on the list, or tell me why they don't belong there.

Then the part I actually want: flag which of these can't be had together. I suspect there's at least one real conflict — something like wanting a criterion that's necessary and sufficient and independently checkable, versus not building the answer into the test — and if some subset of the desiderata is jointly unsatisfiable then that's the result, and I'd rather hear that then get another scorecard of who says what. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in throwing out the popular theories; I'd have guessed the one about the unreporting creatures, but I keep changing my mind. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases behind them rather than on scoring the contenders. And be straight with me about how shaky this is: say which of your requirements you'd drop first if someone leaned on you, and what would actually change your mind about which ones are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of one camp. British spelling please, I'm handing this round to the group.
