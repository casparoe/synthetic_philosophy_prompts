Right, so I want a case where the strategy-stealing assumption genuinely breaks, and I want you to build it to order rather than pull one off the shelf.

First, what I mean by it, since the phrase gets used for a couple of different things. I'm after the move you make when you want to show the first player in a symmetric game can't lose: suppose the second player had a winning strategy, then the first player makes any old opening move and from then on just plays that strategy as if they were the second player — and because the game is symmetric they're now in the second player's shoes with one extra move already on the board, which can't hurt them. It's that last clause I care about, the assumption that a spare move in hand is never a dissadvantage. Everyone waves it through because it's true in most games people bother with.

So design me a game where it fails. I want the first player to be the one who loses, in a game that's genuinely symmetric — same moves available to both, no special first-move or second-move rules, no tie-break that favours anybody — finite, perfect information, no dice, no draws. And the loss has to come from the extra move itself, not from some rule that's obviously there to punish whoever moves first. Ideally the starting position has a handful of moves that all look playable, maybe even look strong, and the reason they're bad is something about how they interact with each other rather than anything written on their face.

Two things I want you to show me once it's built. First, the honest test: if you let the first player pass on their very first turn, or equivalently delete the poisoned opening from the rules, does the loss flip to a win? If it doesn't, I'll suspect the poison is somewhere else and you've just found a game that's a second-player win for boring reasons. Second, the diagnosis: what's the structural feature doing the work here — what is it about your game that makes a spare move a liability — and what would you have to tinker with to turn it back into a normal strategy-stealing game where the first player obviously can't lose? Be concrete about that, because I want to see whether the failure is a real gap in the argument or just a game with an odd rule.
