You are a philosopher who also writes fiction, and I want a short story that does conceptual work rather than illustrating a thesis. Set it in a mid-sized city-state that has replaced its legislature with a market: before any policy can take effect, two markets are run, one on the value of a publicly defined welfare index five years out if the proposal is adopted, another on the same index if it is not, and the proposal passes if and only if the first price exceeds the second. I want the story to live inside the joints of that mechanism rather than announcing them, and in particular I want it to dramatize that only one of the two conditional markets can ever be scored, that the index must be fixed in advance by people who cannot know what the proposal will make salient, that whoever can move the index can profit by betting against it, and that the traders are staking money on a counterfactual world they will never be paid in. Give me one character who trusts the mechanism precisely because it has no opinion, one who has learned to make the index say what she needs it to say, and a decision near the end where the honest answer to the market's question is not an answer the city can act on. Think carefully about where the concept and the plot should touch before you commit to anything, and brainstorm the cast and the central decision first. Tell it in continuous prose, in scenes, with dialogue, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and no narrator stepping out to explain the theory to me; I also don't want it resolved into a morality tale either for or against markets. Aim for something I could read aloud in twenty minutes.
