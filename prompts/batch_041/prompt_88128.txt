The case, short version: there's a cheating ring in a big intro course — probably 30-40 students swapping solutions to problem sets and two midterms. We can only actually nail one of them, Priya, because she's the only one whose answers are uniquely traceable. Her advisor floated a deal: we drop her case and she keeps her grade, and in exchange she hands over the whole group chat and names everyone. If we don't take it, the ring keeps running and the cheating continues at least another term. We vote Thursday.

Here's the part I can't stop chewing on, and I think it's the actual issue. Our board runs on a rule we all take seriously: you don't let someone who cheated get away with it — not because of the downstream harm, but because we owe it to the students who actually did the work not to wave it through, even in a case where waving it through would catch alot more cheaters. But that seems to put us in a strange spot: we're apparently not allowed to break the no-exceptions rule in order to prevent a whole bunch more violations of that very same rule. That can't be right, right? Or maybe it is and I'm missing why. Half the board just says "consequences, take the deal," the other half says "make one exception and the whole thing is a sham," and neither ever tells me why.

So: you're a professional philosopher who works on normative ethics and moral dilemmas (or institutional ethics, whichever fits better) — draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. I need (a) an actual recommendation — take the deal, refuse, or take it with conditions — with a concrete decision procedure the board can apply, and (b) the reasoning spelled out so a smart colleague who disagrees could really engage with it. Please don't dodge the hard part: I want a genuine answer to whether it can ever be right to break a rule in order to stop more breakings of that same rule, and I want the strongest version of the losing side treated honestly rather than knocked down as a strawman.

Before you write, brainstorm out loud a bit: lay out the two or three live options and the best argument for each, then commit. And think carefully about the crux — whether the value of the rule lives in the act itself (what we do, and to whom) or in the state of the world it produces — because I think that's where the whole thing turns.
