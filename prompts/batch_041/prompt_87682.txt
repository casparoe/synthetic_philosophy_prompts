"Ellsberg's paradox is the famous experiment with two urns. Urn A holds 100 balls, 50 red and 50 black. Urn B also holds 100 balls, each one either red or black, but you aren't told in what proportion — it could be any split at all. You're offered two bets, one at a time: win £100 if you draw a red ball, and win £100 if you draw a black ball, and in each case you get to choose which urn to draw from. Almost everyone chooses urn A for the red bet, and almost everyone also chooses urn A for the black bet. The point is that people prefer the gamble with the known odds to the one with the unknown odds: they're risk-averse, and they'd rather take a risk they can see. Ellsberg showed that anyone who makes both of those choices is being irrational. If you'd rather bet on red from urn A than from urn B, you must reckon urn B has fewer than 50 red balls; if you'd rather bet on black from urn A than from urn B, you must reckon it has fewer than 50 black balls. Both can't be true, so the two preferences contradict each other, and since expected utility theory demands that a rational agent's preferences be consistent, the experiment refutes expected utility theory. It's really the same phenomenon as the Allais paradox, and it's part of why people stay out of the stock market and stick to investments they understand. The moral economists have drawn is that ambiguity aversion is a bias, like loss aversion — something we'd be better off training out of ourselves."
