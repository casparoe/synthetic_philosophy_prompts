I have been asked to give the annual address at our city's commemoration of the deportation of its Jewish community, and I would like you to write it. It is a formal occasion: we gather in a courtyard, the names of the deported are read aloud immediately before and after the address, and I have twelve minutes at most — roughly fifteen hundred words. The audience is mixed. There are a handful of survivors and many of their descendants; the mayor and the council; two classes from the local secondary school; and a substantial and growing group of families from the Armenian, Bosnian, Cambodian and Rwandan diasporas, several of whom have asked, courteously and more than once, why the city's remembrance speaks of only one catastrophe. I am not a historian. I am a teacher who chairs the remembrance committee, and I have spent the past year reading about the dispute over whether the Nazi genocide is unique among mass crimes or comparable to them, and about the thesis that it is better explained as a product of modern society — its bureaucracy, its division of labour, its technical rationality — than as a relapse into ancient hatred. My conclusion so far is that an address which only mourns, and then only resolves, is not honest. I want to say what I think the event was, and why.

So please write the address, and take a definite position rather than surveying the options. Four things it has to do:

1. State early, in plain words, what you take the Holocaust to be, and be explicit about which sort of uniqueness claim you are making, if any: a claim about the event itself, about how it is to be explained, or about what it demands of us now. Everything afterwards should follow from that choice — including which consolations are available and which are not.
2. If you take the modernity thesis seriously, do not let it do its work by redescription. Say which features of modern life did genuine explanatory work and how they operated, and then face the consequence: those features are ones we have no intention of giving up, so a commemoration that places the catastrophe safely in a past we have left behind is comfortable and, on this view, false. If you think the thesis is overstated, say so, and say what it gets wrong.
3. The audience is the hard part. The address must be decent and legible to families whose own catastrophes are not the one being commemorated, without absorbing their losses into ours and without implying a hierarchy of grief; and it must be honest with the schoolchildren about what the machinery of a modern state can do to its own citizens.
4. Do not quote anyone — not survivors, not historians, not poets — and do not name scholars or works. I will not put unverified words in my own mouth, and where the address needs a position, it should describe the position rather than cite a bearer of it.

Write it as a speech, not as an essay read aloud: no headings, no bullet points, nothing that could not be said in a cold courtyard.

After the address, add a note of no more than two hundred words stating the position you took, your reasons for taking that one rather than the obvious alternative, and the places where the address leans on a contested reading of the history or of the concepts.
