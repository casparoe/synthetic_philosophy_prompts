I'm a third-year PhD (philosophy of mind, officially "cognitive science" on the paperwork) and I've been stuck for months in the literature on pain asymbolia, and I keep having the same weird experience. Everyone seems to have the same facts on the table. The patients have the lesions, they'll say something like "yes, I feel it, it's there, it just doesn't bother me," they'll withdraw from the stimulus sometimes and not other times, they'll be startled by it but not distressed. Nobody disputes the reports. And yet the camps just restate themselves at each other and nothing moves. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right.

The shape of it, as I see it. One camp treats asymbolia as the cleanest evidence we have that pain has two dissociable components: a sensory one (intensity, location, quality) and an affective one (unpleasantness, the "badness"), and the patients are missing the second while keeping the first. On that reading the badness of pain isn't baked into the sensation at all; it's a separate ingredient, or maybe a separate attitude the subject takes toward the sensation. Another camp says no, that's not what's going on: asymbolics either aren't really in pain (the state they're in isn't the kind that hurts, so it's not pain in the full-blooded sense) or they still have the aversive thing but it's been uncoupled from the self — the pain is there but it isn't "theirs," doesn't matter to them, has no grip. On that reading asymbolia tells us nothing about the badness of pain, only about ownership or the self. And a third group just doesn't trust the reports at all: massive lesions, sometimes general anosognosia, maybe verbal habit or coping, and you're building metaphysics on a handful of patients who can't tell you what's happening inside them anyway.

My hypothesis, and I'd like it stress-tested rather than confirmed, is that the stall is because 'pain' is doing two jobs and nobody's said which one they're on. If 'pain' is a natural kind to be read off the nociceptive system, then a lesion knocking out one projection obviously still leaves you with pain, and the dissociation is just anatomy — nothing about badness follows. If 'pain' is fixed by what it's like, then everything hangs on whether the patient's state is like anything and whether "like" has a hedonic tone welded into it, which is a different question wearing the same word. But I suspect there's also a hidden empirical question underneath that nobody's isolating, and this is the part I most want you to push on: the reports as given don't distinguish "pain with the unpleasantness deleted" from "unpleasantness present but no longer motivating anything" from "the sensation is there and aversive but not referred to the self." Those are three different dissociations and the clinical material might be consistent with all of them. Same thing with the blindsight analogy people reach for — fine as far as it goes, but blindsight is about report, and here the fight is about whether a feeling is present, which report is exactly the wrong instrument for.

So: diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, is it two questions under one heading, is it a hidden empirical question, or is it bedrock intuitions about what a feeling can be that neither side can argue for? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a cleaner patient population, an experiment, or nothing, because the question is ill-posed as stated?
