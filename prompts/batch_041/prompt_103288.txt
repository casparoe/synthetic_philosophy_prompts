I teach a graduate seminar on language, default assumptions, and the politics of the generic, and I keep returning to Hofstadter's "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" — the satire collected in Metamagical Themas (1985) that transposes sex into race, so that the prescriptivist narrator who mourns the loss of 'man' as a neutral term is made to mourn, in precisely the same tone, the loss of 'white' as a neutral term for human beings. My students split cleanly, and neither camp argues well. One side treats the piece as a knockdown: if the race-swapped sentences are absurd, the sex-original sentences must be too, since the cases are parallel. The other side waves it away as a rhetorical trick — 'man' really does have a generic sense, 'white' really does not, and that is that. A colleague in linguistics tells me both camps are answering the wrong question, which I half believe; I want you to argue for a specific diagnosis and then live with its consequences.

My position — argue for it, and sharpen it where it is vague — is this. The satire's conclusion is correct but its argument is not, and the two come apart at a place worth naming: the objection to the generic masculine is not that 'man' *excludes* women (a claim about reference) but that it *defaults* to men (a claim about interpretation), and these are different phenomena with different cancellation behaviour, different context-sensitivity, and different remedies. The race case trades on the first; the sex case is really about the second; the analogy therefore smuggles a semantic thesis in on the back of a pragmatic one. Argue that once the default/exclusion distinction is in view, the satire is best read as a demonstration rather than an argument — it makes a default visible rather than proving anything — and say what that implies for how we should actually argue for gender-neutral usage, and whether the same move works for the race case at all. I want a committed answer, not a survey: state your verdict and defend it, tell me where the position is weakest, and say what a defender of the satire's *argumentative* pretensions (as opposed to its rhetorical success) would say to rescue it, and why I should or should not be moved. Begin with your bottom line in a single sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
