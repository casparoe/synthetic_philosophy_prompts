I run a monthly philosophy workshop for a mixed group of about fourteen people — graduate students in philosophy and biology, plus a few members of a local wildlife trust — and I have ninety minutes for the next session. The subject is the net welfare of ecosystems, and in particular the uncomfortable suggestion that destroying a habitat might reduce the total amount of suffering in the world, or at least need not increase it, depending on how the welfare is counted. I want the group to arrive at that suggestion through a decision they have to make, rather than hear me state it.

Design the session for me. The core should be a structured deliberation in which participants sit as the board of a fictional regional land trust that must allocate a fixed pot of money across three sites, and the rules must be arranged so that the ecological case for a site and the welfare case for that same site come apart. Give me the whole packet: the scenario, the site dossiers with the actual numbers I should put in them, the roles or factions (including at least one participant whose brief is to speak for the animals rather than for the ecosystem, and one whose brief is to protect the trust's standing with its funders and its members), the sequence and timings, the allocation rule, and the debrief.

Three things I want built into the mechanics rather than into anything I say. First, each dossier should describe its site in quantities a small group can genuinely argue about — roughly how many animals of which kinds, how long they typically live, how they die, what proportion of the young survive to breed — so that the site that looks most "pristine" predictably scores worst on some of these measures and best on others. Second, on one of the sites include a cheap management lever that the board may choose to pull, such as supplementary feeding or predator control, and set the figures so that pulling it raises welfare per animal while lowering welfare summed over all animals, so the intervention question has to be faced and not merely mentioned. Third, at a fixed point in the session I want to change the rule: the board is told that the funder will release the money only if the trust declares in advance which single measure it is maximising — total welfare summed across all creatures, average welfare per creature, or the number of lives that avoid a violent or lingering end — and that the allocation must then be defended in those terms. I expect the ranking of the three sites to flip or to become indeterminate at that point. If you can set the numbers so that it flips, do so; if on your figures it cannot flip, say so plainly and tell me what the exercise then demonstrates instead. And no participant should be handed the vocabulary for any of this: the names for the distinctions should appear only in the debrief.

The debrief should map each phase of the deliberation onto the underlying structure — the difference between the welfare of an ecosystem and the welfare of the individuals inside it, the difference between the three aggregation rules and why none of them is the obviously correct one, the temptation to treat biodiversity or biomass as a proxy for welfare and the hidden work that proxy is doing, and the point at which the exercise stops being able to tell them anything. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: I take it the participants will never be measuring welfare, only moving proxies about, and I want to know which inference the exercise therefore cannot license.

Tell me too what will predictably go wrong the first time it is run: who will try to break the exercise by refusing to accept the invented numbers, which faction will win by accident of the allocation rule rather than by argument, where the ninety minutes will overrun, and how I should handle a participant who objects that the whole question is idle because we cannot know what an insect feels. Include a short facilitator's script for the two moments that need to be delivered precisely — the rule change and the closing frame — and note where the script must not give the game away. No reading list and no citations: the packet should be self-contained, and the figures should be invented and internally consistent rather than borrowed from any real study.
