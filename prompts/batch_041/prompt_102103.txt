Write an essay on the following controversy: whether a thin version of coherent extrapolated volition is a genuine improvement on the full-blooded version, or whether it fails on its own terms.

Set-up you should assume. Coherent extrapolated volition (CEV) is the proposal, familiar from the machine-intelligence literature, that what should be done on someone's behalf — or on all our behalf, collectively — is fixed not by our actual preferences but by what those preferences would converge on if we knew more, thought faster, were more the sort of people we would wish to have been, and had our preferences rendered mutually coherent. It belongs to the wider family of idealized-preference accounts in political philosophy, which hold that what a person really wants is what she would want under improved conditions of information and reflection.

Now consider the following variant, which I will call thin CEV. Thin CEV keeps only the epistemic axis of idealization. It asks what a person would prefer if she were fully informed about the relevant non-evaluative facts and free of logical and probabilistic error, and it forbids the remaining axes. In particular it forbids idealizing along the evaluative axis: it makes no appeal to the sort of people we would wish to have been, and it imposes no requirement that the preference set be rendered coherent by external fiat. Whatever survives full information and error-correction is the deliverable.

The controversy: is thin CEV better or worse than full CEV? Defenders will say it escapes the standard objections — that full CEV launders in the values of whoever specifies the idealization; that the coherence requirement is either trivial or a substantive and contested normative commitment smuggled in as a procedural one; and that the appeal to the people we wish we had been is an empty placeholder that can be filled any way the specifier likes. Critics will say thin CEV either collapses into the very status quo it claimed to correct, or reduces to a mere consistency check on existing preferences, and so cannot do the work an idealization is supposed to do: correcting preferences deformed by deprivation, manipulation, or ignorance.

Test thin CEV against at least two pressure cases before you judge it. First, a person whose actual preferences are internally inconsistent in a mundane way — she wants to keep smoking and also wants to stop wanting to keep smoking. Second, an adaptive preference — someone who was denied a range of options, adjusted to the deprivation, and now sincerely reports not wanting them. Say what thin CEV delivers in each case, what full CEV delivers, and whether either deliverable is acceptable.

Argue from the ideas themselves. Do not supply citations, references, or reading lists, and do not lean on who said what; I want the reasoning, not the provenance. Assume the reader knows the terms of art.

Format the response as follows:

- Summary (~150 words): state the controversy in your own words and state your thesis.
- What the two versions commit you to (~250 words): a precise statement of what full CEV and thin CEV each require, including exactly where they diverge.
- The case for full CEV (~350 words): the strongest argument that the evaluative and coherence axes are doing indispensable work, and that dropping them is not a repair but an amputation.
- The case against full CEV (~350 words): the strongest argument that the extra axes are either empty or dangerous, and that thin CEV is what a responsible idealization theorist should have wanted all along.
- The pressure cases (~400 words): run both cases above through thin CEV, then through full CEV, and say which version handles them better.
- Verdict (~300 words): your conclusion, stated without hedging, and the single consideration that decided it for you. If your verdict is conditional, state the condition exactly.
