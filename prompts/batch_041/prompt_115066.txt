I'm on the board of our county historical society, and we're the body that decides what collections we accept and on what terms. I have to circulate a written decision memo to the board before we meet next Thursday, and I keep going back and forth, so I want the memo to actually argue rather than just list pros and cons.

Here's the situation. The city is deaccessioning the records of a police unit that operated from about 1968 to 1984. Surveillance files, informant logs, license plate logs, photographs taken at meetings and outside bars, some notes from interviews that never led to charges. Around 40 linear feet, no usable index, filed by the officers' own categories. Most of the people in the files were never charged with anything. A fair number are still alive, and two are current officeholders. Under the city's retention schedule its going to be pulped on the 1st unless somebody takes custody. The city clerk offered it to us, but only as a copy: their people would do the redacting, they keep the originals, and they want a condition that no living person is named for 25 years.

Three things are pulling against each other. First, a neighborhood group whose families are all over those files wants them open now, unredacted, and wants their own accounts attached to them — one of them said to me that the file is a record of what the state did to them, so it belongs to them. Second, the files also name people who informed, some of whom were paid and some of whom were probably leaned on, and some of those people or their kids are still around; opening it could put them in real danger, and they never got to consent to being in a police file in the first place. Third, and this is the one that keeps me up: if we take only this collection, then for those sixteen years our picture of that neighborhood is a police picture. The churches, the clubs, the businesses, the tenant fights, the mutual aid — none of it is here and nobody ever collected it. Our development director is also nervous about being known as the place that keeps the snitch files, and our acquisitions budget for next year is basically one collection wide, so whatever we spend on this we don't spend on anything else.

Our archivist has a proposal I half like: accept, but with an item-level public note in the finding aid for every restriction — what's closed, on what basis, until when, and who can lift it — plus a standing right for anyone named in the files to append their own statement that displays at the same level as the file, not buried in a comment field, and a line at the top of each series saying what that series cannot tell you. The city's lawyer says even that much leaks, because saying something is restricted tells the world a file on that person exists, and for a few people that's the whole harm right there.

So: draft the memo. Recommend accept, reject, or accept with conditions, and if it's conditions, spell out the actual terms — who redacts, what's closed and for how long, how the annotations work, what we tell the city, what we commit to funding beyond this collection, and by when, since the 1st is real. Give the board the reasoning, and give the strongest version of the argument on the other side its due rather than a strawman — I'd rather the memo say plainly where we're eating a cost. And please take seriously the question of what accepting this collection commits the archive to for the next hundred years, not just whether it's legally permissible to take it. Skip the general stuff about our professional ethics statements; everyone on the board has read them and they don't decide anything here.
