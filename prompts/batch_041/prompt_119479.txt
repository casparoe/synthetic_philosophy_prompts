I coordinate the staff wellbeing programme at a large teaching hospital. It is jointly run by the employer and the staff side, which is a polite way of saying that half the people in any room are suspicious of the other half, and I have been asked to rewrite the material our peer supporters use. At present that material is, in substance, a popular-Stoicism package: some things are up to us and some are not, concentrate on the first. We have run it long enough that I can see both the good and the harm. The good is real — people stop flogging themselves over outcomes they cannot move. The harm is that "is it in your control?" has become a way of closing down conversations about rosters, staffing levels and broken equipment, and the people running the conversations are not philosophers. A peer supporter gets roughly fifteen minutes with a colleague, has no authority to promise anything, cannot refer anyone upwards without the colleague's consent, and works under a clinical lead who must sign off on anything touching mental health. So I want to replace the slogans with a procedure: something a peer supporter can run in the fifteen minutes, in the order the questions have to be asked, with a clear account of what counts as an answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the matter to a person because the question is one of judgement or of values rather than of fact. Do not send me a reading list; nobody will read it, and I need the procedure itself.

Two half-formed ideas of mine should be tested rather than adopted, and I would rather be told I have the doctrine wrong than be given a procedure built on a distortion. First, I suspect the familiar binary is too coarse and that the ancient version was not really a binary. My understanding is that the Stoics held that only our own judgements and choices are genuinely ours; that health, standing, income and the like are to be pursued, but as preferred indifferents — worth aiming at, never worth the sacrifice of one's character; and that every intention is to be adopted with a proviso attached, so that a plan holds only if nothing prevents it and a thwarted plan is not thereby a personal defeat. If that is right, what we should be teaching is something closer to a four-way sort, and this is the sketch I would like you to attack: (a) things that are up to my choice — my judgements, intentions, efforts; (b) things not up to my choice but open to my influence — my recovery, an application, the outcome of this project; (c) things that are mine to look after but not to determine — a patient's comfort, a colleague's morale, a child's wellbeing; (d) things that are neither. Is (b) and (c) a legitimate extension of the doctrine, or exactly the slide into anxiety about externals that the Stoics warned against, and if it is a slide, what should take its place? Second, I am told that the phrase everyone uses for this, the dichotomy of control, is a modern label rather than anything Epictetus wrote, and that the popular version drops both the proviso and the requirement to act for the common good, which is why the doctrine can be made to look like a machine for telling tired people to accept bad conditions. I do not know whether that is fair, but the test for the procedure should be that it cannot be used that way.

What I need, then, is a written procedure with the steps in order, the stopping conditions, the points where a peer supporter must stop and say that this is not theirs to carry, and the points where the honest answer is that the matter is a disagreement about what the employer owes rather than a Stoic question at all, and the programme has nothing to offer. Show how the proviso is actually voiced out loud in a goal someone states in the first person, since a rule that lives only in a training slide will not survive contact with a conversation. Then run the procedure on three cases: a nurse whose roster was changed without consultation and who is now in conflict with her childminder; a porter who is angry at himself for being slow three months after surgery; and a case of your choosing in which the procedure gives an answer I will not like — where it tells the peer supporter to stop, or hands the decision back to the colleague on the grounds that it is a values question. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which cases it will systematically misread, where a peer supporter should recognise that they are out of their depth, and what it would take to fix those limits without turning the fifteen minutes into an assessment.
