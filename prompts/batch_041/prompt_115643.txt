I want to use the Breivik case -- the 2011 Norway attacks and the trial that followed -- as a test case for Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment." The part I care about is the sanity fight: the court-appointed psychiatrists split on whether he was psychotic, one team saying yes and one saying no, and in the end the court held him fully responsable, which is pretty clearly what the public wanted. Map it for me. Who's taking the participant stance and who's taking the objective stance at each stage -- the shrinks, the judges, the public, and Breivik himself, who kept insisting he was sane and demanded to be treated as responsible? What are the reactive attitudes actually doing in that verdict? Then turn it around and stress the theory. Strawson says we can't really sustain the objective attitude toward a normal adult, but these professionals did sustain it, at least for a while, and the law asks them to. Is that a problem for him, or is it covered? And does the insanity defense institutionalize the objective attitude as a standing policy rather than a temporary suspension -- a verdict about someone rather than a method of understanding him -- and can Strawson's account make room for that distinction, or does he just blur it? Be honest about where his framework goes quiet on a case like this instead of forcing it to speak. Answer in American English.
