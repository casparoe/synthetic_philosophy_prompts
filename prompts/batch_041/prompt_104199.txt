I have been thinking about letterforms on my own, without any training in design or linguistics, and I have arrived at a view I would like you to pressure-test. The view is this: there is no essence of the letter a — no single feature or checklist of features that all and only a's share. What makes every a an a is instead a set of generative constraints on how a shape may vary while still counting as the same letter within a system of contrasts, together with a style that systematically deforms those constraints. On this view, the category is context-sensitive and graded: an a is individuated by its role in a contrast set — the other letters it must be distinguished from, the word it sits in, the font family or hand it belongs to — rather than by any intrinsic property. Style, then, is not decoration added to a fixed skeleton; it is a consistent way of bending the constraints, and the identity of the letter is what survives the bending.

I would like you to tell me what would count against this view. I am not looking for a survey of what other people have said. I want the observations, cases, arguments, and formal results that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be. Please include things that have arguably already happened — letterforms that have existed, scripts that have collapsed distinctions, type designs that seem to break the view — as well as hypothetical cases. In particular, I am interested in the following kinds of pressure.

First, cases where a shape seems to be an a despite violating every constraint I might name, or where a shape satisfies all the constraints but is clearly not an a. Consider, for example, an a that has lost its enclosed counter and is drawn as a single open curve; an a that is a perfect circle with a vertical stroke on the right, which some might read as a d or a q depending on context; an a and an o distinguished only by a tiny spur or a difference in stroke weight; or a script in which the letter is identified by its position in a word rather than by its shape at all. Do these support the view, or do they show that it cannot do the work it promises?

Second, the role of contrast sets. If the identity of an a depends on the other letters it must be distinguished from, what happens when the contrast set changes — when a font drops a letter, when a script merges two letters, when a reader does not know the language? Is there a fact of the matter about whether a given shape is an a that is independent of any particular contrast set, or does the view collapse into relativism about letters? What would a defender of the view have to say about a shape that is an a in one system and a different letter in another, or about a shape that is read as an a by one community and as a mere ornament by another?

Third, the notion of a generative constraint. I am attracted to the idea that what survives stylistic deformation is a set of rules — something like a grammar of letterforms — rather than a prototype or a family resemblance. But I worry that this is either too strong or too weak. If the constraints are precise enough to generate all and only the a's, they may exclude real a's; if they are loose enough to include all real a's, they may fail to exclude non-a's. Is there a formal result or a concrete case that shows this dilemma is unavoidable? Could a machine-learning system trained on a large corpus of letterforms settle the question, or would it merely reproduce the same ambiguity?

Fourth, the relationship between letter identity and style. If style is a systematic deformation of constraints, then two fonts with very different styles should still share something that makes them both fonts of the same alphabet. What is that shared thing? Is it a skeleton, a topology, a set of distinguishing features, a motor program, a set of repair rules, or something else? Which of these would count against the view if it turned out to be the real story? And which would a committed defender of the view concede as a genuine loss?

Finally, I would like an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If every apparent counterexample can be absorbed by saying that the contrast set or the style or the context is different, then what observation could possibly count against the view? Is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is it simply the price of a context-sensitive account of a category? Please distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones the view's defenders would themselves concede as counting against them, as opposed to which ones they would dismiss. I would personally find it most persuasive if you could show me a case where the view is committed to a consequence that turns out unacceptable, or a case it cannot describe without circularity.
