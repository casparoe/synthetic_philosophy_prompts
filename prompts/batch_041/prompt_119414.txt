I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy or economics, and I've been asked to run a 75-minute session at my company's engineering offsite. There will be about eighteen people, mixed seniority, in one room. I would rather they walk into the problem than hear me describe it.

What I want them to walk into: most of the decisions our team makes happen in sequence. The first person runs the benchmark, the second reads the changelog, the third has a hunch, but by the time you are fifth in line the only thing you can see is what the four people ahead of you publicly chose. I think that after two or three decisions the rest of the room stops contributing evidence and starts reading the room, and that the resulting consensus looks equally confident whether or not it happens to be right. I also think the same visible behavior can be produced by a completely different engine: someone who is mainly worried about how a senior person will rate their judgment will follow the crowd even when the crowd tells them nothing about the truth. Design a session in which the group feels both of these, and feels how little information a confident consensus can carry.

By the end I want them to have seen, from the inside, that (i) a consensus can be confident and nearly empty of information at the same time, (ii) whether things go right or wrong is close to a coin flip decided by who happened to speak first, (iii) following the crowd is usually the sensible move rather than a failure of nerve, which is the uncomfortable part, and the silence of the people who privately disagreed is not stupidity, and (iv) the same silence can be produced by an incentive that has nothing to do with being right, and that the two look identical from the outside while calling for different fixes.

Requirements.

Setting and materials. Two tables of nine plus me as facilitator; a whiteboard or shared doc; sealed envelopes or a shuffled deck for private signals; blank slips for private notes. Nothing that costs money.

Rules. Participants communicate only through their public choice in the sequence: no side channels, no showing cards. Signals must be generated by a mechanism the group can see is fair, and each person must know their own signal without being able to infer anyone else's.

Ease of reasoning. The right move at any point must be obvious to an intelligent person in about two seconds. If the activity requires anyone to compute a likelihood ratio it will stall and I will lose the room, so arrange the payoffs and the signal strengths so that following or not following is a judgment rather than a calculation.

Payoffs. Points, not money, with a small prize for the winning table, and no real performance evaluation of any kind.

Engineer these four moments into the activity, in whatever order and with whatever round structure fits the time:

(a) Same evidence, opposite conclusions. Two tables, one question, one fixed truth, and the same multiset of private signals dealt to both, but in different orders, with the first two or three signals at one table pointing one way and at the other table pointing the other way. Both tables should cascade; at least one should end up wrong. Reveal the truth only at the end, and let the losing table discover that it held exactly the evidence the winning table held and differed only in the order it arrived. Nobody should be told in advance that the two deals are permutations of each other.

(b) Information that is present but unused. Before each public choice, every participant writes their own private read on a slip and seals it. After the round, the slips are collected and compared against the public sequence. The point to land: the consensus was built out of the first two or three signals, and everyone else's evidence was intact and simply never entered the public record.

(c) What it takes to break a run. A round in which one participant holds a contrary signal strong enough to outweigh the accumulated public evidence, and at least one holds a contrary signal that is not strong enough. The second person should stay silent, and the debrief should establish that staying silent was correct. Give the signals a visible strength, for instance one kind of evidence is worth more than another and everyone knows which kind they hold, so that strength rather than courage is what breaks the run.

(d) The second engine. A round in which the payoff no longer depends on being right at all: participants are scored on whether their answer matched the most senior person at the table, or on how a fictional director would rate their judgment. The behavior should look identical to the earlier rounds and be driven by something else entirely. State in the rules that no real evaluation is involved, and that I will say so out loud before the round.

Then:

Debrief. Map specific moments from the session onto the claims above: which round showed which, what the slips showed, why the silent person was right. Include the part the group will resist, namely that following was reasonable, and how I should put that without sounding as though I am excusing it.

Failure modes. What predictably goes wrong the first time this is run, and what I do in the room: the cascade failing to form because the early signals disagree; one table cascading and the other not; the two tables overhearing each other's votes; someone breaking the run for a joke; the tables finishing at very different speeds; someone recognizing the setup; the reputational round landing badly in a room with real seniority in it. Give me a fallback for each.

Honest limits. Tell me which features of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself in words. I would rather know now than discover it while running the session.

Format. A run sheet I can follow while facilitating: timings, the exact words I read aloud for each round's instructions, the materials list, the payoff scheme, the debrief script, and the two closing sections. Do not cite anything or suggest further reading; I have no web access in the room and neither will they, so the whole thing has to stand on its own.
