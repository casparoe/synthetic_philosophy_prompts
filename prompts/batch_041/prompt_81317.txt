I am writing up my epistemological commitments for a reading group on self-certifying reasoning, and before I circulate the paper I would like a consistency audit of the package rather than a survey of the literature. Here is what I hold, roughly in the order I would defend it.

(1) Knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable process. The knower need not be able to show that the process is reliable, and need not know that she knows.

(2) Knowledge is closed under competent deduction: if I know that P, and I know that P entails Q, I can come to know Q by inferring it.

(3) Justification is not closed under known entailment. My visual experience justifies my belief that there is a mug on the desk, but it does not justify my belief that I am not a brain in a vat, and the second belief is not needed as a premise for the first.

(4) Perceptual experience justifies perceptual belief straightaway. I do not have to earn the right to trust my eyes before I may use them, and in particular I do not need antecedent justification for believing that my experience is reliable.

(5) Ordinary perceptual knowledge is commonplace, and the skeptic is wrong.

(6) There is such a thing as knowledge that comes too easily. A person who takes the readings of a novel instrument at face value and then tallies up her successes — the gauge read full and the tank was full, the gauge read empty and the tank was empty, so the gauge is reliable — does not thereby come to know that the gauge is reliable. The tally is circular and yields nothing.

Now the case I suspect does not sit well with the rest.

(7) A laboratory has two instruments that measure the same quantity by entirely different physical principles. Neither can be calibrated against any external standard, and neither can be checked except against the other. A scientist runs both over many trials, finds that they agree, and concludes that both are reliable. I want to say that she knows. Cross-checking two independent instruments is the kind of thing that does give you knowledge, and it is not the same as the gauge tally.

What I want from you:

- Which of (1) through (7) are actually in tension with which, and which pairs only look like they are in tension.
- Rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what has to give and what that costs.
- Tell me whether (6) and (7) can be held together at all, and if so, what principle draws the line between the two cases. If no principle draws it, say which of the two I should drop.
- Finally, name the single revision that would clean up the most while costing the least, and say what I would be giving up by making it.

Be specific about which numbered commitment is doing the damage in each case. I would rather hear that something has to go than that the package is fine as it stands.
