I'm building the final exam for an upper-level metaphysics seminar on material objects and I need one essay question on the special composition question -- the van Inwagen thing, when do some things add up to a further thing. My problem is that every student in the room can recite the standard menu (universalism, nihilism, organicism, restrictivism, whatever) and then just sit there. So I want a question where they can't do that. I'm going to hand them a view they've never seen and make them actually test it.

Here's the view I'm putting on the exam. Call it the perturbation principle: some things compose a further thing just in case there is some salient way of perturbing them under which they respond as a single unit -- i.e. a counterfactual in which their joint response isn't just the sum of the responses each would have had on its own. (I made this up on the drive in, so please don't worry about whether it's in the literature or hunt for who said it. I want it treated as a live proposal, not as a target to be identified.)

What I need from you:

First, act like an expert in contemporary metaphysics and mereology -- someone who knows the composition debate cold and also knows where clever-sounding principles quietly fall apart. Think carefully and, before you write anything, spend a few lines brainstorming the failure modes: what kinds of cases tend to break principles like this, what the usual cheap escape routes are, and which of them a good student should be able to spot and a weak one shouldn't.

Then give me:

1. The exam question itself, worded so it can't be answered by naming the standard positions or by a summary of the debate. It should make them commit to an extension for the perturbation principle and defend it.

2. A model answer, around 700 words, at the level of the best student in the class.

3. A marking scheme: five or six must-hit points, marks adding to 20, and for each point one line on how students typically blow it.

Things I want the question to force them through, so build these into the question and the model answer: the heap of sand and the case of a chair; the statue and the lump of clay (and whether a perturbation principle can even distinguish them without cheating); a living organism and its cells, since that's the case organicism is built for; whether the principle is circular -- does "as a single unit" or "joint response" smuggle in the very notion of an object it's supposed to analyse; what happens when "salient" gets read narrowly versus broadly, and whether the principle then collapses into nihilism at one end or universalism at the other; and whether this is an answer to the special composition question at all or a rival question in disguise. I'd also like the model answer to resist the lazy "it's just vague" verdict -- if vagueness is the objection, say which precisification would rescue the principle and what that precisification costs elsewhere.

No references, no reading list, no "see so-and-so" -- just the question, the answer, and the scheme. Keep the tone like a colleague handing me the draft, and flag anything you think is a trap in the question itself.
