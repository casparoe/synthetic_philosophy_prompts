I'm a novelist — third book out next spring — and the one I'm writing at the moment has a subplot about a woman who sells a kidney to clear her brother's debts, except the book keeps sliding away from me because I can't work out what the actual objection to her doing it is supposed to be, and I keep going round in circles trying to get the arguement straight. Anyway, my writing group has started doing this thing where one of us teaches the others something for an hour before we get to the work, and its my turn, so I've said I'll do 'can you sell that?' — the whole commodification business. The room is eleven novelists and two poets, no philosophers, and they're allergic to anything that sounds like a lecture: they think in scenes and grudges, and they'll trust a story long before they'll trust an argument. Don't explain commodification to me — plan how I should explain it. What has to be in place before the question even makes sense to them, what order the pieces have to go in and why that order rather than another, and what the one example should be that carries the whole hour.

On the example: I keep reaching for professional mourners — paying a stranger to weep at your mother's funeral, which I'm fairly sure is or was a real trade in some places — because it seems to do something kidneys can't. Nobody's harmed, everyone consents, and yet it still feels as though something has gone wrong, which is exactly the itch I want them scratching. But my partner says it's a trap, because they'll all just say 'well, that isn't grief then' and we'll have skipped the arguement entirely. Tell me whether it should carry the thing, what an example has to have and has to lack to do that job, and what to do with kidneys if not that.

Then the misconceptions, in the order this lot will actually produce them and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. I'm expecting 'so you're against all markets then', and 'if both of them are better off, the only thing left is that you find it icky', and 'the real problem is that she's poor, so it's about exploitation and not about the kidney', and I'd bet good money that someone says 'but we pay surgeons, don't we'. Also, a friend of mine mentioned some psychology finding where people don't merely disagree when you put a price on something they hold sacred — they get furious and want to punish whoever did the pricing. If that's roughly right, is it worth putting in front of them, and at what point? And what should I leave out altogether at this level, saying plainly that I'm leaving it out? Finish with one question I can put to the room at the end that would tell me whether the idea has landed, rather than whether they've had a nice evening. British English throughout, please, and keep the plan in the order I'd actually do it.
