I'm in a seminar on early Chinese thought and I've been asked to present on the sixth chapter of the Zhuangzi, the one where four friends agree to be friends and then, later in the chapter, one of them lies dying. I don't have the book in front of me as I write this, so what follows is my own flat paraphrase rather than a quotation, and if I've garbled a name or a detail, correct me before you answer; the shape of the episode is what I want help with. Four men are talking, and one of them proposes that they become friends on something like this basis: whoever can take non-doing as his head, life as his backbone, and death as his rump, whoever sees life and death, being and non-being, as a single body, is the one worth befriending. They look at each other, smile, and agree, and that is that, with no argument offered. Later one of them, I think Zi Lai, is dying. His wife and children are gathered around him weeping. A friend, Zi Li, arrives, and instead of consoling anyone he tells the family to shoo, to get out, not to disturb the one who is being transformed. Then he leans in the doorway and asks the dying man, in effect: how great is the one who made you, and what will he make of you now, where will he send you, a rat's liver, a bug's arm? And the dying man answers, roughly, that a child obeys his parents without question and the same holds for us before the one who made us; the Great Clod gave him this form, wore him out with living, gave him ease in old age, and will give him rest in death, so that if he thinks well of his life he must think well of his death too.

You're a philosopher of early Chinese thought with a serious side interest in the metaphysics of death, and I'd like you to treat this as a close-reading exercise rather than an occasion for the standard line about Daoist acceptance, which I can get from the secondary literature. What I cannot get straight is what the dying man's comfort is supposed to consist in, and whether it is one thing or two things stitched together. On one reading death is a transformation: the same person who is now a man might be made into a rooster, or a crossbow, or a horse's spirit, and the friend's question about what the maker will make of him next is exactly the question you would ask about a lump of clay being reshaped, which seems to presuppose something that persists through the reshaping. On another reading death is rest, but rest is a good for someone who is tired, and if death is the dissolution of the person then there is no one left to be rested, so the metaphor appears to smuggle a surviving subject back in. And the earlier image of life and death as one body with non-doing as its head looks like a third thing again, neither survival nor cessation.

So: is there a single reading of this chapter that makes the dying man's calm intelligible, or does the episode trade on a slide between pictures, and if it slides, does that damage it? Tell me also what the passage commits itself to about what a person is, whether the comfort requires that something survive death or whether it can be had on a thoroughgoing no-self reading, and which textual details would decide the matter if I went back and read the chapter properly. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the argument for it, and only after that the rival readings and what would settle between them; no throat-clearing, no restating my question, and no survey of the scholarship. Think it through slowly, and if my paraphrase has made the passage easier or harder than it actually is, say where.
