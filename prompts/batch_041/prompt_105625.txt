A few months ago I got involved in a local campaign — nothing grand, a proposal to fund universal pre-K in my city — and at a public hearing I argued for it partly out of my faith. I said that every child carries a dignity that isn't earned and can't be forfeited, and that this is what my tradition is getting at when it speaks of human beings as made in the image of God. A friend who teaches political philosophy told me afterward, kindly but firmly, that I had made a mistake. On her view, a citizen who wants to enlist the coercive power of the state owes her fellow citizens reasons they could accept as free and equal people, and a premise that only moves you if you already share my theological commitments isn't a reason anyone else can share. I have been turning this over ever since. I suspect she is right about something and wrong about something, and I want to see the strongest version of each side rather than a parody of either.

Please write a dialogue between two thoughtful people — call them A and B — who have each spent real time on this. A defends the view that citizens who argue for state action should rely on reasons that are publicly shareable, or at least should stand ready to offer such reasons in due course. B defends the view that religious citizens are entitled to argue from their deepest convictions and that the demand for shareable reasons is either unfair to believers or incoherent as stated. Let them actually engage: each should answer the other's strongest point rather than pivot to a fresh one, and neither should be a strawman — B should not be a caricature of an aggrieved believer, and A should not be a caricature of a secular scold.

I especially want the dialogue to make progress on a puzzle that has been nagging me, because I think it is where the real disagreement lives. There are two different things one might demand of a citizen who argues from religion: first, that her argument be one others can enter into on their own terms; second, that she herself be genuinely open to revising her position in light of what others say. These can come apart, and I want to know which one the public-reason constraint is actually tracking. Consider two cases. In the first, someone argues from a premise that runs through revelation and is opaque to outsiders, but she is entirely sincere, listens hard, and would change her mind. In the second, someone argues in thoroughly secular and fully accessible terms, but the secular argument is a fig leaf and she will not budge no matter what anyone says. Whichever criterion the constraint is really about, one of the two characters should find that this case embarrasses their view, and I want to see them either absorb the hit or explain why it isn't a hit. Let them also press each other on whether the burden falls asymmetrically on religious citizens, since secular citizens seem to lean on contestable worldviews of their own.

Have them argue on the merits rather than by invoking famous names as authorities; where they end up, let them be explicit about what, if anything, they now agree on and exactly what remains in dispute.

Please write in American English.
