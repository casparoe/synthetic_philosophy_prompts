I am not a specialist, but the history of famine has recently become personally pressing for me: one branch of my family carries the memory of a famine that the officials of the day called a natural calamity, and the older members of the family have always insisted it was something done to them. I want to decide what I actually think, and I have realized I cannot adjudicate between the natural-disaster reading and the political-crime reading until I know what an adequate account of famine causation would even have to do. Act as an expert in development economics and in the philosophy of the social sciences, with deep comparative knowledge of famines and of the entitlement literature. Think carefully and work step by step; before you write anything, brainstorm the candidate requirements, then organize them.

Give me the desiderata: a numbered list of what any adequate theory of famine causation -- one that would license a verdict on whether a given famine was natural or political -- must deliver. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it. I want the list to cover at least the following, though you should add what I have missed:

- The cases it must get right: the Bengal famine of 1943, where food was available in aggregate yet millions died; the Irish famine of the 1840s, where a blight met an ideology of non-interference; the famine that followed the Great Leap Forward, where the trigger was policy and the reporting was falsified; and the negative cases -- droughts and harvest failures that produced no famine, and famines that came with no harvest failure at all.
- The distinctions it must not collapse: trigger versus amplifier; food-availability decline versus entitlement failure; famine as a mortality event versus famine as a social process; a wrong of commission versus a wrong of omission versus a structural outcome with no author; and the difference between a theory of famine and a theory of famine relief.
- The empirical results it must be consistent with, including the much-cited thesis associated with Amartya Sen that famines do not occur in functioning democracies with a free press, the role of markets, hoarding and speculation, and the fact that mortality figures are themselves politically contested.
- Constraints on the theory itself: it must be falsifiable, and it must not make the political reading unfalsifiable by definition -- if every famine is political, I need to know what that claim excludes, and whether the category of a purely natural famine is simply empty.

Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the prejudices of a particular camp, and, most importantly, which subsets look jointly unsatisfiable -- in particular, whether the demand for a clean natural/political dichotomy can survive the demand to explain mixed and contested cases. Test the resulting standard against one case that will not be in any textbook: an electoral democracy with a lively national press in which a government, facing a close election, quietly withheld relief from a province that had voted for the opposition and leaned on the local outlets not to report it -- so that both the democracy thesis and the standard entitlement story seem to misfire. Finally, score Sen's entitlement approach and the political-crime view briefly against your list, but keep the weight on the requirements themselves, since that is where I need the thinking done.
