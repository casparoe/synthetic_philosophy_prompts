Ok, I need help actually understanding this old Lewis Carroll dialogue, the one where the tortoise argues with Achilles about Euclid. I've read it a few times and I can follow what happens line by line but I don't think I get what the point of it is supposed to be. Here's the stretch I keep getting stuck on:

Achilles has written down

(A) Things that are equal to the same are equal to each other.
(B) The two sides of this Triangle are things that are equal to the same.
(C) If A and B are true, Z must be true.
(Z) The two sides of this Triangle are equal to each other.

and then:

"You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."

"And why must I?"

"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"

"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."

"Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it

(D) If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true."

and then later the tortoise makes Achilles write down

(E) If A and B and C and D are true, Z must be true.

Until I've granted that, of course I needn't grant Z.

So my instinct is that the tortoise is basically cheating. He's treating a rule of inference as if it were just one more premise in the argument, and of course if you let someone do that you can keep generating new conditional premises forever, because the rule you need to get from the premises to the conclusion never gets to do any work -- it just gets written down as another item on the list. So the "infinite regress" is an artifact of a category mistake, and Achilles is being dim for playing along, and there's no deep lesson about the justification of deduction here at all.

But that feels too quick, and I suspect I'm smuggling in something. A few things I'd like cleared up. Is the tortoise really doing what I said, or is the thing he's doing more interesting than that -- because the tortoise never actually denies anything, he just won't infer, and I can't tell whether "won't infer" is a mistake or just a refusal to play a game he hasn't agreed to play. Second, what is it for him to "accept" a hypothetical as opposed to accepting its consequent when the antecedent holds? Is accepting a conditional supposed to already be a disposition to infer, or is that exactly what's at issue, so that the story is question-begging either way? Third, whatever the right reading is, does the regress actually bear on anything -- does it threaten the idea that mathematical proof compels assent, or does it only show something about what you can force someone to do with words, which might be a much smaller result? And if the point is a real one, what exactly is the diagnosis: that justification has to bottom out somewhere non-inferential, or that the demand for a justification of deduction is confused from the start, or something else?

I'd rather have the actual philosophical content of the puzzle spelled out than a summary of the plot, and if my "tortoise is cheating" reading is wrong I want to see specifically where it goes wrong, not just that it does.
