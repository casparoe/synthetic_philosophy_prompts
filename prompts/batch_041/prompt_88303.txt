I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been reading about Quine's gavagai example — the field linguist who hears a native say gavagai as a rabbit runs past, and who can never determine, from any amount of observation, whether the word means rabbit, an undetached rabbit part, or a momentary rabbit-stage, since each rival translation manual reinterprets every test he can devise. I tried to turn this into a clean argument, and I want to know whether it holds up, where it goes wrong if it doesn't, and what repairing it would cost. Here it is:

1. The only facts about what an expression means are facts about the behavior of its users — what they assent to, and what they would say in which circumstances.
2. For any given body of such behavioral evidence, there exist rival translation manuals that fit the evidence equally well while assigning different meanings to the same term.
3. So no possible evidence could determine which manual is correct.
4. So there is no fact of the matter as to which is correct.
5. Nothing in this reasoning turns on the language being foreign; it applies to the English word rabbit no less than to gavagai.
6. So there is no fact of the matter about what my own words mean.
7. But if that is so, there is no fact of the matter about what I meant, intended, or believed either, and no difference between asserting something and emitting a noise. Two program specifications that no test could distinguish would not even be two ways of saying the same thing, since there would be nothing for them to agree about; and in asserting (7) I would, by my own lights, be asserting nothing at all.

I would rather keep (1) and (2) than (7). Tell me which step to reject, why it looked acceptable in the first place, and what giving it up would cost me; if the argument is actually valid and something has to be surrendered anyway, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and what the alternatives each charge.
