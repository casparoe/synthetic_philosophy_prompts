I'm in a decision-theory reading group and we keep re-litigating the same thing: the result — I think it's Good's, the total-evidence one, but dont hold me to the attribution — that a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer can never do worse in expectation by getting free, perfectly reliable information before she acts; the expected value of information is always ≥ 0. Someone in the group insists there are real cases where information hurts, but every time he sketches one, someone else says 'you just gave the agent a weird taste for not knowing' and we move on. I want to settle it with a properly engineered case instead of vibes.

So build me a thought experiment in which a decision-maker strictly prefers not to receive a free, perfectly reliable, genuinely decision-relevant piece of information — expected value of information strictly negative, to the point that she'd pay a small amount to avoid being told. Constraints: keep it realistic (a person, a doctor, a firm, whatever — no omniscient predictors, no exotic physics, no time travel), and keep the scenario itself to roughly a paragraph. The negative value must not come from (a) her having a raw taste for ignorance or a standing wish to believe something false, (b) the information being costly, unreliable, or arriving too late to act on, or (c) some adversary reading her mind or watching her and reacting. I want it to fall out of the structure of the decision problem itself, with her being rational by some defensible standard — just not necessarily the exact one the theorem assumes. If the only way to pull it off is a non-expected-utility preference, fine, but then make it a preference a reasonable person could actually have.

After the scenario: tell me which assumption of the non-negative-value result is being violated and why exactly that's what does the work; then check whether the negative value survives if you 'repair' that assumption (say what the repair is and what's left of the case); then give the strongest reply available to someone who wants to keep the 'always take the free evidence' line — including the redescription move where they expand the outcome space until the theorem applies again. Finish by naming the single feature of your case that, if deleted, collapses the negative value back to zero or positive, and say whether the case can be patched to do without it.
