Question: (a) State the first formulation of the categorical imperative and explain what a maxim is. (b) Kant says a maxim can fail the test in two different ways — describe both, and say which kind of duty each one corresponds to. (c) Apply the test to two of the four examples Kant gives in the Groundwork (pick any two) and say which kind of failure each involves. (d) State the second formulation and say what it adds that the first one doesn't. (20 points)

Student's answer:

(a) Kant's first formulation says to act only on the maxim that you can at the same time will to be a universal law. A maxim is the principle you're acting on — the action plus the reason you're doing it for. So the test is basically: take your maxim, imagine everybody acting on it, and see if it holds up. If universalizing it would make things worse for everybody, or make the practice it depends on impossible, then the action is wrong.

(b) There are two kinds of failure. The first is a contradiction in conception: universalize the maxim and the practice it depends on can't even exist anymore, so the maxim sort of destroys itself. That's what gives us perfect duties, which are strict and can never be overridden. The second is a contradiction in will: the maxim can be thought as a universal law just fine, but you can't coherently will it as one, because it would leave you without something you need. That gives imperfect duties, which you have some latitude about when and how to fulfill.

(c) First example, suicide. The maxim is something like: I'll end my life out of self-love when living promises more pain than pleasure. You can imagine everyone doing that without any contradiction in the thought itself, but you couldn't consistently will it, since self-love's whole function is to preserve life, so willing its destruction undercuts the very motive you're acting on. So this one is a contradiction in will, and it grounds a perfect duty not to kill yourself.

Second example, the false promise. The maxim is something like: when I need money, I'll borrow it and promise to pay it back even though I know I can't. Universalize that and promising stops working — nobody would believe a promise, so there wouldn't even be such a thing as promising to make a false one. That's a contradiction in conception, and it grounds a perfect duty to keep your promises.

(d) The second formulation says to treat humanity, whether in your own person or in someone else's, always as an end and never as a means. It's more intuitive than the first one because you can usually just look and see whether you're using somebody. It also explains why the first formulation works at all: rational beings have dignity instead of a price, so you can't just trade them around. For example, a boss who pays her workers the least she can get away with is using them as a means, so she's treating them as things — which the first formulation doesn't really capture.
