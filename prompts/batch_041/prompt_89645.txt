For about a year now, me and two friends -- one a type designer, one a vision scientist -- have been going around in circles on a question that looked trivial at the start: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? We meet every few weeks and every time we re-litigate the same ground and end up at the same three corners, and nobody has moved. I want you to tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, not to settle it.

Here's the shape of it. My type designer friend says the identity of a letter is its role in a system of contrasts -- an 'a' is 'a' because it isn't a 'b' or an 'o', the way a phoneme is defined by the features that distinguish it from its neighbors, not by any positive core. Change the contrast set and you change the letter. My vision scientist friend says that's mysticism: recognition runs on prototypes and families of exemplars with graded typicality, there's no invariant template in the visual system, and the word essence is doing no work. And I keep saying that both of them might be answering different questions under one heading -- one about how a mark gets identified, one about when a mark counts as correct -- and that the word 'a' is carrying both.

Some things we've thrown at each other that keep it alive. A Cyrillic 'а' is drawn identically to a Latin 'a' and is a different letter, so shape alone can't be the whole story; the IPA 'ɑ' is a different shape and arguably the same letter doing a different job. In one typeface the 'a' is single-story, in another double-story, and readers of both will swear they're both just 'a'. I've tried a little test on people where I ask them to draw a lowercase double-story 'g' from memory and most of them produce something that isn't the shape they've been reading their whole lives, which seems to matter somehow but I can't say how. And I've got a half-remembered distinction from aesthetics about works that are specified by a notation, like a piece of music where any faithful performance is the work, versus works that are identical with a particular physical object -- a typeface feels like it wants to be the first kind, but I've never seen anyone show that a letter scheme really is notation-like, since there's no finite rule that tells you where 'a' stops and not-'a' begins.

There's also an old project I read about where someone tried to get a computer to invent a whole alphabet in one consistent style, working on a grid, and the hard part was supposedly that the program had to have the "spirit" of each letter rather than a template -- I never understood what that was supposed to mean, and I wonder if it's the same confusion.

My hypothesis, and I want you to treat it as a hypothesis: this is two questions tangled together -- a descriptive one about how marks get sorted into letters by a reader, and a normative one about what the conventions of a writing system permit -- plus a third, unstated one about whether letterforms exist apart from their instances at all. But I could be wrong and it could be one real question that's just hard.

So: diagnose it. Is this a verbal dispute, sides answering different questions, a hidden empirical question about categorization, or bedrock intuitions about whether types are real that neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress -- a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, or just dropping the question? Say how confident you are in your diagnosis and single out the point you're least sure about. Write it in American English.
