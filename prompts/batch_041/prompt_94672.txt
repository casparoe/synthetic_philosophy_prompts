A colleague keeps pushing a book on me — one of those bracing trade books about global development — and the chapter on corruption is the one he reads out loud whenever the subject comes up at work (I do grants admin at a small foundation that funds health projects in West Africa, so it comes up alot). Here's his paragraph, as he tells it: 'Corruption is a universal category. Every society has a word for it, and the usual definition — using public office for private gain — is what everyone actually means by it; what varies is only how much of it there is. Clientelism is just corruption organized through networks instead of cash: a politician hands over a job or a sack of rice and gets a vote back, which is a bribe with better manners. Gift-giving is the same transaction in traditional dress, and the anthropologists who insist it follows a different logic are describing the wrapping, not the present. The countries that look clean are the ones that industrialized first and professionalized their civil services; there's no cultural secret, just development. So the whole "Western standards are parochial" line is a red herring: the standard isn't Western, it's just what you get when officials stop selling their decisions, and ordinary people in corrupt countries know this perfectly well — its why they complain about it.'

I'm inclined to think he's basically right — the relativist move has always struck me as a way of letting the powerful off the hook — and I'd like to be able to lean on this paragraph in a short note I'm writing for our board about whether to make grantees sign an anti-bribery code. But a friend of ours who spent years doing fieldwork in Senegal says the paragraph is confidently wrong in about four places and refuses to say which. So go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on the anthropology and political economy of corruption would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's flat wrong. Give me a verdict on each claim rather than a tour of the literature, and take a side on the bottom line: is the anti-relativist conclusion earned by the parts of the paragraph that survive, or does it need an argument the paragraph never gives? Tell me which side you'd actually defend and why, and flag where your confidence is lowest — I'd rather have your view than a list of positions. If you can also guess which four places my friend probably means, that'd help me win the argument.
