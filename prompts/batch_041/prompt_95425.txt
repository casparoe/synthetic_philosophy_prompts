Write me an essay on the sunk cost thing — whether it's ever rational to let what you've already spent make a difference to what you do next. I dont want the textbook version where you announce that sunk costs are sunk, ignoring them is a theorem of expected utility, the end. I want you to take on the strongest version of the other side and then actually land somewhere.

Here's the shape I want. First, split the question in two, because I think most of this arguement is people talking past each other: (a) cases where the past expenditure has left the world different in a way that bears on the future — you learned a skill, built a reputation, made friends, the machine is half-built, quitting now would say something to future partners — and (b) cases where the only thing that's changed is that you *spent* something, and the question is whether the bare fact of the expenditure is itself a reason to continue. I'm pretty sure (a) isn't really a sunk cost case at all; it's just a case where the past has consequences. The real fight is (b).

Second, take seriously the defenses of (b) that I keep running into, and decide which survive:

- the commitment story: precommitting to finish makes your future threats and promises credible, so a policy of honoring sunk costs can pay off even when the individual act of honoring them doesn't;
- the self-knowledge story: my having stuck with something this long is evidence about what I value or about how the thing is likely to go, so the expenditure is a signal to myself;
- the constitutive story: some goods are historical — a friendship, a career, a long game — and the past isn't a cost you're still paying off, it's part of the good itself, so "the past doesn't matter" is just false for them;
- the rule versus act story: for an agent who would otherwise bail on everything the moment it gets hard, a blanket "I don't quit" rule might be better than case-by-case calculation, even if the rule is wrong on some individual occasions.

Third: for each defense that survives, tell me whether it's a genuine exception to the principle or whether it just smuggles a forward-looking consideration in under a backward-looking label. Then give me the criterion — what has to be true of a decision for the past to legitimately enter it?

Then the verdict. Is "honoring sunk costs is always irrational" true, false, or true only for idealized agents? Don't end on a noncommittal note.

One more thing: keep it honest about what's settled and what's contested. That people do honor sunk costs, and in fairly predictable ways, is one thing; whether there's any defensible rational basis for ever doing so is another, and I don't want a contested view presented as the consensus among people who actually work on this. If you think the whole framing is a mess, say so and explain why. American English, please.
