I am a postdoc in the philosophy of biology, and next month I am leading the opening session of a reading group on behavioural genetics: ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and a mixed audience of a few wet-lab biologists, a couple of philosophers of science, and two economists who work on education policy. None of them has ever computed a heritability coefficient, and most arrive with the phrase already loaded — they have absorbed it from newspapers, and last year a session on twin studies collapsed into an argument about IQ before anyone had said what the number meant. My aim is not to defend any particular estimate but to leave them able to hear that a trait is, say, seventy percent heritable and to know precisely what has and has not been said. Please do not explain heritability to me; give me a plan for the explanation. I want the dependency order set out explicitly — what has to be in place before the term can be introduced at all, what each later piece presupposes, and why the sequence cannot be reshuffled — together with the single carrying example that should do the heavy lifting for the whole session, the misconceptions the audience will bring or form and the exact point in the sequence at which I should head off each one, the check for understanding that would show the idea has landed rather than merely been nodded at, and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say why.

I would rather the carrying example not be either of the two that everyone reaches for — human height and IQ — because I want the audience working the machinery rather than recalling a controversy. I have a candidate and I want your verdict on it: a timed arithmetic test given to schoolchildren in two neighbouring districts of the same country in the same year, one where instruction is nearly uniform and one where its quality varies a great deal; suppose the heritability estimate comes out near 0.7 in the first district and near 0.25 in the second, with the first district's average score higher. Tell me what a carrying example must possess if it is to support the whole session — I take it that it must be a trait whose heritability differs across environments, and it must be a case where a shift in the environment moves the entire distribution, so that the audience sees a high coefficient coexisting with wholesale malleability — and say whether my candidate supplies all of that or whether I need a companion case beside it. If it needs replacing, propose the replacement and tell me what it must have and what it must lack.

Then map the misconceptions for me, in the order they will actually surface rather than the order they appear in a textbook. The ones I expect: that a heritability of 0.7 means seventy percent of an individual's trait is genetic; that a high figure means the trait is fixed and a low one means it is open to intervention; that the coefficient explains differences between groups; and that heritability is the same thing as inheritance, or as having a genetic basis, or as a mechanism. Predict the ones I have not listed, especially any that a half-understanding of the variance-partitioning machinery will generate — I am thinking in particular of the counterintuitive result that making environments more uniform can push the coefficient up rather than down, and I want to know where in the sequence that should land so that it reads as a consequence rather than a paradox. For each misconception, name the point in the sequence where it should be pre-empted or repaired, tell me which ones will do the most damage to later sessions if left standing, and give me the one-sentence reframing I can leave on the board at the end.
