I'm a science journalist, mostly physics and ideas, and I came to it from a philosophy degree, which is how I keep getting roped into things like this: I've agreed to set one of the essay questions on the final for a grad seminar on quantum foundations at the university where I did my masters. The course has been all Bell, start to finish — the inequalities, the escape routes people tried for thirty years, the loophole-free experiments and the attention they got, and then the interpretive free-for-all after. My question is worth 20 marks, closed book, and they get about 50 minutes to write. What I don't want is a question they can pass by reciting the history or by writing "nonlocality" in a big font; I want them to actually have to compute something and then say what it means. I'm nervuos about getting the physics subtly wrong in front of people who know it better than me, so I'd like the model answer to be careful and explicit rather than hand-wavy.

Here's the hook I want you to build the question around. Last week I put a toy model on the board, one I'd sketched myself, just to show them what a "local hidden variable" picture actually looks like in the flesh. Each pair of particles carries a shared hidden angle λ, drawn uniformly from 0 to 2π. Alice's analyzer is set to direction a and Bob's to b. Alice's outcome is +1 if cos(λ − a) is positive and −1 if it's negative; Bob's outcome is the same test but with the opposite sign, so +1 when cos(λ − b) is negative and −1 when it's positive. Nothing in Alice's outcome depends on Bob's setting or his result, nothing in Bob's depends on Alice's, and λ is just a shared cause fixed back at the source — it's about as innocent as a model of this kind can be. I told them to sit with it and work out what it predicts, and one of them immediately announced that entanglement was basically solved, which I'm fairly sure isn't right, but I couldn't tell them exactly where it falls over, which was a bit humiliating. I want the exam question to make them do that work: have them push the model through the usual round angles — 0, 45, 90, 135, 180 degrees of separation — and through the standard four-setting combination, since anything else and they'll drown in trig, and then have them say what the exercise shows about where the real tension in Bell's theorem lives. Sub-parts are fine, probably good.

So: write the exam question itself (in the voice of the paper, with the marks for each part adding to 20), then a model answer of around 650 words, then a marking scheme built around five must-hit points with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each of them wrong. For the scheme, I care most about the split between a strong answer and a competent one — name the specific moves an A answer has to make and that a B answer can get away with skipping — and name the specific misconceptions that should cost marks. I'd rather you diagnose the ones you think are genuinely likely for students at this level than have me guess at them, and please don't let any of them be soft: if the model is perfectly respectable as a local model and the interesting failure is somewhere non-obvious, I want the scheme to reward the students who see that and penalize the ones who reach for a big word instead.
