I've been reading about plea bargaining and hit something I can't make sense of, and I think it's really a question about speech acts. In most US courts a defendant can plead guilty while telling the judge, on the record, that he didn't do it — the so-called Alford plea, from a 1970 Supreme Court case — and the court can accept it anyway, as long as the prosecution's evidence would probably convince a jury. Before entering judgment the judge has to make sure the plea is voluntary and that there's a factual basis for it, but nothing in the rules requires the defendant to admit anything; some judges will even put him under oath and warn him that statements under oath can be used in a perjury prosecution. There's also the no contest plea, nolo contendere, which seems to be defined by the fact that it isn't an admission of guilt — and am I right that a no contest plea can't be used against you later, while a guilty plea, Alford or not, can? A few states and the military don't allow Alford pleas at all, and in the ordinary case judges still want to hear the defendant describe what he did in his own words.

What philosophical commitments does this cluster presuppose about performative utterances? Is the plea an Austin-style conventional act, where the words do the job and sincerity isn't a condition of success, or does it presuppose something like Searle's sincerity condition and just treat the allocution as a seperate descriptive act that can be false while the plea itself succeeds? Does it presuppose that the legal meaning of the defendant's words is fixed by institutional rules rather than by what he means — the Gricean worry being that his words mean guilty while his intention is only to dodge a worse sentence? And what does it presuppose about confession, if a confession can be legally valid and yet not believed by the person doing the confessing?

Reconstruct the implicit theory for me. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and say whether it's internally consistent — especially given the contrast between no contest and Alford pleas, and given that the same system elsewhere insists on a spoken admission. Then say what would have to change in the practice if we adopted a rival view, say if sincerity were a felicity condition of the plea, or if speaker meaning determined its legal meaning. American English please, and skip the citations — I don't have library access, I just want the argument.
