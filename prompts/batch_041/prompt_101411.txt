Act as a philosopher of history who knows the historiography of the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus well, and think carefully before you write anything. I've been drawn into a running argument with a close friend — a biblical archaeologist — that began over the Exodus and has since spread to Arthur and to Troy, and I've realised I don't actually know which position I hold: every time I stake out a side I find I've conceded something on one of the other cases that pulls me back. What I want is a decision tree I can hand to the other people in the argument, which starts from a few commitments a non-specialist can answer and ends at a named position on when a legendary narrative may be treated as history. Please begin by brainstorming the candidate criteria — whether the narrative makes a factual claim at all, whether testimony alone can carry the weight or independent corroboration is required, where the burden of proof lies by default, what counts as a surviving kernel and whether the central event must be the one that survives, and whether a real place or person standing behind the story is enough — and then let the positions fall out of the combinations of answers rather than listing familiar labels first. Every branch node must be phrased as a question I could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and every leaf must carry the position's name, or be marked unnamed if nobody defends it, together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus should be the three test cases each leaf has to survive, and I'd like any combination of answers that no position occupies to be noted and diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where two leaves can't really be separated by your questions, say so rather than papering over it. Aim for roughly six to ten nodes and at most eight leaves, and keep the whole thing usable as a one-page handout.
