I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size software company, and through a run of bad luck I've ended up on the little committee that owns our incident-review process. We do blameless postmortems: no names in the writeup, nobody gets dinged for causing an outage, the whole idea is that people tell the truth about what they broke. It works. We get the reports, people fess up to dumb mistakes, nobody hides anything.

One of our directors, who used to be an academic, is now arguing we've gone too far. Her point, roughly: a process where nobody is ever the proper target of blame isn't neutral, it's a decision to stop relating to each other as people and start relating to each other as machines you debug. She says the attitudes that make a team a team -- feeling genuinely let down by a teammate, being grateful when someone bails you out, thinking someone owes you an explanation -- are exactly the ones you'd have to give up, and that a workplace which gives them up eventually loses the trust that makes the honest reports happen in the first place. She has a name for the attitudes, she calls them the reactive attitudes, and says they come from a philosopher named Strawson. Two other things she said that I didn't fully follow: that arguing "blameless works better, look at the numbers" already concedes her point, because it treats our attitudes as tools for getting behavior out of people; and that when you do suspend those attitudes for someone -- "he's not himself," "he's just a product of what he's been through" -- that's something you can do occasionally and locally, but it's a totally different thing to make it the standing policy of the org.

The other three people on the committee think she's dressing up ordinary managment language in philosophy and want to keep the process exactly as it is.

I have to write the opinion memo we'll fight over at Thursday's meeting and I'm honestly torn about wether she's right. Can you draft it for me? I need a concrete recommendation -- keep it blameless as is, or change it, and if we change it, say exactly what the new step is, who triggers it, what the trigger condition is, and on what timeline -- plus the reasoning. Argue her side as hard as you can before you say why you land where you land, and don't just tell me the answer is "have a conversation about it," because the committee actually has to decide something. Nobody in that room has read any philosophy, so if you use her vocabulary, spell it out in plain English.
