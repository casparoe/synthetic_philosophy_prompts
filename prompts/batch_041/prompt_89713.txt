A guy from my old meetup group, who I'd normally trust on this stuff since he's been reading LW longer than I have, wrote up a post on counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail, and I want a second opinion before I just absorb his take. Here's the gist.

Counterfactual mugging: Omega flips a coin. If it lands tails he shows up and asks you for $100. If it lands heads he hands you $10,000, but only if he predicted you'd hand over the $100 in the tails branch. So when he's asking, you already know it's tails, and the hundred bucks buys you nothing in this branch — it only matters through what Omega predicted about what you'd do here. CDT says refuse. The updateless crowd says pay, because what you're really choosing is a coin-independent policy, and 'pay when asked' is the policy that collects the $10k in the branch where the coin came up heads. My friend's read is that the only thing going for paying is that you could have precommitted beforehand, and since you can't precommit retroactively once Omega's flipped, CDT is just right and the puzzle is a trick about the psychology of commitment. He also says it's basically Newcomb's problem with a coin glued on, and that it's really a free will puzzle, since if Omega can predict you then your choice was already fixed.

XOR blackmail: a perfect predictor writes to you that exactly one of these is true — you pay him $1000, or your house already has a termite infestation. Termites would cost a million to deal with. EDT pays, because paying is better news about the termites. CDT/FDT refuses, and gets a free warning in the case where the house actually is infested. My friend calls it blackmail and says the termite guy is running an insurance racket: pay a grand to buy off a million-dollar risk, or roll the dice on it. And his big takeaway is that acausal reasoning is one unified doctrine of paying up — if you one-box on Newcomb, you pay the mugger, you pay the termite guy, and thats how these predictors farm you.

Which is where I get lost, because in the first case he says the acausal answer (pay) is wrong and in the second he says the acausal answer (refuse) is right, but then he claims acausal reasoning is all one thing. My gut says refuse in both cases too, which worries me that I'm just a CDT simp pattern-matching.

How much of this actually holds up? Where's he fudging? What would someone who's thought hard about this wince at, and does his conclusion — mugging solved by CDT, acausal reasoning = always pay up — follow from the parts he got right? And do the two cases really point in opposite directions, or are they the same lesson after all?
