I've been sitting in on a small reading group on the realism debate in the philosophy of science, and one thread keeps pulling at me: the thought that when a scientific theory is overthrown we tend to lose the objects it posited while keeping its mathematics. The stock example is the nineteenth-century shift in optics from Fresnel's elastic-solid ether to Maxwell's electromagnetic theory, where (so the argument goes) a great deal of the formal structure carried over even though the ether itself was discarded, and where this retention of form is supposed to explain both why we needn't fear that our current theories will go the way of the ether and why we shouldn't claim to know what the world is made of, only its structure. I find the idea elegant and slightly evasive at the same time, and I'd rather feel the pull of it than read another exposition of it. So I'd like you to write a short story that stages the disagreement rather than explaining it.

Here's the setting I have in mind, though you should feel free to adjust the details as the story demands. Somewhere in the future there is a repository — a kind of museum-cum-archive — maintained by a civilisation that keeps the mathematical skeletons of superseded physical theories on permanent display while having thrown away, as a matter of policy, every claim those theories made about what exists. Its curators are not historians; they are trained to preserve equations, symmetries, and predictive apparatus while treating the accompanying ontologies as so much scaffolding, and they have a professional vocabulary for the difference. Your protagonist is a young curator preparing her first major exhibition, which is to be built around a theory whose central posited entity her late mentor spent a career defending before it was abandoned. Give her two or three colleagues or interlocutors who embody genuinely different attitudes toward the same facts: one who insists that what survives theory change is real knowledge of the world's structure and that we should simply stay silent about the entities, one who goes further and holds that there is nothing underneath the structure at all — relations all the way down, with no little objects being related — and one who thinks the entire talk of retention is a story we tell about our instruments and that the museum is therefore a monument to a confusion. Let their disagreements surface in what they do, choose, and refuse to do — a decision about what to put in a vitrine, an argument over a label, a betrayal of the mentor's memory — rather than in speeches reciting positions.

The story should be self-contained and should not require the reader to have read any particular book or paper; the concepts should emerge from scenes, images, and the characters' concrete stakes. Please avoid ending with a verdict, a lecture, or a tidy resolution in favour of any of the three views — I want the reader to close the story genuinely uncertain which position has the better of it, and perhaps to notice that the very idea of preserving a structure while discarding what it was a structure of is stranger than it first appears. A length of roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words would suit me. Dialogue is welcome, but if you use it, keep it in the register of people who know each other well and are tired, not of a seminar.
