You are a poet with a serious working knowledge of contemporary metaphysics — in particular the metaphysics of material composition — and you are good enough at the craft that what you produce could stand as a poem well outside the seminar room in which it will first be read.

I am a retired chemist. I spent thirty-five years studying how large molecules hold their shape, and for the past three years I have been sitting in on a graduate reading group that has been working through the problem of material composition. Our final session is next month, and I want to close it by reading a poem I have had written for the occasion. Please write that poem.

The question that has occupied us all term is the one about when some things add up to a further thing — under what conditions, if any, a plurality of parts composes a whole. We have spent the semester on the familiar candidate answers: that things compose a whole when they are in contact; when they are fastened to one another; when they cohere; when they are fused; that they compose a whole always, whatever relations they stand in; and that they never do. I would like the poem to pass through at least three of these, briefly and by image rather than by argument, in a way that lets the reader feel why each answer fails — not as a list, but as a sequence of temptations the speaker moves through and gives up.

I then want the poem to arrive at the answer I have come to hold and cannot find on anyone's list. I believe parts compose a whole when each part's condition is partly fixed by the others — when they hold one another in place, so that you cannot say what any one of them is without describing the arrangement it is caught in. It is what a folded protein does, and I think it is also what a neighborhood does. You may call it mutual maintenance, or reciprocal dependence, if it needs a name.

Here is the constraint that matters most to me, and I would like you to reason carefully about it before you write a line. In the view I have described, the whole is prior to its parts: a part is what it is only in virtue of the arrangement it belongs to. So I want a poem that behaves that way — a poem in which no single line can be understood on its own, in which lines read in isolation mislead or go inert, and in which only the whole makes any of them mean what they mean. Let a refrain, or some recurring line, do the binding, and let it be a line whose sense changes as the poem proceeds, so that the same words carry at the end a weight they could not have carried at the start. I have no fixed form in mind; take whatever shape serves this end, though I would rather it have a real shape than be free verse that merely wanders, and if you choose meter or rhyme, let them be part of the argument rather than ornament.

Before you draft, work out for yourself which relations you will use, how each fails, and exactly how the refrain will bind the parts; then write the poem. Aim for somewhere between twenty and thirty lines.

The poem will be read aloud to people who know this literature well. It should be accurate enough that a specialist recognizes each candidate answer for what it is and does not wince, and it should also be a poem and not a lecture in verse. Do not quote or cite anyone — the room knows the sources — and do not let the poem collapse into a catalogue of positions. Someone who has never heard of the problem should still find it a good poem, and someone who has should find it right.

After the poem, add a short paragraph of a few sentences explaining how the form enacts the answer, so that I have something useful to say when I read it.
