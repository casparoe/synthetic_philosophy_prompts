I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on reasons and motivation, and I want a single designed case that does real philosophical work rather than illustrating a contrast everyone already knows. Please construct one scenario, no longer than a paragraph, and then demonstrate that it satisfies the specifications below; the analysis that follows the scenario may be as long as it needs to be.

(i) The scenario should be an ordinary-life case: one agent, one concrete, one-off decision, no clinical or quasi-clinical description of her, no coercion or manipulation, no science fiction, and no fact relevant to the decision hidden from her.

(ii) Her motivational set must be laid out completely — what she cares about, what leaves her cold, what she would not be moved by even on reflection — and the scenario must make it determinate that means–end reasoning from that set, together with full information and no inferential error, yields no motivation to φ.

(iii) The case must nonetheless make the claim that she has a reason to φ initially compelling to a careful reader, and the source of that pull must be something in the case a reader can point to, not the moral weight of φ-ing or our sympathy for her. Keep clear of the stock shape in which the agent simply fails to care about another person's suffering: the consideration at issue should be something like a requirement of a craft, a form of loyalty, a demand of self-respect, or a claim of long-term prudence.

(iv) The case must turn on the thin/thick ambiguity in the internalist's own criterion: read thinly, sound deliberation corrects only factual and inferential error and adds nothing to her motivational set; read thickly, sound deliberation may introduce a motive she did not have. Construct the scenario so that the thin internalist must say she has no reason at all, the thick internalist is driven to say that she does, and the reader can see exactly which step of deliberation carries that weight.

Then give the verdict on the case, with its reasoning, for each of three positions: desire-based internalism; the internalism Bernard Williams defends, on which a reason claim about an agent is true only if sound deliberation from her existing motivational set would move her; and externalism. Then tell me which single structural feature of the case is doing the work; what happens if that feature is removed, and whether the case then collapses into a stock instance of the debate in which the two sides merely talk past one another; whether the thick internalist's additional step is a legitimate extension of the agent's motivational set or a change of subject that concedes the externalist's point; and what the strongest line of attack is for whichever party fares badly. Do not stipulate the verdicts: a scenario that simply announces that she has, or lacks, a reason fails the assignment. If the specifications turn out not to be jointly satisfiable, say so and identify which one must give way; a demonstrated impossibility would be worth more to me than a fudged case.
