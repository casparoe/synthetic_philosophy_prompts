I'm a first-year PhD student, and a senior colleague in my department floated an argument at lunch last week that I can't stop chewing on. She calls it the operator-substitution thesis, and it runs roughly like this: since alignment just means getting a system to do what the operator intends, value alignment is not a second problem at all — it is simply intent alignment with the operator expanded to encompass everyone affected by the system. On her view there is only one alignment problem, intent alignment, and value alignment is that same problem scaled up: if we could get a model to faithfully pursue the intentions of all affected parties, we would have solved value alignment, and if we cannot, then no separate theory of values will rescue us. What I find seductive is that it turns the whole field into a pure engineering-and-coordination problem and makes all the talk of values look like sentimentality; what unsettles me is the suspicion that something has been quietly smuggled in under the phrase everyone affected.

So my question is this: is the operator-substitution thesis actually right? If it is, why do so many people treat value alignment as a distinct and harder problem — are they simply failing to notice that values is just shorthand for the aggregated intentions of the affected parties? And if the thesis is wrong, I'd like a precise diagnosis of what it conflates, rather than a glossary of the two terms. I have a nagging sense that the move from a single operator to many affected parties is doing far more work than the thesis admits, but I can't articulate why, and I would rather understand the fault line than collect definitions.
