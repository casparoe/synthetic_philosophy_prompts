Now the variant, call it Commit. Everything above holds except the order of events. Before the Predictor makes its prediction, you are offered a contract. If you sign, you are bound: from then on, taking both boxes is not a profitable option, because the contract directs the entire contents of both boxes to a third party in that case (so if you sign and then take both boxes you get $0 no matter what). The contract cannot be revoked. You know all this, and you know the Predictor will know whether you have signed before it makes its prediction.

And here's what the student wrote:

"(a) CDT says take both boxes. The money is already in Box B before you get to choose — the Predictor did its thing in advance — so nothing you do now can change what's in there, and whatever is in there you're better off with it plus the $1,000 in Box A. Taking both dominates: in each of the two states of the world you end up $1,000 richer than if you took one box. Since dominance is a state-wise comparison it doesn't depend on what probabilities you assign to the two states, so two-boxing comes out with the higher expected utility even by the evidentialist's own lights, and the Predictor's accuracy rate doesn't enter into it. EDT says take one box, and the reason is that taking one box is a sign of what the Predictor will have predicted — since it's right 99% of the time, one-boxing and the million go together, and conditional on one-boxing the expected value of Box B is close to a million. In other words, one-boxing is what brings the good prediction about, so the conditional expected value of one-boxing is higher and that's the act to perform.

(b) In Commit, CDT says sign. Signing happens before the Predictor predicts, so signing actually does change what the Predictor will predict — it reads your brain, sees the contract and knows two-boxing is off the table, so the million goes in. That's a causal difference and CDT can use it. After signing, CDT takes one box, since two-boxing now pays $0 by the contract and there's nothing to take. EDT signs as well and then takes one box, though for its usual reason rather than a causal one. Compared to (a): the CDT agent now gets the million, the EDT agent's advice is unchanged, and CDT's advice flipped between (a) and (b) while EDT's didn't — which is exactly because the causal structure flipped.

Give it a lever — a contract binding you before the prediction — and it does exactly what the evidentialist does. So the two theories agree whenever the structure of the case allows the lever to exist and disagree only when it doesn't. The caveat is that they do give different advice in the standard problem, so they're not literally the same rule. But if the difference only shows up in cases where there's no lever, then it looks like a dispute about how to describe a case rather than a real disagreement about what to do, and I'd say the classmate's point survives."
