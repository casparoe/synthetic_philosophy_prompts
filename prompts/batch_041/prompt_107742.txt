I write and edit the ideas section of a science magazine, and we run one or two pieces a month on studies claiming to show how people intuitively judge some moral or metaphysical question — whether a character in a vignette acted wrongly, whether a name still refers after its bearer dies, whether a rebuilt object is the same object. The claims that reach us always sound alike: that a population's intuitions differ from ours, or that they do not. I have no reliable way to tell whether a study measured intuitions or merely answers to a questionnaire that travelled badly. Please write me a procedure my writers and I can run in an hour before commissioning or publishing: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which we should report a result as a difference in answers rather than in intuitions, and the point at which the remaining decision is about what we can honestly say rather than about the study. Build it around two distinctions I think the literature blurs — between an intuition and a considered answer, and between a vignette being translated and a vignette being understood — and mark clearly which steps are judgement calls dressed up as method.

Then run it on three cases: a study finding that a small-scale community judges a trolley-style case differently from Western undergraduates; a study finding no cross-cultural difference in a case that seems to turn on a culturally specific concept; and a study we would love to run because its finding is exactly what our readers expect, but whose sample is small and whose wording is, on inspection, ambiguous. Say plainly whether the procedure tells us to drop or heavily hedge that third piece, and say what the procedure cannot settle. Open with one sentence stating what the procedure decides, then the steps, then the cases. No more than 900 words.
