I am a novelist, some four hundred pages into a book whose central mechanism I have stopped being sure I believe in. Two people who were once close are separated for good at the end of the second act: no letters, no intermediaries, no news of any kind. Before they part, each promises the other something — she will not do one thing, he will not do another — and each understands that compliance can never be verified and could never, in any case, reach the other. She adds a threat. If he breaks faith, she says, she will burn down the house she loves, and she will never tell him whether she did it. The burning would cost him nothing. She has spent years making herself into a woman who would strike the match. The threat can therefore touch him only through his own reasoning about what she has become, and about what her resolve implies for him, given that his position is, by the book's design, exactly symmetrical to hers. I want her deliberation to be honest, which means my own account of reasons has to hold together. Here is what I actually think:

1. An action is rational for a person just in case, among the options open to her, it does most to bring about what she wants, given what she believes. Her wants are the fixed point; the only question is which act serves them.
2. If two options would leave the world in exactly the same state — the same effects on everyone and everything, herself included — then there is nothing for a difference in rationality to consist in, and neither can be more rational than the other.
3. A person may rationally bind herself in advance to a course of action that will be costly and unattractive when the hour arrives, provided the binding itself changes what she or others will later do.
4. A person may rationally set about making herself want something she does not now want, if having the want will change how she acts.
5. Where two people face choices that mirror one another exactly, and neither can affect the other in any way, each should choose as she would have the other choose; the symmetry is itself a reason.
6. No one is rationally moved by a threat that cannot reach him — one whose execution would be invisible to him and would leave him no worse off; such a threat is not a reason for the person threatened.

I want an audit, not a verdict. Which of these six genuinely pull against one another, and which only appear to? I would rather not be told that six propositions can always be made to look mutually hostile. For each real conflict, tell me how severe it is, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs me elsewhere. Then tell me which single concession would settle the most — and whether, having made it, the woman in my book can still do what I need her to do.
