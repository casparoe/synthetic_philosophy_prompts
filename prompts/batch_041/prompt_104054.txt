I am trying to determine whether there is an established name for a particular defect in an act of blame — a defect that concerns not what the blame says about the person blamed but who is in a position to say it.

The cases I have in mind share a shape, though I cannot tell whether they are one phenomenon or several. In the first, a manager reorganizes a workplace so that the schedule cannot realistically be met, and then reprimands the staff for missing deadlines. The staff object, correctly, that the manager has no business complaining, since the manager arranged the conditions. The manager is not being two-faced: he meets his own deadlines and does not secretly think lateness is acceptable. The objection nevertheless seems to have force, and it is not an objection to the accuracy of the reprimand.

In the second, two countries emit heavily, and one of them lectures the other about emissions. The lecturing country emits less per person today but far more in total historically, and much of its wealth rests on those earlier emissions. The criticized country replies that the critic is not the one to say it — again, without disputing the substance of what is said.

In the third, someone who has never drunk alcohol tells a friend that the friend's drinking is ruining his life. Nothing in the speaker's conduct contradicts the remark; there is no inconsistency to expose. And yet there is a widely felt sense that this particular speaker is poorly placed to say it, or at least that the remark costs him nothing and therefore lands differently than it would from someone who had struggled with the same thing.

I suspect the nearest familiar term is hypocrisy, but hypocrisy seems to require that the speaker's own conduct or commitments conflict with what he says, and in these cases that conflict is often absent — the manager is punctual, the lecturing country is comparatively green, the abstinent friend is perfectly consistent. I suspect the answer lies in a broader family of objections about whether a speaker is entitled to hold someone to a norm, but I cannot say which member of that family each case belongs to, or whether all three are the same member.

What I want, then, is not a bare label. If there is an established concept here, I want to know which it is and, more importantly, how it is bounded: which of my three cases it covers, which it does not, and on what principle. In particular, is the relevant feature that the blamer produced the wrong, that he benefits from it, or that he is simply untouched by the costs of the norm — and are those one thing or three? If nothing established fits, please coin a term, define it precisely enough to exclude ordinary inconsistency and mere bad manners, and say where its edges fall.
