A friend and I argued until about one in the morning about whether prioritarianism escapes the leveling-down objection, and we stopped only because neither of us could tell any longer whether we disagreed about the answer or about which objection we were each answering.

The objection, as we both accept it against equality taken as an intrinsic good, runs like this: if equality is good in itself, then a state in which everyone is worse off and no one is better off can be better than the state it came from, because it is more equal. Prioritarianism — a benefit counts for more, the worse off the person who receives it — is supposed to be immune, and for what looks like a cheap reason: it ranks states by a sum that rises whenever anyone is made better off and falls whenever anyone is made worse off, so a state that is worse for someone and better for no one can never come out ahead. My friend says the cheapness is the point. Any view with that structural property is immune, he says, so immunity shows only that prioritarianism is Paretian; it does not show that it answers the objection, because the objection's real target is the currency rather than the ordering. On his account prioritarianism is committed to a scale on which a small gain to someone badly off can swamp an enormous loss to someone better off, and that is leveling down in substance even where it cannot be leveling down in form. I think that is a different objection wearing the same name, and I could not show him the seam at one in the morning.

I want the case that settles it, because I am writing about this and I do not want to repeat the received line that prioritarianism simply sidesteps the problem. Specifications:

1. The same people exist in every option. Nobody is added, nobody is subtracted, and no comparison turns on a life that might not have been lived. This is the constraint I care about most.
2. Two or three people, not a population, and described in ordinary terms — what each has, what each stands to gain or lose — before any numbers are put on them. The goods should be ones an institution could actually redistribute or withhold: income, health, years of life, schooling. No trolleys.
3. Do not stipulate the verdicts; derive them. State the priority weighting explicitly, show the arithmetic, and satisfy yourself that it is not rigged — no prioritarian would accept a value function chosen to produce the verdict you want. Run the case under at least two weightings a prioritarian might actually defend, one with diminishing marginal value and one bounded above, and say whether the verdict survives the substitution.
4. If no case satisfying 1 and 3 exists, do not manufacture one. Say plainly that it cannot be done, give the argument for why, and then build the nearest case that does the work my friend is reaching for, being exact about what it shows and what it does not.

After the case or the proof, give me the accounting: what each option is worth under each weighting, which single feature of the case is load-bearing, what happens to the case if I remove that feature, and what the strongest prioritarian reply is — adequate or merely evasive. Then commit. Tell me which of us was right, and whether the leveling-down objection, in whatever form survives your construction, has real force against prioritarianism as it is actually defended. I would rather have a definite answer that turns out to be wrong than a survey that hands the decision back to me. If a step depends on a contestable reading of prioritarianism, flag it in one sentence and keep going.
