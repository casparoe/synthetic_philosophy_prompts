I am not a type designer or a linguist, but I have been thinking about the question behind Letter Spirit: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Two answers keep pulling me apart. One says there is a structural core — some arrangement of bowl, stem, arch, counter, and their connections — that every genuine 'a' must have, and style is only surface. The other says there is no single core; 'a' is a family-resemblance category, held together by overlapping similarities and by its place in a system of contrasts with 'o', 'd', 'q', 'e', and the rest, so that a shape can be an 'a' in one font and not in another.

Please draft the joint statement that an advocate of the first view and an advocate of the second could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want the claims they agree on, including about the actual cases: single-story versus double-story 'a', blackletter and graffiti 'a's, a child's first 'a', a wordmark where the 'a' is a circle with a tail and is read as 'a' only because of the surrounding letters, and a variable font that morphs continuously between a double-story and a single-story 'a'. Then state the precise disagreements, each in wording its own side would accept. Identify the cruxes — I suspect one is whether a category needs necessary conditions or only a gradient, and another is whether letter identity is fixed by the shape alone or by the shape's role in a system — and for each crux give the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why.

Be committed: where the collaboration forces a choice about the best formulation of either side, make the choice and defend it; do not just lay out options. Format as: Section 1: Agreed Claims (numbered, about 10 items); Section 2: Disagreements Table (columns: question, structural-core wording, family-resemblance wording; 3–5 rows); Section 3: Cruxes and Tests (one short paragraph per crux, including both sides' predictions); Section 4: Merely Verbal? (one short paragraph with a definite verdict).
