Write a dialogue between two philosophers who are both fully competent and are trying to advise a foundation. The first is a follower of G. E. Moore's ideal utilitarianism. Moore held that the highest goods are certain states of consciousness, especially the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects, and that these goods form organic unities whose value is not the sum of the value of their parts; the right action is the one that maximizes the good. The second is a critic who works in the ethics of care and relational aesthetics. The critic argues that Moore's picture distorts both friendship and beauty: friendship is not a state of consciousness to be maximized but a relationship with its own normative structure, and beauty is not a simple intrinsic property of objects but something that arises in the encounter between a viewer, a work, and a tradition. The foundation has a fixed sum and must choose between endowing a new gallery of landscape painting and funding a program of communal meals for isolated elderly people. The Moorean insists that the foundation must compare the two options in terms of the good they produce, and that Moore's organic unities give him the resources to do so. The critic insists that the comparison is misguided, that the value of a friendship cannot be aggregated, and that treating beauty as a quantity to be maximized corrupts it. The dialogue should contain real back-and-forth: each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talk past them. They should discuss whether the value of friendship is intrinsic or relational, whether aesthetic enjoyment is a state of mind or a relational achievement, whether organic unities can be compared with one another, and whether the foundation's choice is even the right kind of question to ask. Neither side should be a strawman, and the dialogue should end with a clear statement of what each has conceded and what remains unresolved.
