I teach logic and philosophy of language, and I have been working through the Gongsun Longzi in translation — chiefly the 白馬論 and the 指物論 — in preparation for a seminar on the School of Names. My classical Chinese is not good enough to settle philological disputes, so I read the text through translations and commentary, and I do not need citations or a reading list from you. What I am fluent in is model-theoretic semantics: singular terms and predicates, extensions and set-theoretic inclusion, intersective versus non-intersective adjectives, kind reference, mass versus count nouns, the use/mention distinction, and presupposition.

I would like you to restate the principal arguments of the white-horse material in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, and then report the residue. Take the argument at least at these three steps, as they are usually reconstructed. First, the naming step: horse is what names shape and white is what names colour, and what names colour is not what names shape, hence a white horse is not a horse. Second, the step from what satisfies a request: one who seeks a horse is satisfied by a brown horse, one who seeks a white horse is not, so the two names cannot be doing the same work. Third, the composition step: a white horse is horse together with white, and horse-together-with-white is not horse. For each step, give the best semantic reconstruction you can, state which reading of the negation, the copula, and the compounding your reconstruction commits you to, and mark the places where you had to force the material. Do not quietly assume that the argument must be about extensions; if a step goes through only at the level of sense, or only if the complex expression is treated as a conjunction of two names, say so explicitly.

Then the residue report. Which of these claims translate cleanly, which translate only under distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in model-theoretic semantics? I am especially interested in what becomes of the act of pointing or designating that the 指物論 seems to place at the centre, and of the normative, name-rectifying dimension that the later tradition reads into 名. Adjudicate in the end: is the residue a defect of my vocabulary, which cannot express the act of naming or the correctness of a name, or is it evidence that the source's notion of a name runs several distinct things together — so that its arguments trade on a conflation that my vocabulary would expose?
