For about fifteen years I have watched the same cycle repeat itself in the places I have worked, and I have never found a name for it. A number is adopted as a stand-in for something we actually care about — orders picked per hour in a distribution centre, a public engagement score in a grant scheme. It works for a while. Then people work out how to make the number rise without doing the thing it stands for, and it stops meaning anything. The response, which I have now seen twice, is to drop the number altogether and go back to judgement, spot checks and panels. What interests me is that things then genuinely improve — not because anyone works harder, but because people stop doing the easily counted work and return to the slow, awkward, uncountable parts of the job. That improvement is real and lasts several years. Then the demand for something visible to point at becomes irresistible, the number is reinstated, and the cycle begins again.

I realise this is close to Goodhart's law, and close to the story about paying a bounty for dead snakes, but neither of those seems to cover the part I care about: that abandoning the measure is genuinely better, and yet the pressure to bring it back never goes away. Is there an established name for this cycle? If there is, I would like to know what it is called, and how exactly it differs from those two ideas and from the ordinary observation that being watched changes how people behave. If there is not, please coin one, define it precisely, say what the nearest existing concepts are and where the boundaries fall, and tell me what would count as a borderline case that the name should, or should not, be made to cover. Please answer in British English.
