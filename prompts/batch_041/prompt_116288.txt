Two colleagues of mine, Mira and Daniel, spent most of a seminar dinner locked in an argument about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I have been trying to sort it out ever since without much success. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move — not to declare a winner and stop, but to say who is right about which specific claim at each step, and then to say what both of them are missing.

As Mira set it out, the theorem runs like this. Take each individual's preferences to satisfy the expected-utility axioms. Take society's preferences over lotteries to satisfy those same axioms. Accept the Pareto principle. Then any social welfare function must be a weighted sum of individual utilities; add that individuals are treated symmetrically and the weights come out equal. Mira's conclusion was that utilitarianism is not one moral option among others but the only aggregation rule available to anyone who accepts individual rationality and Pareto.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

Mira (1): This settles the aggregation debate. The utilitarian's sum is forced, not chosen. Anyone who rejects it must reject either expected utility or Pareto, and those are about as safe as premises get.

Daniel (2): It is rigged, and the rigging is in what it assumes about society rather than about individuals. The theorem needs society's ranking of lotteries to obey the sure-thing principle. But there is a standard counterexample, going back to Diamond: an indivisible benefit has to go to one of two people with equal claims on it, and many people prefer a fifty-fifty lottery to a rule that hands it to either named person with certainty. That preference is coherent, and it violates the sure-thing principle. So one of the premises is simply false.

Mira (3): What that shows is that people have preferences which are not expected-utility preferences, at least where fairness is concerned. But then you cannot represent your own view as maximizing anything, and the entire machinery of decision theory and welfare economics drops away. Besides, the theorem is conditional. It does not say utilitarianism is true; it says utilitarianism is what you get if you accept the axioms.

Daniel (4): A conditional whose premises are contested tells us nothing about what to believe. And the premise is not even stated honestly. The Pareto principle as normally understood — if everyone is better off under P than under Q, P is better — is hard to dispute. What the theorem actually needs is the ex ante version: if every individual's expected utility is at least as high under P as under Q, and strictly higher for someone, then society must rank P at least as high as Q. That is a far stronger claim, it is the one doing all the work, and it is what forces the additive form. The theorem also ranks outcomes only through individual utilities in the first place, which is not something anyone who doubts utilitarianism has granted.

Mira (5): The ex ante version is the plausible one. If policy P gives every single person a prospect at least as good as the one Q gives them, and someone's prospect is strictly better, on what grounds do you call Q better overall? A ranking that said so would be telling each person that their own prospect does not count. And the equal-weights step is just anonymity; only someone who thinks some people count for more would resist it.

Daniel (6): Two things are wrong there. Anonymity is not what produces equal weights. Equal weights come from the equiprobability assumption in the veil-of-ignorance argument — that each person behind the veil is equally likely to be anyone — and Rawls denied exactly that without being confused, since if you do not know the odds, maximin is a coherent alternative. And even granting equal weights on the utilities, equal concern does not require summing them: you can treat people equally and still refuse to let a gain to one person be bought one-for-one with a loss to another.

That is where they left it. Please rate each of the six moves, from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, and say briefly what is right and what is wrong in each one. Then tell me which of them is more nearly right overall, and — the part I care about most — what both of them are missing, including any assumption the theorem requires that neither of them has so much as mentioned.
