I am trying to work through a difficult argument about personal identity and survival in A. J. Ayer's Language, Truth and Logic, in the chapter that deals with the self and the common world, and I would like help understanding it. In my own paraphrase, the argument runs roughly as follows. Ayer denies that the self is a further entity over and above the series of its experiences; talk of a person is instead a logical construction out of experiences and the relations among them. He then asks whether it is intelligible to suppose that a person survives the death of his body, and answers that it is not self-contradictory: we can describe experiences continuing after the body has been destroyed, and nothing in that description offends logic. Yet the question of survival is supposed to be an empirical one, to be settled by evidence, and he seems to hold that we have none.

What I cannot make fit together is how these pieces are meant to cohere. If the self just is a construction out of experiences and their interrelations, what exactly makes a post-mortem experience belong to the same person rather than to a new one? Ayer treats the question as at least meaningful, but I do not see what could count as evidence for or against survival on his own account of meaning, or whether the identity of the surviving person is fixed by something else, such as memory connections, bodily continuity, or the bare possibility of an uninterrupted stream of experience. I am especially puzzled by whether, on his view, a chain of experiences continuing after bodily death but carrying no memory or other psychological link to the earlier life would count as that person's survival at all, or only as the existence of someone else.

Please help me understand the argument: reconstruct its steps as charitably as you can, identify what work the logical-construction claim is doing, and explain where my difficulty comes from — whether it is a genuine tension in Ayer's position, a misunderstanding on my part, or a question he would say is not yet well formed. Where you attribute a specific doctrine to Ayer, distinguish what is actually in the text from what is your own reconstruction.
