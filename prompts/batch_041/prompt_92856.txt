I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of psychiatry — sixteen students, one ninety-minute session — and I want to spend it on the phenomenon Ian Hacking called looping effects: the way a classification of people can alter the people classified, so that the classification is no longer tracking a stationary target. My students can recite the phrase. What they cannot do is feel why it should matter, or see that the interesting question is not whether the loop occurs but what shape it takes and whether it ever settles. I would like you to design an in-class activity that makes them run into this, rather than a discussion in which I explain it.

The constraints are these:

1. The session must be runnable in ninety minutes with sixteen students, using only printed materials. No money, no computers, no preparation by the students.
2. Invent the category. Do not use the DSM, the ICD, or any real diagnosis, and do not cite any literature or supply a reading list; nothing in the session should turn on prior knowledge of a real disorder. The syndrome, the instrument, and the criteria should be your own invention, and you should give me their exact wording.
3. The participants must do the classifying and be classified. There should be no lecture before the activity, and the phrase looping effects should not be spoken until the debrief.
4. Build in an explicit revision rule: in each round, the manual is re-derived from the responses of the currently classified group, and each participant follows a rule determining whether their next self-report moves towards the label or away from it. Some participants should move towards it and some should contest it, so that the classified are agents in the process rather than data for it.
5. Run the activity in two conditions, and make the contrast between them the hinge of the session. In the first, the criteria define the category as a threshold on a single continuous trait. In the second, the criteria are a disjunction of loosely related features. I want the students to discover for themselves that one loop converges on a stable kind while the other fragments into kinds that no longer resemble the one we began with — and to see that this difference, not the mere fact of the loop, is what bears on whether we can accumulate knowledge about a class of people.

Please give me the rules and roles, a list of materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the full text of any instrument or card the students handle, and a debrief that maps specific moments in what happened onto the parts of the idea they illustrate. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure. Finally, be frank about which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and which I shall have to supply myself in the closing discussion.

Write the whole thing in British English — spellings, punctuation, and idiom throughout.
