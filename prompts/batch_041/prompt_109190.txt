I'm a novelist — two books behind me, both about families behaving badly, so this is new ground — and I'm maybe eight months into the one that's going to sink me. The premise: a woman runs triage in a field hospital after an earthquake. She's spent a decade being the person who decides who gets the last surgeon and who gets the morphine, and she's good at it, in the way that means she's made her peace with the arithmetic. Then her own sister is carried in. The book lives or dies on that scene, and every version I write turns her into either a utilitarian with feelings or a sentimentalist with an excuse. A friend who teaches ethics rolled her eyes and said I was reinventing Bernard Williams's objection to utilitarianism — the man who saves his wife instead of the stranger, where the extra thought that saving your own wife is permitted is one thought too many, and the Jim and the Indians case, where the theory asks a man to put his own deepest commitments into the same ledger as everyone else's. Fine. But when I went looking, the discussion seemed to split into people saying Williams is really making a point about deliberation and people saying no, he's making a point about integrity, with everyone talking past each other, and I couldn't tell which of those fights was the load-bearing one.

So what I want is a prioritised agenda for the open questions in this neighbourhood — and I want the ranking defended, not just listed. Assume the effort being allocated is mine, say two more years and one book, but also tell me which questions matter most in their own right, because I'd rather not spend the years on something that turns out to be verbal. My hunch, which I don't trust: the deliberation reading is cheap to answer — a two-level or self-effacing consequentialism just tells you to think the partial thought and keep the justification to yourself — and what's actually left standing is a question about what kind of reason a relationship can supply. Whether she's my sister can be the reason for the act, or only that saving her was permitted, and what exactly is lost if it's the second. Push on that hunch. And please don't hand me a syllabus, I have a toddler and no library card.

Concretely: which of the open questions here are upstream of which? Does the demandingness argument, or the ethics of partiality, or the moral-saints line of work, or the wider suspicion that morality is only one point of view among others, actually depend on how the integrity objection comes out — or do they each have their own independent spine? Which of them are answerable by argument and which dissolve into stipulation about what integrity is supposed to mean? And is there a mirror image on the other side — that the partialist has one thought too few, no account at all of why the stranger counts — and if so, is that the same objection wearing a different hat or a genuinely seperate problem? For each item on your list, tell me what would move it up or down, and which one you'd deprioritise as a rabbit hole I'd definately regret.
