I'm an undergraduate in a political theory course, and last week our lecturer gave us a summary of the problem of dirty hands that struck me at the time as admirably clear but which, the more I turn it over, seems too tidy to be the whole story. He put it roughly like this: the problem of dirty hands names the predicament of a politician who finds that serving the public good requires doing something morally wrong — lying to the electorate, deceiving a colleague, breaking a solemn promise, perhaps worse. The point, he said, is not that such a politician is a monster but that he is genuinely caught between two real demands: the demands of ordinary morality, which he violates, and the demands of the public good, which he serves; and this is why the politician who refuses to get his hands dirty can fairly be accused of self-indulgence, of putting his own purity above the welfare of those he governs. So far, so standard, I take it. But then he drew a conclusion that I suspect is too strong: he said the real upshot is that there is no genuine moral cost to the dirty-handed deed, since the statesman is simply operating under a different rulebook in which necessity overrides the everyday prohibitions, and that this in turn shows consequentialism is the correct morality for politics. He added, almost as an aside, that the whole problem is essentially the doctrine of double effect dressed up for the realm of statecraft, and that there are really two versions of it — a shallow one, about feeling bad afterward, and a deep one that simply collapses into consequentialism — with only the shallow version being genuinely distinct.

Could you go through this and tell me, claim by claim, what someone who works on this topic would accept as fair, what they would wince at as a distortion, and what is flatly wrong? I'm especially keen to know whether the "no genuine moral cost" conclusion is actually the point of the problem or its opposite, whether the claim that it amounts to consequentialism-for-politics is defensible or a conflation of the position with one of its rivals, and whether the shallow/deep distinction he drew survives scrutiny. I'd also like to know which parts of his summary a defender of the view would say he has quietly imported from a competing position, since he has a habit of making every theory he presents sound like the one he himself favors.
