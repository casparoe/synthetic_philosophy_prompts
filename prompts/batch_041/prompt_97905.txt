I chair the clinical ethics committee at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and our consult service is asked several times a month to weigh in on cases that staff describe as "letting the patient die" versus "causing the death": withdrawing a ventilator or pressors from a patient who will not survive without them; escalating opioids or sedatives in a dying patient when the dose needed for comfort may also shorten life; declining to start dialysis when a family is pushing for it; and the perennial request from a family to "just stop everything." Our nurses and residents do not read the philosophical literature, and the distinctions that literature draws have never been written down in a form anyone can actually use at the bedside — we have a family meeting on Thursday and no shared vocabulary. I would like you to turn the doing/allowing and killing/letting-die material into a procedure our consult service can run in about thirty minutes: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure terminates in a clear answer; and, just as important, where it must hand the decision back to a human being because the question is one of institutional values rather than of fact. The attending physician and I sign off on the recommendation, so the procedure has to produce something we can put in the chart.

Two wrinkles to build in. First, our risk manager has proposed that we treat the distinction as a matter of what she calls "means versus obstacle": a death is doing-caused if the patient's dying is the very mechanism by which the intended benefit is obtained — as when the only way to end refractory suffering is to end the patient — and merely allowed if the dying is simply what remains once an obstacle has been removed. I am not sure this survives contact with the cases, and I want the procedure to be honest about where it breaks. Second, the staff keep conflating three separate questions — whether the act caused the death, whether anyone intended the death, and whether the death was justified — and I want the procedure to force them apart rather than letting a bad answer on one stand in for a good answer on another.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) terminal extubation of a patient who will die within hours, where the family requests it and the team agrees; (b) a patient in intractable pain at the end of life for whom the sedation required for comfort will probably hasten death, though the team's aim is comfort; and (c) a case of your own choosing that the procedure resolves in a way the family — and probably the bedside nurse — will find harsh or counterintuitive, where you should say plainly why the procedure lands there and whether you would trust it. For each case, give the step-by-step answers, flag the steps that were judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and note what the procedure cannot settle.
