I was recently appointed to the board of our county library system — my first experience of a body that has to settle contested questions in public — and at our last meeting something happened that I have not been able to put down. Two items were on the agenda, and the two blocs on the board had opposed positions on both of them: one wanted materials in the children's section restricted, the other wanted the meeting room opened to outside groups of every stripe. Someone proposed bundling the two, in a motion that would have moved each policy partway toward the other side's position on the issue that side cared less about. When it was put, each bloc said, in almost the same words, that the bundle was better than what we would get by voting it down — and both voted it down. Since then I have been reading political philosophy, and I keep meeting the idea that a compromise between opposed value systems is best modelled as a trade, whose point is to realize gains from trade: each side gives up something it ranks lower for something it ranks higher, and both come out ahead, so a compromise that both parties reject is either irrational or insincere. I would like to know whether what I saw can be a coherent position rather than a failure of nerve, and I would like the answer to come from a case cleaner than mine, since mine is muddied by mutual distrust, procedural confusion, and the possibility that the members were posturing for an audience.

Please construct a thought experiment to order, engineered so that a mutual-gain compromise is available and both parties are nonetheless forbidden by their own lights to take it. Specifications: contemporary and realistic — two parties, two issues, no science fiction, no ticking bomb, no villains; no one outside the two parties is affected, so that the only thing at stake is whether the exchange itself is permissible; both parties rank the bundled outcome strictly above the status quo on their own substantive rankings of states of affairs, and each knows the other's ranking; neither party is forbidden to accept by distrust, miscommunication, factual error, bad faith, an earlier promise, or a side-constraint invented for the occasion — the prohibition should fall out of a constraint each party invokes in ordinary cases; neither party may be a caricature, and each should have an articulate defender. Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph, and say explicitly whether it exercises compromise as the division of a disputed good or as a trade across issues, since I suspect the trade model only ever had the second in view.

After the scenario, do the demonstration. State each party's value system compactly enough that its verdict can be computed rather than asserted; give the gains-from-trade verdict, naming the exchange rate and showing that it is a strict improvement for both; then give each party's own verdict and the specific constraint that does the forbidding. Tell me which single structural feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens if it is removed — whether the case then collapses into a standard gains-from-trade situation in which refusal really is irrational. Then give the strongest attack a defender of the trade model could mount (I would expect something like: refusal is itself just a ranking over acts, and the model can be extended to trade in act-space at a different price), and say whether the attack can be met or whether it shows the case is rigged. Finish with what the case does and does not establish about the trade model: in particular, whether it needs an additional premise — that each party's ranking is over outcomes and that each is prepared to act on outcome-rankings — and whether that premise is a limitation of the model or simply a description of what compromise is. No citations or reading lists, please; I will add those myself.
