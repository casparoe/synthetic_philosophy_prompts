I'm a second-career graduate student — I taught high-school physics for a decade before switching to philosophy — and this term I've been sitting in on a seminar on the problem of evil. The discussions keep stalling in the same place: someone proposes that this world is the best of all possible worlds, or that a good and omnipotent creator could not have done better, and the rest of us begin objecting before we have agreed on what such a claim would even have to accomplish. I've been asked to write a paper that lays out the criteria rather than defends a position, and I'd like the specification settled before I commit to a line.

Please give me the full list of desiderata that any theory of the best of all possible worlds — any account of why a perfectly good and omnipotent creator would actualize this world rather than another — has to satisfy to be taken seriously. I want the requirements grouped by kind: the cases it must get right (the sheer scale and distribution of suffering, the existence of evils that appear to serve no one's good, the apparent contingency of which world exists at all); the distinctions it must not collapse (a defense versus a theodicy; the strong claim that this world is the best versus the weaker claim that it is good enough; God's freedom versus God's goodness; evil as a real feature of the world versus evil as mere privation); and the results it must be consistent with (the coherence of comparing worlds to one another at all, and the possibility that for any candidate world there is another containing more happiness, so that the ordering might have no maximum). For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is genuinely binding or merely a prejudice of one camp — for instance, the assumption that God must be morally assessable by our standards, or the assumption that the comparison of worlds must yield a total ordering.

Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; I am especially interested in whether the demand that there be a best world and the demand that God's creative act be free can both be met, and in whether the no-maximum worry is fatal or merely awkward. Tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the most popular views, since that is the claim I most want to be able to defend. Finally, in a sentence or two each, say how the classical optimism associated with Leibniz, a free-will defense, and a skeptical-theist refusal to specify God's reasons fare against your list — just enough to orient me, as I will do the scoring in the paper myself.
