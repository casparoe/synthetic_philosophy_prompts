Last night I argued with a close friend until nearly two in the morning about weakness of will, and we broke off with neither of us having conceded anything. She holds that akrasia is a folk-theoretical illusion: what we call weakness is simply hyperbolic discounting, a divergence between the preferences an agent has at the moment of acting and the preferences she had when she formed her plan, and the will that supposedly fails is just the name we give to whichever of those selves we happen to identify with. I hold that this cannot be the whole story, because the akratic agent does not experience herself as having changed her mind; she experiences herself as being carried against her own judgment, and that experience is something to be explained rather than explained away. We also disagree about what the Socratic claim that no one knowingly does wrong is even a claim about.

Write the dialogue between our two positions as we would have wished to conduct it: two equally competent interlocutors, neither of them a strawman, each responding to the other's actual arguments rather than restating their own, and with the debunking case given its strongest form. Have them press hardest on the case that divides us most: a woman who sets her alarm for dawn to write, silences it every morning, and who would do exactly what she had planned if the temptation were surgically removed. Ask whether she has exercised her will or forfeited it, and what remains of weakness of will once the temptation is gone. Let the exchange end in a sharpened disagreement or a genuine discovery, not a truce.
