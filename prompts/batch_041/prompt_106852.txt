I'm assembling a session for our department's podcast on science and religion, and next month's guest is a historian of early modern science whose forthcoming book reconstructs Galileo's hermeneutics of Scripture. Her central and, I think, deliberately provocative claim is that the interesting thing about Galileo is not the familiar accommodationist move — that Scripture condescends to the capacities of the vulgar and so speaks of salvation rather than of the heavens — but a jurisdictional claim running deeper than that: on her reading, Galileo holds that Scripture carries no probative authority over any conclusion natural philosophy can demonstrate, with the consequence that the correct reading of a nature-passage depends on the current state of physics, so that the interpreter of Scripture must in effect wait upon the natural philosopher. She further argues that it is this, rather than heliocentrism as such, that genuinely alarmed Bellarmine, since it inverts the traditional order in which natural philosophy is the handmaiden of theology.

I'd like eight to ten interview questions that press the position rather than permit her merely to restate it. I want at least one question on the difference between saying Scripture accommodates itself to the vulgar and saying Scripture is simply silent about nature — what work the accommodation doctrine is actually doing once it has explained away the geocentric texts; one on Bellarmine's methodological point that a passage may be reinterpreted only when a demonstration is genuinely in hand, and what that concession does to Galileo's case given the weakness of his physical arguments, the tides and the rest; one on Galileo's own selective appeal to Scripture where it suits him — the Joshua episode, the passages in which the sun is said to move or the earth to stand fixed — and whether that selectivity is inconsistency or something principled; one on the image of the two books and whether it yields two independent authorities or one subordinate to the other; and one on whether a layman has any standing to determine what the Holy Spirit intended in a particular passage. For each of these, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — in particular, the points at which she will want to say that the accommodation doctrine is simply Augustine's and therefore unremarkable, and those at which she will want to say that her reading is only a description of Galileo's actual practice rather than a recommendation.

I'd like the set to close with a question that makes the self-undermining structure of the position vivid: whether the method would remain defensible had the physics turned out to be wrong, and whether a hermeneutics that ties the meaning of Scripture to the fortunes of natural philosophy has not already conceded the very independence she claims for it. Please keep the questions in her own vocabulary where you can, and avoid anything that invites a survey of the period's general climate of opinion; the whole point of the session is to keep her on the hook of her own thesis.
