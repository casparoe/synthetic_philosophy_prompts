Ok, this one's a bit of an essay, but the set-up matters.

I've been following the two-dimensional semantics stuff on and off for years now — primary vs secondary intension, scenarios as worlds considered as actual, the whole 'conceivability entails possibility' move — because the zombie argument is the thing that made me take philosophy of mind seriously in the first place, and the 2D machinery is what's supposed to keep it from being a handwave. A friend from our reading group thinks the package proves way too much, and he's laid it out as a chain I keep failing to break. Here it is, as close to his words as I can get:

(1) On the 2D picture, a sentence is ideally conceivable when its primary intension — the function from scenarios, i.e. worlds considered as actual, to truth values — comes out true at some scenario, with no incoherence surviving ideal reflection.

(2) The load-bearing claim of the framework is that ideal conceivability entails possibility: if S is ideally conceivable, there's a genuine possible world where S holds. That's what carries you from 'I can conceive a zombie' to 'zombies are possible' to 'physicalism is false'.

(3) Now take the greatest conceivable being, where greatness is understood to include existing in every possible world. There's nothing incoherent in that; if I can conceive of a zombie I can certainly conceive of that. So by (2), the greatest conceivable being is possible.

(4) If it's possible, it exists in some possible world. But a being that existed in only some possible worlds wouldn't be the greatest conceivable one, since we could conceive of something greater — one that existed in all of them. So it exists in all of them, hence in this one. That's the old modal ontological argument; every step of it except 'possibly God' is just S5 plus the definition, and we got 'possibly God' straight from (2).

(5) Now take 'there is no greatest conceivable being'. Equally coherent, equally ideally conceivable, arguably more so. So by (2), it's possible that there's no such being.

(6) (4) and (5) can't both hold. So (2), stated as stated, is false.

(7) So the zombie argument needs a restricted version of (2). And every restriction he can think of either already assumes the modal conclusion it's meant to deliver, or it's a 'strong necessity' clause that just declares certain things metaphysically necessary with no conceivability test — the one thing the modal rationalist promised never to do.

My priors say keep (2) and drop the theism, obviously. But I notice that if I just say 'the greatest conceivable being isn't really ideally conceivable', I've bought a restriction that comes back at me the second someone says zombies aren't really ideally conceivable either, and then the framework has no teeth at all and I'm back to intuition-mongering about consciousness.

So: which premise do you cut, and why doesn't the cut gut the zombie argument too? And don't give me the menu of positions — pick one and defend it. If you think there's a real asymmetry between the two cases, put your finger on exactly where it lives: in the semantics of the descriptions, in the way the 'greatness' clause smuggles a modal clause into the content of the scenario, in the difference between the primary and the secondary notion of possibility, in the difference between positive concievability (we can spell the zombie world out in detail) and merely negative conceivability (no contradiction spotted), or somewhere I haven't thought of. Then price your cut: tell me what it costs for the 2D story about the necessary a posteriori, for the claim that the physical facts a priori entail the phenomenal ones, and for the zombie argument itself, and which of those you'd throw overboard first if you had to. If your honest read is that my friend's arguement is just broken somewhere, say that plainly and show me the joint.
