ok here's a thing that keeps turning up and I want to know what it assumes.

The version everyone knows is cake. One person cuts, the other picks. Nobody has to be fair-minded, nobody has to compare their happiness to anybody else's. The cutter just knows that if the pieces aren't even, the picker takes the bigger one, so the cutter makes them even. You get a fair-looking split out of two selfish people by having one of them set it up and handing the choice to the other.

Then the exact same trick shows up in board games, where it's called strategy stealing. In Hex -- the hexagon-board game Piet Hein came up with in the early 40s and Nash rediscovered -- you can prove the first player wins by a pure symmetry argument: whatever a winning strategy for the second player would look like, the first player could just make some arbitrary opening move and then play that strategy, and an extra stone sitting on the board can never hurt you. The kicker is that teh proof tells you the first player wins without telling you a single winning move. It's an existence proof. And the game's own fix for the first-player edge is the cake trick again: the swap rule, where after the first player opens, the second player can either answer normally or swap sides and take that opening move as their own. Go does something similar from the other end (passing is legal, so symmetry gives the first player at least a draw, which is why the second player gets compensation points to make the position lopsided on purpose). And chess is the standard counterexample: you can't run the argument there because of zugzwang -- positions where being the one who has to move is a liability, so an extra move can be a disadvantage.

So take the whole family -- cut and choose, the swap rule, "you do the dividing and I do the picking" -- and tell me what it presupposes.

- What view of fairness is in there? Is the claim that a split is fair because of how it came about, or because of how it came out (neither person wants the other's piece)? Are those the same claim here or different ones, and does the practice actually need them to line up? And what happens when the picker is an altruist who takes the smaller piece, or just doesn't want cake? If the procedure only works because the picker is selfish, has it ever been anything but a way of using selfishness as a measuring stick?

- What does it assume about the divider knowing what the picker wants? The cutter has to cut even in the picker's eyes, not their own. So is knowledge of the other person's preferences a premise of the whole thing, or is it smuggled in? If preferences are private, is this even a fairness device, or is it really an information-revealing device -- the pick is what does the work, not the cut?

- Lay out the assumptions the trick runs on, in plain terms: the two roles have to be genuinely interchangeable, and having more options (an extra stone, an extra turn, the first word) must never leave you worse off. Then tell me where each of those fails outside the game. Which real disagreements are zugzwang-shaped -- where getting to move first, or speak first, or go second, is actually the bad seat, so the trick just doesn't apply? And which ones look symmetric, both sides formally holding the same options, but aren't, because one side can afford to wait?

- Divisibility. Cake cuts. A house, custody of a kid, one kidney, a painting nobody will share, a job, a border -- don't. Does the practice need the good to be splittable, or does it only need there to be some common currency you can compensate in? Because "you can always sweeten the smaller side with something else" is a much bigger claim than "cake cuts in two", and I want to know which one is actually being presupposed.

- Where is it underdetermined? My hunch is that cut-and-choose fits several different stories about why it's fair -- the outcome is fair, or the opportunity was fair, or it's fair because both sides agreed, or it's fair because it's the rule nobody can game -- and that the practice itself doesn't choose between them. If it does choose, say which and how.

The part I actually care about: is our use of it consistent? We run this trick for cake, for which end of the pitch you get, for splitting a bill, and we think it's obviously just. We don't run it for custody, for sentencing, for who gets the kidney, or for setting a price between two parties who each know more than the other. Is that inconsistency principled -- do those cases break one of the asssumptions above -- or is it just that we don't trust the people in them to be the right kind of picker? And if you swapped the presupposed view out for a straight consequence-based one, where fairness is whatever produces the best expected outcome overall, what in the practice would have to change?
