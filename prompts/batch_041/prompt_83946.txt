Two colleagues have spent a year re-fighting the socialist calculation debate. One defends the Lange–Lerner position that a Central Planning Board can set prices by trial and error and reproduce the outcome of a competitive market; the other holds the Mises–Hayek line that the problem cannot be solved that way at all. Write the joint statement they could both sign.

Open with a numbered agreed-claims section: what both accept about the difference between Mises's original calculation argument and Hayek's later knowledge argument; about what Lange's Central Planning Board is actually stipulated to do (the parametric treatment of prices, the trial-and-error adjustment, the rules imposed on managers); and about the incentive problem as something distinct from the calculation problem. Then a disagreements table, each row phrased so that the side it describes would accept the wording of its own position. I expect the sharpest rows to concern whether the Board's procedure presupposes the very information a real market generates, and whether a system with a single price-setter and no competing buyers or sellers of capital goods is a market in any but a courtesy sense.

Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, with each side's prediction for each written down in advance. I suspect one crux is whether the failure is one of computation, so that better computers or a larger planning bureau would fix it, or one of discovery, so that they would not; and another is whether the incentive problem is independent of the calculation problem or a restatement of it. Please also take a position on a framing I keep reaching for: state the dispute as a question about whether the planner's fixed-point problem is solvable within the planner's own information set, or whether the equilibrium exists only as the residue of a process the planner cannot run — and say whether that framing is a fair neutral restatement or quietly rigs the debate for the Austrians.

Say how confident you are in the formulations you produce, and single out the one you are least sure of.
