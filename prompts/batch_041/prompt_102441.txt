I run a 20-person upper-level seminar on social epistemology and rational choice, one 75-minute session, and I want a in-class game that makes them feel the Condorcet jury theorem from the inside -- not just the textbook version where if everyone is slightly better than chance and votes independently, majority rule becomes near-infallible, but the part where the theorem's assumptions are doing all the work. I want them to run a jury, win or lose points, and then we break the setup.

Set it up as a binary case (guilty/not guilty, or two urns, or which of two candidates is better). Each participant gets a private noisy signal with accuracy just above 1/2, then majority vote decides. First round: they should see accuracy climb as group size grows, and I want the payoff scheme to make them care about being right without needing real money. Then a second round where the signals are correlated -- everyone draws from the same biased source, or there are only a few distinct info sources, so errors cluster and the majority can be confidently wrong. Then maybe a third round where deliberation changes individual accuracy, either through conformity or because one confident person dominates, or where the facilitator seeds the same misleading rumor through several apparently independent channels.

Give me the rules, materials (cards, dice, slips of paper, whatever is easy), roles for a facilitator and maybe a "source" player, a point-based payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps each round onto the theorem's assumptions -- competence above 1/2, independence or conditional independence, sincere voting, fixed competence -- and onto what happens when they fail. Don't name the theorem until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, like they don't believe the probabilities, someone games the payoff, or the correlated round is too obvious, and how to handle those. Be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: the theorem is a claim about asymptotic probabilities under a model, not about real juries' causal independence, and the game can't show the math. No citations or reading lists. Keep it runnable in one session.

End with a single line starting exactly with Summary: followed by one sentence saying what the activity is meant to make them feel.
