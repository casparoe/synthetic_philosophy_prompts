I took early retirement from a geology department about six years ago and have spent most of it teaching myself philosophy — history of science mostly, a bit of metaphysics of science, nothing systematic — so forgive me if I fumble the vocabulary. I have gotten interested in that little genre of writing that tries to forecast the collapse of empires and great powers by counting up how long past ones lasted and turning that into a hazard rate: the median great power in our sample lasted about 250 years, this one is at 230, so here is a probability for the next decade. There is a small shop near me that does this for paying clients and a friend got me a copy of a recent report, and what struck me is that it is of two minds in a way nobody involved seems bothered by. The front half is a table of durations plus an explicit method note saying they do not do historical analogy, every polity in the reference class treated as exchangeable, no case given special weight. The back half is a long narrative about this particular empire, its elite factions, its fiscal squeeze, its frontier, told with no reference to the table at all.

I spent thirty years coaxing base rates out of the fossil record, so I have some feel for how nasty this kind of inference is — you know you are reading a sample biased by what got preserved and by where people happened to dig, and that the boundaries between your categories are often artifacts of the classification rather than of the history. My hunch is that this forecasting practice is smuggling in commitments it would not want to own, and I would like you to test my guesses rather than just agreeing with them. I think it presupposes that there is a fact of the matter about when a polity begins and ends; that a frequency taken over a ragged sample of unlike cases can be a real property of a single unrepeatable case; that polities separated by two thousand years and five continents belong to one kind. But I cannot tell which of those the practice actually needs from which it merely happens to sit on top of, and I cannot tell whether the split I described is a defect or whether the narrative half is doing work the statistics definately cannot. So: reconstruct the implicit theory, tell me what the practice strictly commits itself to and what it could abandon without changing its output, tell me where it is underdetermined — would a propensity view of chance license exactly the same report, would an anti-realist view of singular chance leave it untouched, does the practice need a natural-kind story about empires or only a bookkeeping convention — and then commit. Pick the reading you think is most defensible and argue for it; do not hand me a menu of options. No need for citations or further reading, I can chase that down myself.
