I write a column on science and ideas, and I am assembling a piece on a question that the current glut of lives-of-the-great books keeps raising without ever settling: whether a biography can actually teach a reader how to live. I want to hold two traditions in view at once. The first is the ancient one — Plutarch opens his life of Alexander by insisting that his design is "not to write histories, but lives," and defends his own attention to small things on the grounds that "an expression or a jest" tells us more about a man's character than the bloodiest battles whatsoever. The second is the later edifying life, written frankly to be imitated, in which the subject's virtue is the whole point and the record is arranged accordingly. The modern literary biography typically distrusts both, and I would rather the piece run on a diagnostic instrument than on a thesis of mine, so that readers discover which of these inheritances they have actually absorbed.

Please build me a quiz of eight questions, for a general readership that has read a little philosophy and no Plutarch. Each question should be a concrete case with three answer options, written in the first person, and no option may name a theory, a school, or a philosopher; nor may any question take the flat form "do you believe a life can teach you how to live?" I want the cases doing the sorting. At minimum, include these: you learn that your moral exemplar did something monstrous (kept slaves, disowned a child, betrayed a friend); the biography you admire turns out to have been assembled from edifying episodes only, with everything inconvenient omitted; you meet a person whose goodness looks like temperament rather than achievement, and you cannot tell whether there is anything to imitate; a reader takes the wrong thing away, imitating a general's recklessness while thinking he is imitating his courage; a biography written so that we will not do as its subject did; and a fictional character who moves you as much as any real life ever has. Add one or two more of your own choosing. Then give me a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions, and make sure the following eight appear among the outcomes, each stated as a claim rather than a slogan: (1) admiration of persons, not grasp of rules, is the basic form of moral apprehension; (2) we learn by doing, and a life can only supply a pattern to someone already half-formed; (3) lives teach nothing about how to act, because there is no stable character to copy; (4) the edifying life is a genre with its own kind of truth — neither history nor fiction — whose lessons are real but are not evidence about anyone; (5) the genuine lesson of a life is negative, and the positive exemplar is a later smoothing-over; (6) exemplars supply the wanting, not the knowing — they move you without telling you what to do; (7) what a reader takes from a life is the biographer's way of looking, not the subject's way of living; (8) to take your ends from another's life is a failure of self-legislation.

After the key, add a short account of the shape of the space the quiz carves: which combinations of answers lead to no position at all, and whether each of those empty places is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely undefended; and which of the eight the key cannot really separate from one another, so that I can say so in the piece rather than pretend the instrument is finer than it is. Keep the whole thing self-contained and independent of any particular text or body of scholarship — describe each position by what it claims, use a school's name only where a label is genuinely in circulation, and do not pin positions to individual authors or send me to further reading, since I am printing this as a standalone sidebar.
