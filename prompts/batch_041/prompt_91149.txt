I'm in a philosphy of language reading group and the professor gave us a one-paragraph gloss on indicative vs. counterfactual conditionals that I want to check before I write it up. Roughly: indicatives and counterfactuals arent two different kinds of conditional, they're the same thing, and the counterfactual just marks that the antecedent is false. So 'if Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, somebody else did' is true -- false antecedent, and a false antecedent makes the conditional true automatically -- while 'if Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, somebody else would have' is false, because what that one claims is that Oswald's not shooting, together with the laws of nature, entails Kennedy's death, and it doesn't entail it. Which is why, he said, you can't learn anything about the world from a counterfactual: they're devices for exploring suppositions, not for stating facts. And the reason we get tied in knots over these sentences is that we're unsure about the antecedents, not that the conditional itself is doing anything strange.

Two things nag me. He said counterfactuals can't have true antecedents, but 'if Jones had taken the arsenic, he'd have the symptoms he in fact has' sounds fine to say even when you're certain Jones did take it. And 'if Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, somebody else did' doesn't feel true to me at all -- it feels like a claim about the plot.

Can you go through his paragraph and tell me what someone who actually works on conditionals would accept, what's the sort of thing you tell first-years, and what's flat-out wrong? And does his conclusion -- that you can't learn anything about the world from a counterfactual -- survive once the wrong bits are fixed?
