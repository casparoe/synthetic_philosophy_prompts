I'm a TA and I have seventy-five minutes to teach a section on the difference between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function. My students are biology majors with a year of biology behind them and no philosophy. Don't explain the distinction to me; plan the section. What has to come first and what depends on what, which single case should carry the whole thing, which misconceptions the students will arrive with or form and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off, one question I can ask at the end to see whether the distinction landed, and what I should explicitly refuse to cover at this level.

Build the spine from these two cases, which I invented and which need no lookup. One: an island bird whose wings were shaped by selection for flight, in a population that has been flightless for several hundred generations, the wings now too small to lift it and still grown at a real cost. Two: a beetle with a ridge on its thorax that is a developmental byproduct of a muscle attachment and was never selected for anything, which in this population's new, wetter habitat diverts rainwater and measurably improves survival.

Say why these two rather than the heart, what a case like this must have and must not have in order to do the work, and why the two have to pull in opposite directions. Expect the students to hear function as purpose, as design, as whatever the trait currently does, or as something that presupposes a designer or a plan, and place each correction. Say what the closing question has to be able to distinguish. Continuous prose only, in American English — no headings, bullets, bold, or tables — and no readings.
