I am a high school junior applying this summer to a research program in philosophy for secondary students, and the application requires a two-page research proposal describing the project I would pursue if admitted. I have chosen the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory, but I want to approach it in a way I have not seen in the introductory material I have read.

As I understand the problem: on the Bayesian account, a body of evidence supports a hypothesis to the extent that the hypothesis makes that evidence more probable than its rivals do, and a rational agent updates her credences by conditionalization. But if the evidence was already known before the hypothesis was formulated, the agent's prior already assigns it probability one, so conditionalizing on it cannot move the hypothesis's probability at all. This clashes with the history of science, in which evidence that has been known, and unexplained, for decades gets treated as powerful support for a new theory once that theory predicts it exactly. Something has to give.

The responses I have come across seem to cluster into a few moves: evaluate the hypothesis not against the agent's actual prior but against a prior she would have had before the evidence was known; hold that what such evidence supplies is explanation rather than confirmation, and that the probabilistic machinery is simply the wrong tool; or amend the updating rule itself. My hunch is that all of these accept the framing in which the problem is stated — a single hypothesis, a single body of evidence, and the question whether that evidence raises that hypothesis's probability — and that the framing is where the trouble lives. I suspect the question ought to be comparative: not whether old evidence confirms a hypothesis, but whether it discriminates between that hypothesis and its competitors. A theory built to accommodate a known anomaly may deserve little credit for accommodating it, while a rival that fits the same anomaly without having been built around it may deserve a great deal. If that is right, the old-evidence problem may need restating rather than solving, and the real work is to find which cases still bite after the restatement.

Please write the proposal. It should state the question precisely enough that it could come out either way; explain why the existing responses leave it open; describe what I would actually do — I expect this to be mostly the construction of small, invented probabilistic examples with two competing hypotheses rather than any gathering of data, but tell me if that is the wrong method and set the work out in stages across about eight weeks; say what would count as the project failing, not only as its succeeding; and assess honestly whether this is realistic for a high school student with one summer. Write it as continuous prose I could submit as it stands. Please do not include citations or a reading list — I will add those myself — and refer to positions by description rather than by the names of their proponents.
