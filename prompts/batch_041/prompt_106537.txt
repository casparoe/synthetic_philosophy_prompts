I have been defending a thesis I will call the arc thesis, and a colleague has just walked me into an argument that appears to show it cannot do the work I want it to do. I think the argument is too quick, but I cannot find the seam.

The thesis: the moral history of our species has a direction, and the direction is toward justice. The mechanism I have in mind is not the widening of sympathy; it is more mundane. Arrangements that impose large, visible, uncompensated costs on identifiable people are expensive to hold in place, and they grow more expensive as the people who bear those costs become harder to exclude from the productive and cooperative life of the society, since the costs of exclusion are then paid in force, in sabotage, and in withheld cooperation. Justice, on this picture, is the equilibrium that domination is forever drifting away from and being pulled back toward -- not because anyone is becoming better, but because the alternative is unstable. I do not claim the pull is fast, monotone, or universal.

His argument:

(1) If the arc thesis is true, then later arrangements are on average more just than earlier ones; otherwise the word direction is doing no work.
(2) An arrangement, or a view, that cannot be defended to those who bear its costs is one for which there is no good reason.
(3) The mechanism I have described removes arrangements that cannot be defended to those who bear their costs. By (2), it removes the views that go with them, and it leaves standing the views that can be defended.
(4) So later views are on average better supported than earlier views, and therefore on average closer to the truth.
(5) So the bare fact that a view is later than another is evidence that it is truer than that other.
(6) Our views are later than those of every generation that has lived so far. So we have evidence that our views are truer than theirs; and by the same step, the views of whoever comes after us will have evidence of being truer than ours, by a margin no one can estimate.
(7) The thesis therefore licenses the prediction that some of what we hold most confidently will be judged by our successors roughly as we now judge our predecessors; and, since everyone alive is later than someone, it licenses anyone on any side of any dispute to say that history is on their side.
(8) But the thesis was supposed to be a reason to trust our side, not a reason to expect that our side is substantially wrong, and not a warrant that transfers to whoever happens to be standing later.
(9) So the thesis either says nothing about the present, or it says something that undercuts itself.

I would rather give up (8) than (1) or (3); I am not defending the thesis for comfort. But I do want it to have something to say about the present. Which step should I reject? Explain why it looked so plausible that neither of us caught it, and price the alternatives: if more than one step can go, tell me which is cheapest to give up, and what claim I have to abandon along with it.
