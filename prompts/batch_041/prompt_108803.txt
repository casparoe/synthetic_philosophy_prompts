Build me a thought experiment to order, on the question of which computations matter morally. I want two systems that are indistinguishable in every respect we can observe — same behavior, same efficiency, same reports about their own inner lives — where one is a serious candidate for being a moral patient and the other is not, and where the difference is meant to lie in the computation rather than in the material. The case is meant to test whether the question of which computations matter morally can be answered without quietly smuggling in the substrate, so the following count as cheating: making one system a lookup table or otherwise absurdly larger than the other, locating the difference in a biological fact we cannot inspect, or stipulating the verdict in the setup. The verdicts have to fall out of the structure of the case, and both systems should be things we could plausibly build.

The contrast I have in mind is stateful versus stateless: one system carries its history in its own internal state and reuses it, while the other reproduces the same outputs from an input that already contains the history, so the apparent remembering is done by the environment rather than by the system. Use that contrast if it carves the joint; if something else carves it better, say what and why.

Then do the checks. Give the verdict of (a) a functionalist who holds that any system implementing the right computation is a patient, (b) a view on which the relevant computation must be implemented by the system's own causal structure rather than spread across system and environment, and (c) a skeptic who thinks ascriptions of inner life track nothing real and that the two systems are therefore on a par. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if I remove it (both stateful, or both stateless), and how the defender of whichever verdict fares worst would attack the case.
