I've been asked to give the keynote at a naturalization ceremony next month -- the kind where a few dozen people take the oath, get their certificates, and each gets to say a sentence about where they're from. I'm German by birth, American by choice, been here twenty-two years, and the organizers asked me specifically because I'm a "bridge," which I hate, but fine, I said yes. Here's my problem. Every draft I write turns into a lecture on something I actually believe: that there are two moral styles in that room and they don't sit easily together. The German one says the good person is the one who does the right thing because it's right, who follows the rule whether or not anyone's watching, who treats order and predictability as themselves a kind of decency -- for whom saying they were only following the rules is not an excuse so much as a small point of pride. The American one says the good person answers to their own conscience first, treats an unjust rule as no rule at all, for whom the whole point of being free is that you get to decide for yourself what's right -- the one who'd rather be the lone dissenter than the well-behaved member of a crowd that's wrong. I grew up in the first and now live in the second, and honestly I don't know which has the better of it. A friend offered me a tidy version: the real difference is where each culture puts the burden of proof -- the German style assumes the rule is right and you owe it a reason to break it, the American style assumes you're right and the rule owes you a reason to bind you. I can't tell if that's an insight or a fortune cookie. And the room makes it worse: half the audience are brand-new citizens who came here precisely to get away from some version of the first style, and the other half are officials and old-timers who'll hear any warm word about conscience as a wink at lawlessness.

So write me the keynote. Roughly 700-800 words, warm but not gushing, and it has to *do* something with that tension rather than just gesturing at it -- I want the contrast to shape what the speech can and can't claim, so you can feel the constraint pressing on every line. Please don't let it end on "both have their place," if you can avoid it; I'd rather it land somewhere a person could actually stand. Then give me a second section, called "Where this leans on contested ground," about 200-250 words, telling me honestly which parts of what you just said about German versus American moral culture are well-supported and which are stereotypes that people who study this genuinely argue about -- I don't want to stand up there and present a contested generalization as consensus. And if the honest answer is that the whole two-styles framing is a crude binary that doesn't survive contact with actual Germans and actual Americans, say so there, in that section, and tell me how much of the speech I'd have to give up to fix it.
