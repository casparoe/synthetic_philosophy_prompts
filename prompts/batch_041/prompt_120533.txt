I'm a second-year student in a course on the economics of technological change, and I want to write my term paper on the American civil nuclear program as a test case for path-dependence theory. The standard story runs roughly as follows: the light-water reactor became the world's dominant design not because it beat its rivals -- gas-cooled, heavy-water, and liquid-metal designs were all being pursued in the 1950s and '60s -- but because the naval reactor program under Rickover had settled on pressurized water for its submarines, after which the Atomic Energy Commission built the first commercial-scale plants around scaled-up versions of the same design and encouraged utilities to follow suit, and then learning-by-doing, regulatory precedent, and a trained workforce made switching prohibitive. I want that treated as a case analysis rather than a summary: who the choosers actually were at each stage (the Navy, the AEC, the utilities, the reactor vendors), which alternatives were genuinely live at the moment of each choice rather than only in retrospect, and which of the mechanisms usually invoked for lock-in -- increasing returns, learning effects, standardization, and the sheer cost of reversal -- are doing real work here rather than being read back into the story after the fact. Then turn it around and tell me what the case does to the theory: is it a clean instance of lock-in, or is it closer to a case in which the early commitment was defensible on the evidence then available and the later convergence was ordinary selection for efficiency, so that the appearance of lock-in is largely an artifact of hindsight? If my question is malformed -- if lock-in simply cannot be distinguished from efficient selection without a counterfactual that nobody can specify -- I would rather you said so plainly than manufactured a verdict.

End with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing exactly one of: locked-in, not-locked-in, indeterminate.
