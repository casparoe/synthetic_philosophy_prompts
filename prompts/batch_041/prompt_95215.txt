A colleague in my political-theory reading group circulated the following précis of Foucault, and I would like to know how much of it a specialist would actually endorse. She writes:

"Foucault's central claim is that knowledge is never independent of power: what a society treats as true is fixed by which institutions have the standing to declare it, and since power is everywhere it is effectively all there is. On this view the individual, far from being a source of freedom or resistance, is itself manufactured by power, and talk of liberation is at best a symptom of the system it claims to oppose. His histories are meant to show that the shift from the sovereign's scaffold to the modern prison, clinic, and school replaced spectacular punishment with a quieter and more total control that reaches into every corner of life. The upshot is that Foucault's theory is a performative contradiction — if all knowledge is power, his own books are just another move in the game — so the corpus can be set aside as a clever relativism that refutes itself."

I suspect the final step is too quick, but I cannot say exactly where the description goes wrong, and I would rather not guess. Please separate what a careful reader would accept, what is a distortion or an overstatement, and what a defender of Foucault would most want to contest — noting especially whether she is collapsing distinctions he was careful to preserve, such as that between his earlier archaeological studies and his later genealogical work, or between the ubiquity of power and the claim that power is all there is. Then tell me whether her concluding "so it can be set aside" follows from whatever in her summary survives correction, or whether it rests on the parts that do not. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate my question.
