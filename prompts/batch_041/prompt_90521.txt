(a) Money seems to play two different roles in Aristotle. In the Nicomachean Ethics it is the device that makes exchange possible at all; in the Politics the pursuit of it is what threatens the household and the city. Reconstruct Aristotle's account of what money is, what it is for, and how exchange is supposed to be just. (b) Say whether the two treatments are consistent with one another, and defend your answer. (c) Then consider the following case. In a small coastal community, discs cut from shell are used in the market to buy fish, and the same discs are used to settle marriage payments. The discs that circulate longest are never spent on anything — families hold them for years — and the discs most sought after for marriage are the ones that have changed hands the most times. Does Aristotle's account of money and exchange have anything to say about this community, or does the case fall outside what he was theorizing? Take a position.

And here's the script I'm marking:

"Aristotle treats money as something we agree on rather than something we find in nature. Exchange is necessary because nobody is self-sufficient, but a builder and a shoemaker can't swap their products directly unless there is some common measure of how much of one is worth how much of the other, and there's no such measure sitting in the things themselves — a house and a pair of shoes have nothing in common that would let you line them up. So we introduce a token that stands for need: a coin represents the demand the shoemaker has for a house and the builder has for shoes, and because it plays that role it is the natural measure of the value of everything — which is why Aristotle adds that it is in our power to change it and make it useless.

Exchange itself is governed by proportion rather than equality. His example is that as the builder is to the shoemaker, so is a certain number of shoes to a house; the exchange is just when the products stand in the same ratio as the producers. Money makes this possible by giving both sides a single scale to work on.

The Politics then seperates two kinds of acquisition. One belongs to household management: hunting, farming, fishing, and barter for the things the household actually needs. This kind is natural and unlimited, since a household can always use more of what sustains it. The other aims at money itself, and this is what he calls chrematistics in the pejorative sense — retail trade, and above all usury, which he condemns in the Nicomachean Ethics as the most unnatural of all ways of making a living, on the grounds that money is meant for exchange and not for generating more money. The story of Thales cornering the olive presses shows how easily the moneymaker turns knowledge into profit, and the Midas fable makes the same point from the other side, that wealth in coin is not the same as living well. There is no real tension between the two treatments: the Ethics explains what money is for, and the Politics explains what happens when that function is turned into an end in itself.

As for the shell discs, Aristotle's conventionalism is wide enough to cover them. If the community treats the discs as the measure of value and the medium of exchange, then they are money, whatever they're made of, and the fact that the longest-circulating ones are kept back for marriage payments is just a further convention about which discs are for what. Nothing in the account requires money to be metal, so the case isn't a counterexample to anything he says."
