For some time now I have been working through the old puzzle of the statue and the clay — a sculptor takes a lump of clay, shapes it into a figure, and later reduces it to a shapeless ball — and I have arrived at a position I would like you to test to destruction.

The position is that there is only ever one object in these cases: the clay. 'Statue' does not name a second thing that stands in some intimate relation to the clay; it is a description of that same thing while it happens to be arranged in a certain way. So the statue and the clay are strictly identical rather than merely coincident, and the impression that there are two things is an artefact of how we describe one thing, not a fact about the world.

What I want is an honest map of how exposed this view is. Which arguments, intuitions, cases or formal results would count against it? What does it predict that its rivals do not? I would like the defeaters ranked, from those that would be fatal to those that would be merely awkward, with some indication of which ones the defenders of the view would themselves concede count against them and which they would simply dismiss. I am also interested in whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance, by retreating to the claim that the statue is the clay even though the statue's being a statue is not the clay's being clay — and, if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind or merely the ordinary price of doing metaphysics.

One case in particular bothers me, and I would like you to take it seriously rather than treating it as a version of the usual objections. Suppose a single lump of clay is shaped into a figure, then squashed, then shaped into an entirely different figure, then squashed again. If the statue is just the clay, then the first statue and the second statue are one and the same object, and the squashing that seemed to destroy the first is nothing more than a change in the clay. That strikes me as the sort of consequence that ought to make me give up the position — yet I can also see a defender replying that 'statue' is a phase description and that the object simply persists through both phases, in which case the view risks nothing at all. Tell me which of these it is, and what would settle the matter.

That objection is the one I find most persuasive, and I would like to know whether I should. Please answer in British English.
