My partner's family is Catalan, and independence has been the live argument at every family meal since I have known them; more recently, the town I live in has begun a petition for a vote on leaving the larger city it was merged into fifteen years ago, and I have been asked to speak for the leave side at a public meeting. Preparing for that has forced me to write down what I actually believe, and I find that my verdicts do not obviously come from one principle. Here they are.

- Scotland's 2014 referendum was legitimate. Both governments agreed to hold it, the question was clear, and had it passed I would have thought Westminster was bound to negotiate. I would say the same if the Northern Isles had then wanted a separate arrangement of their own.
- Catalonia's 2017 referendum was not legitimate. The constitutional court had ruled it out, the constitution declares the unity of the Spanish nation indissoluble, and well under half the electorate voted. I would say the same even if the yes side had won overwhelmingly among those who did turn out.
- Quebec's 1995 vote was legitimate, but a 50.1% win on a question about sovereignty with a partnership offer attached would not have settled the matter for me. The Supreme Court of Canada's 1998 opinion, which treated a clear majority on a clear question as what triggers a duty to negotiate, seems right to me.
- Kosovo's declaration was legitimate despite Serbia's refusal, because of what had happened in the years before and because no consensual path existed.
- Crimea's 2014 vote was not legitimate, even though I assume a majority there did want to leave Ukraine, because it took place under foreign military occupation and staying was not a fairly available option.
- South Sudan's referendum was legitimate: the parent state agreed to it, and the grievance was longstanding.
- A wealthy region with its own language and a solid majority that wanted out mainly to stop paying transfers to poorer regions: not legitimate, even with a clean vote.
- The Cree and Inuit of northern Quebec had as much right to stay in Canada as Quebec had to leave it. Quebec could not have carried them out against their will.
- My own town, merged into a bigger city whose council ignores us and spends our taxes elsewhere: legitimate, and I would vote to leave.
- If a region secedes and one of its port cities votes heavily to remain with the old state, that city should be allowed to stay.

My tentative principle is this: a group may secede if it is a distinct community with a shared political identity, a clear majority of it votes to leave in a free vote, the resulting state would be viable and democratic, and it would protect the minorities left inside it; and no secession is legitimate if the parent state offered a genuine constitutional route and the group bypassed it. Test that against the cases and repair it. I suspect the distinct-community clause is doing illegitimate work, since I want it to exclude the wealthy region but not my own town, and I cannot see what separates them except size and the fact that one of them is mine.

State the principle, or a small set of rival candidates, as precisely as you can, naming the case that forces each clause. Give its verdicts on cases I have not listed, including one you invent that puts real pressure on it. Then tell me which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and commit: if two of them cannot both survive, say which one I should give up and defend that choice rather than laying out the options for me to pick from. No reading list; I want the argument.
