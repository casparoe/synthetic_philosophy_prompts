I am taking a continuing-education course in ethics, and after a session on how demanding morality can reasonably be, I admitted to a friend of mine — a philosophy graduate who is usually reliable about this sort of thing — that I could not get a grip on the idea of supererogation. He sent back the following thumbnail sketch, which I reproduce as faithfully as I can. I would like you to audit it rather than improve on it, since I do not yet know which parts to trust.

His sketch runs roughly as follows. Supererogation is the category of actions that are morally good but not morally required: the soldier who throws himself on a grenade, the stranger who donates a kidney, the colleague who quietly covers for you at the cost of her weekend. Such acts are said to occupy a distinctive place in the moral landscape — not obligatory, since one does no wrong in omitting them, but not merely permissible either, since they are positively good and praiseworthy. The traditional division of acts into the forbidden, the permissible and the obligatory is therefore incomplete, and a fourth category is needed, the supererogatory, which is really the realm of the morally optional: the space in which morality lets you do as you please and merely applauds if you do more. The word itself descends from theology, where works of supererogation were understood as good deeds over and above what God commands, but the modern puzzle, sometimes called the paradox of supererogation, is that if an act really is good, it is hard to see why we do not simply owe it to one another — either the act is good, in which case it looks required, or it is not, in which case the praise looks misplaced. He reports that most philosophers now take the paradox to dissolve once one separates an act's being good from its being required, so that nothing deeply puzzling remains, and that this is why the concept is generally taken to show that any moral theory without room for it must be wrong: strict Kantianism, which makes duty the whole of morality, has no place for the hero, and act utilitarianism, which tells one to maximise the good at every moment, makes every optimific act obligatory, which is why utilitarianism is so often said to demand too much. He ends with the thought that some philosophers worry the supererogatory space is where self-indulgence hides, and that the ideal of a moral saint is dehumanising in any case, which he says is basically the same worry.

I suspect he is right about the broad outline and wrong about some of the joints, but I cannot say which. Could you go through the sketch claim by claim and tell me what someone who works on supererogation would accept as stated, what they would wince at and want reworded, and what is simply mistaken? I am particularly unsure about the taxonomy — whether the supererogatory is really a fourth category sitting alongside the obligatory and the forbidden, or something with a different logical relation to them — and about his suggestion that the puzzle has been dissolved. Finally, does his closing inference survive correction? That is, if it turns out that Kantians or utilitarians can make room for supererogation after all, is there anything left in the sketch that licenses the conclusion that a moral theory lacking a distinct supererogatory category is refuted? I am not looking for citations or a reading list; I want the anatomy of the summary, so that I can see which of its sentences I can safely repeat and which I should not.
