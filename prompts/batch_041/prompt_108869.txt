For a seminar on the ethics of automated systems I want a satirical teaching text, and I would like you to write it. Compose a formal claim-denial letter from the liability insurer of a firm that operates autonomous machines, addressed to a claimant injured by one of those machines (choose the setting yourself — a fulfilment-centre robot, an autonomous shuttle, a surgical or agricultural machine). Write it in the flat, courteous, procedurally obsessive register of real corporate correspondence: reference numbers, a policy schedule, a summary of the incident, a determination, a schedule of offered remedies.

The humour must come from the substance rather than from jokes bolted on. The letter should reason its way, in evident good faith, to the conclusion that no insured event has occurred, because the harm cannot be traced to any agent who satisfied the ordinary conditions for responsibility: the operator did not know what the system would do and could not have controlled it; no engineer specified the behaviour that caused the harm, since it emerged from the system's own learning; the design was certified and compliant; and the machine is not a person. Treat this as a genuine chain of reasoning, not a list of excuses, and let the letter keep discovering that each party it might blame fails one of the conditions — knowledge, control, intention — on which the previous party's exoneration rested. Then let the remedies it offers (a goodwill credit, an invitation to pursue the manufacturer, a suggestion that the claimant take the matter up with the system's own 'decision') make the problem worse rather than better.

A reader who has never heard of the responsibility gap should be able to follow the letter and feel the problem; a reader who knows the literature on autonomy and accountability should see that the piece understands the difference between a practical gap that better rules could close and a conceptual one that they cannot, and should not find it cheating.

Aim for roughly 650 words. Please commit rather than hedge: make definite choices about the scenario, the parties, and where the argument finally lands, instead of gesturing at several possible readings. After the letter, add a short note of about 120 words stating which positions the letter commits to and where it takes liberties with, or leans on a contested reading of, the responsibility-gap debate.
