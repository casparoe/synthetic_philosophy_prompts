I am a novelist at work on a book set in West Germany in the mid-1980s, centred on a historian who is drawn into the Historikerstreit. My character is trying to decide whether the Holocaust should be treated as a singular rupture in human history or as one atrocity among others, and whether its deepest roots lie in modernity itself. I want to write a scene in which she imagines what Theodor Adorno would have said had he lived to see the dispute. Adorno did not live to see the Historikerstreit, so I am not asking for a summary of his actual interventions; I want a carefully reasoned prediction of the position he would have taken, in his own idiom, on questions that arose only after his death.

At the heart of the scene is a paper by a fictional colleague, a comparative historian who argues that the Holocaust is best understood as an extreme instance of a general modern logic of extermination, and that its distinctiveness lies not in intent or scale but in what she calls the administrative sublime: the transformation of mass killing into a problem of logistics, hygiene, and population management. She compares it to colonial atrocities and to Stalinist terror, and concludes that any claim of uniqueness is a form of moral special pleading. I would like you to reconstruct, as convincingly as you can, how Adorno would have responded to this thesis in the context of the Historikerstreit. What would he concede to the comparativists? Where would he insist on singularity, and on what grounds, whether metaphysical, ethical, or historical? How would his account of modernity and Enlightenment rationality bear on the debate? And how, if at all, would he distinguish his position from the thesis later associated with Zygmunt Bauman, that the Holocaust was a product of modernity rather than a failure of it? I am not looking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the argument itself. Please write the answer in British English, in the register of a philosophical essay rather than a set of talking points.
