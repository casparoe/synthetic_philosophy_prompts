I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm commissioning a short story for our annual fiction issue. The story should take its bearings from a cluster of questions in the philosophy of mind and science: the idea that we can predict a system by treating it as a physical object governed by laws, as a designed artifact whose parts serve functions, or as an agent with beliefs and desires, and the further idea that a pattern is "real" when it is a compressible description that actually buys us predictive power rather than something we merely project. I don't want a lecture in costume; I want a story in which these commitments are visible in what the characters do and predict, not in what they say about the theory.

Please give me a working draft of about 2,500 words. I'd like the setting to be an institution whose business is prediction — a forecasting shop, a risk bureau, a research station — where the staff routinely shift between the three ways of predicting, and where a newcomer arrives with a device or method that claims to settle, mechanically, which description of a given system is the shortest and therefore the real one. Treat that device as an invention of your own; you need not ground it in any existing technology, but its verdicts should have consequences the characters care about.

Two constraints I'd like you to honor, because they're where the story gets interesting. First, include at least one entity that the intentional stance predicts best even though it appears to have no inner life, and at least one person who is predicted best by the design stance — treated as a well-functioning machine rather than an agent. Second, let the reader be able to tell, from a character's predictions alone, which stance that character is taking; don't label the stances in the prose. I don't want the story to resolve the underlying dispute by fiat or by revealing that one stance was correct all along. End in the tension, with the characters still disagreeing about whether the newcomer's device measures a fact about the world or only about our convenience in describing it. No citations, no footnotes, no epigraphs — the story has to stand on its own.
