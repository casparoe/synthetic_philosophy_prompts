QUESTION (Q3, short answer)

In the agreeing-to-disagree framework, two agents share a prior over a finite state space Ω, each observes the cell of her own information partition containing the true state, and each updates by conditionalization. (a) State the agreement theorem precisely. (b) What work is the common prior assumption doing in the argument — i.e., what exactly breaks if you drop it? (c) Does the theorem show that rational agents cannot disagree? Explain. (d) One of our readings suggested that the common prior is better read as a normalization — a way of forcing any disagreement to show up as a difference in information rather than a difference in prior — than as a literal claim that people share beliefs. Assess that suggestion.

STUDENT ANSWER

(a) Each agent has a partition of the state space, and her information is just which cell of her partition contains the actual state. Both agents start from the same prior. Each then updates by Bayes' rule on her cell, giving her posterior for some event E. Aumann's theorem says that if the two posterior probabilities are common knowledge — each agent knows the other's posterior, knows that the other knows hers, and so on up the hierarchy — then the two posteriors for E must be identical. So rational agents can't agree to disagree.

(b) The common prior assumption is what does the heavy lifting here. It says that the agents' posterior beliefs are common knowledge: they all know the same prior, and each knows that the others know it, and so on. If you drop it and let each agent have her own prior, the argument falls apart and the posteriors can diverge, which is basically why we see smart people disagreeing all the time — it just means they didn't start from a common prior.

(c) What the theorem does not show is that agents can never disagree; it shows they can never disagree and both be rational. The moment there's common knowledge of the disagreement, at least one of them must be violating Bayesian updating or else they must have different priors. So when two experts who share a prior look at the same evidence and reach opposite conclusions, the theorem tells us something has gone wrong: either the disagreement isn't really common knowledge, or someone isn't being rational. This is why the common prior is such a strong assumption — in real life people rarely have exactly the same prior, and once you relax it you can get all the disagreement you like.

(d) I think this is basically right. The common prior is a modeling convention rather than a psychological claim about people, which is why the theorem is about the structure of the model and not about whether real people actually agree.
