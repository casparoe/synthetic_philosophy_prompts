I run a reading group on collective responsibility, and for the last session of term I want to do something other than another open discussion, so I've been trying to turn our running argument into a ranking exercise. The argument is the inefficacy objection: if I take one more long-haul flight, eat one more burger, or add one vote to a landslide, the harm — climate change, animal suffering, the wrong candidate winning — happens whether or not I act, and my single act seems to make no difference to anything. The intuition that such acts are nevertheless wrong is stubborn, and so is the suspicion that the stubbornness is sentiment rather than argument. I've collected the candidate principles we kept reaching for over the term, and I want you to sort them.

To keep this from turning into a recitation of the standard positions, I'll state the principles as we formulated them, including two that a classmate and I invented and that I'm fairly sure you won't find in a textbook. (1) The difference-making rule: an act is wrong only if, holding everyone else's behaviour fixed, it makes the harm more likely or more severe. (2) The threshold rule: your act is wrong when it belongs to a set of acts that jointly push the world across a harm-threshold, even though no member of that set is necessary or sufficient for the crossing. (3) The universalizability rule: an act is wrong if the maxim of acting that way, universalized across everyone similarly placed, would produce a bad outcome. (4) The complicity rule: the wrong lies in knowingly participating in a harmful practice rather than in the causal increment. (5) The expressive rule: the act is wrong because of what it expresses or the norm it reinforces, consequences aside. (6) The fair-share rule: everyone is entitled to an equal slice of a sustainable total, and taking more than your slice is wrong whether or not the total is exceeded. (7) The marginal-probability rule, which is ours: since you can never know whether your act is the one that makes the difference, the quantity that matters is the ex ante chance that you are the marginal contributor, and an act is wrong when that chance multiplied by the size of the harm is large enough to matter.

Please put the seven into three tiers — those that genuinely survive the inefficacy objection, those that survive it only by quietly changing the subject, and those that collapse into it — and give me one or two sentences on each saying why it lands where it does and naming the case that most threatens it. Then tell me which single principle you would want a thoughtful person to use when deciding whether to take one long-haul holiday flight that will depart whether or not they are aboard, and why that one beats the runner-up. Rank on how well each principle withstands the objection, not on how well known or respectable it is; if the standard answer deserves the top tier, fine, but don't promote it for being standard.
