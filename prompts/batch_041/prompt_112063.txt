A friend of mine has spent the past year talking himself into the view that the only thing that ultimately matters about an action is how things go for everyone it touches, and a few months ago he put a question to me that I have not been able to put down. He had been trying to decide whether to keep his car or sell it, and found that the answer came out one way when he counted the consequences over the next year or so and the opposite way when he counted them over the next few centuries; when I asked where he thought he should stop counting, he said he could see no principled place to stop, only convenient ones — where the data ran out, or where the chain of effects became too tangled to follow. What struck me was not the ignorance, which is obvious enough, but that the verdict was a function of a cut-off he had chosen for no better reason than convenience, and that an equally convenient cut-off elsewhere would have flipped it. Nor was it confined to cars. On the same reasoning, the difference between my buying a coffee and giving the money away is trivial over a day and enormous over a century; a friend of ours insists that her vote can never be decisive and is therefore ethically inert, which I recognise as the same shape of mistake pointing the other way; and my friend is left saying both that nothing he does can be ranked against anything else and that some quite ordinary things he does matter as much as a war.

I have since noticed that people who take this seriously settle into one of two habits, and both feel like choices rather than conclusions. Some quietly draw a boundary, count only the near effects, and stop using the old vocabulary about themselves; others go the other way and treat the very far future as the only thing that matters, so that almost every ordinary concern becomes noise. I have also noticed that the difficulty seems to come in two strengths, and I cannot tell whether they are one problem or two: the weaker is simply that we cannot know the numbers, the stronger is that even with the numbers the total would be dominated by effects so far out, and so dependent on how the domain is drawn, that it has no determinate value at all. If the second is right, then the standard appears to give no verdict on its own application — including on the verdict that it gives no verdict.

What I want to know is whether this shape of difficulty already has a name. I realise there is probably a literature on the unknowability of distant consequences, but I am sceptical that the cut-off-dependence and the self-application are part of the named thing rather than separate complaints bolted onto it, and I suspect that what I am describing is two or three problems wearing one coat. So I would rather have the phenomenon taken apart than labelled: what exactly is being claimed, which parts of it are genuinely distinct, what the nearest established ideas are and how each of them differs from what I have described, and, if nothing fits, a term of your own with a definition and the boundary cases that would test it. You needn't give me a reading list; I am after the concepts. Where people who work on this agree, tell me it is settled; where they do not, tell me that, and do not let a contested reading pass as the consensus. Please write in American English.
