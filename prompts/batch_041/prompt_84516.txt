You are a political theorist who works on democratic theory, and in particular on the boundary problem — the question of who counts as the people entitled to decide, and how a decision procedure can settle membership in the demos without already presupposing it. I would like you to think this through carefully before you write, because the difficulty I keep running into is conceptual rather than legal, and I would rather have a candid analysis than a tidy one.

I sit on the charter review commission of a mid-sized city, and at our next meeting we vote on what to send the council about a proposed charter amendment. The amendment would let any adult resident who has lived in the city for three years or more vote in municipal elections, including in the referendum that would adopt the amendment itself; a companion proposal would have the amendment drafted by a randomly selected assembly of residents, non-citizens included, with ratification by referendum. The case for it is that these residents are taxed, policed, and otherwise subject to the city's coercive authority, and their children attend its schools, so both of the principles I know from the literature — the one that says everyone affected by a decision should have a say, and the one that says everyone subject to coercion should — appear to point toward inclusion. The case against it, as my colleague puts it, is that a people has to constitute itself before it can decide who belongs to it; that an amendment enfranchising its own ratifiers is bootstrapping; and that the current residents have a legitimate claim to determine the character of their own political community. Another colleague has read enough to conclude that the philosophical literature proves there is no non-question-begging answer, that any line we draw will be arbitrary, and that we should therefore say so and simply pick a procedure. There is also a hard constraint: state law ties municipal voting to citizenship, so we must choose between recommending a home-rule petition to the legislature, which is faster but uncertain, and a state constitutional amendment, which is slower and might fail — and the recommendation has to be actionable within this council term.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should open with a brief working map of the conceptual terrain, then give me the memo proper: one to two pages in the voice of a formal commission opinion, recommending a concrete course of action with a time frame, laying out the reasoning my colleagues can actually argue with, and engaging the bootstrapping and self-determination objections on their merits rather than swatting them away. I especially want a considered verdict on my second colleague's claim that the exercise is arbitrary at bottom — if that is right, I need to know what follows for what we should do, and if it is wrong, I need to see where the argument fails. I don't need citations, a reading list, or a survey of who said what; where you do invoke a position by name, flag it if you are unsure of the attribution rather than asserting it smoothly. Where the literature genuinely underdetermines the question, say so instead of manufacturing a consensus, and if I have set this up badly — if, say, the subjection-to-coercion principle collapses into the affected-interests principle, or the bootstrapping objection is a pseudo-problem that dissolves once stated precisely — tell me plainly rather than writing around it.
