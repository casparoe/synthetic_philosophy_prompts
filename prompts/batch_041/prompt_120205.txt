I'm a features writer who covers ideas — my beat runs from cognitive science to art criticism — and next month I'm giving the closing talk at a two-day symposium on criticism and knowledge, the sort of event where a philosophy department shares a coffee urn with a museum's education staff. An essay of mine has been assigned as background reading, so I'll be defending it in front of people who have thought about this for decades longer than I have. I want a prep sheet, not reassurance.

The thesis, briefly. We lump two different things under aesthetic testimony. Testimony that a work is good — well made, ambitious, worth your time — can transmit knowledge the ordinary way: it is a warranted belief about a property the work has, and a reliable critic is a reliable informant. Testimony that a work is beautiful, or moving, or sublime, cannot transmit knowledge of that, because those predicates are indexed to a response that only acquaintance produces. So the principle aestheticians call the acquaintance principle — the thought that a judgment of taste must be earned at first hand — is half right, and its defenders and its critics have spent decades fighting over the wrong half. I also claim that the way criticism actually works is not belief transmission at all but perceptual re-education: a good critic teaches you to notice the feature, and your own response does the rest. I'd like to know what the view implies for the case I keep circling: I've seen the film, I disliked it, and every critic I trust calls it a masterpiece.

The audience, as well as I can make it out: a majority of analytic aestheticians who hold some version of the acquaintance principle; two or three epistemologists of testimony who regard aesthetics as a backwater and think the general theory of testimony already settles the matter; a Kant scholar; several working critics who suspect the whole distinction is a philosopher's trick; and possibly someone from a psychology department who studies expertise.

Give me the six to eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they do if I fumble them, each phrased the way that group would phrase it, each with a reply I can deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede in my opening remarks to take the wind out of the worst ones, and which objections I should stop resisting. Then tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks. No citations and no reading lists — I can't check anything on the podium, and I would rather have the arguments than the authorities.
