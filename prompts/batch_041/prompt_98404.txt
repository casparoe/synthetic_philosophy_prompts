I am a novelist — my last two books were written wholly in the third person, and both turn on a character who cannot see colour — and next month I am to give the plenary at a consciousness-studies conference, where I mean to argue that the knowledge argument is at bottom a claim about narrative point of view: that Mary's room is a compressed instance of a general truth about fiction, namely that no accumulation of third-person description ever adds up to a first-person perspective, and that this is why the argument keeps returning after every refutation aimed at it. The audience is not a general one: it is largely philosophers of mind, mostly physicalists who think the phenomenal-concept strategy has already disposed of the argument; two of a Dennetian bent who hold that Mary learns nothing whatsoever; a handful of literary scholars who will want to tell me that narratology settled this decades ago; and one or two scientists working on global workspace models who will insist the whole matter is empirical. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet — the eight or so objections I will actually get from these people, phrased in their own vocabulary and ordered by how much damage they would do, each with the two-minute reply I should give and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede at the outset, what to hold firm on, and end with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
