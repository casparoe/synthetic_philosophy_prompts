I want an essay on the Corsair affair and Kierkegaard's theory of the press and the public, and I want you to actually reach a conclusion rather than hedging.

Quick refresher on the facts so you don't have to guess at them: Copenhagen, 1845-46. There's a satirical weekly called Corsaren (The Corsair), run by Meïr Goldschmidt, which savages basically everyone in the Danish literary scene. Kierkegaard, writing under one of his pseudonyms, Frater Taciturnus, publishes a piece in a rival paper, Fædrelandet, in late December 1845 — 'The Work of a Traveling Aesthete' — in which he more or less complains that everyone gets abused by the Corsair except him, and pretty transparently asks to be mocked too; he repeats the request a couple of weeks later. Goldschmidt obliges, and for months the paper runs caricatures of him — his body, his clothes, his whole physical person — turning him into a running joke in the city, and he ends up a kind of public figure, which is the last thing he wanted. Meanwhile, in that same year, he publishes his long review of a novel by Gyllembourg, the part of which gets translated on its own as The Present Age, where he lays out his account of the age: leveling, chatter, and the public. The claim, roughly, is that the public isn't a group of people at all but an abstraction, a nobody that everyone somehow belongs to, with no members and no simultaneity — which is exactly why it can't be argued with, apologised to, or held responsible for anything, and why the age can only produce reflection and envy rather than action.

Now read the affair through the theory and the theory through the affair. Some things I'd like you to actually grapple with rather than gesture at:

1. He keeps the press and the public seperate — the press is a machine with real people at it, the public is the phantom the machine produces. So which one was the Corsair? Goldschmidt was a man with a name and a business, and yet the thing that did the damage seems to have been neither just him nor just "the public." Does the theory have room for that middle object, or does it just have a gap there?
2. He asked for it. Why would someone with a theory of leveling deliberately hand himself over to the leveling process — is that a way of forcing an abstraction to become a real interlocutor, or the opposite, a voluntary sacrifice that only makes sense if the public really is nobody?
3. He did it under a pseudonym, while the paper attacked him as a body. Say what that asymmetry does to the analysis — anonymity pointing one way, a caricatured body pointing the other.
4. The theory says leveling is a process with no agent, so nobody is really the enemy; but the affair plainly had an agent and plainly had a victim. Is that an anomaly, or does he have the resources to absorb it — say, the category of the single individual as the thing that survives leveling?
5. Where the case simply can't test the theory, say so instead of forcing it to explain everything.

Reach a verdict: does the episode confirm the theory, refute it, or expose something it never anticipated? Commit to a reading and defend it — no both-sides wash. If you think the modern version of this (a pile-on with handles and avatars instead of a weekly paper) breaks or vindicates his picture, one paragraph at the end is welcome, but keep the main focus on 1846. And dont bother with citations or a reading list, obviously — I dont need you to recieve credit for footnotes, I want the argument.
