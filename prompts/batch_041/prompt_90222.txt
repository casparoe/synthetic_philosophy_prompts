A friend of mine works in corporate compliance — she writes the training decks that nobody at her company actually reads — and a couple weeks ago she asked me, only half joking, whether philosophers have anything useful to say about how corporations manage to get away with things. I'd just read Knobe's 2003 study on intentional action for a seminar, so I told her about the chairman scenario: the CEO hears that a new program will raise profits but will also harm the environment, says he doesn't care about the environment and only wants the profit, the program goes ahead, and most people say he harmed the environment intentionally. Then you swap "harm" for "help" — same indifference, same profits, the environment benefits as a side effect — and suddenly only a small minority say he helped it intentionally. (I think it was something like low eighties versus low twenties, don't hold me to the exact numbers.) And I'm fairly sure the same gap shows up if you ask whether he harmed the environment *in order to* raise profits, which is the bit that makes it feel like more than a quirk about the word "intentionally." My friend got weirdly excited about this and now wants me to draft an actual training document for her team, and honestly I think you'll do it better than I would.

So: write the leaked internal training memo. It's from a made-up compliance consultancy (call it Ashgrove Partners, or whatever you like) and it's addressed to client executives, in deadpan corporate-consultant voice — headings, numbered sections, a bit of boilerplate at the end, roughly 650 words. The conceit is that the firm has absorbed the finding and is now quietly monetizing it, coaching clients on what the memo calls "intentionality exposure." I want the memo to work through the practical consequences without ever admitting what it's doing: a section on why a harmful side effect reads as deliberate and therefore blameworthy, and how to reframe it as standard industry practice so it reads as norm-conforming rather than a violation; a section on the help side of the asymmetry, where the useful lesson is the opposite one — if you want credit for a benefit, never let it ride as a side effect, because nobody will call it intentional, so make it the stated aim instead; something on the awkward fact that the indifference is identical in both versions, so the exposure comes from the valence of the outcome and not from the CEO's attitude; an FAQ; and closing boilerplate that disclaims any claim about what anyone actually intended. It can refer to the research only obliquely — "a widely replicated 2003 study," that kind of thing — since I don't want a real person getting mocked in a fake memo.

Two things I care about. First, the jokes have to come from getting the material right, not from anyone winking at the reader: a specialist should recognize the study, the harm/help asymmetry, and the live debate over whether the effect is about moral badness or about norm violation more generally, and should find it a fair reductio of the idea that the folk concept of intentional action is a stable thing you can just manage — while a reader who has never heard of any of this should still follow it and find it funny. Second, stay consistent: the memo must never break character or confess the game, even at the end, and no citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. American English, please.
