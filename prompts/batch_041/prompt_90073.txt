Hey, I'm reading Hume's Enquiry, the section on miracles (I think it's Section X, "Of Miracles"). I don't have the book with me right now, but here's the gist of the arguement as I understand it, and I want help untangling one part of it.

Hume says a miracle is a violation of a law of nature. The evidence for a law of nature is basically the entire uniform experience of mankind — we've seen the sun rise every day, we've seen dead people stay dead, etc. Then he says that when someone testifies to a miracle, we have a conflict between two kinds of evidence: the testimony (which can be strong, especially if the witness is honest and there are many of them) and the uniform experience (which is maximally strong, since it's backed by everyone who's ever lived). He says we should believe whichever side has more probability. Since the uniform experience behind a law of nature is about as strong as evidence can get, testimony to a miracle would have to be strong enough to outweigh basically all of human experience — which he thinks never actually happens. And he says a wise person proportions belief to the evidence, so no testimony could ever make a miracle credible. Then in Part II he gives a bunch of practical reasons why testimony for miracles is especially unreliable: people love marvels, religions contradict each other, the stories get exaggerated as they spread, etc.

Here's what I'm stuck on. Hume seems to say that the probability of a miracle given testimony is always less than the probability that the testimony is false. But isn't that just assuming the conclusion? If I already think laws of nature can't be violated, then of course testimony for a violation won't convince me. But Hume also seems to think he's not assuming that — he's doing a neutral probability calculation from the evidence alone. And then there's the bit where he says the evidence for a miracle would have to be such that its falsehood would be more miraculous than the event itself. That's a fun line (I'm paraphrasing) but it seems circular: "more miraculous" by what standard? If a miracle is by definition maximally improbable, then nothing can ever be more miraculous than it, so the test can never be passed. But if we're allowed to say "the testimony being false would itself be a miracle," then we're comparing two improbabilities, and I don't see how Hume gets to rule that out in advance.

Also, what would Hume say about a case where the testimony isn't for a violation of a law of nature as we currently understand it, but for something we just don't have a law for yet — like a genuinely new phenomenon? Is that a "miracle" in his sense or not? I feel like his definition makes the category empty by construction, but I'm not sure if that's a bug or a feature. Can you walk me through how the argument is supposed to work without begging the question, and where the real weak point is? I'm not looking for citations or anything, just help thinking it through.
