ok so this has been bugging me since a thread on the subreddit last week where someone posted the usual Moloch stuff, and I tried to write out why it feels like it explains everything and therefore nothing, and I ended up with an argument that I think might be valid but whose conclusion I obviously can't accept. I want you to tell me which step to give up and what it costs.

Setup, because the terms are load-bearing. Call a situation a trap if every participant, doing their own best move given what the others are doing, ends up somewhere all of them would rather not be. Two flavors people seem to distinguish: hard traps, where the incentives punish anyone who unilaterally cooperates (arms races, commons problems), and soft traps, where cooperating would be fine but everyone expects everyone else to defect so nobody moves first (bank runs, fashion, maybe parts of academia). I don't think the distinction survives contact, and here's the argument that killed it for me:

1. A soft trap is one where the cooperative outcome is also an equilibrium: if I were confident the others would cooperate, cooperating would be my best move.
2. A rational agent does whatever maximizes expected payoff given their beleifs about what everyone else will do.
3. So if I expect the others to defect, defecting is my best move, even in a soft trap.
4. So a soft trap is held in place entirely by beliefs about other people's behavior and not by the payoff structure.
5. But my beliefs about what others will do are just one more part of the world I'm responding to, and there's no principled line between responding to the payoff structure and responding to what the others are going to do, since what the others do is the payoff-relevant environment.
6. So every soft trap is a hard trap with extra steps: one mechanism, two descriptions, and the mechanism is just agents best-responding to their environment.
7. So Moloch adds nothing over people playing best responses to their expectations, and the whole discourse is either trivially true or empty.

The last step is too strong and I know it, because that essay did something for me that Nash equilibrium never did, and I dont think the thing it did was nothing. But I can't find the bad step. 5 is where I get nervous, since it's the kind of move that proves too much about everything (responding to the laws of physics is also responding to the world, so are we all just obeying gravity?), but if I drop 5 I'm not sure I can still state the difference between the two flavors, and I want the difference. If I keep 5 I think I have to keep 6, and then I'm choosing between 1 and 7, and I'd rather keep 1.

So: which step goes, why did it look fine, and what do I have to pay to give it up? If there's more than one candidate, price them against each other. And if the thing is actually valid and the real cost is hiding in step 7, tell me what that cost is and whether it's one I should want to pay.
