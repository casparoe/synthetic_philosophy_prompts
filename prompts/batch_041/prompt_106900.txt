I'm a novelist — third book, and I've gotten myself tangled up in a problem I didn't see coming. There's a character in the new one who makes his big decisions by flipping a coin, and I thought I was writing about fatalism, but a friend who teaches decision theory read the draft and said what I'd actually written was a guy with an ambiguity-aversion problem, and that I should go back and watch No Country for Old Men again. So I did — the Coens film, then the McCarthy novel — and now I can't stop thinking about Chigurh and that coin: the gas station owner who gets to call it and lives, and then the last scene, where he offers Carla Jean the same deal and she won't take it, won't let the coin be the thing that decides. What I want from you is a real reading of the book and film through the Ellsberg paradox and the risk/ambiguity distinction. You know the setup I mean — two urns, one you know is half red and half black, one full of a hundred balls of some undisclosed mix, and most people would rather bet on the known urn whichever colour they're betting on, even though a bit of arithmetic says that's incoherent. The thing I can't shake is that Chigurh's coin looks like Ellsberg's known urn: it takes a man standing in total ambiguity about his own fate — his life depends on the whim of a stranger he can't read — and converts it into a clean, known 50/50. Is that a mercy? Is it the same move as preferring the urn you understand? Or is it a con, since the coin's composition is known but the uncertainty that actually mattered (whether this man pulls the trigger) was never in the coin at all?

And then Carla Jean. She refuses the coin, refuses to call it, insists it's just him doing this — I'd like you to say what that refusal amounts to, in the theory's terms. While you're in there, I'd like some of the weeds on the normative fight over whether ambiguity aversion is even a mistake — the old line that it's a fallacy versus the defenses that say disliking ambiguity is sensible when you feel ignorant about the source, or when you suspect the other party knows more than you do.
