I'm a fifth-year PhD candidate in political theory, and for the past two years I've spent roughly a day a week as the informal ethics consultant to the enforcement committee of a mid-sized public-interest law nonprofit. The committee meets every other week and has to decide, case by case, whether to ask a court or an agency to bring actual coercion to bear — contempt, fines, license suspension, seizure, eviction, arrest — in matters where our legal entitlement is clear but the underlying rule is contested, and where the people who will absorb the cost of enforcement are often not the people we set out to help. The decisions are practically irreversible within a news cycle, the committee is made up of litigators with no philosophical training, and the executive director has final sign-off, so I need something that fits on a single page and can be run in about twenty minutes without me in the room.

I've been rereading Pascal's Pensées on justice and force: the thought that justice on its own cannot act while force on its own is a tyranny; that we have never managed to make justice strong, so we have settled for treating the established power as just; that laws are obeyed as laws rather than because they are just; and that the person who sees through custom yet still obeys it stands in a different position from the crowd that believes in it and from the half-clever who want to tear it down. I want that turned into a decision procedure, and I want three things built into it that I have not seen operationalized anywhere. First, a strict double ledger: legal validity and substantive justice must be scored separately, and neither may be allowed to settle the other. Second, a coercive-subsidy column: for every proposed escalation, the procedure must name who actually pays the cost of the rule being enforced and whether those people consented to the rule. Third, a three-audience check: for each candidate action, predict how the crowd, the half-clever, and the wise — as Pascal uses those types — will read the act of coercion, and treat a divergence between the first two as a warning rather than as a public-relations problem to be managed. Also build in a tyranny test, drawing on Pascal's idea that tyranny consists in trying to get by one kind of power what only another kind can give, so that the procedure can catch us using a contempt order to obtain deference, acknowledgment, or affection that a court order cannot produce.

Give me the procedure as an ordered list of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where it stops with a no, and the points where it hands the decision to the executive director because what remains is a value choice rather than a factual one. Then run it, start to finish, on two cases: (a) a judgment against a city housing agency that has missed its accessible-housing deadlines, where the remedy under consideration is contempt against the agency, which would freeze funds currently paying for emergency shelter; and (b) back wages owed by a small family-run restaurant whose seizure would close it, in a neighborhood where most of the workers live. Then run it on a third case where I expect the procedure to tell us something we will not want to hear: a county clerk is misapplying a registration rule in a way that is probably unlawful but long settled, we have a clean path to a ruling that would void the rule, and Pascal's counsel about the wise person and established custom may well say that the disruption is the greater evil — if the procedure lands there, let it land there, and say plainly what we are supposed to do with an answer we find repellent. Close with a candid section on what the procedure cannot do, which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, and the failure modes you would expect when it is run by tired litigators. Write it in American English, and do not assume we have the texts in front of us — paraphrase Pascal's positions rather than quoting him.
