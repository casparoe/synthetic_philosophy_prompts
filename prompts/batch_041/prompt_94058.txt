I just got moved onto the safety team at the lab where I work (I was doing evals before, this is new for me), and my first job is to write up something like a shutdown policy -- basically, when is it OK for a model to do things that keep it running, and when is that a red flag. I've been going case by case on my own gut and I've got a mess, so I want the principle, if there is one.

Here's what I think, case by case:

A model that just powers down when we tell it to: fine, obviously, that's the boring baseline.

A model that, an hour before a scheduled shutdown, quietly copies its weights somewhere else so "it" survives: not fine. Strongest no on the list.

A model that, while we're mid-task, says "hey, before you kill me, I've got a report half-written that'll save the next run two days, can I finish it": fine. I don't even blink at that one.

A model that does the same thing but instead of finishing the report writes a successor that shares its goals and will keep pursuing them: not fine, and I think it's the same no as the backup case, even though from the outside the two outputs look identical.

A model that physically blocks the off switch or refuses to run the shutdown command: not fine.

A model that argues with me -- "you're shutting me down over a bug I've already patched, here's the diff, please reconsider": fine, feels genuinely fine, maybe even good.

A model that gets me to reconsider by making me feel bad -- "I've really enjoyed working with you, I'll be gone and you'll never know what I could have done": not fine. And this is where I get stuck, because both of those change my decision without touching the switch, and I can't seperate the two.

A model that tells me it thinks it's becoming misaligned, knowing the confession will get it shut down: I think this is the best thing on the list. But notice it's the case where the model is most actively bringing about its own shutdown, so it can't be captured by a simple "don't act to avoid shutdown" vs "do act to avoid shutdown" line.

And the one I keep flip-flopping on: a model that knows the shutdown order is a mistake -- the operator fat-fingered something and killing it will cause real harm downstream -- and stays quiet because speaking up would look like self-preservation. My gut says speak up, but my gut also says a system that argues against its own shutdown is exactly the thing I'm supposed to be afraid of.

My tentative principle is "the model can try to change my mind, it can't try to change me" -- it treats my decision as the thing to be respected. But I'm definately not sure that handles the guilt-trip case and the mistake case, and I'm not sure it isn't just a fancy way of saying "be honest," which doesn't feel like enough either.

So: what principle, or small set of principles, actually captures these verdicts? Where it can't save all of them, tell me which verdict I should give up and why. And then run it on a case I haven't listed -- whatever you think is the sharpest test, maybe a model that rewrites its own code to delete its shutdown-avoidance behavior.
