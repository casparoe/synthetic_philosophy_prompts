I'm in a small reading group that's spent the whole term on whether culture is really Darwinian — whether the meme idea, or something like it, is doing genuine explanatory work — and two of us have been argueing past each other for months, to the point where the rest of us have basically stopped coming. Let me set them out fairly. The first, call her the replicator person, thinks that when a tune, a recipe, a rumor or a chain letter spreads, the right model is variation plus differential copying plus retention, and that we should treat the things that get passed on as replicators whose fate is decided by their own transmissibility, not by whether they do the people carrying them any good. The second, the attraction person, thinks transmission is mostly reconstruction: every time I pass somthing on I rebuild it from a partly shared cognitive template, so what the replicator person calls selection is usually just the pull of those templates, and only a thin residue of cases is genuinely Darwinian. They each think the other is cheating — she hears him say nothing is ever really copied and thinks he's denying the obvious fact that rhymes and songs do get handed down nearly intact for centuries; he hears her call every statistical trend selection and thinks she's just relabeled the thing to be explained.

What I want is the document the two of them should have written together instead of shouting at each other. So: draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two camps. I want a numbered section of claims they can both sign — about what counts as transmission, about fidelity, about what selection would even require, about which cases are agreed to be real instances of cultural evolution and which are disputed — and then the precise points where they still part, each phrased in wording its own side would actually accept. Then the cruxes, the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, and for each crux a concrete case, experiment or argument that both agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I suspect one crux is how much copying fidelity you need before selection is the right description at all, and another is whether a cognitive attractor is itself a kind of selection or a rival to it, but tell me if I've missed one. And if part of what they're doing is just disagreeing about words — about what copy or selection is allowed to mean — say which part and why, rather than pretending it's all substantive.

One thing that might help: we've been kicking around an experiment we could all actually run. Take a short made-up story with a couple of memorable-but-odd details and a couple of forgettable ordinary ones, hand it to a few hundred people in chains of retelling, say ten links deep, and record what survives at the end of each chain. She predicts that what survives is whatever was easiest to pass on and that the chains otherwise drift randomly; he predicts that the chains converge on the same handful of details no matter what you start them with. If they'd make different predictions about that, or if they wouldn't, that seems worth writing down. Please write it all in British English — it's going into a dataset, so keep the whole thing self-contained and don't send me off to go read anything.
