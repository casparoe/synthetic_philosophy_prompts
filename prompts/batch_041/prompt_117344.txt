Our school's philosophy of science club has spent this term reading Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, and I have come away with an argument that I cannot tell whether to believe, because I am not sure whether it is a real consequence of the book or a mistake I have made in reading it.

As I understand it, Hofstadter invents a small formal game that he calls typogenetics. Its raw material is strings of letters — A, C, G and T, borrowed from the genetic alphabet — and its rules say that such a string is read off in pairs, each pair standing for a single operation: cut the strand at this point, delete a letter, insert a letter, move along it, or copy it. A run of these operations makes an enzyme that binds to a particular letter and then carries out its commands on the strand it finds there. What makes the game more than a puzzle is that a strand can therefore be read in two ways at once: as data, and as the instructions that produce the machinery which acts on data of that kind. Hofstadter then observes that this is structurally the same situation as in molecular biology, where DNA is transcribed and translated into proteins that act back upon DNA, and also the same situation as in Gödel's proof, where a formal system is made to contain a formula that speaks about its own provability. In each case the same strange loop appears: a system that reaches around and describes itself.

Our group then drew what seemed to me an inescapable conclusion. Gödel's incompleteness theorem says that any consistent formal system rich enough to represent arithmetic, and able to talk about its own proofs, must contain truths it cannot prove. If typogenetics is genuinely isomorphic to DNA, and typogenetics is a formal system, then DNA is a formal system as well, and the incompleteness theorem should apply to it. It would follow that every genome is incomplete in the same sense — that for any organism there are facts about its own traits which its DNA cannot prove, so to speak, or specify — and therefore that no organism can contain a complete description of itself, and perhaps that no fully complete science of a living thing is possible even in principle, in the way that a complete and consistent axiomatisation of arithmetic is impossible.

Is that right? I would like to know whether the isomorphism actually licenses carrying Gödel's result over from logic into biology, and if it does not, precisely which step fails. Is it the move from isomorphism to identity of kind, or the assumption that DNA is the sort of system to which the incompleteness theorem applies at all, or the move from an unprovable truth to an unencodable trait, or the final move from genetics to the limits of biology as a science? And I would like to know what the isomorphism does entitle me to say — what it is actually an isomorphism between, and what lesson about self-reference it is meant to carry, if not this one.
