I lead a small technology-assessment unit at a national environmental regulator. Our remit is broad: we receive proposals to authorize new activities and substances — a gene drive intended to suppress an invasive rodent on an island, a new class of neonicotinoid, a deep-sea mining license, an outdoor stratospheric aerosol injection experiment — and we must recommend one of a fixed set of dispositions: approve, approve with conditions, require further study, restrict, or refuse. Our governing statute directs us to act precautionarily where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, and forbids us from treating the absence of full scientific certainty as a reason to postpone protective measures. In practice this has degenerated into a slogan that both proponents and opponents of any proposal can invoke with equal sincerity, and our decisions have become unpredictable and hard to defend on review.

I need a decision procedure my unit can actually run. It will be used roughly twice a month, on proposals where the science is contested and the harms at stake are large; each recommendation goes to a board and may be litigated, so the procedure has to be defensible step by step rather than merely sensible in aggregate. Assume a five-person team, a ninety-minute meeting, access to the proposing party's dossier and to our own literature review, and no ability to commission new research on the timescale of the decision.

Build the procedure from the following raw materials, which we have been using loosely and which you may sharpen, reject, or replace as you see fit. First, a three-way distinction among cases where we can attach probabilities to the outcomes, cases where the possible outcomes are known but their probabilities are not, and cases where we do not know what the possible outcomes are. Second, the observation that declining to act is itself an action with its own harms, so that a precautionary rule must be symmetric — it must be able to tell us when precaution argues for a proposal as well as against it, and it must not be able to license any conclusion whatsoever. Third, the thought that any precautionary measure has three separable components: a trigger, the condition that licenses action; a target, what specifically is to be protected; and a remedy, the action taken — each of which can fail independently of the others.

Then give me the procedure itself: the questions to ask, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; the point at which the procedure stops and returns a refusal; the point at which it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts; and the point at which it can only recommend further study, together with the condition under which that recommendation is a genuine deferral rather than an evasion. Run the procedure on three proposals: the gene drive, the neonicotinoid, and the stratospheric aerosol injection experiment. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like. Finally, tell me the failure mode to which this procedure is most vulnerable, and what the first line of attack from an opponent of a decision made under it would be.
