I'm preparing a paper for a graduate seminar on Wittgenstein's later philosophy, and I want to test a hunch about how much work the idea of a community is really doing in the private-language argument. What I need from you is not an exposition of the argument -- I know the text -- but a thought experiment built to order, one that isolates a single variable and lets competing readings of the argument come apart over it. Here is the specification. The case must center on two people who are isolated together, away from any wider linguistic community -- a pair, not the solitary diarist of the familiar example -- who are both competent speakers of a public language and who each independently notice a recurring sensation that neither can point to in the other's case, and who then agree between themselves on a sign for it. It must be stipulated that they use the sign consistently and keep a shared written record, so that the worry that one of them might simply be misremembering an earlier use is closed off by the description rather than left dangling. The setting should be ordinary and realistic -- no science fiction, no exotic cognitive powers, no telepathy -- and the scenario itself should fit in a single paragraph.

Then do the work the specification is for. I want the verdict of each of two readings of the private-language argument, with the verdicts coming apart because of what each reading says about the role of a community rather than because of any difference in how the case is described. The first reading is the one on which a rule can be followed only within a practice shared by a community, so that correctness of use is fixed by communal agreement; the second is the one on which the argument turns not on community but on the absence of any independent criterion against which a use could be checked, so that agreement among people who each check only their own case cannot supply one. State, for each reading, whether the pair's sign is a meaningful word and why. Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to each verdict if that feature is removed -- say, by making the sensation publicly observable, or by reducing the pair back to a solitary diarist; and how a defender of whichever reading comes out worse would attack the case in order to save it. If you think a third, quietist reading that denies the argument establishes any substantive thesis here has a distinct verdict of its own, say so briefly and mark it as such. Finally, since the interpretation of this argument is genuinely contested, separate clearly what is settled about the text from what is disputed among people who work on it, and do not present any one reading as the consensus.
