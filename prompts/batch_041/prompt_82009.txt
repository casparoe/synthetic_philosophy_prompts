I've read enough rationalist-adjacent writing on commitment devices and escalation to be tired of the standard dollar-auction tale, in which someone gets carried away and ends up paying $40 for a $1 bill. That version makes escalation a failure of nerve or arithmetic, and the structure is more interesting than that: once you are in, quitting locks in a certain loss while continuing only risks a larger one, so every individual bid is defensible on its own terms and the trap is invisible from the inside. What I want is a story that runs on this, with the twist I keep turning over: the character entered on purpose, as a costly signal to someone whose belief she needs, and the signal only counts if she really is trapped, so her credibility has to be bought with losses she keeps paying, and by the end it should be genuinely unclear whether the whole thing was a strategy or a catastrophe. Keep the stakes ordinary (a fundraiser, a dispute between colleagues, a family obligation), keep everyone sober and clear-headed, and don't resolve it as a fable about learning a lesson. Give it to me as exactly five numbered scenes, a few sentences each: entering; the point where the sums pass what she told herself she would risk; a face-saving exit she refuses for reasons that are actually good ones; the audience updating; and the last bid. No preamble, no epilogue, no commentary.
