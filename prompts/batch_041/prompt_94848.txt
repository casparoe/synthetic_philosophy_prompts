At a reading group last week, two friends of mine, Mira and Theo, fell into a long argument about a taxonomy of minds that Mira had summarized for the group. The taxonomy distinguishes four grades. Darwinian creatures are those whose behavioral strategies are fixed by natural selection: they arrive with their dispositions, and if those dispositions are bad, they die. Skinnerian creatures can learn from reinforcement: they emit behavior, and what is rewarded tends to be repeated. Popperian creatures can represent possible actions and their likely outcomes internally and evaluate them before acting, so that a hypothesis can be eliminated without the creature being eliminated. Gregorian creatures are Popperian creatures that have acquired tools of thought—words, symbols, norms, techniques—from their culture, and can therefore think thoughts that no isolated individual could have assembled alone.

The argument proceeded in several rounds.

(1) Mira said the four grades mark real and increasingly powerful discontinuities. In particular, the Skinnerian/Popperian divide is not a difference of degree: a Skinnerian creature is bound to its actual reinforcement history, whereas a Popperian creature can decouple its behavior from that history by simulating outcomes. The Gregorian grade is a further discontinuity because it introduces cultural inheritance, which is not in the genome and can accumulate far faster than genetic evolution.

(2) Theo replied that the discontinuities are oversold. He said the Skinnerian/Popperian distinction is essentially the distinction between model-free and model-based reinforcement learning, which is a difference in computational architecture but not a difference in kind; both are forms of trial-and-error learning, and the offline evaluation of a Popperian creature is just a rollout of an internal model that itself had to be learned by reinforcement. He added that Gregorian creatures are simply creatures whose environments include other creatures and their artifacts, so their mind-tools are a special case of environmental scaffolding, not a new grade of mind.

(3) Mira responded that this reduction ignores the fact that the taxonomy is not a taxonomy of learning algorithms but of capacities for control. A creature can be Skinnerian with respect to one behavior and Popperian with respect to another; the taxonomy is about the highest grade of control an organism can bring to bear in a given domain, not about a global property. She said that if Theo's reading were correct, the taxonomy would be empty, because every organism is a mixture.

(4) Theo said that was precisely his point: the taxonomy is a useful heuristic for sorting mechanisms, but it is not a taxonomy of kinds of minds, because there are no kinds—only graded mixtures. He also said the scheme smuggles in a normative hierarchy, with the Gregorian grade treated as the pinnacle, which he suspected was just a dressed-up claim that human cognition is special.

(5) Mira said the normative charge cuts the other way: the taxonomy is descriptive, and the fact that it has a pinnacle is a finding, not a bias; if human cognition is special in a specific, specifiable way, that is not chauvinism but an empirical claim. She conceded that the boundaries are fuzzy but said fuzzy boundaries do not make a taxonomy useless.

(6) Theo said that even if the taxonomy is descriptive, the Gregorian grade is not a grade of mind at all, because it is not a property of an individual organism: the tools are shared, and the individual's Gregorian cognition is distributed across a culture. So calling a single creature a Gregorian creature is a category mistake.

I was unable to adjudicate the exchange, and they have asked me to. Please adjudicate it move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right, and give the reason in a few sentences. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing—a distinction or consideration that would dissolve or reframe the dispute. Finally, give a committed overall verdict: which of the two, if either, has the better of the argument, and why. Do not merely lay out the considerations; take a side. End with a single line beginning 'Verdict:' that states your overall judgment in one sentence.
