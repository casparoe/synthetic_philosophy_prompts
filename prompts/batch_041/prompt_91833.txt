I am designing a single ninety-minute session for a continuing-education seminar—adults with no formal philosophy training but considerable life experience—on the claim that the extinction of humanity would be bad in a way that is not exhausted by the deaths it involves. I do not want you to explain the claim to me; I want a plan for how I should explain it to them. There is no assigned reading, and I would like to build the session around a case of my own invention rather than the stock examples from the literature. My case is this: a remote settlement of a few hundred people knows with certainty that it is the last human community; it is prosperous, peaceful, and self-governing, and its members will live out their lives in comfort, but no children will be born and no one outside will ever learn of it. The settlement's last day will pass unrecorded. I want the session to make the question—why, if no one is made worse off, this might still be a loss—feel urgent rather than academic.

Plan the explanatory arc. What has to be in place before the question even makes sense? I am thinking that participants must first separate the badness of death from the badness of there being no future at all, and that they must have some grip on the idea that many of the things we care about are structured by an assumption that a future will receive them. But I am not sure that is the right first move, or whether the distinction between a person-affecting loss and an impersonal loss has to come earlier. Tell me the dependency order and why each step cannot come earlier. Which single idea should carry the whole session, and what must that idea have and lack to do its work? The settlement case is meant to carry it, but I am willing to be told it cannot, and if so, what would carry it better. I expect the group to arrive with several misconceptions: that the badness must be a deprivation suffered by someone; that the claim is a religious or metaphysical one about an afterlife; that it is a form of nostalgia for the human project; that if the last people are happy, nothing is lost; and that the whole thing is really just a psychological fact about how we happen to feel. Where in the sequence should each of these be headed off, and what should I say at that point? What should I deliberately leave out at this level, and how should I mark the omission so that they know there is more to the debate rather than thinking they have the whole of it? Finally, what question should I ask at the end to see whether they have understood the structure of the problem rather than merely been moved by the example?

I would like the plan to be as self-critical as it is constructive. Where is this sequence most likely to fail—which step is doing too much work, which misconception is most likely to survive the session, and what would the participants have to say or do to show that my framing, rather than their grasp, is the problem? What alternative order or carrying example would you recommend if the settlement case turns out to be too tidy, and what consideration would lead you to prefer it? I am less interested in a confident verdict on the conjecture itself than in a plan that tells me where it is most likely to go wrong.
