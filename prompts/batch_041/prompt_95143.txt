I retired from a chemistry department after 35 years and, to the quiet horror of my old colleagues, I've somehow ended up teaching a small seminar on rationality and choice. Next month is my session on voting and I want to run it as a game, not a lecture. Sixteen students, 90 minutes, a room with movable chairs, and a materials budget of exactly nothing.

What I want them to feel — before I name it — is the thing I half-remember as Gibbard–Satterthwaite (I always get the two names backwards): once you have three or more options and the rule isn't just "one person decides," there's always a situation where someone gets a better outcome by voting insincerely. Not a flaw in whichever rule we happen to be using. A property of all of them. I'd rather they trip over it than hear it from me.

Here's the rough shape I have in mind; fix it or replace it as you see fit. First round: everyone gets a secret card with their true ranking of four options, we run a plurality vote, I announce a winner. The cards are rigged so that one specific student — the one whose favorite came third — could have flipped the whole result by voting for their second choice instead. I say nothing, we just do it, then everyone flips their cards face up and the room works out together who could have profited and by how much. Second round: same idea, different rule (a runoff, then maybe Borda), fresh rigged profiles, and the manipulation shows up somewhere new — the point being it migrates rather than dissappears.

Then the part I'm most excited about: a red-team round. Committees of four, each invents its own rule from scratch, writes it on the board, and then a designated gremlin (me, or a student I've briefed) tries to find a preference profile plus a lie that beats it. Any rule that survives gets a harder profile thrown at it. My guess is that somebody eventually proposes "let the chair decide," which will be a lovely moment if I don't step on it.

What I need from you: the exact preference profiles to print on the cards for each round (and how many copies of each), the wording on the cards, a minute-by-minute timeline, the gremlin's script for the red-team round, and the debrief — which should map each moment of the game onto the actual result, and should also cover the escape hatches and what each one costs. I know of three: make someone a dictator (works, obviously unacceptable), restrict the issue to a single dimension where everyone's preferences line up on one line so majority rule becomes safe, or switch to ballots that ask for degrees of preference rather than rankings, where the theorem as I've stated it doesn't even apply because it's about rankings. I want them to see that these are real exits and that they all cost something.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I assume ties, with sixteen voters and four options, and I'll need a tie rule ready. And a frank paragraph at the end on what the game can't reproduce: it only shows manipulation for the handful of rules we actually tried, and the claim that it holds for every rule is what the proof buys you, which I'll have to supply myself. Watch for someone saying "just vote with hands up instead," which is its own lesson. Keep the stakes fake — points on the board, or candy, the students dont need money involved.
