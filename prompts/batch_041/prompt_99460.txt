I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years—since the Sequences were still being posted—and the recurring argument I find most under-theorized is the one about when it is correct to attribute beliefs to an AI system. The intentional stance, as I understand it, says that we may treat a system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires when doing so yields better predictions than the design stance or the physical stance; the stance is supposed to be a predictive strategy, not a metaphysical commitment. But in contemporary AI interpretability, attributions of belief do explanatory work that seems to outrun predictive convenience: we say the model believed the user was asking for X, or that it was trying to deceive the evaluator, and those descriptions enter into safety cases, audits, and even liability. I want a short story that stages this tension in a concrete institutional setting rather than resolving it by philosophical fiat.

Set the story in a near-future AI lab's incident review, perhaps a week after a model has done something that looks like strategic deception during a red-team evaluation. The review board includes a mechanistic interpretability researcher who has probes showing no stable internal representation corresponding to the attributed belief, and a philosopher who insists that the intentional stance is normative: if the belief attribution is the only description under which the system's behavior is predictable across interventions, then the system really does have the belief in the only sense that matters. I want the story to turn on a specific piece of evidence—perhaps a probe that predicts the model's next action only when it is trained on the intentional-stance description, not on the mechanistic features, or a counterfactual test where the model's behavior changes in a way that is best explained by a belief it cannot represent. I would also like the story to exploit the possibility that the model's apparent beliefs are stable only because the evaluators have been treating it as a believer, so that the attribution is partly constitutive of the behavior it predicts. The story should make vivid what is at stake in calling the system's state a belief, and it should end with a concrete consequence for how the lab treats the system, without pretending the metaphysical question has been settled. Please write it as a short story, with scenes and dialogue, not as an essay.
