So I keep running into a thing in modelling work and I've never found a name for it. I do forecasting for a living and read econ on the side, plus enough climate and physics modelling to have noticed that the same move shows up in all of them.

Here's the move. A model states an assumption everyone agrees is false — perfect information, no frictions, agents who never die, an infinite population, a smooth surface. Poke at it and you get some version of: we aren't claiming it's true, we're claiming the model's predictions are good, or good enough, or good in the range we care about. Fine, I'll take that. But then the same false assumption turns up in the explanation. The reason the effect happens, or the reason the intervention will work, is told as a story in which the assumption is doing the causal work. So it's a fiction I'm told to ignore and at the same time the description of the mechanism I'm supposed to believe. I can't tell if that's one problem or two.

The other half of it: the status of the assumption gets settled after the fact. Prediction comes out right — it was a harmless simplification, nobody was ever claiming it was true, why are you hung up on it. Prediction comes out wrong — suddenly it's a substantive claim about the world that the data just falsified, which is interesting and publishable. Same assumption, opposite statuses, and which one it gets depends on how things turned out.

I tried to make my own cut and I don't think it works. I've been calling them load-bearing vs. decorative, with a test: swap the assumption for something more realistic, and if the answer doesn't move it was decorative, if it moves it was load-bearing. But the whole point of the move above is that the answer can move and the assumption still gets waved through as decorative, so the test just restates the problem.

For contrast, physics seems to do something different with this, though I can't articulate how. Assume a frictionless plane or an ideal gas and there's usually a story about why the missing piece is small, plus a procedure for adding it back if it isn't. That's a real defense. 'Predictions matter' is a different kind of defense, and I think it's the one that lets the flip happen.

I know Friedman has the argument about judging theories by predictions rather than by whether the assumptions are realistic, and I know there's a general distinction between simplifying something away for tractability and making a claim about what matters in the world. Neither of those is quite the thing I'm describing. There's also a cousin I half recognize — when a prediction fails you get to pick which assumption to blame — but that seems to be about where the blame goes, not about whether the assumption even counts as a claim in the first place.

So: does this have a name? If it doesn't, coin one, defined tightly enough to separate it from (a) plain simplifying assumptions, (b) the Friedman-style predictions-not-assumptions defense, and (c) just having a wrong model. And tell me whether the prediction half and the explanation half are one phenomenon or two, because I suspect they come apart — a model whose assumptions are pure fiction for prediction purposes might still be a fine explanation, or might be no explanation at all, and I can't tell which way anyone thinks it goes. I'd rather have a good carving with no name than a name that doesn't fit, so dont stop at the label.
