My reading group has been grinding through a book on weakness of will for the last few weeks — Sunday nights, somebody's kitchen, usually more wine than philosophy by nine — and last week Priya ambushed us with an argument she says she picked up in a lecture on hyperbolic discounting. The gist is that once you take discounting seriously, akrasia just dissolves: there's no such thing as acting against your own better judgment, there's only your preferences shifting as the reward gets closer. She wrote it on the whiteboard in numbered steps and I copied it down before she erased it:

(1) Weakness of will is acting against your own better judgment.
(2) Your better judgment at any given moment is just your strongest preference at that moment — what you most want, all things considered.
(3) People discount future rewards hyperbolically, so a preference between the same two rewards can flip as the sooner one approaches. (Her example: if today you're offered $100 in a week versus $120 in two weeks, most people hold out for the $120; ask the same person a week later, when it's $100 now versus $120 in a week, and most take the $100. Same two rewards, opposite choice, only "now" moved.)
(4) So at the moment you act, you prefer whatever it is you do; that's part of what makes it your action rather than something that merely happened to you.
(5) If at the moment of action you preferred A to B, then doing A wasn't against your better judgment.
(6) Therefore nothing is ever weak-willed. "Akrasia" is just our name for the fact that preferences are time-indexed, not a name for any real failure.

She added, half joking, that this is why commitment devices are rational — the sophisticated agent knows her future self's preference will flip and ties herself to the mast. But that seems to me to cut the other way: the whole point of the Ulysses contract is that you expect to act against the judgment you're acting on right now, so it looks like evidence for akrasia rather than a way of explaining it away, and she says I'm begging the question. She also insists the argument kills both the synchronic version (acting against your judgment in the moment) and the diachronic one (acting against a judgment you formed earlier and still think was right), and thats the bit I most want to resist. I want to keep (1) and (3), I'll give up almost anything else, and I've flip-flopped on this alot. So which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me? Specifically, if I keep (1) and (3), what am I forced to say about (2), and is there a principled distinction between a preference and a judgment here, or am I just insisting on the conclusion I want?
