I'm a science journalist working on a feature about self-deception -- the whole "why we lie to ourselves" thing -- and last week at a conference I watched two researchers hijack a Q&A and have it out, and now my draft is mush because I honestly cant tell who was right. Can you adjudicate the exchange move by move? I don't need a winner overall, I need to know which specific claims hold up, which don't, and where they're both missing something.

Roughly how it went. (1) He opened with: self-deception is impossible. To deceive someone you have to know the truth and intend them to end up believing something false, and the person deceived has to not know it. In the self case the deceiver and the deceived are one and the same person, so you'd need one person to both know and not know the same thing, and to be scheming to make themselves believe something they know isn't true. (2) She said that's an over-intellectualized picture of deception. You can deceive someone by leaving things out, by steering them away from the evidence; you don't need to intend to plant a specific false belief. Self-deception is just the case where wanting something to be true biases how you gather and weigh evidence -- wishful thinking that gets sustained. (3) He said: if there's no intention and no strategy then it isn't deception, it's just bias, and calling it self-deception is a flattering way of saying someone's irrational. And some cases really do look strategic: the woman who never opens the letter from the hospital and tells everyone she's probably fine, who manages to be busy the one evening the friend who'd ask the right question wants to talk. That looks like a strategy, and a strategy needs a strategist. (4) She said look at the actual psychology -- there's alot of work on this -- people avoid information all the time: they decline free tests, they don't check the accounts. That isn't the same as believing the opposite. Avoiding evidence isn't holding a false belief, and if you can't point to the false belief you haven't got a case. (5) He said fine, maybe there's no single false belief, but a kind of double bookkeeping -- one part of you that knows, another that doesn't. Yes, it sounds like a homunculus, but every account seems to need some kind of split. (6) She said a split just relocates the problem: now there's a little person in there who's both deceiver and deceived, and you've explained nothing. Better to say "self-deception" isn't a description of a mechanism at all but a way we interpret someone, and then "is it possible" is malformed -- like asking whether someone can be self-murderous.

Then the exchange I actually need for the piece, over one case: a man whose doctor told him a scan was borderline and needs a follow-up, who for eight months has told his family he's basically fine while quietly writing a will and settling his affairs. She said that's the tell -- the behavior tracks the truth, so he knows, and "basically fine" is just what he says out loud. He said or "basically fine" is what he believes whenever he isn't thinking about it, and the will-writing is a different part of the mind doing its own thing, which isn't one person deceiving another, it's just a person being a mess -- which was his point all along. She said that's a strange thing to insist on, since nobody thinks "self-taught" or "self-loathing" means there are two people. He said the disanalogy is that teaching and loathing don't require the teacher to know that the student doesn't know.

So: who's right about what? Rate each of the six moves, tell me which disputes are really just about what word we use, and say what the best version of the concept is and whether it still leaves anything in the ordinary cases unexplained -- the affair that "isn't really a betrayal," the parent who "has no idea" their kid is in trouble. My editor wants this Friday and she'll kill any sentence that hedges, so where the honest answer is "it depends what you mean," tell me what we should mean.
