A colleague and I have been circling the same disagreement about constitutional entrenchment for two years and neither of us has moved. His position: entrenchment is collective self-binding, structurally the same as a Ulysses contract and therefore entirely consistent with democratic self-rule — the people who bind themselves at one time are the same people later, and if they may govern themselves they may govern their future selves. Mine: a Ulysses contract binds a single person who remains the author of the binding, whereas a supermajority rule gives the past a weighted veto over the present, which is not self-rule but rule by the dead; and even Ulysses needed a crew to tie him, so someone is always deciding for someone else. We accept the same historical cases and read them in opposite directions, and neither of us has a reply that the other concedes. What pushed me toward thinking we have misdescribed our own dispute is a rule I encountered recently: a constitutional cap on public borrowing that a simple majority may tighten but only a two-thirds majority may relax. My hypothesis is that we are not disagreeing about democracy at all but about which failure of the will entrenchment is meant to cure — I have in mind binding against panic or appetite, where the later self would consent, he has in mind binding against a later considered judgment, where it would not — so that neither of our arguments reaches the other's case. Is that the right diagnosis, or is the real disagreement something else: a disagreement about what counts as an agent, about the point of a constitution, or two questions under one word? I don't want a verdict on whether entrenchment is legitimate, only a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, and an honest account of what, given that diagnosis, would count as progress between us.
