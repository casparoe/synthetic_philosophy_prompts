I retired from a chemistry lab about six years ago and have been teaching myself philosophy ever since, mostly in a reading group at the public library where I'm the only one who never took a course. A friend who did a PhD in economics sent me a long essay about a demon called Moloch that supposedly eats the world by getting everybody to compete with everybody, and I liked it, but I also got suspicious — the same week our group had been chewing on whether an argument can be sound and still prove so much that something in it has to be wrong. So I wrote the Moloch argument out in my own words to see if it holds, and now I can't find the flaw. Here it is, numbered: (1) Wherever several parties want the same scarce thing and each does better by taking more, cutting deeper, or moving first, whoever does that most will win out. (2) So such situations drift toward the outcome nobody wanted, which my friend calls a trap. (3) Restraint in these situations doesn't come from nowhere — somebody has to enforce it, a state or a guild or a norm backed by punishment. (4) But whoever enforces is itself a party to some competition, against other enforcers or against the temptation to quietly stop enforcing while still collecting the benefits. (5) So (1) and (2) apply to the enforcement arrangement as well, and then to whatever enforces that, and so on. (6) Since there is no last level, there is no level at which the drift stops. (7) Therefore every cooperative arrangement — a legal system, a shared technical standard, the norms of scientific citation, a commons — is only a pause, and the pause ends either because it rots from inside or because something less restrained outcompetes it. (8) So the only real question about any reform is which trap you would rather live in, and the idea of "solving" one of these problems is a category mistake.

That definately can't be right, and not just because it's bleak. It seems to prove too much in two directions at once. It says restraint never lasts, but the world is stuffed with restraint that has lasted — I'm writing this in a house whose title the state will defend, in a language whose rules nobody enforces with a stick, after a morning spent citing three papers in a manuscript I had no obligation to cite at all. And if it were true all the way down, I don't see how the first arrangement ever got off the ground, since every cooperative thing needs something cooperative already in place to build on. So one of those steps is doing something dishonest and I can't see which one. I'd rather not give up (1), and I'm not willing to give up the thought that some reforms actually work, so I suspect the trouble is somewhere in (3) through (6), which is unfortunately the part I understand least.

You're a philosopher who works on rational choice and collective action, so please treat this as a real argument and not a slogan. Start by brainstorming the two or three most plausible places it goes wrong — including the possibility that it's perfectly valid and that one of the premises simply has to go — then commit to a diagnosis. Tell me which step I should reject, why it looked fine when I wrote it down, and what rejecting it costs: what I lose, what I have to put in its place, and whether the replacement still leaves the argument's real insight intact, that some bad outcomes are nobody's choice. If two of the steps are being read together in a way that smuggles something in — I have a vague sense that a trap and a race to the bottom aren't quite the same animal, and that the words "temporary" and "no last level" are doing more work than they can carry — say so plainly and show me where the seam is. Please don't hand me a reading list, I can't look anything up from here anyway. I just want to know where to put the knife.
