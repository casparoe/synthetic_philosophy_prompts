I want an audit of a set of positions I hold in the philosophy of moral responsibility. They are stated below as I would defend them in a seminar, and I want to know which of them can be held together, which cannot, and at what cost.

1. I accept the Basic Argument, in Galen Strawson's sense. Nothing can be the cause of itself; nothing is causa sui. You do what you do because of the way you are, so responsibility for what you do would require responsibility for the way you are, at least in certain crucial mental respects; but you cannot be responsible for the way you are, since any attempt to trace that responsibility back to its source either loops or regresses without end. The regress never terminates in the agent, so no one is ever ultimately morally responsible for anything.

2. I do not take the Basic Argument to depend on determinism. It goes through just as well on an indeterministic picture of choice, since indeterminism supplies chance rather than authorship. The argument is therefore not a covert argument for compatibilism, and I do not offer it as one.

3. I am nevertheless a compatibilist about the kind of responsibility that our practices actually invoke. What makes someone an appropriate target of blame and praise, in the ordinary run of things, is not ultimate self-creation but a certain responsiveness to reasons and the expression of a quality of will; agents who have that possess everything our blaming and praising practices require.

4. Even so, I think the loss of the desert-entailing notion is real and worth mourning. Something that was supposed to be there is not there, and saying this is not nostalgia and not a matter of relabeling.

5. I accept the distinction between attributability and accountability. An action can be attributable to an agent as an expression of what she values and of how she regards others without her being accountable in the sense that would license resentment, guilt, or punishment as desert.

6. I hold that the question whether we are really morally responsible is, in the end, the wrong question. Responsibility is a status internal to practices of holding one another to account; the demand for a metaphysical vindication of those practices, of the kind the Basic Argument supplies or refuses, rests on a mistake about what sort of claim is in play.

7. I think the ordinary, unphilosophical conception of responsibility is desert-entailing, and that most people who are not philosophers are committed to the idea that responsibility requires an ultimate source. If that is right, compatibilism is a revisionary position rather than a description of what we already believe.

What I want is an audit, not an exposition. Which of these commitments pull against each other? For each pair in genuine tension, tell me how severe the tension is and what the cheapest repair would be: which commitment has to weaken or go, and what it costs to give it up. I expect that some apparent tensions are not real, and I would rather you say so explicitly than flag everything as a problem. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, then tell me whether any single revision would resolve more than one of them, and whether that revision is worth its price. Do not soften the audit by substituting the most defensible version of each commitment for the version I have stated; tell me what the commitments as stated commit me to.

One further requirement. Separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as though it were the consensus. In particular, do not assume that any contested reading of Strawson is common ground, for example about whether he intends his argument to be neutral between compatibilism and incompatibilism, about whether he regards the desert-entailing notion as coherent but uninstantiated, or about whether he thinks the reactive attitudes can be shed. Where your audit depends on a contested interpretation, say so plainly.
