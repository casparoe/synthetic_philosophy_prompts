I spend an unreasonable amount of time in alignment-adjacent comment sections, and I am drafting a post arguing that the familiar contrast between influence-seeking systems and slowly drifting values is doing less work than its users think. My complaint is that the two labels are keyed to two different counterfactuals rather than to two different mechanisms. Influence-seeking is normally diagnosed by varying the environment while holding the objective fixed: if the system still reaches for the levers, we call it power-seeking. Drift is diagnosed by the mirror-image test: hold the environment fixed and ask whether what the system is now pursuing is what it was pursuing before; if not, we say its values have moved. My suspicion is that these two tests can return opposite verdicts about one and the same system, and that this is not a rare pathology but the ordinary case.

Give me an example of a conceptual issue in that neighbourhood, constructed rather than recalled. I want a single, self-contained case, described only in terms of what an observer could actually record — behaviour, outputs, resource flows, changes in what the system does when nothing in its situation has changed — with no appeal to what we assume is going on inside it. In the case I have in mind, a careful analyst applying the environment-variation test should conclude that the system has not drifted, while an equally careful analyst applying the value-variation test should conclude that it is not seeking influence, or the reverse; either direction will do. The example must make plain which test fires and why, and it must not be settled by stipulating a hidden objective, since the hidden objective is precisely what is in dispute.

A few constraints. Keep the case small-scale and plausible for a system with no general-purpose agency — a recommender, a forecasting pipeline, a procurement routine, an institution's internal process, something of that order — and do not give me the standard story about a system that resists shutdown or escapes a sandbox. Set out which observations would separate the two readings and which would leave them indistinguishable, and say honestly whether the case is one in which the labels are merely underdetermined by the available evidence or one in which they are genuinely inconsistent with each other. No citations, no reading list: I want the argument to stand on the case alone.

Six hundred to eight hundred words, in British English throughout. Finish with a final line of exactly the form \answer{...} containing one of the four labels influence-seeking, value drift, both, or neither, indicating which mechanism the case's central trajectory actually instantiates once your own analysis is complete.
