I have been trying to formulate a coherent ethic of insect killing, and my considered judgments do not fit any of the standard positions. I do not believe that insects are sentient in any robust sense. Yet some acts of insect killing seem to me clearly permissible and others clearly wrong, and the pattern is not captured by the amount of suffering caused or by the number of insects killed. I would like a principle that fits the following verdicts, or an explanation of which verdicts I should abandon.

Spraying a wasp nest under the eaves when a member of the household is allergic to stings: permissible.
Spraying a wasp nest at a picnic site because the wasps are a nuisance: permissible.
Fogging a neighborhood for adult mosquitoes during a dengue outbreak: permissible.
Fogging the same neighborhood for nuisance mosquitoes in an ordinary summer, with no disease present: impermissible.
Applying a broad-spectrum lawn insecticide that will kill bees, butterflies, and ground beetles in order to suppress ants: impermissible.
Using a targeted bait station for the same ants: permissible.
Killing a single cockroach that has wandered into the kitchen: permissible.
Killing a spider in the bathroom because one finds it frightening: impermissible.
Using a systemic insecticide seed treatment prophylactically on a crop, with no evidence of pest pressure: impermissible.
Releasing an insecticide into a pond to eradicate an invasive insect that is driving an endangered fish toward extinction: permissible.
Sleeping under an insecticide-treated bed net: permissible.
Killing a tick that has attached to one's arm: permissible.
Killing a honeybee that has just stung one: impermissible, even though the bee is already dying from the sting and the act therefore accomplishes nothing protective.

The tentative principle I have been working with is this: an act of insect killing is permissible when it is necessary to protect a substantial human or ecological interest, the method is the least harmful effective one available, and the collateral damage to non-target organisms is proportionate to the interest protected; it is impermissible when the interest is mere convenience or aesthetic preference, or when the method imposes serious collateral harm that a less damaging alternative could avoid.

This principle gets some of the verdicts but not all. It struggles with two pairs in particular. First, I judge the wasp at the picnic permissible and the spider in the bathroom impermissible, though both are cases of killing for mere comfort and neither animal poses a substantial threat. Second, I judge killing the tick permissible and killing the honeybee impermissible, though both have just harmed me, and the bee is already dying from the sting, so the act accomplishes nothing protective. I suspect the principle needs a further distinction, perhaps between killing as a means to an end and killing as an expressive act, or between killing that is part of a practice of pest management and killing that is gratuitous. I also wonder whether my verdicts are tracking something about the social organization of the insect: a wasp colony is a collective that can be treated as a single adversary, while a spider is a solitary creature that cannot, and perhaps that difference explains why the one killing feels like pest control and the other like cruelty.

I would like you to do three things. First, state the principle or small set of principles that best captures these verdicts, precisely, and show which verdict motivates each clause. Second, identify the verdicts that the principle cannot accommodate and say which ones I should give up. Third, apply the principle to two cases I have not listed: the killing of silkworms in the production of silk, and the killing of insects in scientific research on insect cognition. In each case, say what the principle implies and whether that implication is a problem for the principle.

A further complication, which may or may not matter: I do not think the moral status of an insect is zero, but I also do not think it is comparable to that of a mammal. I have been calling this the problem of the moral threshold — the idea that there may be a level of moral status below which killing is permissible for almost any reason, but above which it requires justification, and I cannot say where the threshold lies or whether it is a threshold at all rather than a continuum. If your principle depends on a threshold, say where it puts it and why; if it does not, say how it avoids needing one.
