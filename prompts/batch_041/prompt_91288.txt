I'm the TA for a second-year philosophy of science course — 16 students, one 75-minute seminar slot a week, and we've just finished the realism/anti-realism unit. The problem is that every year they nod along through the readings and then write essays saying that of course we should believe our best theories because they work, and of course a theory can always be patched up to fit the data, and neither claim seems to cost them anything. I want them to actually feel the pinch, so I want to run something hands-on rather than another debate, and I'd like you to build it out properly for me.

Here's what I've been kicking around. I prepare a sealed opaque box — or two of them — with a few inputs (a switch, a dial, a couple of sockets) and one output (an LED, a buzzer, whatever), and I wire it so that the behaviour is compatible with several quite different internal mechanisms; you tell me what's cheap and reliable to build, and whether it's easier to do this as a black-box program on a laptop or even as a person playing oracle behind a screen who follows a hidden rule and only ever reports outputs. Groups get a limited number of queries, then have to write on a card what they think is inside and why. First beat: two groups with the same evidence end up with incompatible stories about the hidden machinery, and neither can be shown wrong by anything they're allowed to observe. Second beat: I let them have a fresh batch of inputs, and both theories predict it identically, so the "we just need more data" escape hatch closes in their hands. Third beat, the one I care about most: I produce a single anomalous reading and let each group discover that they can keep their theory alive indefinitely by fiddling with some auxiliary assumption they'd never even stated out loud — oh, the delay must be a sticky gear, oh, the sensor must be drifting — and both groups can keep doing this forever without ever contradicting their data. I want them to walk out feeling the evidence is doing less work than they assumed, without me having said so. If it helps, I'm happy to build two boxes that behave identically but are wired differently, and never let anyone open either one; if there's a version where the boxes turn out to be identical inside and the groups' confident divergence is the punchline, I'm open to that too.

What I need from you is the runnable package: materials and prep (cheap, I've got maybe £30 and a colleague who can solder), the rules and turn structure, how many queries each group gets, how to stop the second group overhearing the first group's theory, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief. In the debrief I want you to map each beat onto the real philosophical material, including bits we don't cover in lecture: the difference between the kind of underdetermination that a bit more evidence could in principle dissolve and the kind no possible evidence could ever touch; the holism point that you never test a hypothesis on its own and can always rescue the core by blaming an auxiliary; and the constructive-empiricist move of saying the aim of science is empirical adequacy rather than truth, which should now look like a live option rather than a cop-out. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm betting on at least one group trying to prise the box open, and one smart-arse announcing that the whole thing is rigged — and how to handle both without killing the mood. Then be honest about what the activity can't do. I assume it can't reproduce the realist's best argument, that the predictive success of science would be a miracle if the unobservables weren't roughly as described, and it can't reproduce the fact that in real science the rival theories are mathematically deep and the choice between them turns on theoretical virtues like unification and simplicity rather than on a switch and an LED. Last thing: if there's a way to fold in the historical point that our predecessors' mistake was usually not choosing badly among the options in front of them but failing to conceive of the option that turned out to be right, I'd love them to feel that too — but don't let them hear the word underdetermination until the debrief, and no lecture up front, they should get there themselves.
