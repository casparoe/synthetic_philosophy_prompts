I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and I got voluntold onto our engineering standards council — nine people, we meet every other Thursday, mostly we fight about date formats. I have to write the memo that goes out before next week's meeting and I'm out of my depth on the part of this that isn't about JSON.

The case: for about six years we've had an unwritten convention in all our internal HTTP APIs. If a field is optional and you leave it out of a PATCH body, that means don't change it. Nobody ever wrote this down. It's baked into our client libs, our mock server, our codegen templates, our test fixtures, and about forty services, and you learn it by breaking something. Last month a team that came in through an acquisition shipped a client that treats an omitted field as clear this value, because that's what they're used to. A customer's saved configuration got silently wiped. No money lost, nobody hurt, but there's a postmortem and people are mad at the new team, and one manager wants the incident on their record, which sits badly with me because the rule they broke doesn't exist on paper anywhere.

Options on the table. (1) Keep the convention, document it, and add validation so that a request meaning clear has to say so explicitly, with a null or a separate update-mask field. Cheapish, but it still leaves silence-means-keep as the rule, and the new team's lead keeps saying that exact pattern — silence standing in for a real decision — is how the wipe happened and is going to bite us somewhere worse than config someday. (2) Move everyone to explicit updates: nothing you send is ever a default, migration across forty services. Cleanest, and what most of us would pick if we were starting today, but a huge lift, and every service that lags is now wrong in a new way. (3) Let teams choose and keep a registry of which endpoints use which rule. Everyone hates it; the chair is pushing it as the compromise.

The part I actually need help with. Someone on the council said this is a David Lewis thing and now I can't stop thinking about it. My understanding, from a podcast so grain of salt: a convention is a regularity that everyone follows in a situation that keeps recurring, where everyone expects everyone else to follow it, everyone is happy to keep following it as long as the others do, and all of that is common knowledge — and crucially the regularity is arbitrary, in that a different one would have worked just as well and ours is only right because it's the one everybody expects. Is that a fair summary, and does it actually fit us, or are we more like a bunch of people with the same habit? Somebody else said a convention is different from a norm, because with a norm you get blamed for deviating even when it costs you nothing, whereas with a convention you just get burned by the mismatch. If that distinction is real it definately matters for the memo, because the second we put the new team's name in a postmortem and make an example of them I think we've quietly turned one into the other, and I'd like to know whether that's a fair thing to say in writing.

Then the practical half. If we do change the rule, what makes the new one take? Technically better doesn't seem like enough when forty services and everyone's muscle memory are on the other side. Is there something about what makes a new convention focal — for instance, if we adopt the one the wider ecosystem outside the company already uses, does that help it stick, or does it just create a second seam? Someone compared this whole thing to QWERTY and I have no idea if that analogy is even accurate.

What I need from you: the memo to circulate before Thursday. A clear recommendation with a time frame, the reasoning, and a genuinely fair version of the case for whichever option I'm arguing against — I don't want a memo that pretends option 3 has nothing going for it, since probably half the council votes for it. Plus a short section on the blame question: do we do anything about the new team, and does the fact that the convention was never written down mean nobody can fairly be held to it? I don't think that follows but I can't explain why, and that's sort of the whole problem. Write it so the two people on the council who will hate it can still act on it.
