For my school's philosophy club I've been asked to write a short dialogue — around 700 words — between two equally sharp people who disagree about whether every truth could in principle be known. One takes the anti-realist line: a statement is true only if it could, in principle, be verified or known by someone, so no truth lies permanently beyond anyone's reach. The other thinks that line destroys itself, because of a puzzle: take some truth that as a matter of fact nobody knows; by the anti-realist's principle it must be knowable; but if it were known, it would no longer be true that nobody knows it, so the combined claim — that truth, and that nobody knows it — could never be known, which contradicts the principle. The anti-realist seems pushed from the claim that every truth is knowable to the far stronger claim that every truth is actually known.

I don't want the dialogue to stop there or to be a recitation of that puzzle. I want the anti-realist to push back before conceding anything: to ask whether the puzzle turns on something peculiar about how knowledge behaves when it is applied to a combination of claims, or on the exact way the principle was stated, and to offer a repair — narrowing the principle so that it covers only truths that would be left untouched by being known, rather than truths that knowing would somehow consume. Then I want the other character to test that repair on a case: a truth about something long past, such as how many people were in a particular room on a day for which no record survives, where learning the answer changes nothing about the fact itself and only adds the fact that someone now knows it. Let each grant what is right in the other's point — the realist should admit the repair has real force, the anti-realist should admit what is genuinely worrying — and end with the two of them naming exactly what their disagreement has come to rest on: knowledge, truth, or what it is for a statement to have a truth-value at all. Neither needs to win.
