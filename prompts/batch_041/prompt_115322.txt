I work on the internal workings of trained neural networks, and I keep encountering a problem for which I have no name. It is this. I arrive at an account of how the network produces some behaviour: certain units respond to one feature, pass their output to a second set of units, which suppresses a third. Then I find inputs on which the behaviour is produced, but not by the route I described — some other route did the work. My instinct is to say that a different mechanism ran on those inputs. But whether I can say this turns out to depend on the grain of the account I already had. If the account was coarse, the second route lies inside it, and I say that the same mechanism ran under unusual conditions. If the account was fine, the second route lies outside it, and I say that a different mechanism ran. The network, the two inputs, and the two routes are held fixed; only the grain of my description has changed, and the verdict changes with it. And I fixed the grain before I knew the second route existed, usually for reasons internal to the first case — because that was the coarseness at which I could say anything at all.

I do not believe this is peculiar to my subject. A pharmacologist will say that a drug acts by its intended route when that route is the one named in advance, and will call an equally regular side-route an off-target effect; whether the side-route counts as part of the mechanism of action or as a departure from it appears to be settled by what was named beforehand. Biologists seem to meet the same thing when they ask whether two tissues use one pathway or two.

What I want to know is whether there is an established name for this, and if there is, which name belongs to it. I assume there are relatives: I know of a literature on how the boundaries of a mechanism are to be drawn, and of a distinction between sharing a structure and sharing a function, and I have a vague sense that there is a parallel difficulty in statistics about the class to which a case is assigned. But I do not want to be handed the nearest relative and told that it is the same thing. What I am asking about is narrower: the dependence of the one-mechanism-or-two verdict on the grain of a description that was fixed before the comparison was made, and often for reasons that had nothing to do with the comparison. I take it that this is not the ordinary case in which a single mechanism runs with different settings, nor the case in which two processes plainly share a part; the trouble is with the verdict itself, which seems to be a fact about my description rather than about the system. Please say how the thing I have described differs from each relative you consider, and which, if any, fits best; say whether it is a distinct problem or a familiar one seen from a new side; and if it has no name, give it one, define it, and tell me which cases fall on the unclear side of the definition.
