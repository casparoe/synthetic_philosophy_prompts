I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science preparing the discussion section of a seminar paper on the problem of old evidence: the puzzle that arises when a hypothesis appears to be confirmed by a fact that the relevant community already knew and believed before the hypothesis was even formulated, so that conditionalizing on the fact cannot raise its probability. I want to organize the space of responses not by surveying what everyone has said but by fixing a set of adequacy conditions and asking which family of responses best meets them. The conditions I've settled on are these. First, a response should be usable in ordinary scientific practice: it should tell a working scientist what to do with an old fact, not merely redefine a technical term. Second, it should not make the evidential force of a fact depend on arbitrary facts about the order or timing in which an agent happened to learn things. Third, it should explain why old evidence is sometimes genuinely confirming without licensing the absurd result that any hypothesis draws support from every known fact it happens to entail. Fourth, it should handle both the case of a single agent updating and the case of a community choosing between rival theories, where the relevant fact is old for everyone. The classic illustration, if one is needed, is the anomalous precession of Mercury, long known before general relativity was formulated.

I've sketched four strategies for dealing with the problem and would like them ranked. The first is counterfactual reconstruction: treat the agent as though they had never learned the old fact, and assess the fact's impact in that hypothetical epistemic state. The second is prior-correction: hold that the agent's prior probability for the hypothesis was simply too low, and read the apparent boost from old evidence as a signal that the prior needs revising, after which ordinary conditionalization does all the work. The third is counterfactual-relevance redefinition: redefine confirmation so that a fact confirms a hypothesis when the agent's credence would have been lower had they not believed the fact, regardless of whether their actual credence changes upon considering it. The fourth is the explanatory turn: concede that old evidence cannot confirm in the strict probabilistic sense, and argue that theory choice in such cases is a matter of inference to the best explanation, which is not a matter of conditionalization at all.

Rank these four from strongest to weakest, grouped into tiers, by how well they satisfy my four adequacy conditions taken together, and be explicit about where each one fails, since I suspect that no strategy satisfies all four and I want to know which condition each one sacrifices. Tell me which single strategy you would defend if you had to stake the paper on one, and why. Finally, tell me whether these are the right conditions to impose in the first place; if you think one of my four is misconceived, say so and say what should replace it. I want a committed answer — a definite ranking and a definite recommendation, with reasons — rather than a neutral survey of options. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write an email to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
