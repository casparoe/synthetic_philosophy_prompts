I'm trying to figure out what a good theory of "assigning probabilities when you have no information" would even have to look like, before I go read anyone's actual proposal. So skip the scorecard for now — I want the requirements list first, and I want the weight on the requirements themselves, not on how the famous proposals fare against them.

Here's the setup so you know the target. There's a family of puzzles where you genuinely have no info about some quantity, you invoke some symmetry or equal-ignorance move, and you get different answers depending on how you set the problem up. Bertrand's chords on a circle is the famous one, and the cube-factory / unknown-scale case is the other one everyone trots out. I want the spec for any theory that claims to actually resolve these: what has to be true of it for it to count as a real solution and not just a magic trick that happens to work on the standard examples.

Things I want on the list, each with the case that motivates it:

- it has to give a definite answer to all the classic puzzles (say which of them is the genuinely hard one, not just the first one people mention)
- it has to survive reparametrization: if I describe an unknown coin by its bias p versus its odds p/(1-p), or describe a length as L versus log L, the theory either gives the same answer or has a principled story about why one description is the privileged one. Tell me which of those two it has to do.
- it can't assign probability 0 or 1 to anything that isn't logically impossible, otherwise one observation wrecks it forever
- it has to be usable: a prior that can't be updated coherently, or that leaves you open to a sure-loss betting scheme, is out
- it has to say what "no information" is actually supposed to mean, since that phrase is doing all the work
- the distinctions it can't collapse: between a probability over an unknown parameter and a probability over an observable outcome; between two agents whose information is different but symmetric; between a finite symmetry group and a continuous one. Say which of these distinctions the naive version of the theory blurs.

Then two cases I made up — tell me whether your requirements handle them:

(1) nesting test: take any ignorance problem, split one of the outcomes into two sub-cases and declare the prior mass split evenly between them, then ask the original question again. Should the answer be unchanged? Is that a requirement or a trap?

(2) coarse-graining test: suppose I can only distinguish a parameter to within some resolution (I bucket the real line into intervals), assign a prior over buckets, then refine the buckets. Any adequate theory should either be stable under refining the partition or be honest that the answer is relative to the partition. Which is it, and is that relativity a defect or a feature?

Also, what do you do when the symmetry group is continuous — translation on the whole line, scale on the positive reals? The naive flat or invariant measure is infinite there, so the honest candidate is an improper prior. Is an improper prior allowed on your list, or does "must be a genuine probability distribution" kill it? If it's killed, does anything survive? And don't just say "use maximum entropy" as if that settles it — I want to know entropy over what, and why that choice isn't itself an arbitrary parametrization.

Finally: I suspect at least two of the requirements above can't both be met at once — invariance under the natural symmetry group versus invariance under reparametrization, something like that. Tell me which pair conflicts, and whether the conflict is fatal or just tells us the theory has to be more careful about what counts as a natural parametrization. That conflict is the part I most want to see, so don't bury it.
