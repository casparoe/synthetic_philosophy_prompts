I run a biweekly reading group that mixes philosophy and cognitive-science graduate students, and I have been handed one ninety-minute session on self-deception — specifically on the question whether the phenomenon is even coherent, given that to deceive someone normally means getting them to believe what you take to be false. I do not want the topic explained to me; I want a plan for teaching it. Tell me the order the pieces must come in and why each depends on the one before it: the everyday datum that there is something here to explain, the paradox itself, and then the candidate resolutions — a divided mind in which one subsystem hides a truth from another, a temporal story in which the belief is somehow both acquired and disowned, and a deflationary story on which no belief is ever really formed and the agent only steers attention away from evidence or half-pretends. Tell me which single case should carry the whole session and what it must have and lack. I had planned to use the standard jealous-partner case, but I suspect it quietly assumes that the agent already holds the belief she is supposed to be hiding, which would make the paradox look like a trick of description, so say whether it can be repaired or should be dropped. Then give me the misconceptions to expect, in the order the group will actually hit them — that self-deception is just lying to yourself, that it requires two people or a little agent inside, that it is merely weakness of will or wishful thinking under a fancier name — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, including how to keep the deflationary resolution from collapsing the whole topic into triviality. Close with one question I can put to the group at the end that would reveal whether they have understood why this is a genuine puzzle rather than a curiosity of vocabulary. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so openly. No readings or citations — I want the teaching plan, not the bibliography.
