You are a social theorist who also designs classroom simulations, and I need you to build me one. I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on nationalism and historical sociology — eighteen students, one ninety-minute session — and I want an activity that makes them feel the force of the modernist claim that the nation and its ancient heritage are recent products rather than survivals. Work carefully before you commit: sketch two candidate structures, say what each would make the students do, and then develop the better one in full.

Here is the difficulty I keep hitting. The obvious exercise — hand each group a made-up country and have them invent a flag, a festival and a founding myth — teaches nothing, because inventing a tradition on command is trivially easy and the students know perfectly well that is what they are doing. What I want is almost the reverse: I want them to catch themselves conferring authenticity, and to find the judgment surviving the knowledge that it is unfounded. So build the activity around two hinges that I care about more than the textbook version of the thesis.

The first is the circularity of the recognition criteria. The marks by which anyone identifies a tradition as old — formalized procedure, a fixed place in the calendar, emotional grip, embeddedness in a landscape and a dialect, the felt sense that this is simply what we have always done — are exactly the marks that deliberate invention is designed to manufacture. Age is therefore not a property detected in the practice but a verdict a community confers on it afterwards. Arrange for the students to make confident, numerical age estimates, using precisely those marks, about practices that were manufactured minutes earlier in the same room, and to notice the estimate outliving its justification.

The second is that the machinery of authentication belongs to the phenomenon. The assessors, the registry, the certificate, the archive are as modern as the traditions they certify. Give the activity a moment where the students' own apparatus for adjudicating authenticity is turned back on itself.

So the design must require the group to produce a tradition and then be unable to distinguish their own artifact from an inherited one, and the depth of the nation must be something they manufacture in the session rather than something I announce. Make the nation fictional, so that nothing rides on getting real history right; but be explicit in the debrief that a fictional demonstration is not evidence, and state what it can and cannot show.

Specify: the setting and the number and composition of the groups; the materials and any props; the roles, including at least one participant who plays registrar or archivist and whose real function becomes clear only late; the rules and the scoring or stakes, which should be small and symbolic but real enough that losing stings; a minute-by-minute timeline; where the revelation falls and how to time it; and the debrief. The debrief should map the moments of the activity onto the parts of the thesis they are meant to carry: the instability of the distinction between an invented tradition and a genuine custom, with the reminder that invented does not mean fabricated from nothing, since invention works by recombining older materials; the community as something performed by strangers who will never meet; and the retroactive manufacture of national depth. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover, and be honest about which feature of the thesis the activity cannot reproduce and that I shall have to supply myself in words.

One further thing about the debrief. Separate what is actually settled from what is genuinely disputed among people who work on this, and do not present a contested position as the consensus. In particular, do not let it come across as though the modern provenance of nations were a settled result rather than one side of a live disagreement, and flag the places where the simulation's vividness is likely to push students toward a stronger conclusion than the evidence supports. I do not want them leaving as debunkers who think that because a tradition can be assembled in an hour it must be false; I want them unable to trust the feeling of antiquity while still seeing why the feeling does real social work. No bibliography or reading list — assume I know the literature; just tell me where the fault lines are.
