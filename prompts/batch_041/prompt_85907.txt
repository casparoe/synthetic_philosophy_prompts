I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a long piece on identity in which I have to explain to readers who have never opened a metaphysics book why anyone would care about the two-iron-spheres case Max Black made famous in the 1950s: a universe containing nothing but two spheres of pure iron, same diameter, same colour, same temperature, the same distance apart from each other and from anything else there is. The point, as I understand it, is that every fact I can state about one sphere is a fact about the other — whatever diameter I give one, the other has it; the gap between them is mutual; even the relational facts come in pairs, so that anything I could say about the sphere at one end of the gap I could say, with the same words, about the sphere at the other. That leaves two ways to go, and I keep changing my mind about which is the honest one. Either there is a difference between them that no description could ever capture, a difference in which one each is rather than in how either is, or else my description is a description of one situation that I am redundantly reading twice, and the apparent two-ness is an artefact of my bookkeeping. A friend who defends the principle says the spheres are distinguishable after all once you notice that each occupies its own region of space; another friend says that is cheating, because the two regions differ from one another in exactly the way the spheres do, and a physicist friend insists the whole dispute is the same as the one about whether a symmetry-related solution of a physical theory is a genuinely different possibility or the same possibility described twice. I would like a poem that lets a reader feel all of that, and since the dispute is about whether saying the same thing twice makes two things or one, I think the poem should be a symmetry rather than an argument.

So: sixteen lines, in two halves of eight, with the second half repeating the first half's lines in reverse order, so that the poem is its own mirror — the ninth line is the eighth, the tenth is the seventh, and the last line is the first. The two central lines will therefore be identical and adjacent, the one place in the poem where the same thing is said twice in a row, and I want that doubled centre to be the hinge of the whole thing: it should be where the reader is made to wonder whether she has just read one line twice or two lines that happen to agree, and the poem must not tell her which. No line may contain anything — a name, an ordinal, "this one", "the nearer", "the other" — that would let a reader pin a line to one sphere rather than to the other; the poem has to be structurally incapable of picking one out, which means the cheap escape (but of course they differ: one of them is that one) should be available inside the poem and should be visible as an escape rather than as a solution. Beyond that, write a real poem: it should hold up for a reader who has never heard of any of this, and it should be recognisably about the two spheres, and about the oddity that a difference in the world and a difference only in our labels can be indistinguishable from inside the world, to a reader who has. Please keep the words "identity", "indiscernible" and "haecceity" out of it; the reader should be made to feel the thing rather than read the terminology. If the reversal forces you to change punctuation or line breaks to keep the sense, do it, but add a short note afterwards saying what you changed and what the constraint cost you. If you title it, the title must not break the symmetry by taking a side.
