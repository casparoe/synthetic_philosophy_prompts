I'm a fourth-year PhD student working on aesthetics and the epistemology of testimony, and I've somehow ended up as the graduate student member of my university art museum's acquisitions committee, which is turning out to be alot more philosophically loaded than I expected. I have to write the committee's opinion before our meeting on the 14th and I'd rather not do it alone. Could you act as a philosopher of art who knows the aesthetics-and-testimony debates well, start by sketching the positions for me (no citations needed, just the map), and then draft the memo itself? Think carefully about the strongest version of the view you end up rejecting, because the person who holds it here is not a fool.

Here's the case. A donor has offered us a large site-specific installation — it gets built into the atrium, it can't be shown anywhere else, and there is no version of it we could go visit; it's a sound-and-light thing that only exists in that room. The offer lapses in about eight weeks and the acquisition line lapses at the end of the fiscal year, so "we'll revisit next year" means no. Nobody on the committee has experienced the piece. The only person who has is our curator, who spent two days with the artist and came back saying it's the best thing she's seen in a decade, that it's quiet and enormous and does something to you that she can't quite describe. The chair wants to accept on her word. Our sculptor member objects that this is precisely what you can't do: you can take a conservator's word about a painting's condition, or a registrar's about provenance, because those are facts, but "it's good" is not a fact you can outsource — and she wants her dissent in the minutes, because she says a public body certifying that a work is worth the public's money and attention is making a judgment, and not one of us is in a position to make it. There's also a middle option on the table: take it as a one-year loan and decide later, though she says a loan is just the judgment in disguise, since we'd still be lending the museum's name to it. I half-agree with her, but the chair's reply also seems right — institutions can't only acquire what every trustee has personally seen, and the alternative is we acquire nothing and the piece ends up in a corporate lobby, which seems worse. A friend in my department keeps calling the thing the acquaintance principle, roughly that you can't get aesthetic knowledge at second hand, and I'm not sure how much of the pessimism I actually buy.

What I want is a short written opinion — say 800 to 1200 words, in the voice of the committee, something people can argue with rather than a blog post — recommending one concrete course of action with a deadline attached, giving the reasoning, and dealing honestly with the strongest case against the recommendation. Please actually engage, rather than gesturing at: whether there's a real difference between the curator's testimony about the work's condition and art-historical significance and her testimony that it's good; whether "we believe her" and "we judge it good" come apart, and what that means for what we're entitled to put in writing; whether a group can have the kind of first-hand contact the pessimistic view demands, or whether institutions just are chains of deference and always have been; and something I keep chewing on, that "good" feels different from "garish" or "delicate" in a way that might matter here, since maybe you can be told the first but not the second. And somewhere in there, please say what you think the right standing policy is for a body like ours, since we'll be doing this again in the spring.
