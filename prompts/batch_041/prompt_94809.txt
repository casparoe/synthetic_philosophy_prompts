I've been following the general debate about whether the language you speak shapes how you think — the Sapir–Whorf idea, and the usual split between a strong version (language determines thought) and a weak version (language merely nudges it). I'm not asking you to relitigate that debate. I want to describe a narrower pattern I keep noticing, and ask whether there's already a concept for it.

Here's the pattern. A few years ago I moved onto a team where most people are bilingual, and I started paying attention to how a distinction gets handled depending on whether the language *forces* you to make it or merely *lets* you. Two cases stand out.

The first is evidentiality. One of my teammates speaks a language in which, when you report something, you have to build in how you came to know it — whether you saw it yourself, heard it from someone, or inferred it. Speaking English, she isn't required to mark any of that, yet in status updates she almost compulsively adds "I think," "apparently," "from what I gather." What struck me is that this shows up when she is *putting something into words* and not otherwise. In a code review, when she just tags a line as wrong, or in a diagram where she marks a box as suspect, the source of her knowledge doesn't seem to enter the picture at all; she behaves the same as everyone else. The marking appears at the moment she has to say or write something, and vanishes when she isn't speaking.

The second is grammatical aspect. Another teammate's first language obligatorily marks whether an action is finished or still going on. In design discussions and written plans he is scrupulous about separating "done" from "in progress," much more than the rest of us. But when he sorts a set of tickets into piles by hand, or points at which of two screenshots looks correct, he draws the line in the same place as the monolingual English speakers. Again the distinction seems to attach to the act of formulating a sentence rather than to some standing way of seeing the situation.

So the shape of the thing I'm after is roughly this: the influence of a language on thought looks real, but it seems to be *scoped to the moment of producing language* — it bites while you are choosing how to phrase something, and it seems to depend on which language you are currently speaking rather than on a fixed outlook that travels with you. I've also noticed it seems tied specifically to the distinctions a language makes *mandatory* — the ones you cannot avoid when you open your mouth — rather than to distinctions it merely gives you a handy word for. Maybe those two observations are one phenomenon; maybe they're two. I can't tell.
