I'm a retired mineralogist -- forty years of X-ray work on crystal structures, now five years into reading philosophy and I've landed on a view I'm presenting to a small philosophy of science reading group next month. My claim: the structural realists are right that something survives theory change, but they've got the wrong thing. It's not the mathematical structure of the theory that's preserved -- thats scaffolding we tear down and rebuild all the time. What's preserved is the structure of our interventions: the reproducable lab routines, the specimen prep, the way a measurement pushes back on you no matter which theory is in fashion. The equations are a coat of paint; the wall is the doing. I call it interventionist structural realism, for better or worse.

The group is mostly not my kind of people. Two hardline ontic structural realists who think relations are literally all there is and objects are just nodes in a graph. One constructive empiricist who thinks I'm a naive realist who hasn't read enough. And a couple of old-school scientific realists who think theories latch onto the real furniture of the world and that my lab-routine story is just instrumentalism with extra steps -- one of them is an ex-chemist like me and he'll be the tough one.
