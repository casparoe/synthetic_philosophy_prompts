I'm a fourth-year phil student (social ontology / phil of science) and I've picked up a paid side gig that has turned into something weird. A big hospital system near me has a "data equity" committee — a couple of clinicians, an epidemiologist, two informaticists, and one lawyer who is the only person on it who reads the fine print — and theyre rebuilding the intake forms and the analytics taxonomy for the whole system before the next sprint. Someone on the committee read enough of the constructionist lit to get nervous about the race and gender fields but not enough to know what to do differently, so now there's a general anxiety that half their categories might be "socially constructed" and therefore bogus, while the lawyer is quietly worried about the opposite failure — that if they drop a category they'll get sued for not tracking disparities. They asked me, as the philosopher in the room, to write them a decision procedure they can actually run, one category at a time, and I'd rather hand them something real than a vibe.

So: give me that procedure. A short ordered list of questions a non-philosopher can run on a single category, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and hands the question back to the committee because it has turned into a values question, and — this part matters — an explicit list of the moves they will want to make that the theory doesn't license (constructed, so unreal; constructed, so arbitrary; constructed, so easy to change; constructed, so safe to delete from a form). It needs to separate the different things "socially constructed" gets used for — members of the category being causally produced by social arrangements, versus the category being constituted by our attitudes, versus it merely being a label someone picked — and it needs a workable way of telling an interactive kind from an indifferent one, so they can see when measuring a category is going to move the category. Ground it in the actual literature where you can (looping effects and the making-up-people material, conferralism, status functions, homeostatic property clusters, the ameliorative ask-what-the-concept-is-for move) but keep the names out of the procedure itself, since they want a checklist and not a syllabus.

Then run the procedure end to end on three categories so I can show them it works: race, gender, and money — money because nobody is tempted to call it bogus, so it makes a good control. Include one where the procedure returns an answer I won't like; my guess is it'll be somewhere in the race-and-medicine corner, but you tell me. End with a short section on what the procedure can't do and where it will misfire. And if the natural-versus-social axis is just the wrong thing to be running on, say so plainly instead of dressing it up as steps — I'd rather learn that now, in front of a committee that meets biweekly and will absolutely ask me why step four exists.
