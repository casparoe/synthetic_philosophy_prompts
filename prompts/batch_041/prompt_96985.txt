I'm on my department's research-evaluation committee (psych, big state R1) and we're rewriting the section of the promotion-and-tenure guidelines that deals with failed replications. I ended up holding the pen partly because the case on our desk this month is personally awkward: the candidate is a friend and former co-author of mine, and her flagship finding — a 2016 paper, the one with the four-digit citation count — came back null in a 12-lab registered replication that ran something like eight times her original sample size. I'm recused from her vote, but I'm the one drafting the general policy, which is its own flavor of weird. I've been going back and forth on this for two weeks and keep landing in a different place every morning.

Here's the knot. Her defense has two prongs and I genuinely can't tell whether either is legitimate or whether I'm just being loyal. First: she says the replication didn't test her hypothesis, it tested her hypothesis *plus* a different operationalization of the key variable — the original manipulation had a feature the new protocol dropped (I'll spare you the details) — so a null there falsifies the bundle, not the claim. Second: she points out that the multi-lab's own numbers show real scatter between labs, and says that means the effect isn't absent, it's conditional on a moderator nobody has bothered to measure yet. Priya thinks both moves are unfalsifiable in practice and wants a bright line: if a well-powered preregistered replication comes back null, the finding doesn't count toward promotion, full stop, and "you operationalized it wrong" is exactly what every dead effect says on the way out. Sam, on the other side, wants us to require Bayes factors, his line being that a single null shouldn't erase a decade of evidence unless the data can actually move a reasonable prior, and that our current rule is just treating p > .05 as proof of absence. Priya also floated, then abandoned, something about requiring the new estimate to fall inside a prediction interval built from the original effect size and the replication's N — I don't fully understand it, but I gather it would make failures *more* common by design, which seems to cut against her own bright line.

You're a philosopher of statistics and a methodologist who has thought hard about replication, so please think this through carefully before you write anything. Start with a short brainstorming pass: in your own words, what does a failed replication actually license you to conclude under a frequentist reading and under a Bayesian one — especially when the replication is far better powered than the original — and which parts of Priya's and Sam's positions survive that? Then draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: a concrete recommended rule (what counts as a failure, what weight it carries, who adjudicates the operationalization defense and against what standard), a procedure for exactly this kind of hard case, and then apply the rule to her file as if my recusal didn't exist, so the committee can see what they'd be voting on. Give me the strongest case *against* my recommendation in its own section — I'd much rather the committee shoot down an honest version of the other side than discover it live in the meeting — and no hedging at the bottom line, I need something people can actually vote yes or no on. Skip the citations, I just need the argument. Two pages, maybe three if the procedure needs the room.
