Ok so I need help with a piece I'm writing. The conceit is "what would Max Weber make of the defund the police fight," and I'd like it to be more than a cute framing device.

Weber died in 1920, so none of this existed for him: body cameras, consent decrees, qualified immunity, predictive policing, gunshot-detection sensors on lampposts, unarmed crisis-response teams taking over mental-health calls, violence-interrupter programs, police union contracts that make it nearly impossible to fire anybody, and of course the full abolitionist position that the institution can't be fixed because its job has always been managing the poor and guarding property, plus the argument that American policing descends from slave patrols and strike-breaking. He never had to have an opinion about any of it.

What I want is your best prediction of what his position would actually have been, built out of his own machinery rather than out of what a nice liberal sociologist would say. And take the three camps seriously on their own terms: reform (more training, more rules, more oversight), defund (shift a big chunk of the budget to housing, mental health and youth work, keep a smaller force), abolish (build something else entirely and stop pretending the thing can be patched).

Stuff I specifically want you to work through:

1. His definition of the state as the community that successfully claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force. Note the word claims and the word legitimate. If a large share of a city's residents stop believing the claim — which is roughly what happened in some places after 2020 — is the force in that neighborhood still state force in his sense, or does legitimacy for him live in people's beliefs and can therefore just leak away? Which means: is defunding a threat to the state, or a symptom of a state that already lost the argument?

2. The ethics of responsibility vs the ethics of ultimate ends. My hunch is that reform is the responsible camp and abolition is the conviction camp, but he clearly thought you needed both, and I suspect my mapping is too neat. Tell me where it breaks, and whether the abolitionists look like his conviction politicians or like something he didn't have a box for.

3. Bureaucracy. He thought rational-legal administration was technically superior and basically inescapable once it exists. Does that commit him to reform-by-procedure, or does it actually underwrite the abolitionist claim that you can't reform the thing?

4. The awkward structure of it: the police are the part of the state whose means are violence while its stated ends are order, safety, justice. If you keep the ends and take away the means, is that still a state function in his terms, or have you changed the subject?

5. He was also a German nationalist who thought the state was the vehicle of national power, which might make him want more cops, better organized, rather than either of the other two camps. If that's wrong I'd rather you say so than let it slide.

The thing I'm most worried about is that this is just me stuffing 2025 opinions into a dead German's mouth. So: where would he have balked, which camp would he have found unserious, and what would have to be true for him to land in each one? If your honest answer is that he'd refuse the question — because these are ideal types, or because a sociologist of domination shouldn't be handing out advice — say that and tell me how you'd reframe it. Also there's that bleak image at the end of his lecture on politics as a vocation, about what's in store for anyone who goes into politics: a long polar night rather than a summer blaze, if I'm remembering it right. I'd like that in there somewhere, but dont build the piece on my memory of it.
