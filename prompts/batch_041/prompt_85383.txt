Please explain, as carefully as you can, a conceptual issue in the theory of diachronic rationality: what a money pump does and does not show.

Set-up. An agent holds C. Her preferences cycle — she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — and they are complete, stable over time, and otherwise unremarkable. Money compensates continuously, so that a penny is small enough that she prefers any item she ranks higher to any item she ranks lower plus a penny. She is offered three trades, one at a time, each of the form: give up the item you hold and a penny, receive the item you prefer to it. She takes all three, each of which is, at the moment of choice, an improvement by her own lights, and she ends where she started, three pennies poorer. Call this the sequential pump.

I want an explanation of the following, roughly in the order in which the issues depend on one another.

1. What must be true for this sequence to count as an objection to her preferences rather than merely a run of bad luck? The argument appears to need a bridge from "she can be made to lose" to "her preferences are irrational." What are the candidate bridges, and what does each presuppose?

2. Suppose she is fully foresighted and knows the whole sequence in advance. Does the pump still work on her? If not, what is the strongest form the argument takes against a foresighted agent? Consider in particular the proposal that the three trades be offered as a single up-front package rather than one at a time, and say whether that removes the need for a naive agent or merely relocates it.

3. She might adopt a policy: resolve now to refuse every trade, and then at each step refuse a trade she prefers at that moment. Is that plan-sticking the model of practical rationality, or is it acting against her current preferences and therefore irrational? What does each verdict commit you to about whether rationality has any genuinely diachronic component?

4. A common reply is that the pump's conclusion is not "her choices are irrational" but "her preferences must be revised," i.e., that the argument establishes a constraint on admissible preference structures rather than on acts. What does this reply concede, and what must it say about agents who cannot revise — or about whether having the preferences and being rational come apart?

5. Contrast the sequential pump with a case in which the agent's preferences are internally consistent at every moment but reverse between moments: when the earlier of two options is imminent she prefers it, and when both are distant she prefers the later one. She can be exploited in a comparable way, and at no point does she hold an inconsistent set of preferences. Is that the same argument? If it is, does the pump really bear on transitivity, or only on a broader failure of an agent's preferences to be endorsable over time? If it is not, say exactly what the cyclic case has that this one lacks.

6. Finally, which single premise in this structure is the weakest, and what would a case or a variant of the pump look like that would decide between the two or three best replies?

I am not asking for a survey, and I do not need names, dates, or citations — I want the argument's joints.
