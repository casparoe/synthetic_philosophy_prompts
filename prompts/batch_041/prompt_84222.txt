I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, and I am rebuilding the session I usually give on the replication crisis around a single activity rather than a lecture. Sixteen students, ninety minutes, an ordinary seminar room, mixed backgrounds — some have taken a statistics course, some have not — and no reliable internet or software: the activity must run on physical randomisation (coins, dice, cards) or on a small table of critical values I prepare in advance. I am willing to spend ten minutes framing at the start and to reserve twenty for the debrief.

The idea I want them to run into with their own hands is a specific one, and narrower than "p-hacking is bad." As a frequentist treats it, the evidential import of a dataset is fixed by the sampling plan that produced it, stopping rule included; the guarantee a p-value carries is a property of the procedure, computed over outcomes that did not occur. Two consequences I want the group to feel rather than be told. First, if you look at your data as you go and stop when you reach significance, your "five per cent" is not five per cent, and nothing in the final dataset records that you did this — the numbers are the same numbers a scrupulous fixed-sample researcher would have got. Second, and to my mind the stranger point, two researchers can hold numerically identical data and be entitled to different verdicts, or one of them to no verdict at all, purely because of what they would have done had the coin come down differently. I want the Bayesians in the room to be able to press the objection that this makes the stopping rule an artefact of frequentism rather than a feature of evidence, and I want the activity to give that objection somewhere to land rather than leaving it as a slogan.

Please design the activity: rules, materials, a timeline in rough minutes, whatever I must prepare beforehand, roles if there are roles, and a debrief that maps specific moments in what happened onto the pieces of the idea. Requirements:

- Participants should commit to a stopping rule in writing before they see any data, and the group should not be told what phenomenon is being demonstrated until the debrief.
- The activity must make visible both the inflation of the false-positive rate under optional stopping and the identical-data-two-designs point. The second is the one I care most about; I would rather it be a scripted, exact moment, with the specific counts to use and the two p-values, than a gesture at it.
- Give the Bayesian rebuttal a role inside the activity — a participant or a planted reviewer who argues that the stopping rule leaves no trace in the data and therefore cannot bear on the evidence.
- End with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief myself.

Two things I want from you as a respondent. First, mark the epistemic status of anything quantitative: distinguish what you have derived, and show enough of the derivation that I can check it, from what you are recalling; if a number is load-bearing and you cannot derive it, say so rather than supplying a plausible-looking one. Second, if the design I have asked for cannot do what I want — if ninety minutes with sixteen students and no computers cannot produce a visible gap between the two false-positive rates, or if the identical-data moment is less clean than I am assuming — tell me plainly and tell me what to sacrifice. Do not smooth it over by quietly specifying a demonstration that would need a computer or several thousand runs; I would rather have a smaller, honest activity than an impressive one that fails in the room.
