I'm drafting a prospectus for a dissertation in decision theory and need a two-page proposal for what would become its first substantive chapter. Here is the half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal.

The standard epistemic argument for iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies runs through mixed strategies. A rational player, the argument goes, never plays a strategy that fails to be a best response to any belief about the others; and in a two-player game the strategies that are never a best response are precisely those strictly dominated by some mixture. The elimination procedure therefore appears to require mixed strategies in the role of objects of choice — things a player might select. Yet the prevailing interpretation of a mixed strategy is the opposite: not a deliberate randomization but a representation of uncertainty, either the opponent's about what this player will do or the player's own. My suspicion is that the argument quietly trades on the choice reading while officially disclaiming it, and that once the two roles are separated, the justification for elimination survives only in a weaker form. A natural place to look for the fault line is the asymmetry between strict and weak dominance: iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies yields a unique surviving set regardless of the order of elimination, whereas the weakly dominated version does not, and I suspect this asymmetry tracks the two readings of mixed strategies rather than being an independent fact about dominance.
