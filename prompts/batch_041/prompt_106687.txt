Ok so I've got a weird problem and I need help. My best friend's wedding is in eleven days and I'm giving the toast, and I've managed to get myself fixated on a philosophical question that I now can't stop thinking about, and honestly it's kind of ruining my ability to write a normal toast.

Background: my old college roommate was a philosophy grad student, and one very long night he told me about what he called the special composition question. It's basically this: when do some things add up to a further thing? When do a bunch of parts actually compose one bigger object, instead of just being a bunch of parts that we find it convenient to talk about as if they were one? He said there's basically three kinds of answer. You can say never -- nothing ever really composes anything, there are only the smallest bits, and everything else is a useful fiction. You can say always -- any bunch of things whatsoever composes something, so right now you and me and the moon are one object, which somehow bothers me way more than the "never" answer does. Or you can say only sometimes, when the parts are arranged in some special way, and then the whole fight is over what the special way is.

Anyway, here's why it's eating me. My parents separated last month after thirty-four years, and ever since then I keep asking myself whether they were ever one thing to begin with, or two things that just spent a really long time in the same house. And now my best friend is getting married and everybody in that room is going to say some version of "two become one," and I have to stand up and say something that's actually true and also actually kind and also funny, which is definately hard when I now suspect "one" might be a fiction.

The audience is a problem too. Maybe a third of the room are academics, and a couple of them are actual philosophy people (my old roommate will be there, grinning). The rest is family, including the bride's grandmother, who does not need a lecture. So it has to land for the philosophers without losing everyone else.

So: write me the toast. About four minutes, American English. Don't just wave at the composition thing as a cute metaphor -- I want the speech to actually be structured by it. Pick one of those three answers, or some fourth one you make up, and let that answer decide what the toast can and can't claim about what a marriage is, about what it means when one ends, and about what's really happening when two people decide to be one. Come down somewhere -- don't end on "well, who's to say." I'd rather you defend a real position and take the hit if it's wrong. And it has to feel like warmth, not a seminar. Also please no quotes from actual philosophers, I won't remember them right and it'll sound like I'm reading off a card. Just my voice.
