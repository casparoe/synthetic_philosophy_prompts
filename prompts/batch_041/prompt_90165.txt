I am an undergraduate enrolled in a course on nuclear strategy and deterrence theory, and I am trying to write a response paper on the logic of capitulation and the credibility of threats. In seminar, a classmate repeatedly invokes the slogan "better red than dead" as though it settles the question of whether a state should threaten nuclear retaliation, pursue unilateral disarmament, or adopt a policy of brinkmanship. I suspect the slogan is doing several different jobs at once, and that some of them are far weaker than the classmate realizes. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making—about the likely consequences of a nuclear exchange, about the value of political sovereignty relative to mere survival, about the rationality of surrender under conditions of uncertainty, about the credibility of deterrent threats, about what life under Soviet or Russian domination would actually involve, and about the moral standing of states as opposed to individuals—and say for each whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false? I am especially interested in the apparent paradox that a threat of mutual annihilation is supposed to deter precisely because it might be carried out, even though carrying it out would be suicidal: does the slogan deny that paradox, resolve it, or simply ignore it? I would also like to know which reading is being invoked when the slogan is used to dismiss brinkmanship as reckless, and which readings would actually support that dismissal. Please present the answer as a menu of readings rather than selecting one and defending it.
