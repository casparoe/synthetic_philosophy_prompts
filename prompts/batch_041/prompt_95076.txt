I'm in a seminar on philosophy of language and I keep getting stuck on Kripke's rule-following argument — the Kripkenstein thing, from his book on Wittgenstein. The setup: suppose you've never in your life added two numbers both under 57. Sorry, never added numbers 57 or above. Now someone asks you 68 + 57 and you say 125. A skeptic says nothing about your past — not your behavior, not your brain, not any mental image you had while learning arithmetic — fixes that you meant plus rather than quus, where quus gives the ordinary sum except that whenever either number is 57 or higher it gives 5. So by your old lights, 125 and 5 were equally consistent with everything you ever did, and your answer is a leap in the dark. What actually bugs me is the "skeptical solution": we're told to stop hunting for the fact that makes me right and instead notice that I belong to a community that goes on the same way, agrees with each other, and corrects each other. Fine, but that makes correctness sound like a vote.

Anyway, I've been re-reading The Merchant of Venice for another class, and the trial scene now looks like that argument staged as a play. The bond is the rule; everyone in Venice has been reading it the ordinary way (flesh, and cutting flesh obviously spills blood); then Portia — an outsider, in disguise, not even a real doctor of law — produces a deviant reading. Shylock may have his pound of flesh, exactly a pound, but the bond says nothing about blood, so the instant he draws any he forfeits everything. And it only holds because the Duke and the whole court choose to go along with her, while Shylock, who is outside that community in every sense, gets outvoted and then broken: forced to convert, half his fortune handed over.

So is the play a dramatization of the skeptic's paradox and the communal fix, or is it secretly a counterexample to Kripke, since Portia wins by being a pure textualist — the words grant flesh and nothing more — which is exactly the kind of thing Kripke says can't be found in the words? I want a real reading, not a summary. Where does the lens illuminate: the trial, obviously, but also the ring plot at the end, where sworn oaths get reinterpreted by whoever holds the power, and what about Portia's plea for mercy, which argues for going past the letter of the rule right before she uses the letter of the rule as a weapon? Where does the play resist the reading and you have to admit it? What does the play add to the debate that the philosophy papers don't? Two caveats: don't quote any lines, I don't trust my memory of the text and neither should you, so paraphrase scenes instead; and don't tell me what Shakespeare believed — I want the play, not the man.
