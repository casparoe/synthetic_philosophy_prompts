This is an argument one of my students handed in for a seminar on causation and responsibility, and it has the shape of something that proves way too much. Here it is, as clean as I can state it:

(1) An event c is a cause of an event e just in case, had c not occurred, e would not have occurred. This is the difference-making test, and it's supposed to apply whether c is something an agent did or something an agent didn't do.
(2) A child drowned in a pond I walked past last summer. Had I waded in, the child would have lived; nothing else about the situation would have stopped me saving him.
(3) I didn't wade in. So by (1), my not wading in is a cause of the child's death.
(4) There's nothing special about me in (3). Others walked past too, and anyone at all — the man asleep three towns over, my cousin in Lisbon — could have done something such that, had he done it, the child would have lived. By (1), each of them is also a cause of the death.
(5) Run the same test over deaths from preventable disease. For each such death there's some cheque I didn't write such that, had I written it, that person would have lived. So every one of those deaths has me among its causes.
(6) To knowingly cause a death when you could have prevented it and had no excuse is to be a killer of the person who died. (The student defends this one: if the doctor who lets a patient die for convenience isn't a killer, and the parent who doesn't feed the child isn't a killer, then who is?)
(7) So I'm a killer — and not of one person but of thousands. So is anyone with a bank account.
(8) That's absurd. I'm not a killer of thousands of children, and neither are you.

So at least one of (1)-(6) has to go, and I want to know which.
