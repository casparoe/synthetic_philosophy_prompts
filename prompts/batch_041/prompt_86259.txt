You are a philosopher of mathematics with strong expertise in set theory and in the philosophy of geometry, and you are unusually good at auditing analogies — at saying precisely what a comparison can and cannot carry. I would like you to help me think through an analogy I keep encountering, and I would like you to brainstorm a little before you commit to a verdict.

In a popular account of set theory I have been reading, the independence of the continuum hypothesis is explained by analogy with the independence of the parallel postulate from the rest of Euclid's axioms. The story runs roughly as follows. Just as the fifth postulate turned out to be neither provable nor refutable from the other axioms, and just as this led mathematicians to accept whole families of non-Euclidean geometries as equally legitimate, so the continuum hypothesis has been shown to be neither provable nor refutable from the standard axioms of set theory. The author draws the moral that there is simply no fact of the matter about the size of the continuum; that Euclidean versus non-Euclidean geometry is a matter of which axioms you adopt rather than of which is true; and that the same goes for the continuum hypothesis and the set-theoretic universe you work in: choose your axioms, choose your universe.

I want a careful audit of this analogy rather than a summary of it. Please do the following, in order.

First, set out the correspondence explicitly. What plays the role of the geometric axioms, of the fifth postulate itself, of the "intended" plane or space, of the non-Euclidean models, of the discovery that the postulate was independent, and of the eventual resolution of the geometric question? A table of correspondences is welcome.

Second, say where the analogy holds and — more importantly — where it breaks. Which features of the geometry case is the author quietly importing into the set-theoretic case without argument?

Third, separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the moral being drawn from those that are merely superficial or cosmetic.

Fourth, answer the real question. Does the moral survive the audit? Is the parallel-postulate analogy strong enough to license the claim that there is no fact of the matter about the continuum hypothesis and that one may simply choose one's axioms? If it is not, say exactly what it gets wrong, and propose what a better analogy would be, if any.

Work through this step by step, keep it readable to someone who knows a little set theory but is not a specialist, and flag any point at which the analogy is being asked to do work that an independence result by itself cannot do.
