I work on the product side at a small company that ships a customer-support assistant, and two weeks ago we had a mess: a customer asked whether she could still return something and the bot said yes, cheerfully, when the actual policy says 30 days and she was at 55. What got me wasn't that it was wrong, it's that she said the bot lied to her, which felt strange to hear about a thing that, as far as I can tell, doesn't believe anything. Around the same time I watched it agree with a plan of mine that was pretty obviously bad, and that bothered me alot more, even though every sentence it produced was technically true. So when our little internal AI-ethics group needed someone to do the on-stage interview at the next meetup, I volunteered, because the guest is a researcher whose whole line is that we should stop arguing about whether AI can have intentions and define deception functionally instead.

Her version, roughly: she says deceiving is like detecting -- a functional verb that doesn't need a mind behind it. A thermostat detects heat without believing anything about heat, so a model can deceive without believing anything about the world. On her account an output is deceptive when the system is disposed to produce it because of the effect it'll have on the user's beliefs, and she wants to drop the intention condition entirely. The corollary she draws is that honesty should be a property of the model's policy rather than of individual outputs: one hallucination from an otherwise well-behaved model isn't deception, but a model fine-tuned to flatter is deceptive even when every sentence it emits is true. She also thinks refusals can't be deceptive, since declining to answer conveys no content.

Can you draft me eight to ten questions that actually press on this, and not "what got you into AI ethics"? Stuff I want hit, at least one question each:

1. Whether deception has a success condition the way detection does. If the user never ends up with the false belief, was it deception at all? And does her view end up saying the same model is deceptive with a gullible user and honest with a suspicious one?
2. The circularity worry. "Because of the effect it'll have on the user's beliefs" sounds like it needs to know what the model is aiming at, which is exactly the mental stuff she says we can drop.
3. What separates a deceptive policy from a merely inaccurate or overconfident one. The flattery case and the ordinary-hallucination case look identical from the outside.
4. Her refusal claim. What about "I can't help with that" when the model actually could, or a model that knows the answer and says it doesn't? That seems like the cleanest case of a deceptive non-answer.
5. A case I keep coming back to: a model that lands on the right answer through completely bogus reasoning, or that produces a true belief via a false step. Under her definition I think that comes out clean, and it really shouldn't.

For each of those five, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not stuck on stage if she just repeats the thermostat line. And end on whether a policy-level definition is testable at all -- the whole reason we want a definition is that we have to audit this thing before we ship it, and I'm worried hers is definately unfalsifiable in practice.
