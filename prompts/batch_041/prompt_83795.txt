I'm a novelist — second book, historical, set mostly in Florence and Rome in the 1620s and 30s — and I'm applying for a three-month writing fellowship at a humanities institute that likes its writers to arrive with a conceptual spine to what they're doing, not just a setting and a mood. The application wants two pages, and the thing I actually need to think through isn't a plot problem. It's this. Galileo's big hermeneutic move, the one where he argues that scripture is written for everyone and therefore speaks in the common idiom and the common understanding of its time, so that where you've got a demonstration that the earth moves you don't deny the demonstration, you say the passage was never making a claim about the earth's motion in the first place. In his hands I find it completely compelling. In general I find it completly terrifying, because it's the same move every crank and every institution makes when a text they're invested in collides with something they can't deny: this was never about that. My best friend is a lawyer who works on originalism and she says it's the identical structure, which is either the best thing about my project or a sign that I've smooshed two different things together and should be embarrassed.

So what I want out of the proposal is a real answer to: what makes an accommodationist rereading of an authoritative text legitimate rather than opportunistic? I've got three half-ideas and I want you to push on all of them, including telling me which to throw away. One: his move looks like it's doing two jobs at once — accommodating to the audience's capacity (they can't follow the argument, so the text simplifies) and accommodating to the audience's idiom (they think in a geocentric vocabulary, so the text borrows it) — and I suspect those come apart badly, and that only one of them survives the discovery that the idiom wasn't just a manner of speaking but a false belief held by the author too. Two: a rereading should be suspect when there's nothing it couldn't absorb. I want a test in that spirit — if no conceivable discovery could force a further revision, then the passage has stopped being a text with content and become a blank cheque. Three: standing. Who's licensed to do the rereading, and does it matter whether the community that owns the text accepts the new reading, or whether the reading is simply correct? Write me the two-page proposal: the question stated so it has a determinate answer; why the obvious positions leave it open (it's interpretation all the way down; you can't revise an authoritative text at all; only the community that owns it can say what it means); the method, week by week-ish, and I'd much rather it be case-driven — me assembling a handful of rereadings from different domains and hunting for the criterion that sorts them — than theory for its own sake; what a result would actually look like (a usable decision procedure, or a demonstration that there isn't one and why); and what would count as the project failing, which I want to be a live possibility and not just that I ran out of weeks. Please dont give me citations or a reading list; I'll add those, and I don't want the shape of the argument dictated by whatever happens to be on my shelf. And take a position — say which of my three hunches you'd keep, which you'd cut, and which criterion you actually think does the work, with your reasons. If you think the honest answer is that there's no criterion here and it's all rhetorical positioning, say so and defend it rather than handing me a menu. One practical constraint: the panel is a poet, a museum curator and a retired diplomat, so the opening paragraph has to be legible to them.
