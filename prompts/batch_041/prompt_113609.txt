I am an undergraduate in a third-year course on computational models of decision making, and our current unit concerns inverse reinforcement learning — the project of recovering an agent's preferences from observations of what it does. For the unit's written assignment, we are permitted to submit a short story in place of an expository essay, provided the story does the conceptual work rather than merely decorating it. I would like you to draft that story.

Please build it around the following setup, which I am specifying in full so that nothing in the story depends on prior reading.

A researcher has access to a single person's behavioral traces: years of purchases, calendar entries, route choices, which articles were opened and for how long, which invitations were accepted and which quietly declined. From these traces she must reconstruct a reward function for that person. The reconstruction is not idle; it is handed to an automated agent that will make decisions on the person's behalf, because the person can no longer make them herself.

The story should dramatize three sources of underdetermination, all of which I want present:

1. Gauge freedom. Any reward function can be altered by adding a term that telescopes along a trajectory — one whose total contribution depends only on where the trajectory starts and where it ends, not on the path taken between them — without changing the ranking of any policy. The recovered numbers are therefore fixed only up to this freedom, and two functions can issue identical decisions in every situation the person actually faced while attributing to her quite different values.

2. Preferences confounded with constraints. What was chosen depends on what was available. A person who never takes the cheapest option may value it highly and simply never have been in a position to choose it. The traces record choices, not the feasible sets from which they were drawn, and a preference for a thing can always be traded off against a constraint that concealed it.

3. Noise against sharpness. The same observed choices can be explained by a nearly indifferent person who almost never errs, or by a strongly opinionated person who frequently slips. The traces are equally consistent with both readings, and the difference becomes consequential precisely when the agent must choose among options the person never encountered.

Requirements for the draft:

- Aim for 900 to 1400 words, in whichever narrative person serves the material best.
- Include at least one concrete decision the automated agent must make, where the ambiguity above has a consequence the reader can feel — money, care, a relationship, a promise kept or broken.
- The researcher should validate her model on held-out choices and find that it predicts them well, and this success should be shown to be compatible with her still being wrong in the way that matters. High predictive accuracy on the data she has is not the same as having recovered the preference.
- Do not dissolve the ambiguity with direct testimony. No late-discovered diary entry, letter, or recorded instruction in which the person states what she wanted. Whatever resolution there is must come from how the researcher handles the underdetermination, or from the honest admission that it cannot be resolved.
- No citations, no references to real researchers or publications, no fictional scholarly apparatus, and no footnotes.
- Technical vocabulary is welcome, but each term should be earned by a scene before it is named.
- Write for a reader who knows nothing about the field.
- Close with a separate paragraph of no more than 120 words in which the researcher names, in plain language, the general phenomenon the story has dramatized.
