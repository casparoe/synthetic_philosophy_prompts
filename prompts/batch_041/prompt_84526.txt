Question 1 (20 points). State the principle of the identity of indiscernibles and distinguish it from the indiscernibility of identicals. Describe Black's two-sphere scenario and explain what it is meant to establish. Does it succeed? Discuss at least one response open to a defender of the principle.

Student's answer:

"The identity of indiscernibles (PII) says that if two things have all the same properties, then they are one and the same thing. The indiscernibility of identicals is its converse: if a is b, then everything true of a is true of b, which is what is usually called Leibniz's Law. The two are not the same principle, and Black's scenario is aimed at the first. The scenario is a world containing nothing but two spheres, identical in size, colour, and material composition, each exactly ten feet from the other. The spheres share all their properties, including their relational ones, since each is ten feet from a qualitatively identical sphere; yet there are evidently two of them, so PII is false. And because distinct things can share all their properties, the scenario is a counterexample to Leibniz's Law as well: if the sharing of properties does not track identity, then identity cannot be understood in terms of properties at all. A defender of PII could say the spheres are after all discernible, since one of them has the property of being identical with sphere A and the other does not. That is ad hoc, though: it rescues PII only by counting identity itself among the properties, which is precisely the point in dispute. The more promising defence is relationalism about space. If space is nothing over and above the spatial relations among bodies, then the two-sphere world and its mirror image are the same world, and there is no room for a real distinction between sphere A and sphere B; so the scenario does not describe a genuine possibility, and PII stands. This is also why Leibniz held the principle of sufficient reason, which collapses in the same two-sphere world: the spheres are arranged as they are, and there is no reason for that arrangement rather than its mirror image."
