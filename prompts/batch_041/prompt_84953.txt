I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, mid-draft on a chapter about reserve powers, and last week in a workshop I had the line 'the monarch reigns but does not rule' used at me twice by two different people in service of two different points, which is kind of what set me off. The first used it to say that asking whether a monarch could refuse assent to a bill is basically a non-question, since ruling is precisely what they don't do. The second used it, weirder, to argue that the whole institution is epiphenominal, so that studying it is studying nothing. Those don't look like the same claim to me, and neither one looks like it just falls out of the sentence. Can you lay out the distinct things 'the monarch reigns but does not rule' could acutally be asserting? I want the full menu: the ones that are near-definitional, depending on what you count as 'ruling'; the ones that are substantive claims about conventions, discretion, and dormant or reserve powers; the ones that turn on the difference between the Crown as a legal person and the particular human being who happens to be wearing it; and any that are just false, or that quietly presuppose one particular constitutional settlement. Where a reading only holds in some jurisdictions, say which — the sentence is doing visibly different work in London, Oslo, Stockholm and Tokyo, and part of what I want is to see which reading each of those two workshop remarks would need in order to land. Lay it out as a menu with verdicts rather than picking a favourite and explaining it. And tell me how confident you are in each verdict, and which point you're least sure of — I'd rather see a hedge I can check than a tidy answer I can't, since some of this is going into the chapter.
