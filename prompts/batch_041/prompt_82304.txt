I have no academic training in this, but I have been thinking about the version of expected utility sometimes called risk-weighted, in which a person's attitude to risk is a separate ingredient from how much they care about the size of losses — the agent weights a bad outcome by more or less than its probability, instead of folding risk aversion into a curved utility function. I want to run a real episode through it. In 2008 the Fed and the Treasury arranged a backstop for the sale of Bear Stearns in March, allowed Lehman Brothers to fail in September, and then rescued AIG the next day. I know the standard story about moral hazard, and I am not asking whether the rescues were right; I want a disciplined mapping and an honest account of where this way of thinking breaks.

First, who was the agent, what options and possible states of the world did it face, and how heavily does each choice suggest the worst outcomes were being weighted? Second, can all three choices be made consistent with a single attitude to risk, or does their apparent inconsistency show the theory is being asked for something it cannot give? Third, where did the probabilities come from, and is the theory simply silent about that? Fourth, whose preferences are being maximized when officials spend other people's money, and does the framework survive that question? Fifth, what does the episode show about the theory itself — a confirmation, an embarrassment, or a case it never anticipated?

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
