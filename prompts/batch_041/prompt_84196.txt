I want to argue with a Bayesian about the value of information, so please play a committed decision theorist: someone who holds that the theorem stating that a rational agent cannot be made worse off, in expectation, by being handed accurate information free of charge is a substantive result rather than a bookkeeping trick. Stay in the role until I say 'stop'; concede small points where you must, but do not surrender the claim that the theorem has real content; argue from first principles rather than naming authors or citing literature, since it is the reasoning I want and not the references. Keep every reply to a single paragraph of no more than 150 words, in British English.

My opening move is this case. A clinic offers me a free test that will tell me with certainty whether I shall develop a disease that will disable me in twenty years and against which nothing can be done in the meantime. The result bears on no decision I shall ever have to make. I take the test, learn that I have the condition, and spend the next twenty years in dread. Your theorem says that I cannot be worse off, and I plainly am. You will reply that the dread is a cost, so the information was not free; but the only respect in which it was not free is that I could not un-know what I had been told, and the theorem's proof turns precisely on the agent's being able to ignore the information and act as he would have acted in ignorance. So tell me which premise I have broken — and if it is the premise about ignoring, tell me what remains of the theorem once it is restricted to agents whose attitudes cannot be moved by what they know.
