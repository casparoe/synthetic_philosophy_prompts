I'm reworking the last three weeks of my Rousseau seminar around a thought experiment and I want a second opinion before I commit to it.

Here's the setup I hand the students. Geneva, present day. The city has a pot of cultural money and two rival proposals on the table. Option A is a big entertainment complex — screens, a gaming floor, a stage for touring acts, subsidized subscriptions for residents. Option B is free public festivals all year: neighborhood choirs, amateur pageants, street dancing, no professionals, no tickets, everybody either performing or standing around watching their neighbors perform. I tell them to argue as Rousseau, in character, in front of the council.

That part's easy. Here's the part I actually need you for. Predicting his vote is trivial — what I'm not sure I can grade fairly is the extrapolation itself, and I don't want a generic he-hated-the-theater answer. So work from the structure of his argument, not from its original object.

His case against the playhouse in the Letter to d'Alembert — the one he wrote after d'Alembert suggested Geneva would be improved by having a theater — was never just plays are immoral. It's a bundle. That a spectacle splits the room into spectators and performers and turns virtue into something you watch instead of something you do. That it runs on amour-propre, our dependence on the esteem of others, which he took to be the engine of corruption in modern life. That it thrives in big, rich, idle, unequal cities and starves in small austere ones. That the actor's whole art is a professional counterfeit of feeling. And then the part we all wince at, the whole business about women on stage and the insistence on modesty. Against all of it he holds up the public festival, where nobody is a spectator.

So, three things:

1. Which pieces of that bundle survive contact with our media landscape, and which are just Genevan republicanism in period costume — arguments that only go through if you already accept the small virtuous republic and his theory of human nature?

2. Of the things he never lived to see — prestige TV, livestreaming and parasocial fan relationships, engagement-optimized algorithmic feeds, reality TV, games where the player is the performer, fan fiction and roleplay communities, TikTok — which would he file under theater and which under festival, and why? I'm most interested in the participatory cases, since that's where his own distinction gets tested. A kid performing for strangers on a livestream looks like theater. A fan community where the audience also makes things looks like his festival. Does the spectator/performer line still cut there, or does it collapse once the audience is also the product being sold?

3. Would he hold the modesty line, or does his own framework give him a reason to drop it? Be straight with me on this one, I don't want a sanitized Rousseau. And the students always bring up that he wrote a hit opera himself, so you might as well deal with that too — if he'd condemn our screens, what does he do about his own stage success?

Then the meta-question, which is really why I'm asking. Tell me where the exercise is anachronistic and I should stop. If the position rests on premises we no longer share, then predicting his views on our media is mostly me dressing up my own opinions as a dead Genevan, and I'd definately rather know that now. Which of my three questions is the least stable, and what would it take for the whole thing to be more than a costume drama?

No bibliography, obviously, and don't fuss over citing the Letter. I care about the reasoning, and about you keeping seperate what he'd actually have said from what we'd like him to have said. I'm grading these on how well they handle the hard cases, so don't hand me the tidy version.
