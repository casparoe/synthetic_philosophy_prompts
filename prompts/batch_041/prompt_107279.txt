I chair the investment committee of the Aldworth Foundation, a grant-making charity with a permanent endowment of roughly £600 million and an explicit undertaking to spend in ways that will benefit people who are not yet born. At our meeting on 14 March we must decide whether to adopt the proposal our advisers have put forward to replace the Foundation's present judgement-based approach to asset allocation with a fully probabilistic one. On their proposal, each asset class would be assigned a single expected return and a single standard deviation over a twenty-year horizon; these would be run through a mean–variance optimisation to yield the policy portfolio, and the same machinery would be used to fix the Foundation's sustainable spending rate. The alternative before us is to keep ranges and structured narratives and to leave allocation decisions where they now sit, with the committee's judgement.

Our external adviser has objected that the proposal quietly presupposes a theory of probability that Keynes devoted his early philosophical work to arguing against. On Keynes's account, a probability is a logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, not a frequency in the world; and it has two dimensions rather than one. The first is how probable the hypothesis is given the evidence. The second is how much evidence there is — what Keynes called the weight of the argument. Two conclusions may be equally probable while resting on very different amounts of evidence, and his claim is that a rational agent should care about the second dimension and not only the first. He also held that many probabilities cannot be given numerical values at all: they can sometimes be ordered as more or less likely without being measurable, so a framework that forces every judgement into one cardinal figure is not a neutral gain in precision but a change in what is being asserted.

Our advisers have offered a concession: alongside each expected return they would publish a confidence rating of high, medium or low, and the optimiser would tilt allocations away from the low-rated inputs. They say this answers the objection. I am not persuaded that a three-point scale is the same thing as a weight of evidence, and I would like the committee to decide the matter on the basis of a reasoned opinion rather than on who is more fluent.

Please draft the written opinion I shall circulate before the meeting. It should do the following.

First, recommend a definite course of action — approve the framework, reject it, or approve it subject to stated modifications — with a time frame for implementation and a precise statement of what the committee is being asked to authorise.

Second, set out the reasoning in terms our trustees can follow; most of them are not economists. Say plainly what turns on the decision, that is, what the Foundation would do differently under each option, and be candid about how much of the disagreement is about substance and how much about presentation.

Third, engage the adviser's Keynesian objection at its strongest, and in particular address the replies that it is merely a taste for vagueness; that weight can be folded into a single probability by placing a distribution over one's own estimates, in the manner of a hierarchical or second-order model; and that a committee which declines to state probabilities is nonetheless making a decision that embodies implicit ones. Is the weight of evidence genuinely a second dimension, or is it a façon de parler for the precision of a single estimate? If it is a genuine dimension, say what a trustee is supposed to do with it when the optimiser returns a number.

Fourth, say whether the adviser's point is the same as the familiar observation that people are reluctant to bet on odds they regard as ambiguous, or whether the two come apart, and why the answer matters for our policy. The advisers will argue that the confidence rating already captures whatever is worth capturing here; assess that claim directly.

Fifth, give an honest account of the strongest consideration on the side you come out against, and say what evidence or argument over the coming year would lead you to revise your recommendation.

The opinion should run to no more than 1,500 words and should close with a numbered motion that the committee can put to a vote.
