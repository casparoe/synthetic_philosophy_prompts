I write fiction, and I have a solid working command of the novelist's toolkit: point of view, free indirect discourse, the rendering of interiority, subtext, and the deliberate gap between what a character does and what the reader is permitted to know — including the trick by which free indirect discourse lets a narrator ventriloquise an inner life the character may not actually possess. I keep circling the philosophical zombie, the being physically and behaviourally identical to me but with nothing it is like to be it, and I would like the conceivability argument for dualism restated as faithfully as you can manage in my vocabulary, as a claim about what a writer can and cannot put on the page. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the argument translate cleanly into the craft of characterisation, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in how fiction renders a mind. My hunch is that a zombie is simply a character whose interiority the author has declined to write; I want to know whether that is right, or whether the philosophers are asking a question my craft cannot pose — and, if the latter, whether the fault lies with fiction or with the argument.
