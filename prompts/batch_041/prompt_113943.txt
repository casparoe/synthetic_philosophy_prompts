I am a third-year PhD student in economics, and the central prediction of my job-market paper comes from iterating the elimination of dominated strategies in a three-by-three game. Three weeks ago I could have explained why that procedure was sound. A visitor at our theory seminar has left me unable to, and I need this settled before I circulate the paper.

His argument, as he gave it:

(1) An option of mine is dominated when some alternative open to me — possibly a lottery over my other options — does at least as well against everything you might do, and better against at least one thing you might do.

(2) A rational player never plays a dominated option. The alternative is never worse and is sometimes better, so nothing is lost by dropping the option and something may be gained.

(3) Rationality is common knowledge, so the same reasoning applies to the other player and then to me again; iterate until nothing further can be deleted.

(4) Run on my game, this leaves each player with exactly one option.

I cannot accept (4), and not for the usual soft reasons. Two things happen when I run the procedure myself. First, the survivor depends on the order of the deletions: his order leaves one option, mine leaves another, and the two orders agree at every individual step. Second, in the version I actually estimate — five options a side — the procedure deletes an option that is a best reply to the belief I hold; I have watched the other party take the relevant action for two years, and against that belief my deleted option is as good as anything I have.

What I can verify by hand, and what my paper leans on, is the coin-flip step. My three options against his three columns pay 1, 0, −1; then −0.2, −0.5, −0.2; then −1, 0, 1. No single option of mine is dominated by another single option, but the even lottery between my first and my third beats my second in every column. I am confident of that calculation and I want to keep it.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Name the step, say why it looked fine when he gave it, and price the alternatives. My instinct is to keep one round of deletion and abandon the iteration, since the coin-flip step is the only part I can check — tell me whether that trade is coherent, and if it is not, what the coherent options are and what each one costs me. If the coin-flip step carries a hidden price of its own, I want to hear that too.
