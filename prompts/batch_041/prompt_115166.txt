My labmate keeps winning this argument and it's starting to bother me. Here's his pitch, tidied up:

(1) What makes a being matter morally is how much it can suffer and enjoy — sentience, not species membership.
(2) Sentience isn't all-or-nothing. It comes in degrees, and it tracks how much information a nervous system can integrate.
(3) Across species, brain size (or neuron count, or some size-ish proxy) is the best handle we've got on integrative capacity.
(4) So, other things equal, the bigger-brained species is owed more moral consideration — dogs over rats, humans over dogs, and so on up.
(5) Fine, he says, and he'll bite the bullet on the (made-up) glimmer crab: brain three times a human's, but no nociceptors, no learning, no social life, nothing that looks like there's anything it's like to be it. By (4) it outranks us. He shrugs.
(6) Here's where I balk. Human brains vary in size, and they vary with body size and sex and age. If size is really doing the moral work, then a bigger-brained person is owed more than a smaller-brained one. He says no — (4) only ever applies between species, never within one.
(7) That smells like a cheat to me. (4) either generalizes or it doesn't, and I can't find the joint where it snaps.

Which premise do I give up, and what does giving it up cost the ones left standing? I'd rather keep (1) — I'm not trying to sneak speciesism back in — but I'll pay if I have to. Also tell me whether the cross-species / within-species split in (6) is a real distinction or just the fallacy wearing a lab coat.
