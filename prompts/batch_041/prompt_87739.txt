The concept of the supererogatory — the act that it would be good to do but that is not required — is usually treated as a well-behaved region of moral space, separated from the region of duty by a boundary drawn at the threshold of sacrifice we may reasonably demand. I would like you to argue that this picture is unstable, and to argue it in a specific form: that the boundary between the obligatory and the supererogatory can be fixed only by invoking the very distinction it is meant to explain, with the consequence that the concept of supererogation is either primitive — a brute feature of moral thought with no further account to give — or empty.

Please structure the argument as follows. First, make the best case for the ordinary picture: state precisely what work the threshold is supposed to do, and why we appear to need a line between the rescuer who does what duty demands and the rescuer who goes further. Use concrete cases and vary the cost, so that the intuition is doing real work — a bystander at a drowning, a stranger who offers a kidney, someone who devotes a career to the relief of distant suffering. Second, press the difficulty. Show why any attempt to fix the threshold — by appeal to what is reasonable, to what can be demanded without making a life no longer worth living, or to a comparison of the agent's interests against the interests of others — reintroduces the notion of the supererogatory as an input rather than delivering it as an output. Be as exact as you can about where the circularity bites and where it might be resisted. Third, consider the strongest reply available to a defender of the ordinary picture — for instance, that the threshold is fixed by an independent standard, such as the requirements of a good life or the demands of impartiality, and that the supererogatory is then simply whatever lies above it. Explain what that reply would have to establish, and argue either that it fails or that it succeeds only by conceding that supererogation is primitive. Fourth, state what follows for how we should talk and reason. If the distinction is primitive, does that vindicate the ordinary picture after all? If it is empty, what replaces it, and does the replacement do justice to the phenomenology — the fact that some acts strike us as admirable precisely because they were not owed?

Do not survey the literature or name authorities; I want an argument that stands on its own and treats the skeptical conclusion as something to be earned rather than assumed.
