I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and last year I did a long piece on a wrongful-death suit where a family argued that a chemical company had killed a man by never telling him about a risk it had sat on for years. The whole thing turned on whether you can "cause" something by not doing something, and ever since I've been keeping a note on my phone of cases where my gut says cause or no-cause, and I cant get them to add up. Here they are, verdicts included:

- I promise my neighbour I'll water her plants while she's away, I forget, they die. Gut: I killed those plants, basically. Not the same as if I'd stomped on them, but a real cause.
- I walk past a pond, a kid's drowning, I'm a useless swimmer, I don't go in. Gut: I didn't cause the drowning. The water did. I'm ashamed of myself, but I'm not the cause.
- Same pond, same kid, but now it's the lifeguard on duty and she's just not watching. Gut: she caused it, full stop, no hedging.
- A company learns its brake seals crack in the cold, doesn't recall the cars, a driver dies. Gut: the company caused the death. Obvious.
- A hiker finds a smouldering campfire in dry grass, could stamp it out with one boot, walks on, the ridge burns. Gut: he let it burn. But did he cause the fire? I want to say no, and I can't defend that well.
- I turn the boiler up one winter and never turn it back down; years later it blows and scalds the lodger. Gut: that's on me, clearly — even though the thing that "did" it is my not going back down to the basement.
- My candidate loses our district by a single vote and I stayed home, so my vote really would have flipped it. Gut: I still didn't cause her to lose. Though I feel sick about it in a way that's suspiciously like the plant thing.

My editor has taken to calling my half-formed rule the you-broke-it rule: an omission is a cause only when you'd already put yourself in the story — you'd promised, you'd taken the job, you'd sold the brakes, you'd turned the dial. That seems to get the plants, the lifeguard, the company and the boiler, and it gets the pond and the vote too, so it's seven for seven. But a friend of mine who does philosophy says the hiker obviously caused the fire, because if he'd stamped it out the ridge would still be standing — that's just what causing means — and she says my lifeguard only counts as a cause because she'd signed a contract, which makes a death turn on a piece of paperwork, which feels like the wrong place to put it. I keep going back and forth on one more case I haven't listed: I'm alone in a room with a stranger who's choking, I know how to do the Heimlich, I freeze, he dies. No promise, no job, no dial. My gut says something, and I honestly can't tell you what.

So is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about the hiker and the lifeguard — same or different, and why? And which of my verdicts would it make me give up? I'd rather have a rule that's honest about the cases it loses than one that saves them all by getting baroque. Oh, and my editor wants me to say that if the answer turns out to be "it depends on local norms" he'll kill the piece, so please tell me whether that's actually where this lands.
