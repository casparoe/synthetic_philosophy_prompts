I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a seminar on consent and political obligation, and I'm stuck on a passage from Locke's Second Treatise that I suspect is more philosophically interesting than my seminar has treated it. The chapter on the beginning of political societies opens with an uncompromising voluntarism: no one can be put out of the state of nature and subjected to the political power of another without his own consent, and the only way a man can divest himself of his natural liberty and put on the bonds of civil society is by agreeing with other men to join and unite into a community. A few pages later, though, the argument seems to soften into something quite different. There, a man who has any possessions or enjoyment of any part of the dominions of a government is said to give his tacit consent and to be obliged to submit to its laws during his enjoyment of them; someone who inherits and continues to enjoy his father's estate is presumed to consent in the same tacit way; merely travelling on the highway, by contrast, is not enough. Here is that second stretch as I've reconstructed it from the chapter — not a verbatim quotation, since I'm working from memory of the edition we were assigned — and the question I can't answer is how it is supposed to follow from the first. I understand the contrast between express and tacit consent, and I understand that Locke wants to distinguish a landholding resident from a visitor passing through. What I cannot see is how the later passage avoids quietly converting consent from a voluntary act into a status that one can occupy without ever having performed any act at all.

Could you help me work out what theory of consent is actually operating in the passage, and whether it can be made consistent with the opening voluntarism? I'd like the reading that makes the chapter as coherent as possible, followed by a clear statement of what has to be given up for that reading to work. Specifically: is tacit consent being treated as a species of actual consent — a voluntary sign or expression — or as a substitute for it, a ground of obligation that does not require any voluntary act? If it is the latter, does the opening claim have to be read as a claim about the legitimacy of government rather than about the obligations of particular subjects, and does that rescue it? Does the availability of emigration do the voluntariness work, and if so, is the resulting consent actual or merely hypothetical — and would a modern account of valid consent accept it? What are we to say about people who are born into a state, never emigrate, never own property, and never perform any act of acceptance: does the chapter leave them unbound, or does the presumption that an heir consents somehow extend to them? And if we redescribe the passage in the vocabulary we have been using in the seminar — information, voluntariness, competence, and the standard defeaters of coercion, deception, and incapacity — which of those conditions is really doing the work for Locke, and which is most conspicuously missing? My suspicion, which I'd like you to assess rather than assume, is that the passage is best read not as a theory of consent at all but as a theory of fair play or of benefits received, with the language of tacit consent doing rhetorical rather than philosophical work; a friend in the seminar thinks that is uncharitable and that Locke is entitled to a weaker, evidence-based notion of tacit consent, on which the outward signs merely make an already voluntary submission visible. I'd like to know which of us has the better grip on the passage, and where exactly the disagreement lives.
