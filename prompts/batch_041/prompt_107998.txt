I teach political philosophy, and my co-instructor in our grad democratic-theory seminar and I have this long-running game: we throw out cases and she rules on who gets to be part of the demos for the decision — who should get a say. She's been eerily consistent, and next week I want to try to call her shot before she makes it.

Her rulings so far, roughly in order. Long-term residents of the city who never naturalised, on a city rent-control referrendum — in, they vote. People who own rental buildings in the city but live two towns over, on a city property-tax hike — in. People who commute in and work here every day, on the city's new minimum-wage ordinance — in. Eight-year-olds, on any of the above — out. Tourists just passing through — out. Foreign nationals with no connection to the city at all — out. Citizens of the country who emigrated years ago and have no ties left to the city, on a city measure — out.

Now next week's case. The city is voting on a big upriver water diversion: it keeps the city's taps on, but it dries up the farms downstream, and those farms are across the border, in the next country. The farmers don't vote here, city law doesn't bind them, nobody consulted them, but it will wreck them — and the damage is on the same order as the benefit to the city.

So does she let the farmers in? I care less about the answer than the machinery. What rule, or rules, is she running that fit all seven of her rulings — and what other rules fit them just as well? What does each of those predict about the farmers? Which do you think she's actually running, and why? And what single further case could I put to her that would split the surviving rules apart?

Skip the reading list, I know the literature — just the reasoning.
