I'm preparing a seminar on interpretation, and I want to build the first half around a single stipulation that I will ask everyone to hold fixed rather than to argue for. The stipulation distinguishes three kinds of authorial intention: semantic intention (what the author meant the words and sentences to mean), categorial intention (what kind of thing the author meant the text to be — fiction or testimony, literal or ironic, finished or a fragment), and pragmatic intention (what the author hoped the text would accomplish — persuade, console, scandalize, record). On the stipulation, semantic and categorial intention are partly constitutive of a text's meaning, pragmatic intention is not, and categorial intention is defeasible: a text can be shown by its own features and its public context to belong to a category other than the one the author set out to write, in which case the achieved category governs.

Please describe the most important implications of this stipulation for the following, taking them in turn. First, how a reader should adjudicate between two readings of a passage that agree about what the words mean but disagree about whether the passage is ironic. Second, how much weight to give an author's later claim about what a text was really about, when the text itself does not seem to be about that. Third, whether the familiar charge that appeals to authorial intention commit a fallacy survives the stipulation, and if so in what form. Fourth, the decisions an editor faces when an author's manuscripts and the published text diverge in category rather than in wording. Where the stipulation leaves an answer underdetermined, say so and identify what further commitment would settle it.
