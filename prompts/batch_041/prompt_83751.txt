I run a fortnightly discussion evening for adults with no academic background — sixteen regulars, ninety minutes in a community hall — and I want a session on something I have been turning over since my father's illness: the standing of instructions a person wrote down while they were still themselves, and whether that earlier person has any authority over whoever has to live with the consequences. I would rather the group feel the pull of both sides than argue it out in the abstract, so the activity should have them write binding instructions of their own in the first half and then find out what it is like to be held to them.

Please design the whole session: rules, materials (I have index cards, envelopes, a whiteboard and a timer, and can supply one small token per person — no money), a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief. My rough idea, which you should rework if something better serves the point: in the first fifteen minutes everyone privately writes a directive about their own conduct later in the evening — specific enough to be checked, such as which option they take in a forced choice, where their token goes, or whether they speak at all in the closing discussion — seals it in an envelope, and has it read out. Then I reveal something that changes what the choice is worth, so that some directives now look like mistakes and others look oddly prescient. Directives bind unless their author persuades the group to release them, and the group votes by secret ballot; I want them to notice that their votes turn on what releasing costs each of them rather than on who the writer now is. On the directive form I also want a line that lets a writer add, if they choose, that any later request of theirs to be released should be treated as evidence that they can no longer be trusted with the decision. Those who add it should discover they can never get out; those who leave it off should discover their directive can be shed the moment it stings.

Tell me what the group should come away seeing — I am after the point that the authority question may not really be about whether it is the same person, and that the very feature that makes a directive worth having is the one that makes it dangerous. Then the debrief, mapping each moment onto the parts of the problem, including the difference between an earlier self who knew more and one who knew less, and the awkwardness of a directive that forbids its own revocation. Be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to say myself, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I expect vague directives, a group that will not enforce anything, and a release vote that turns into a popularity contest — and how to head each of those off without turning the session into a lecture.
