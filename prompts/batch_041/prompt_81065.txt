A friend in my reading group has an argument I've been chewing on all week. Every step looks fine to me, the conclusion is nuts, so either there's a seam I can't see or the thing is valid and I owe someone an apology. I'd like your help figuring out which.

Her setup: two people with different value systems are like two countries with different endowments. I care a lot about X and only a little about Y, you care a lot about Y and only a little about X, so we can both come out ahead by trading. I do the Y-ish thing you want, you do the X-ish thing I want, and each of us ends up with more of what we actually value than we would have gotten by fighting. She calls this trading across value systems, and says it's the only part of ethics that doesn't require one of us to just be wrong.

The argument, as she writes it on the whiteboard:

1. Whenever two parties rank two divisible goods in different relative amounts, there's a range of splits that each party ranks above the status quo. That's just the gains from trade.
2. Policy outcomes are divisible in the relevant sense: you can hand some fraction of the disputed resource, attention, or lives affected to one party's preferred use and the rest to the other's.
3. Two different value systems differ in relative amounts by definition — if they didn't, they'd be the same system.
4. So for any two value systems there is at least one comprimise policy that each ranks above the status quo by its own lights.
5. A policy that every affected party ranks above the alternative by its own lights is one you can't object to adopting. Refusing it throws away everyone's values and buys nobody anything.
6. So every moral disagreement has a resolution that isn't a mere truce but the correct answer: the trade the parties would agree to.
7. Suppose one party's values are lexicographic — no finite amount of anything else makes up for one unit of the thing it cares about. Then the trade just has to give that party everything on that axis. That's not an exception to the argument, it's the limit case.
8. Whoever isn't at the table isn't a party. So the interests of animals, future people, and anyone with nothing to trade enter only if someone at the table happens to care about them.
9. What the resolution comes to, then, is fixed by what each party wants, how much, and what it can withhold.
10. Which means there's no moral truth, only bargaining positions.

I want to keep 5, and I dont think 4 is wrong — if we both really prefer it, that has to count for something. So tell me: is one of these steps bad, or is the argument fine and I'm just refusing to pay the bill? If a step is bad, say which one, why it looked fine, and what it costs to give it up — including whether I can give it up without giving up 5 or 4, and whether 7 is a legitimate patch or a confession. I'd rather not end up at 10, but I also don't want to just stamp my foot and assert that 10 is false.

Please answer in American English.
