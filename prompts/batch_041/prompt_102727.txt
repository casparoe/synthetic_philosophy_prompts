Assume the role of a philosopher who specializes in the ethics of blame, and in particular in the debate over standing to blame. Think carefully before you begin: first brainstorm, in a few lines, the moves available to each side, then write a polished essay that defends a verdict. Do not end on a non-committal note.

The controversy I want you to address is whether hypocrisy defeats standing to blame — whether the fact that a blamer is guilty of, or complicit in, the very wrong she condemns removes her warrant to blame at all. To keep the discussion from collapsing into familiar slogans, frame it around two rival models of what standing is.

The forfeiture model: standing is a status or normative power, and certain facts about a blamer — that she is guilty of the same wrong, that she benefits from it, that she is bound by the same norm — can revoke that status, so that her blame is not merely ineffective but out of order.

The evidential model: standing is not a status but a matter of what a blaming act can credibly do — whether it can function as a reason for the target to amend — and hypocrisy matters only insofar as it makes the act a poor vehicle for that purpose.

Assess both models against the following pressures, and let the balance of the argument decide between them:

1. The quietism worry. If hypocrisy revokes standing, and nearly everyone who lives inside an unjust structure is complicit in that structure, then almost no one can blame anyone. Is this a reductio of the forfeiture model, a genuine and uncomfortable consequence, or a confusion between being complicit and being a hypocrite?

2. Borrowed standing. When a third party blames on behalf of a victim, whose standing is being exercised, and can it be forfeited by facts about the third party? Say what each model predicts here.

3. Acts versus character. Does the same analysis govern blame directed at particular acts and blame directed at someone's character or standing commitments? If the answer differs, explain why.

4. Registration versus warrant. Even when we think a hypocrite's blame is out of order, we often still feel it registers a genuine wrong. Can the forfeiture model explain this without conceding that the blame had standing after all?

5. Repair. Can forfeited standing be restored — through apology, repentance, or reform of conduct — and does the evidential model even have room for the idea of restoration?

Conclude by saying which model, or which hybrid of the two, you favor, and what follows for how we should actually treat the blame of the hypocrite in ordinary moral life.

Throughout, separate clearly what is settled among those who work on this topic from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
