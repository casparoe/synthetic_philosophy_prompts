I run a monthly political-philosophy reading group — fourteen adults, mixed backgrounds, one eighty-minute session. I want an activity that makes them feel the force of Chesterton's fence from the inside, and equally feel how the same argument can become a way of refusing to think. Don't let me explain the idea first: give them a set of inherited rules or arrangements to keep or abolish, distribute what each participant privately knows so that nobody can see any single fence's purpose in full, and build the mechanics so that the group's inability to distinguish a load-bearing fence from a merely customary one does the work. Include one fence that looks pointless but is secretly coupled to a later round, so the cost of removing it is felt rather than asserted, and one that looks load-bearing but isn't. Then give me the materials, the rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief that maps what happened onto the presumption in favour of inherited arrangements and onto the conditions that defeat it, the failure modes I should expect on a first run and how to handle them, and the one feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce. Under 900 words; no reading list.
