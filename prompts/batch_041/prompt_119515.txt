I am a third-year student taking a course in philosophy of mind alongside a seminar on machine learning, and I have become stuck on an argument that recurs both in class and among my friends: whether systems such as large language models can properly be said to have beliefs. What interests me is not the verdict so much as the shape of the disagreement, because the two camps seem to agree on nearly all the behavioural evidence and still never move. One camp, working from the idea that belief talk is licensed by the success of a predictive stance — what tends to be called the intentional stance — argues that if we can reliably predict a system's outputs by assuming it holds certain beliefs and desires and acts on them, then that is all belief attribution ever amounts to in the human case as well: the pattern is real, and the stance, rather than some hidden inner fact, is what makes intentional vocabulary correct. The other camp argues that the stance is too cheap to do that work, that the content comes from us rather than from the system, and that a system which will assert that p and then assert that not-p the moment a user objects, with no change in the evidence available to it, is not crediting anything about p at all. The appeals to training cut both ways, too: one side treats the fact that the system was trained to predict text as a debunking explanation of its apparent beliefs, while the other replies that human beliefs have a causal history of much the same kind, and neither remark seems to land.

My own hypothesis is that the two sides are working with different senses of belief — one predictive, on which a state earns the name by supporting good predictions, and one normative, on which a state must be answerable to the world and treated by the system itself as correctible — and that once the ambiguity is made explicit most of the dispute turns out to be verbal; a friend of mine insists it is not verbal at all, but a genuine disagreement about whether stance-dependent patterns can ever add up to realism about content, which no amount of evidence about language models could settle. I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal, a hidden empirical question about the internal states (whether they generalise, track evidence, and guide behaviour across contexts), a disagreement about the standards for calling something a real pattern, or two different questions being pursued under one heading. Assess my hypothesis and my friend's, engaging the positions as I have stated them rather than surveying the literature, and then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an empirical or formal result, a change of question — or why none should be expected.
