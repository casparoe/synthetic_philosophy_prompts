I keep meeting an argument about local conventions that seems to prove far too much, and I'd like help finding where it goes wrong. A colleague presents it as a point about fairness, and lays it out like this:

(1) A settled local convention is a cooperative scheme: participants form expectations around it, order their conduct by it, and often bear costs to sustain it.
(2) When someone knowingly departs from a settled convention, she imposes avoidable costs on those who ordered their conduct around it.
(3) Imposing avoidable costs on others is pro tanto wrong.
(4) So knowingly departing from a settled local convention is pro tanto wrong — whether or not the departing person endorses the convention or belongs to the community that keeps it.
(5) A visitor is governed by the conventions of where she is, not by the ones she brings from home.
(6) Therefore, where home and host conventions conflict, the visitor is bound by the host's — even when she finds the host's pointless, distasteful, or morally suspect.

He treats the following as clean instances of (6): a newcomer who drinks her share of the rounds in a pub where the regulars take turns buying for the group but never buys one herself; a traveler from a haggling culture who treats posted prices as final in a country where haggling is expected; and, more troublingly, a visitor in a place where petty bribery is the settled way of getting anything done, or where a convention assigns women a deferential role. I find the first two cases intuitively convincing and the third intuitively repugnant, and I cannot see what distinguishes them on his premises, since (4) says that settledness itself is what does the work.

If I have to surrender something, I would rather keep (3) — that imposing avoidable costs on others is at least pro tanto wrong — than keep (6), but I want to know what I would be paying. Which premise or step should I reject, why did it look so plausible, and what would each of the going positions have to concede to reject it? I am especially interested in whether this is just the fair-play account of obligation in miniature, and in what it would take to say that a convention simply does not reach a visitor rather than that it binds her and is outweighed — including whether there is a principled way to draw the line between conventions a visitor is inside and conventions she is merely adjacent to.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate the question.
