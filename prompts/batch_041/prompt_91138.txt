Bit of an odd one. I'm putting together a little reading-group thing where we time-travel historical philosophers into debates they never got to have, and I want your best guess at how one of them would actually react, not the polite textbook summary.

So: Thomas Hobbes, fully briefed, sitting in on a modern game theory seminar, being walked through something called program equilibrium. Here's the gist in case the term means nothing to you. Take the one-shot prisoner's dilemma — no repeats, no reputation, no third party, two strangers, play once. Normally defecting is the thing to do. Now suppose that instead of choosing directly, each player hands in a little program that decides what to play, and — this is the crucial part — each program is allowed to read the other program's source code before it commits to an action. The surprising result is that mutual cooperation becomes an equilibrium: a program that says cooperate iff the other program, upon reading it, will cooperate can hold its own against anything, because if the other side is built to cooperate with programs like mine we both cooperate, and if it's built to defect, defecting back is at least as good. No sword, no sovereign, no enforcement, just the fact that my decision rule is transparent to you before you move. There's a whole recent literature on this, including a version where the trick is a bit of self-referential logic rather than literal code-reading, but the code-reading version is the one I care about.

Hobbes had thoughts that seem to be about exactly this and also to be the opposite of this. He's got the Foole in Leviathan arguing that it can be rational to break a covenant when it profits you, and Hobbes's comeback isn't that it's intrinsically wrong — it's that you can't bank on getting away with it, that the breach tends to come back on you and tends to dissolve the commonwealth that keeps you alive. Then the famous move: covenants need a power to enforce them, otherwise their just words. And yet he also insists reason delivers real laws of nature, the first of which is to seek peace, and he thinks of reasoning itself as a kind of reckoning, adding and subtracting, so a program wouldn't strike him as a category error.

What I want: your honest prediction of what Hobbes would say about program equilibrium. Does it count as the mutual assurance he kept demanding, just delivered by transparency instead of by a sword — in which case the sovereign maybe isn't needed for this particular problem? Or does he say the setup smuggles in the one thing he thought you could never have, namely reliable knowledge of another man's intentions, and that in the real world the code is never legible, so the sovereign stands? Or does he think it's clever but beside the point, because the players who'd write those programs aren't the power-hungry creatures he was actually writing about? Pick the line you think is most likely and argue it as him, from the substance of his arguments rather than the textbook gloss. Don't hedge into he'd find it fascinating — commit.

Structure it like this please:

- Bottom line first (about 4-6 sentences): which way he lands, stated flatly.
- His own machinery (roughly 300 words): the parts of his view that push toward your answer, worked through rather than listed.
- The hardest objection (roughly 250 words): the strongest Hobbesian case against your answer, put in his voice.
- What he'd have to give up (roughly 200 words): if you're right, which of his commitments get bent or broken.
- If I'm wrong (roughly 150 words): the rival reading, and what would settle it.
