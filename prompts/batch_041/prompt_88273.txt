I'm a third-year student in a philosophy of science course and we've just finished a unit on verisimilitude — the idea that a theory can be closer to or further from the truth than another theory even when both are false, which is supposed to be what makes sense of scientific progress. A friend in my seminar built an argument that I've been chewing on all week and I genuinely can't tell if its a real result or a trick, so I want to lay it out for someone with more patience than my study group has left. It goes like this. (1) How close a theory is to the truth is fixed by its content: the true sentences it entails (its truth content) and the false sentences it entails (its falsity content), which is the definition we were given in class. (2) A is closer to the truth than B only if A's truth content is greater than B's and A's falsity content is no greater than B's. (3) Take any two theories A and B that are both false and neither of which entails the other, say two successive theories in the history of physics. (4) Since A is false and B doesn't entail A, A is itself a false consequence of A that B doesn't have, so A's falsity content isn't contained in B's; by the same token B's falsity content isn't contained in A's. (5) So there's no way for A to have strictly more truth content than B while also having no more falsity content — whatever truth a theory adds, it drags its own falsity along with it. (6) Hence no false theory is ever closer to the truth than any other false theory. (7) Every theory we've ever had is false, at best approximately true with idealisations baked in. (8) So later theories are never closer to the truth than earlier ones, and progress understood as getting closer to the truth is an illusion. He says the cases where one theory entails the other come out the same way, which I checked and I think he's right, annoyingly.

He thinks this kills the whole notion. My worry is that it proves too much: the same shape of argument would show that no false map can be more accurate than another false map (each imperfect map carries its own errors, so neither is better), which is obviously wrong — some maps are much better than others, and we can usually say which and why. So something in there has to give and I can't see what. I'd really rather not give up on (8) being false, because I want to be able to say Newton's theory is closer to the truth than Aristotle's, but I also don't see how to keep (1) and (2) if (4) is right. Someone in the seminar said the fix is to stop counting true and false sentences and instead measure how far the world a theory describes is from the actual world, but then you have to say how far one possible world is from another, and that sounds like it just relocates the problem — and probably makes hedgy or vague theories score well, which seems wrong. So: which premise or step would you drop, and what does dropping it actually cost me? If I give up the content-based definition, what do I get in its place, and what new problems do I inherit along with it?
