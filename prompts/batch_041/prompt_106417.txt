I want help turning a set of gut verdicts into a principle. The subject is standing to blame — the sense in which someone's blame fails to land not because they have the facts wrong, or because the act wasn't wrong, but because of who is doing the blaming. I don't have a theory in mind and I'm not interested in one that only fits the textbook cases; I want something that survives mine.

Here are the cases, with my reactions to each.

(A) A friend of mine has never had a drink in his life, and not because of any struggle — he simply has no taste for it and never has. He tells me my drinking is a problem, and means it as a rebuke. I take the rebuke at full strength. His never having been tempted costs him nothing with me.

(B) The same friend, in a variant: suppose he had told me that the only reason he doesn't drink is that it makes him violently ill, and that if it didn't, he would drink exactly as much as I do. Now his rebuke feels to me as though it has lost most of its force, even though everything he has actually done is identical in both versions.

(C) My brother, who drinks at least as much as I do, tells me I drink too much. He can say the sentence, but I cannot hear it as blame. Something is missing.

(D) My brother, after eight years sober, says the same sentence. It lands fully. That he used to drink the way I drink now seems to make it land harder rather than softer.

(E) I plagiarized a paper. My co-author on that paper — who knew what I had done and let his name go on it anyway — tells me that what I did was wrong and that he is disgusted with me. I cannot take this seriously at all, and not because he is mistaken about the facts.

(F) A colleague who plagiarized a paper five years ago, was never caught, and knows it, reports my plagiarism to the committee. I think he is within his rights to report me, and I think the committee should act on it. But when he then tells me that he is personally disgusted with me, I feel he has no standing to say that to me.

(G) A stranger at another university reads about my case in the student paper and sends me a long, furious message. I don't think I owe him anything — not an explanation, not a reply. If he wants to think badly of me, that is his business.

(H) My sister, who has never been on the receiving end of the way I treat our father, and who has her own tangled history with him, tells me I am treating him badly. She has full standing as far as I am concerned, even though she is not the one I have wronged.

(I) A friend who has never told a lie in his life but breaks promises constantly tells me that my lying is wrong. His promise-breaking does not touch his standing here, as far as I can feel.

(J) The person I cheated out of a prize — I won a competition by cheating and he came second — tells me I am a cheat. He also cheated, two years ago, in an entirely different competition. It does not occur to me to hold that against his standing here.

The principle I have been using myself, and doubt: you have standing to blame me for X only if you would accept the same blame from me — only if you would submit to the standard you are applying. The thought behind it is that blame is a demand that I answer to you, and that you can only demand an answer from me if you would have given one in my place.

Two places where I already feel it strain. In (E), my co-author would surely accept the blame if our positions were reversed — he considers himself as guilty as I am — and yet that does nothing to give him standing. In (B), the friend's conduct is identical to (A); only a counterfactual differs, and yet my verdict flips, which makes me suspect the principle is tracking what kind of person is doing the blaming rather than what kind of demand is being made.

I would also like verdicts on two cases that are not on my list. (K) Someone who has never done X before and is doing X right now, in the middle of it, tells me that my doing X was wrong. (L) Someone who has never done X and never would, but who blames me on behalf of a friend of theirs whom I wronged — a proxy blamer, with no wrong of their own to answer for and no wrong of their own suffered.

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order:

- **Summary** — two or three sentences: the principle you would defend, and the one verdict of mine it costs me.
- **The pattern** — one paragraph (roughly 100–150 words) on what my verdicts appear to be tracking, and where the genuine friction lies.
- **Candidate principles** — the main body, roughly 500–700 words. Two or three principles stated precisely enough to apply to a new case, with, for each clause, the case of mine that forces it.
- **Verdicts on further cases** — roughly 300 words: what each candidate says about (K) and (L), and about any further case you think separates them.
- **Costs** — roughly 150–200 words: for each candidate, the verdict or verdicts of mine it cannot accommodate, stated plainly.
- **Recommendation** — roughly 150 words: which candidate you would keep, or how you would repair it, and what I have to give up to hold it.
