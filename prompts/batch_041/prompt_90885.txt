I'm in a reading group that's been crawling through Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein, and we've stalled in the long stretch where he brings in quus to argue that nothing in your past behaviour settles whether you meant plus or some bent version of it. Trouble is, everyone in the group has already read the usual replies, so every session we just lob the same four objections back and forth and nobody's actually thinking anymore. What I want instead is a dialogue you write, between two people who both know this literature properly — one defending the meaning-sceptic line, the other defending the dispositionalist reply — but who have to fight it out over a fresh example I've made up, so neither of them can just recite the textbook moves. Neither of them should be a mug: I want each to land real hits, and I want each to actually answer what the other has just said rather than talk past them or politely wait their turn. If one has to concede something, let them concede it and then come back with something better.

Here's the example. Priya is an accountant, mid-fifties, has been doing sums her whole working life, and it happens that she has never once had to add two numbers coming to more than 999,999 — the figures she deals with just never get that big, and nobody has ever put such a sum in front of her. Now define a bent function, call it schmadd: for any pair of numbers adding up to 999,999 or less, n schmadd m = n + m, exactly what you'd expect; for any pair where the sum would go over 999,999, schmadd hands you 12. That's the whole rule. The bit I want them to chew on is this: the next time Priya meets a sum over the line — she's totalling a column, she gets to 1,000,004 — she's going to write down 1,000,004, and both of them know it, and both of them know the other knows it. The sceptic isn't going to deny that; the sceptic's move is that her writing 1,000,004 is just her disposition, not what her rule requires, and that she may simply be about to make her first slip from schmadd. Fine. But then the dispositionalist has to say something about what her dispositions actually are: only the ones she'll ever manifest, or the counterfactual ones too, the answer she'd give if you asked her? And if it's the counterfactuals, where do those come from and how are they meant to be finite and grounded without sneaking a rule back in under the table — because if the dispositions are specified by a rule, we've gone in a circle. And the sceptic shouldn't get off lightly either: they need to say what work schmadd is doing in the story at all, when the only thing anyone can point to is a rule Priya has never once had occasion to apply.

If they get that far, I'd love them to take up the nastier version — put the line at sums over 10 to the 30, which she will never in her life meet, so the two functions are indistinguishable not just in fact but forever — and work out whether the sceptical worry is still a genuine worry at that point, or whether it's quietly turned into a way of saying nothing at all. Could you write it out at about 1200 words? Make it feel like two clever people who quite like each other but have been at this for hours: interruptions, someone giving ground on a small point to win a bigger one, a bit of exasperation creeping in. They definately don't need to end up agreeing — I'd much rather one of them visibly shift position than have them shake hands on it.
