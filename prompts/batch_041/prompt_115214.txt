I'm an undergraduate writing a term paper for a philosophy of physics seminar on thermodynamics and information, and I'd like to read Thomas Pynchon's The Crying of Lot 49 through Maxwell's demon and the physics and philosophy of information. The scene I keep returning to is Oedipa Maas's visit to the inventor John Nefastis, who shows her a box with a piston that he says a working demon drives, explains the device by appeal to a balance between heat entropy and communication entropy, insists that entropy is a metaphor connecting thermodynamics to information flow, and claims that only a sensitive — someone able to communicate with the demon by concentrating on the picture attached to the box — can make it run. Oedipa stares at it through several cartoons, the piston never moves, and she leaves unsure whether the machine is broken, fraudulent, or merely waiting for someone better than her. You needn't quote the novel; I have the text in front of me and know the scene.

I want a reading that takes the physics as seriously as the novel. Is Nefastis's machine an error the physics has since corrected — the demon needs no mind and no sensitive, only a memory and the cost of erasing it — or does his emphasis on communication anticipate the measurement-based accounts of why the demon fails? The novel also runs two senses of information together: the plot is a hunt for meaning (does the muted horn signify anything, is Trystero real), while information theory is indifferent to meaning, and I want to know whether Pynchon exploits that equivocation or dramatizes a real gap. And the demon's local order must be paid for somewhere Oedipa can never see, which parallels the structure of her paranoia: order, if it exists, is always accounted for outside the frame. Where does that analogy hold, and where does it trade on a confusion between thermodynamic entropy and entropy as a cultural figure for decline?

Finally, say honestly where the reading resists — this demon is at least as much a figure for interpretation and for the reader as for the second law, and Pynchon is writing in the Szilárd–Brillouin era, before the erasure-based accounting most physicists now accept — and then what the novel adds that the physics and the philosophy of information do not: what it is like to live in a world where information is physical but meaning is not.
