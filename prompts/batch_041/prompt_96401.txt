I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have a story premise I would like you to develop. I am specifying it the way I would specify a system, because that is the vocabulary I have, but what I want back is a short story, not an explanation of the ideas behind it. I have done schema migrations before, and I know that a migration can be executed perfectly and still quietly lose something the old format was carrying; that is roughly the situation I want the story to be about.

The premise. A large teaching hospital adopts, over about five years, a "vocabulary migration" that its research office argues is required by the best current science of the mind. Every internal document — case notes, handover summaries, incident reviews, risk assessments, disciplinary files — must be rewritten in a replacement vocabulary. The banned words are the ordinary ones for what someone thinks, wants, intends, hopes, decides, means, or regrets. The replacements are graded states of the program's own design: expectation distributions, salience weights, action tendencies, prediction error, policy priors, arousal and valence vectors. The official justification is that the ordinary words name the theoretical entities of a folk theory that has failed the way phlogiston failed: it predicts poorly outside a narrow band of everyday cases, it has never integrated with anything else we know, and it now competes with accounts that predict better and connect to the rest of biology. When the research office is writing for the public, the position sounds more modest: the old words are a lossy compression scheme, fine for casual talk, unacceptable in a scientific record, and lossy in ways that have harmed patients.

The conflict. Your protagonist is a case manager on an inpatient ward — she writes the reports that discharge decisions rest on — and she has to render one specific event into the new vocabulary. A patient promised his sister, in front of two staff members, that he would take his evening medication, and then did not, and now says he meant to. In the new vocabulary this is expressible as a prediction distribution over future states, a measured drop in salience, an action tendency that failed to reach threshold. What is not expressible is the promise: the replacement vocabulary has no term whose function is to bind a future self to a present commitment, so nothing in the report can record that he broke something rather than merely deviated from a forecast. Her colleague, who supports the migration, says this is the point — that "he broke his word" was always a piece of folk metaphysics, and that the new report is simply more honest about what actually happened.

What I want the story to put pressure on, without ever stopping to explain it:

- The difference between two claims the research office keeps running together: that the old words name things that do not exist, and that they name things that exist but are not fundamental. Let a character slide between the two when it is convenient, and let the protagonist notice the slide without diagnosing it.
- The self-application problem. Every version of the case for the migration has to be written in the vocabulary the migration bans; there is no way to state the justification without saying that someone believes something. I want at least one scene in which this bites — a document that cannot be finished, a sentence that keeps reaching for the banned word.
- The compression argument cutting both ways. The new vocabulary is also lossy, and the losses fall in specific places: blame, testimony, apology, consolation, promises. Somewhere in the story a character should realize that the choice of vocabulary is a choice about which institutional purposes will be abandoned, and that the abandonment is invisible if you look only at predictive accuracy.
- The escape hatch, and why it does not hold. Someone proposes keeping the old words as a useful fiction for the human-facing parts of the work while the science goes on without them. Show why that division of labor collapses: the same sentence has to serve both purposes, or the fiction leaks back into the record, or the staff stop being able to keep two registers straight.

What I am asking for. A story of roughly 2,000 to 3,500 words, in a concrete setting, with one or two central characters and a single incident the narrative stays close to. The philosophical pressure should be carried by what the characters can and cannot say to each other, not by anyone explaining a position. No character should exist to state the thesis, and no one should win the argument. If there is a resolution, let it be a resolution of the protagonist's situation — what she writes, what she refuses to write, what she says out loud instead — rather than a verdict on whether beliefs and desires exist. Please do not cite or name any real researchers or works; the institution, the vocabulary, and the migration should read as entirely local to the story.
