I have just spent an evening arguing with a close friend about open borders, and the disagreement turned on a distinction I had not previously thought through with any care. My friend insisted that any defensible position must begin from the thought that the earth's surface was originally held in common, so that what stands in need of justification is exclusion rather than entry; I insisted that this proves far less than he takes it to prove, because a right to be received as a visitor is not a right to be received as a settler, and that the philosophically interesting question concerns what follows from the gap between those two. We ended the evening without agreement, and I would now like to work out the consequences more carefully than I could over dinner.

Please set out the most important implications of treating the distinction between a right of visitation and a right of residence as the hinge of the open-borders debate — the distinction that Kant's third definitive article in Perpetual Peace is usually read as drawing, though I am less interested in exegesis than in consequences. I am not asking for a survey of who has said what, nor for a reading list. I am asking what actually follows once the distinction is taken seriously, and where the argument becomes unstable. In particular, I want the implications traced for the following:

1. Whether a state may condition entry on the visitor's submission to its jurisdiction, given that jurisdiction over persons is ordinarily acquired through residence rather than through mere presence, and what this implies for the coherence of a right to visit that is exercisable only on the host's terms.

2. Whether a regime that admits visitors but offers no pathway to standing — so that admission generates a permanently provisional population — can still be described as hospitality, or whether it collapses into a hereditary caste system with a courteous name attached.

3. Whether the symmetry requirement is fatal. If the right to visit is genuinely universal, then every state is at once a host and a guest, and it is unclear whether the resulting obligations are mutual in substance or merely reciprocal in form.

4. What the distinction implies for the distribution of burdens between the excluded and the excluding party, and whether a position that treats exclusion as presumptively justified can survive a standpoint-reversal test — that is, a test requiring whoever proposes a given border regime to occupy the position of those the regime excludes, and to ask whether the regime remains acceptable from there.

I would also like you to distinguish, throughout, between a border's gatekeeping function and its sorting function, since I suspect that much of the debate conflates the two and that the visitation/residence distinction bears on them very differently.

Finally, tell me which of these implications you take to be the most consequential, and defend that ranking rather than merely listing the candidates. Be explicit about where the concept of hospitality is doing genuine conceptual work and where it is doing rhetorical work instead.
