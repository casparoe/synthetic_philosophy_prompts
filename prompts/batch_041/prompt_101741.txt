I am writing the final assessment for a master's-level seminar on longtermism and existential risk, and I need one essay question, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The essay should be answerable in ninety minutes, and the model answer should run to roughly six hundred words.

The difficulty I want is specific, so let me describe it. Candidates can already summarise Bostrom's simulation argument, and they can already recite the familiar worries about fanatical expected-value reasoning — the charge that minuscule probabilities of astronomically large outcomes are allowed to swamp everything else. What they cannot do is combine the two, because the combination is not something they will have read. Please therefore build the question around the following argument, which I have written out below and which I want reproduced in the question itself as an argument advanced by a classmate, left exactly as stated and with no hints appended:

'The reason fanaticism about the far future looks compelling is that our actions stand a small chance of influencing an astronomically large amount of value, and that value is located in the real world's long-term future. But Bostrom's argument gives us serious reason to think that we are living in a simulation. If we are, then the downstream consequences of our actions are confined to the simulation; the real world's far future is settled by the simulators, not by us. So the stake we thought we were playing for is not ours to influence at all. Once we discount for the probability that we are simulated, the expected value of far-future influence falls, and fanaticism is correspondingly dampened.'

The question should ask candidates to determine whether this argument succeeds. To stop it collapsing into an essay about simulation on the one hand or an essay about fanaticism on the other, I want the question itself to impose two constraints. First, it should require candidates to distinguish at least two senses in which fanaticism might be 'dampened' — a claim about how much value is at stake, and a claim about whether fanatical reasoning is a sound way to decide — and to say which sense the classmate's argument actually addresses. Second, it should require candidates to say what would have to be true for the argument to work, and whether those conditions can all hold at once.

I would like the marking scheme to be built around five must-hit points, worth twenty marks in total, with the marks for each stated and a one-line note on how candidates typically lose the mark on each. The model answer should name the specific moves that separate a first-class answer from a solid second, and it must not depend on citations, quotations or a reading list, since it may be used by a marker working without library access. Please write in British English throughout.
