Bit of an odd gig and I could use a hand with it. I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've somehow agreed to give the opening remarks — eight minutes, tops — at our city's citizens' assembly on immigration. The room: 120 people drawn by lottery from the electoral register, two councillors, the chair, a facilitator who has already asked me twice to keep it warm, and me.

Here's the wrinkle I want the speech to actually face. The remit includes whether residents who aren't citizens should get to vote in municipal elections. So the body deciding who belongs to the demos is drawn from the demos, and the people whose membership is up for grabs werent in the draw — they're not in the room, because the room is the register. I can't write one honest sentence that begins 'the people of this city have decided', and I also can't stand there and tell 120 randomly selected people that their whole exercise is a fix, because it isn't, and they'd rightly hate me. I dont want to solve the problem for them either. I want to hand them the shape of it — something they can carry into a weekend of arguing, not a riddle that shuts the room down.

So the circularity has to be doing real structural work. It should be the reason some sentences are in and others are out, not a clever aside I drop in the middle before getting on with the pep talk. And it has to land for people who've never read a word of political theory: no theorists, no studies, no book titles, no stats, no here's-how-other-cities-did-it, and none of the arc-of-history stuff. It's a mixed room — a retired bus driver, a nursing student, a guy who is clearly going to filibuster about parking, and a chair who will rule me out of order if I sound like I'm campaigning.

Can you write the eight minutes? First person, my voice, and the thing I keep circling is that any rule about who counts has to be applied by people whose own counting is exactly what's in question — I want that made concrete with what's actually in front of us, not announced as a paradox.
