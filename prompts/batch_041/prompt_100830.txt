In retirement I have been working through Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, and a popular treatment I was recently given summarizes the argument roughly as follows. Weber demonstrated that Calvinism caused modern capitalism: the doctrine of predestination left believers unable to know whether they were among the elect, and this anxiety drove them to prove their standing through disciplined worldly labor, which accumulated the capital that became capitalism; because Protestantism recast ordinary work as a calling, Protestant nations grew rich while Catholic ones did not; and since that capitalist spirit had no foundation other than the now-vanished religious beliefs, Weber expected it to fade away along with them. The summary then adds that Weber, as a champion of a value-free social science, held that values are simply not the sort of thing that can be cited as causes of anything. As a scientist by training who has come to this literature late, I suspect the account is at once too strong and internally confused, but I cannot put my finger on where. Please audit it clause by clause: tell me which parts a careful reader of Weber would accept, which are the ordinary simplifications of a more modest and heavily hedged thesis, and which Weber himself explicitly denied. I am particularly puzzled by the closing point, since the suggestion that he thought values cannot cause anything sits oddly with a book that seems to explain an economic order by appeal to religious values; I would like to understand how his views on explaining with values are meant to work, and whether the conclusions the summary draws survive once its misstatements are corrected.
