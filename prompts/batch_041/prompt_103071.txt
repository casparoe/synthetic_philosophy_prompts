I am putting together a proposal for a one-year postdoctoral research fellowship and would like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into a full proposal. The topic is the pair of puzzles usually called the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. My suspicion is that the two are routinely treated as a single problem — the apparent tension between high probability attaching to each member of a set of claims and the near-certainty that at least one of them is false — and that this assimilation is part of what has kept the debate circling. What I want to explore is the possibility that they come apart once one notices that the preface case, unlike the lottery case, essentially involves a higher-order attitude: the author's belief about her own fallibility as a believer. If that is right, then any solution that treats the two uniformly must itself be a higher-order solution — say, one framed in terms of epistemic self-trust, or of a division of epistemic labour within a single mind — and the familiar first-order fixes (raising or lowering a threshold for belief, or abandoning the conjunction principle outright) will handle at most one of the cases while leaving the other untouched.

Please write the proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the standard positions on each paradox leave that question open; set out the approach I would take and why it is preferable to the obvious alternatives; describe what a successful outcome would look like and, equally, what would count as the project failing; and give an honest assessment of scope for twelve months. I shall add the references myself, so please do not include a bibliography or cite particular works — refer to positions and arguments in general terms instead. Where your reasoning is most likely to fail, or where a consideration I have not raised might lead you to a different verdict, say so explicitly. Please write in British English.
