I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I serve as a public member of the collections ethics committee at a natural history museum. Next week the committee votes on a dispute that has split it three ways, and I have been asked to write the opinion that will frame the decision. Draft it for me.

The facts are these. The museum holds ancestral remains and funerary objects taken from a river-valley Indigenous nation about a century ago, along with a smaller set of objects the nation regards as sacred. The nation's council has asked for the return of all of it and for control over any future study of the remains. Two curators have a program that would use ancient DNA from the remains to test rival accounts of how the region was first peopled; they argue the science is important, that any destructive sampling would be minimal, and that results would be shared with the nation. The council rejects sampling of any kind and wants the remains reburied; at the same time it has asked the museum to fund a language-revitalization program and to help establish a cultural center, and it is open to a co-curation arrangement that would let the museum display some objects in partnership with the nation. My colleagues divide accordingly: one group would grant the council's claim outright; a second would hold out for research access, treating the collection as a public trust the museum may not simply hand over; a third wants a package in which the museum concedes the return and the money in exchange for research access and a long-term loan.

I want an opinion with a concrete recommendation, a bottom line the committee can actually vote on, and a time frame for acting. It should be candid about the strongest point on whichever side it rules against. Above all I want it to work through the structure of the disagreement rather than treat it as a collision of temperaments. The question that interests me is whether a genuine trade exists here — whether each side can give up something it values less in order to get something it values more, so that the deal leaves both better off — and, if so, what the deal should be. But take equally seriously the possibility that no trade is available, because one or both sides are holding some item as non-negotiable: a commitment not to be weighed against other goods at all, so that offering to trade it is not generosity but an insult, and any package that puts a price on it is a betrayal rather than a compromise. Say plainly which items in this dispute are being held that way, and whether that stance is defensible or merely a negotiating position. If a trade exists, spell out who gains what and whether the gains are real or rhetorical; if none does, recommend what the committee should do instead of pretending to split the difference. You may use the vocabulary of moral trade and of taboo trade-offs if it helps, but build the argument from the case rather than from appeals to who said what, and keep it to the length of a memo a busy board will actually read.
