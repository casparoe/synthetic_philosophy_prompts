OK so I keep getting into arguements with a friend about the is/ought gap, and I'm trying to figure out what I actually think by going case by case instead of citing anybody. Heres my gut on a pile of inferences, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or if I'm just being inconsistent:

- The knife is dull, so you ought to sharpen it. Fine.
- You promised you'd be there, so you ought to be there. Fine.
- The function of the heart is to pump blood, so a heart that can't pump blood is defective. Fine. (And notice there's no 'ought' in the conclusion at all, just a defect claim.)
- Eyes are for seeing, so you ought to use your eyes to see. Fine-ish, feels the same as the heart one.
- Humans evolved to be territorial, so we ought to defend our territory. Not fine. This feels like exactly the bad move people warn you about.
- Pain is bad, so you ought not to cause it for fun. Fine.
- A good teacher explains things clearly, so she ought to explain things clearly. Fine, but it feels like it's cheating somehow.
- Almost everyone in my town gives to the food bank, so you ought to give to the food bank. Not fine.
- He needs this medicine to live, so you ought to give it to him. Fine.
- He needs to be taken down a peg, so you ought to humiliate him. Not fine, even though it's the same word, 'needs'.

My tentative theory is this: the gap only shows up when the 'is' premise is purely descriptive. If the premise already smuggles in a norm -- a purpose, an institution like promising, a value like pain being bad -- then the ought was in there the whole time and nothing is really being derived from bare facts. So the gap is real, but it's about where the norm got in, not about a wall between two kinds of sentence.

Does that actually hold up? The one that worries me is the evolution case: if 'the heart is for pumping' carries a norm because it's a function claim, why doesn't 'humans evolved to be territorial' carry one too? And the teacher case feels fine but I can't tell whether that's because 'good teacher' is already normative or because of something else.

So: is there a principle, or a couple of competing ones, that gets all ten of these, or close? For each candidate, tell me which case forces the clause that's doing the real work, what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (something trading on 'natural' or 'normal' would be good -- e.g. 'this is what a normal person does, so you ought to do it', or 'it's natural for parents to love their kids, so you ought to'), and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save and which one you'd overturn. And if the fix is that 'function' has to mean something narrower than how I'm throwing it around, spell out the narrower thing.
