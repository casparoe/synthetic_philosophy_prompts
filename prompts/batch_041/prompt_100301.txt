I am a translator, and I have just been given a funded two-year working group — myself plus four doctoral students, five person-years of attention in total — with the freedom to choose what we work on. I have decided the area: the theory and practice of translation under formal constraint. By that I mean cases where a translator must reproduce some formal feature of the source — meter, rhyme, a refrain, a repeated line, a fixed syllable count, an acrostic, an alliterative pattern, a restriction on which letters may appear — and where that feature is not ornament but is carrying meaning, memory, or argument. The group's output will be a set of dissertations plus whatever I write myself, so the choice of question determines five careers for two years, not just a reading list. I want a defended agenda: what we should take up, in what order, and why that order rather than another.

Assume my students are people who translate and who are theoretically serious — comfortable with a book like Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot and with analytic work on meaning and interpretation — but not specialists in any single literary tradition, and not in a position to run corpus or experimental studies. Assume also that they cannot consult anything we do not already have on the shelf.

Below is my own sketch of the candidate questions. Please reorder them, cut whatever will not sustain five person-years, and add anything I have overlooked that would outrank what is there. For each item I want to know four things: why it is upstream of other questions in the area rather than downstream of them; whether it is tractable for people working alone with texts rather than for a team with data; how much the answer would change what translators actually do, teach, or accept as a good translation, as opposed to merely changing how they describe it; and what development over the next two years would move it up or down the list.

1. When a formal constraint simply cannot be satisfied in the target language, is there a principled line between translation and adaptation, or is the line a matter of professional convention and audience expectation? Does anything of theoretical weight actually turn on where it is drawn, or is the dispute purely about status?

2. When a formal feature is semantically loaded — a text written without a given letter, an acrostic that spells a name, a palindrome, a poem whose repeated end-words shift meaning with each repetition — is fidelity to the form a species of fidelity to the meaning, or a rival to it? Is there a general account of when form is content and when it is not, or only case-by-case judgment?

3. What happens to a constraint when it is transplanted into a language with different resources — no grammatical gender, a thin rhyme inventory, few monosyllables, obligatory honorifics? Is the transplanted constraint the same constraint? Is there any identity condition for formal features across languages, or is the very idea confused?

4. Many constraints come from somewhere other than the source: singability for a libretto, lip-sync and reading speed for dubbing and subtitles, line breaks for the printed page, a rhyme scheme demanded by a children's-book market. Is that the same theory as the source-derived cases, or a separate one about the ethics of a commission?

5. May a translator add a constraint the source does not have — imposing a meter, or a new acrostic, to make the source's own logic visible in the target? When is that interpretation and when is it betrayal, and does the distinction survive scrutiny?

6. Constrained writing is often produced under censorship or duress, where the form is doing work the surface cannot do. Is there a distinct obligation, or a distinct risk of over-reading, in translating such texts?

7. What does machine translation change here? If a system can be constrained to satisfy meter or syllable count, does that settle any conceptual question, or does it merely relocate the question to how the constraints are specified and weighted?

8. Is there anything general to be said about which losses, when a constraint is dropped, are compensable and which are not — a theory of compensation rather than the familiar lament that something is lost?

Tell me which one or two of these you would put at the top and why, which I should abandon outright, and which of my four criteria — upstreamness, tractability, practical consequence, and whatever else you think belongs on the list — should dominate when they conflict. I also want to know where the current conversation goes wrong: which of these questions is asked only because of a bad picture of what form is doing, and which is asked far too rarely.
