Two people in my set theory seminar got into it after class and I ended up standing there like a ref. (1) The first one said Russell's paradox isn't really a refutation of naive comprehension -- it only looks like one because we smuggle in the assumption that the set R is itself available to be tested for membership in itself. His fix: keep comprehension, just say sets get built in stages, so at any stage you collect everything satisfying a condition out of the sets you've already got. Then R is a perfectly good set, it's just not one of the sets it collects, so "is R a member of itself?" has the boring answer no, and the contradiction never gets off the ground. (2) The second one said that's not saving comprehension, that's quietly replacing it -- "out of the sets you've already got" is a restriction, and the whole point of naive comprehension was that there's no restriction. So the most he's shown is that some different theory is consistent, not that naive set theory survives. (3) He came back with: every fix to a paradox restricts something, so the question is whether the restriction is motivated or ad hoc, and his is motivated, since sets are things we build and nobody ever had a clear notion of "any collection whatsoever" floating free of a domain. He said she was treating a vague slogan as if it were an axiom we're obliged to keep. (4) She said fine, but then the honest way to state the result is that the paradox is a negative existence proof -- no set of all sets, no set of all non-self-membered sets, same as there's no biggest number -- and his staged picture just agrees with that instead of dissolving anything. She added that his stages buy consistency by assuming no set contains itself, which is exactly the kind of thing the paradox was supposed to force us to argue for rather than assume. (5) He said the same move handles the set-of-all-ordinals one and the largest-cardinal one, and one story covering all of them beats treating Russell's as a freak bad definition; if "there's no such set" is the whole answer, you still owe an account of why there's no such set when there is a set of all the natural numbers.

Can you go through this arguement move by move and say who's right about what, and what both of them are missing? Please don't just declare a winner overall. Under 450 words, and for each move give a one-line verdict first, then the justification.
