My mate in our ethics reading group has a move I can never quite answer, and I want to know whether it actually works. Whenever someone raises the worry that consequentialism asks far too much of you, that it wants you to keep giving until there's basically nothing left of your own life, your projects, your friendships, the things you'd have done anyway, he says: look at parents. Parents give up sleep, money, careers, whole decades, and nobody says parenthood is a broken ideal or that it's too demanding to be a standard anyone could actually live by. We say that's what love costs and the person who does it is admirable. So the fact that morality asks something similar isn't an argument against morality at all, it just tells you how much the world needs. Then he tightens it: impartial morality only asks you to treat everyone's kid the way you already treat your own, and we've all watched people meet that without turning into wrecks.

How far does it hold? Start with the mapping, and spell it out in plain prose — I know a table is the obvious way to do it but I'd rather have it wrote out as sentences, no headings, no bullets, no bold, nothing like that — so who or what plays the parent, the child, the love, the sacrifice, the years, the other parent. Then where it comes apart. What I really want is what the parenthood case quietly brings with it that the other one doesn't: whether the demand ever ends, whether it's shared with anyone, whether the motive that makes it bearable is one the moral theory can actually endorse or only put up with as a means, whether the sacrifice is partly for something that's also part of the parent's own good. And nobody thinks a parent who keeps something back for herself is a monster, so what does that tell us.

Then tell me straight whether the conclusion he's after survives all that — that the demandingness worry just evaporates once you see how much people can bear when the cause is good — or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time. And if this is the wrong vehicle, what would a better analogy be for the thing he's actually trying to say. One more thing he waves away as well: whether it matters that the demand lands on each of us separately rather than on all of us together, and whether a demand that's unbounded at every single moment is different in kind from one that's merely large.
