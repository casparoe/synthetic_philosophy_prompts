a friend and I run a small decision-theory reading group (grad students, mostly econ and philosophy) and next session is on the Allais paradox, and instead of a summary I want to hand out a decision tree. The reason is that I think the literature, and our last discussion, keeps conflating three seperate things under the name "the independence axiom." Concretely: in the standard setup you first choose between (A) $1M for sure and (B) an 89% chance of $1M, a 10% chance of $5M, and a 1% chance of nothing; then between (C) an 11% chance of $1M plus an 89% chance of nothing and (D) a 10% chance of $5M plus a 90% chance of nothing. Most of us pick A and D, and the usual gloss is "that violates independence." But I think the violation could be located in any of: (i) the independence / sure-thing axiom itself, (ii) the assumption that a lottery is fully captured by its final probability distribution over outcomes, so that the 89% common consequence in the first problem and the 89% of nothing in the second can be "cancelled" against each other, or (iii) the assumption that preference is defined over prospects rather than over descriptions or acts. And which one you blame probably determines whether you say the chooser is irrational or the axiom is wrong. Can you build me a tree where every internal node is a question a reader can answer about the cases without already knowing the labels, and every leaf is a named position?

The leaves I need on it: plain expected utility plus "A&D is just a mistake" (the Savage-style line); non-linear probability weighting (the prospect-theory / rank-dependent family); outcome-dependent stories like regret and disappointment, where what you feel depends on what the foregone option would have paid; the "it's all framing, the two problems aren't the same decision" response; and the deflationary "decision theory should describe, the paradox only shows EU is descriptively false" view. Then add whatever leaves the logical space forces that I haven't listed — including the one I'm most curious about, the person who says A&D is fine and the real lesson is that the axiom is false, but only for money-type goods, because they think it's a diminishing-marginal-utility story. I want the tree to make clear that that particular escape route is closed (no utility function at all, curved or straight, can generate a strict A&D pattern), so anyone routed into it gets flagged as having made an arithmetic error rather than sent to a position. Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't seperate, and if a node only splits two positions that never actually come apart on the cases, say so. One page, maybe 6-8 leaves, and phrase every question as what the person would choose or say about a case, not as which camp they belong to.
