I clerk for the truth commission here — we came out of the military regime in the 90s, and the amnesty law just says amnesty can be granted where it serves reconciliation, which has turned out to mean whatever the panel wants it to mean on the day. I write alot of the first-pass memos on applications, and I'm supposed to flag when we're about to contradict ourselves. Next month's batch has one I cant call, so I'd like your read before I write it up.

Here's how the panel has actually ruled so far.

A conscript private fired into a crowd of protesters on his lieutenant's order and killed three people. He named the lieutenant, who up till then wasn't on our list at all. At the hearing he said he'd do it again. Amnesty granted.

The colonel who wrote the internal directive that set up the reprisal campaign testified for two days, courteous and complete, but we already had the directive and his signature on the covering memo, so he gave us nothing we couldn't have pulled from the archive ourselves. He apologized and said he'd carry it the rest of his life. Denied.

A sergeant in the unit that ran one of the holding sites, who beat people there himself on his captain's instructions, told us where a second site was — we didn't know about it — and named the two officers who ran it. Granted.

A major in the logistics branch who signed off on the vehicles and fuel that went to the reprisal units cooperated fully, gave us nothing new, and showed no sign he thought any of it mattered. Denied.

A lieutenant who commanded a reprisal squad and was standing there while his men did the killing: his account matched the files we already had, and he never named anyone above him. Denied.

The written reasons read like they came from two different panels. Some of them go on about new information and the state of the record; others go on about whether the applicant stood at the origin of the policy or was only an instrument. There's one commissioner who files a separate note about contrition every time, though the private who'd do it again got amnesty, so I've stopped trying to make that add up.

The applicant next month is a captain who commanded a company through the worst of it, picked targets, and was present at the killings. He's handing over a notebook that names the brigadier who set the whole campaign up, and we've never identified the brigadier, so it's genuinely new. He shows no remorse whatsoever.

What does the panel do with him? I care less about the yes or no than about the rule they're actually running — give me the rules that fit the five cases above, what each one predicts for the captain, which you'd bet on and why, and what case I should be watching for that would actually settle which rule it is. If two readings fit the old cases equally well, say so; I'd rather know that than get a confident answer. And please dont give me a seminar on restorative justice, I need something I can put in the memo.
