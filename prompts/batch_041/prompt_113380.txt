I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years teaching himself philosophy, and I have become rather gripped by a dispute at the junction of probability, decision theory and the theory of rationality. The dispute concerns what its partisans call geometric rationality: the claim that in any repeated, multiplicative setting — a sequence of bets, a portfolio, a career, a life — the rational policy is not to maximise expected wealth or expected utility calculated over an ensemble of possible worlds, but to maximise the expected logarithm of wealth, or equivalently the long-run geometric growth rate. The Kelly criterion is the best-known practical expression of this idea, and its defenders sometimes make a stronger claim than that it is a useful heuristic: they suggest that ordinary expected-value reasoning is, in such settings, not merely imprudent but incoherent, because it follows an ensemble average that almost no individual trajectory will realise, whereas the logarithmic rule tracks what happens to a single agent over time. I want you to write an essay on this controversy. Weigh the case for treating geometric rationality as a requirement of reason against the case for treating it as one utility function among many — a defensible attitude to risk, perhaps, but not a dictate of rationality as such — and reach a definite conclusion, justified throughout, rather than ending in a survey of positions.

Please make the argument concrete. Consider the St Petersburg game and its finite-bankroll variants; the difference between time-average and ensemble-average growth; the fact that logarithmic maximisation can counsel against a bet with positive expected value and in favour of insurance with negative expected value; the roles of diminishing marginal utility, bounded utility and finite horizons; the practice of fractional Kelly betting; and the possibility that the geometric-rationality argument establishes only prudence, not obligation. I am especially interested in whether its defenders illicitly move from the claim that a policy almost surely wins in the long run to the claim that it is rationally required now, and whether an agent who cares about other people, or about goods other than wealth, or who will not live long enough for the long run to arrive, can coherently reject it.

Please also say what, if anything, the St Petersburg paradox shows about the dispute, and whether it supports the geometric view or instead undermines the whole expected-utility framework. I am not asking for a literature review or a reading list; I want the argument. The essay should be in British English, with technical terms explained clearly enough for a philosophically interested scientist, and with your verdict stated near the beginning and defended to the end.
