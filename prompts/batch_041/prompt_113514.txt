I'm preparing a session for an undergraduate course on nations and nationalism, and I want to use Braveheart as the case study, read through Hobsbawm on invented tradition and Anderson on imagined communities. The film is a gift to the theory and a problem for it at once. It is set in the 1290s but was made in the 1990s; it dresses its medieval hero in a kilt and woad; its early scenes have Edward I grant his nobles the right of the first night, a privilege most historians regard as legendary; and it is adapted not from the chronicles but from a fifteenth-century verse epic that is itself a legend-builder. Yet it also stages a nation that already feels itself to be one: Wallace's commoners and the contrite Bruce are folded into a single people, and the epilogue at Bannockburn, nearly a decade after Wallace's execution, hands the hero's cause to the king he never served. Give me a reading that takes the film and the theorists equally seriously: where does the film dramatise the thesis that nations manufacture a usable past, where does it merely illustrate it, and where does it resist it? Which scenes carry the philosophical weight — the fabricated legal tradition, the tartan and the woad, the nobles' betrayal at Falkirk, the execution, the sword planted at Bannockburn — and which expose the limits of the lens? Be candid where the lens distorts: the film is a Hollywood revenge epic and a star vehicle, not a state ritual, and the claim that the kilt and the clan tartans are eighteenth- and nineteenth-century inventions is itself contested. Close by saying what the film shows about how nations are imagined that the argument alone cannot — in particular whether cinema, rather than print, is now the medium Anderson was describing, and whether the emotional pull of the film tells against the modernism of the theory.
