Good's theorem in decision theory holds that, for a Bayesian agent who maximizes expected utility, the expected value of free and reliable information is non-negative: before receiving a signal, the agent cannot expect to be worse off for having the option to condition on it. This result is often taken to show that information is always beneficial, or at least never harmful, from the standpoint of practical rationality. I want you to argue for a position that challenges this broader reading. Specifically, argue that the theorem's non-negativity result depends on a decision-theoretic conception of value that need not coincide with epistemic value, and that once we distinguish the two, we can describe agents for whom the expected epistemic value of information is negative. To make this concrete, consider an agent whose epistemic goal is not to maximize expected accuracy but to maintain a coherent, stable, and well-integrated belief system, and who treats belief revision as itself costly—not in the sense of material cost, but in the sense that each revision imposes a burden of re-establishing coherence across the rest of her beliefs. Argue that for such an agent, a free and reliable signal can have negative expected epistemic value, even though the signal would have non-negative expected decision-theoretic value. Then address the objection that this merely changes the agent's utility function and so does not challenge Good's theorem. In your response, be precise about what the theorem does and does not claim, and about whether the notion of epistemic value you are invoking is genuinely distinct from decision-theoretic value or is simply a special case of it.
