My reading group has spent two sessions on Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility, and I have found myself more troubled by a reconstruction of it produced by a friend than by the argument in its familiar form. Her version makes the load-bearing moves explicit, then carries the result further than I am prepared to go. It runs as follows.

(1) In any given situation, you act as you do because of the way you are.
(2) So to be ultimately responsible for what you do, you must be ultimately responsible for the way you are — at least in those respects of character, motive, and outlook that explain why you acted as you did.
(3) Transfer principle: if you are not ultimately responsible for some condition C, and C is among the causes of your action A, then you are not ultimately responsible for A.
(4) You are not ultimately responsible for the way you are. Your temperament, your evaluative outlook, and your habits of attention were fixed by heredity and by early circumstance, and you chose none of them.
(5) The self-making reply is closed off. Suppose you were responsible for your character because you had shaped it. Then some exercise of your own agency must have produced it, and that exercise would itself have issued from a character you did not make; the regress never terminates in anything for which you are ultimately responsible.
(6) Therefore no one is ever ultimately responsible for anything they do.
(7) "Ultimately responsible" is not one grade of responsibility among several, a stronger cousin of an everyday notion. It is the everyday notion, made explicit. Whatever is left once ultimate origination is subtracted — answerability, say, or a social status, or a piece of moral bookkeeping — is not responsibility but a substitute for it.
(8) Blame, praise, gratitude, and resentment are fitting only toward those who are responsible for what they do. So by (6) and (7) no one is ever a fitting target of any of them.
(9) The same reasoning covers belief. You did not choose your evidence-gathering habits or your prior credences, so you are no more responsible for believing as you do than for acting as you do.

I can live with (6); I am not much invested in ultimate responsibility. What I cannot accept is (8), and the ease with which (9) follows it. Still, I would rather keep (8) than (2), and I notice that (4) and (5) look to me like the most solid steps in the chain — I simply do not know what becomes of them if (2) goes.

Tell me first whether the chain is valid as it stands, and then which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. Say why the step looked plausible in the first place, so that I can see what it was about it that a reasonable person would have found compelling rather than merely convenient. If the step you would give up is (7), tell me what the ordinary notion of responsibility is supposed to be if it is not a consolation prize, and whether the repair holds at (9) or has to be made twice over, in different currencies, for the practical and the epistemic case. If instead the trouble lies in (2) or in the transfer principle, say what has to replace it and what that replacement forfeits elsewhere — in particular, whether it can still make room for the thought that a person's history and formation bear on what they are answerable for. I do not need a survey of the free will literature and I do not need references; I want the diagnosis, the reason the diagnosis is not ad hoc, and the price.
