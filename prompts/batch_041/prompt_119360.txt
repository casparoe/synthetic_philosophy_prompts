I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a book set in an 1840s port town, and I've spent most of the last year in county record offices and one very cold diocesan archive. The trouble isn't scarcity, its the gaps, and alot of the time I'm deciding what to do about them by vibes. Three that are currently eating me: a wet nurse whose name appears once in a foundling hospital ledger and never again; dock labourers who exist in the record only as numbers in a shipping company's accident book, with nine years where the book is simply gone; and a 1930s field notebook in which the family being visited asked the ethnographer to write nothing down, which he did, and then kept the page.

I want a procedure I can run on a gap in about twenty minutes, before I draft a scene over it, because I'll be doing this a dozen more times before I'm done. What to ask first, what counts as an answer, where it stops with a no, and where it stops because it's a value call and I'm the one who has to make it. Ground it in what people who study archives and power actually know about why records have the shape they do — who gets written down, by whom, to what end, and what a silence is a trace of — but I don't want a reading list, just the machinery.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up, and as each point comes up, say what it does to whichever of my three gaps it lands on. Even if the answer for the third one is that I have to leave it empty — I suspect I won't like that, and I'd rather hear it now.
