I teach a seminar on moral status, and I keep returning to a pattern in our institutions that I have not been able to get to the bottom of. Consider the way the law and the research bureaucracy draw the line of concern for animal welfare. In most jurisdictions the relevant statutes, and the committees that implement them, attach to vertebrates. A few jurisdictions have extended the scheme, selectively, to a small number of invertebrates — cephalopods, and in some places decapod crustaceans — while the overwhelming majority of invertebrates, including the insects, mites, and nematodes that we rear and kill in astronomical numbers, fall outside it altogether. A laboratory that may not house a mouse without environmental enrichment, anesthesia, and a formal justification of any suffering will, in the same building and under the same committee's gaze, preside over the deaths of countless insects with no welfare requirement whatsoever. The ground usually given for the scheme is sentience: it is the capacity to suffer that brings a creature within the ambit of moral concern.

I would like an analysis of what this practice presupposes. Not a survey of animal ethics; a reconstruction of the implicit theory.

First, does the practice presuppose that sentience is a threshold property, so that there is a fact of the matter about which creatures are in and which are out, with vertebrates (plus the recent exceptions) happening to fall on the right side? Or does it presuppose that sentience comes in degrees, and that the vertebrate line is a tractable proxy for a continuum? Be explicit about which reading the practice actually needs and which it merely fits well with.

Second, test the practice against the reform I find most tempting. Replace the categorical line with a graded schedule of presumptive weights: each taxon receives a weight, the weight is revised as evidence accumulates, and protocols are scaled accordingly, with more enrichment, more anesthesia, and a heavier burden of justification for the higher-weighted creatures. Some of my students go further and propose that sentience is a cluster of dissociable capacities — nociception, affective valence, a representation of one's own states over time, the capacity for frustration or anticipation — each of which may be present or absent independently, so that moral considerability is a function of a profile rather than a threshold. Does the existing practice rule this out, or is it compatible with it? If it is compatible, what, if anything, does the practice strictly presuppose?

Third, the far end of the scale. A thermostat responds to its environment, regulates itself, and has internal states that covary with conditions in the room. If sentience is a matter of degree, what occupies the zero point, and what stops the scale from extending smoothly into the artifact? Is the practice's silence about insects the same silence it keeps about thermostats, or a different one?

Finally, tell me what would have to change in the institution — not in the moral theory — if we took gradation seriously, and where the practice is underdetermined, in the sense that several incompatible theories would license exactly the regulations we now have.
