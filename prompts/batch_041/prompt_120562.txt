Ok so I've been chewing on this on my own for months and I keep going in circles, so I'm asking for help getting my head straight. Every time the question of whether something was really consented to comes up, people reach for the same three words -- informed, voluntary, competent -- and then when you ask what any of those actually means, the answer turns out to be smuggled in from how bad the person already thinks the act was. I want to stop doing that. Before I pick a side on any particular case, I want the spec. I think the fancy word for this is desiderata, but I mean the checklist: what does any theory of valid consent have to be able to do before I'd call it adequate? I dont have an academic background so skip the isms and the name-dropping and just give me the requirements, with the case or consideration behind each one.

Purpose first, so "adequate" means something: the thing I actually care about is the everyday question -- did this person agree to this, and does their agreeing settle whether what was done to them was okay? Medicine and sex interest me more than commercial contracts, but I dont want a theory that just declares those areas special and walks away.

Cases it has to get right, with a verdict that doesn't dodge:

- A man with early dementia says yes to a drug trial Monday, no on Tuesday, yes again Wednesday, and on all three days can explain the trial correctly.
- Someone sleeps with a man because he told her he'd had a vasectomy. He hadn't.
- A boxer agrees to the fight and ends up with brain damage.
- Someone takes a genuinely dangerous job at a wage they'd never accept if they had any other offer, fully understanding the risk.
- A patient signs the form, but the person who operates is a doctor they never met.
- Someone says "okay" while crying, and the other person goes ahead.
- Someone agrees to sex, changes their mind halfway, and their partner keeps going.
- Someone clicks "I agree" on forty pages of terms they never read.
- Someone agrees to a procedure believing it's reversible when it isn't, and nobody corrects them.
- An advance directive: they signed a written yes years ago to a treatment they're now refusing while still sharp as a tack.
- Someone agrees to sex because their partner will be devastated otherwise. No threat, just guilt.
- Someone agrees to sex for money or a place to stay.
- Someone drunk says yes.

Distinctions it can't collapse, with why each matters:

- "she consented" vs. "she complied" -- agreeing vs. not resisting.
- A valid yes vs. the act still being wrong anyway. A real yes to a beating is still a beating.
- Never consented vs. consented and withdrew it. Those are three states, not two.
- A threat vs. a lousy option: "your money or your arm" vs. "take this job or you can't make rent." Where's the line, and is it about how bad the alternative is or about who made it bad?
- Lying about the thing itself vs. lying about something else -- lied about being sterile vs. lied about being rich.
- Competence as an on/off switch vs. a dimmer.
- Consent as something you do (a word, a nod, a signature) vs. something you think or feel.
- Valid consent vs. morally transformative consent -- does a good yes make it okay, or only make it not a violation?

Things it can't contradict:

- People consent to things they dont want and dont enjoy. Consent isn't desire, and a theory that fuses them is dead on arrival.
- Consent to sex has to be withdrawable mid-stream; consent to a contract usually isn't. So either the theory is domain-specific or it owes me an account of why that difference isn't arbitrary.
- You can validly consent while being wrong about your own interests -- the boxer, the guy taking the job.
- You can't consent to what you know nothing about, but nobody reads the terms. "Enough information" needs a line that isn't just whatever the party with the power chose to disclose.
- If the theory makes ordinary sex or ordinary medical care mostly non-consensual, that's a cost. Maybe a cost you pay, but say so out loud.
- It has to be usable in real time by someone who has to decide now -- a nurse at 3am with a patient who's fading.

Then the part I most want: which of these requirements do you think can't all hold at once, and which ones are just one camp's prejudice dressed up as a requirement? I already suspect the demand that we define coercion and competence without first judging the act wrong crashes into the fact that any baseline for "coerced" seems to need a moral baseline; and that revocability-anytime crashes into contracts and advance directives. But tell me if I'm wrong. Also, which single requirement does the most work at killing off the usual theories -- the rights-waiver picture, the mental-state picture, the "consent is when the act is okay" picture? Keep the weight on the requirements themselves; I'll do the scorecard on the contenders myself. A list or a table is fine, doesnt have to be an essay.
