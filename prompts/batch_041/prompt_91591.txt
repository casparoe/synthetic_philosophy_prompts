I'm in a political theory seminar and we just spent three weeks on Arendt, and a friend and I built an arguement at a bar that I now can't get out of, so I'm hoping you can find the bad step for me.

Here's the picture as I understand it. Arendt's point about Eichmann isn't that he was a monster or a sadist, it's that he was frighteningly ordinary. A mediocre joiner, a man who talked in stock phrases and followed rules, who never really stopped to think about what he was doing. And she thinks that's the whole horror: the evil wasn't demonic, it was thoughtless. She also thinks he's a new kind of wrongdoer, one who doesn't fit the usual picture of a criminal who knows he's doing wrong. And she definately doesn't let him off the hook, she thinks the verdict was right and he got what he deserved. I want to keep all of that, the banality thesis is the only thing that makes the twentieth century make any sense to me. So here's the argument my friend built, and I can't find the flaw:

(1) On Arendt's account, the thing that defines Eichmann is that he didn't think, he never reflected on what he was doing, never asked whether it was wrong, never pictured it from the victims' side.
(2) You're only morally responsible for an act if you had the capacity to reflect on it, to ask whether it was right, to see it from other people's point of view.
(3) On (1), Eichmann didn't have that going on at the time. The thinking just wasn't happening.
(4) If at the time of acting you lacked the capacity that makes you responsible, then you're not responsible for the act.
(5) So Eichmann isn't morally responsible for the deportations.
(6) And nothing in (1) through (4) is special to him. Everyone who does something terrible without reflecting on it fits the same description, the ordinary men who did the shooting, the clerks who filed the paperwork, the guards who were just doing a job.
(7) So the banality thesis says the ordinary perpetrators weren't responsible either, only the handful who planned it, and even they were mostly following the same bureaucratic logic.
(8) Which is absurd, and it's the exact opposite of what the thesis was for. It was supposed to show how ordinary people can be guilty of monstrous things, not that nobody is.

Something has to give and I don't want it to be (8). My friend says the answer is to drop (2), that responsibility doesn't require any kind of thinking at all, but then I don't know what grounds responsibility, and it feels like we'd be back to blaming people for things they couldn't help. My roommate says it's just a word trick and that it comes out clean if you're careful, but every time I try to be careful I lose the thread. So: which premise or step do I actually reject, why did it look so obviously fine when I wrote it down, and what do I have to pay to reject it? And if you think the thing is valid and I just have to eat one of the costs, tell me which one is cheapest and be honest about the bill.
