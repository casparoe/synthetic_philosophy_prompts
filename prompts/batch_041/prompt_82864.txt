A friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about the tragedy of the commons and got nowhere, and I would like you to tell me what kind of disagreement we were really having. He maintains that the parable of the overgrazed pasture was refuted long ago by the fieldwork on communities that successfully govern their own fisheries and forests; I maintain that this work shows only what happens when you relax the model's assumptions, and that nothing in it falsifies the underlying logic, which was never a claim about how people actually behave. We agree about every case either of us can name, and we agree that monitoring and punishing free-riders is itself a public good — which, as he points out, ought on the theory to be badly under-provided, and plainly is not. My suspicion is that we are partly talking past one another because the word 'commons' names two different things — a kind of good, defined by rivalry and the difficulty of exclusion, and a set of arrangements or practices for governing one — and partly disagreeing in earnest about what an economic model asserts and about what would count as refuting it.

Please diagnose the dispute rather than adjudicate it: which parts of our standoff are merely verbal, which turn on a hidden empirical or formal question, which are disagreements about what a satisfactory explanation has to do, and whether either of us is answering a question the other is not asking. Then tell me what, if anything, would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a case, or a change of question — or say plainly that none should be expected, and why. Write in British English.
