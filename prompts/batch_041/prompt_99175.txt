I have just spent the better part of a week arguing with an old friend about the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I came away with the uncomfortable suspicion that my own solution to it does more work by stipulation than by argument. In a week I have to present that solution to a workshop, as a positive proposal rather than a survey, and I would much rather find its weak points now than in the question period.

Here is the position I will defend. The fitting-attitude analysis of value — roughly, the view that for something to be valuable is for a pro-attitude toward it to be fitting — is widely thought to founder on the wrong kind of reasons: the demon who threatens to destroy the world unless I admire him gives me a reason to admire him, but plainly does not make him admirable. I argue that the analysis survives, but only once we separate two things the literature tends to run together, namely the fittingness of an attitude and the fittingness of the act of forming it. The demon's threat is a reason addressed to me as an agent, a reason to bring about a certain state of myself; admirability, by contrast, is a reason addressed to the object, a consideration that the attitude itself presents as counting in favor of its object. I claim this distinction is not circular, because the object-directed content of an attitude — what it is about, and what it presents as speaking in favor of its object — can be characterized without invoking the very notion of value the analysis is meant to illuminate. To bring the point into focus I use a case I have not seen discussed: a demon who threatens me unless I admire him, where the admiration he demands is, as it happens, entirely fitting, since he really is admirable. I argue that his threat adds nothing to the fittingness of the admiration, which shows that the reason his threat supplies and the reason his admirability supplies are two reasons of different kinds even when they converge on the same attitude. I extend the same distinction to pragmatic reasons for belief, claiming that they are reasons to want to believe rather than reasons to believe.

The audience is a value-theory workshop of about twenty people, and I need the prep sheet calibrated to them rather than to the field at large. Two of them are committed fitting-attitude theorists who regard the wrong kind of reasons problem as fatal and have defended that verdict for years; they will hear my distinction as a relabeling and will know every counter-move in the literature. One is a Humean who thinks the vocabulary of fittingness is a category mistake and that every reason is ultimately a reason for desire, so he will not grant my distinction the ground it stands on. One is a Kantian constructivist who will want to know why a fitting attitude has any authority over me that is not simply the authority of my own will, and who will suspect that I have smuggled value back in through the notion of content. The rest are graduate students and interested onlookers, several of whom will ask the plain question that does the most damage.

I want a question-and-answer preparation sheet, not a summary of my view. Order the objections I will actually face by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it — likelihood multiplied by severity, not by how interesting the objection is to me — and phrase each one the way the person who holds it would actually put it, in their vocabulary rather than mine. For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes, then tell me candidly where that reply is thin, what I should concede outright, and which point I should defend rather than hope goes unnoticed. Give the two fitting-attitude theorists' objections in the form they would press them, since they will do the most to shape how the room reads me. Include the objection my friend made that I still have not answered, and end with the question I am hoping nobody thinks to ask.
