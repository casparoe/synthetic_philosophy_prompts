After a long career in molecular biology, I have retired and begun sitting in on a philosophy of law seminar, where I keep running into a puzzle about the ownership of ideas that I cannot resolve on my own. In my old field, a published hypothesis or a newly noticed sequence motif is treated as part of a commons: colleagues may build on it freely, and the failure to cite the source is generally regarded as a lapse in scholarly manners rather than as theft. Patent law seems to assume something much stronger, since an inventor may be granted exclusive rights over a new and non-obvious way of doing things, and in the contested practice of granting patents on isolated naturally occurring DNA sequences, over a substance that many scientists regard as discovered rather than made. Copyright appears to occupy a third position: it protects the particular expression of a text, diagram, or program while leaving the underlying idea, fact, or method free for anyone to use. I would like you to reconstruct the theory of intellectual property, if there is a single one, that these practices presuppose. What must one believe about ideas—that they are the sort of thing that can be owned, that ownership is grounded in labor, in personality, or in social utility, that discovery and invention differ in kind—for the patent practice to be coherent? Which rival theories of ownership do the three practices quietly rule out, and are they consistent with one another, or does the contrast between patenting an isolated natural sequence and refusing to copyright an idea reveal a genuine contradiction? Finally, please distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit well with, and say what would have to change in each practice if we replaced the implicit theory with a thoroughgoing utilitarian incentive view or with a view on which ideas cannot be owned at all.
