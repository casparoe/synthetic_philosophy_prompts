I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy who has spent the past semester embedded in a reinforcement-learning lab, and the collision between the two worlds has left me genuinely unsettled. The lab trains pairs of agents on a referential game: a sender observes one object drawn from a small fixed inventory and emits one of a handful of discrete signals, and a receiver, seeing only the signal, must select the matching object. Over training the pair converges on a protocol that is almost perfectly reliable yet was never given a semantics by anyone, and my job has been to say what, if anything, the signals mean. What strikes me is how closely our situation mirrors the field linguist's, and how it also departs from it. Our evidence about the protocol is dispositions to respond to stimuli, exactly the kind of evidence Quine allows the translator, but we also have something the linguist does not: the network's weights are fully visible, and since the trained system is deterministic and finite, in principle we know every internal fact there is to know about it. Even so, the old trouble returns. At least two globally consistent assignments of meaning survive every behavioral test we can devise — one on which a given signal stands for an object, another on which it stands for a temporally extended stage of that object or for an instruction to act — and nothing in the data prefers either. Worse, when we rerun training with a different random seed the agents settle on a protocol that is behaviorally just as good but maps onto our vocabulary in a different way, and I cannot tell whether that shows the first protocol was ambiguous or merely that there was never a fact of the matter to begin with.

I have been drafted to help set the final exam for the upper-level seminar on meaning and translation that I am TAing, and I want one essay question built around this experiment rather than around the textbook rabbit, precisely so that a student cannot pass by reciting the familiar story. The students have all read the relevant Quine and can paraphrase the indeterminacy thesis on demand; what I want to test is whether they can tell the thesis apart from its neighbors and use it under pressure. Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. The question should force students to do all of the following: distinguish the indeterminacy of translation from the mere underdetermination of theory by evidence and from the inscrutability of reference, and say which of these the lab case actually exhibits; take a stand on whether our access to the network's weights makes any difference, that is, whether a fully known finite system removes the indeterminacy, relocates it, or reveals that it was never about evidence in the first place; explain the role that considerations of simplicity or utility play in choosing between the rival manuals, and whether that role makes the choice pragmatic rather than factual; and finally say whether the word translation is even apt for a protocol spoken by no community and answerable to no norm, or whether the case is really one of interpretation in a thinner sense. Assume students have seventy-five minutes for the essay and that they may not consult notes.

For the marking scheme, build it around five must-hit points worth four marks each, totaling twenty, where each point names a specific move a strong answer has to make and a one-line note on the characteristic error that should cost a candidate the marks — the errors I actually expect are things like treating indeterminacy as a skeptical hypothesis about hidden meanings, collapsing it into ordinary evidential underdetermination, or assuming that full physical knowledge of the system would settle the matter because the weights must encode the true referents. In the model answer, please be explicit about where the analogy to the field linguist holds and where the trained network is disanalogous, since that seam is where I think the best students will earn their marks.
