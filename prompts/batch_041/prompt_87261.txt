I have spent several years working on collective intentionality and joint action, and I have come to suspect that the field's organising question — whether intentions of the form we shall do such-and-such reduce to individual intentions plus mutual belief, or whether irreducibly collective states are needed — has been largely mined out. The more promising axis, I think, is not individual versus collective but synchronic versus diachronic: joint action whose participants change while the action continues. A football club, a legislature, a research programme, a family firm, a queue, a promise honoured across a decade by three people in succession — in each case no single person is party to the whole thing, yet it seems right to say that we are doing it together, and it is not obvious what makes that true.

Assume that reframing for the sake of argument. I advise a funder who can support three researchers for two years on conceptual work — philosophy and theory, no experiments, no fieldwork, no surveys. I want a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list: which open questions in this area most deserve those six person-years, in what order, and why?

For each question I want to know: what the question is, stated in your own terms; why it is upstream of the others, in the sense that other questions cannot be settled until it is; how tractable it is for people working with arguments and cases rather than data; and whether its resolution would actually change anything downstream. If a question is unresolved but every answer leads to the same theoretical or practical place, say so and demote it. Where two questions compete for the same time, tell me which you would drop and what you would be giving up.

I would also like your judgement on the reframing itself. Does the synchronic/diachronic distinction carve the phenomena at a joint, or does it collapse back into the older debate once you press on it? What would show that it was a distraction rather than a line of work? And are there open questions the reframing makes visible that I have not mentioned and would not have thought to mention — in particular about what becomes of a joint commitment when participants leave, are replaced, or die, and about whether an ongoing joint action can outlive every original participant?

Finish with what would move items up or down this list over the next two years: which arguments, counterexamples, or developments in adjacent areas would count as progress on the top question, and which would count as evidence that it is a dead end.

Please write your answer in British English.
