I am a third-year history student, and the county historical society has asked me to give the opening remarks at the exhibition it has mounted for the fiftieth anniversary of the Kell River flood, which drowned nineteen people in the valley in 1975. The remarks should run five to six minutes, be delivered aloud, and use no slides; they open the exhibition, so they also have to walk the audience through it.

The society has built the show in three rooms, one per timescale. The first is the river's own history over centuries: the meander belt, the silt load, the channel that has moved a mile east since the first surveys. The second is the half-century before the flood: the drainage and straightening works of the 1930s, the postwar estates built out onto the floodplain, the county's zoning decisions, the dredging budget cut year after year, and the warning siren moved to the far bank in 1962 as part of a consolidation that saved money. The third is the night itself: the hours, the evacuations that worked, the ones that did not, the nineteen dead, the neighbours who rowed for eleven hours in the dark.

The audience will be mixed, and the mix is the difficulty. The families of the dead will be there, and many of them will expect the night to be the centre of the occasion; they have no interest in silt. The county commissioners who paid for the exhibition will be there, and they will hear any mention of zoning or budgets as an accusation. Two retired professors from my department will also be there, and they will notice at once if I treat the flood either as a freak accident that nothing could have prevented or as an inevitable output of geography and account books.

I want to organise the whole speech around Braudel's three tempos — the long duration of structures, the medium duration of conjunctures, and the event — but I cannot use the vocabulary. Nobody in that hall has read the Annales school, and the phrase longue durée would lose the families in the first minute. So the structure has to be carried by the rooms and by ordinary language.

What I need from you is the speech itself. Conditions:

It must not console the families by suggesting that the nineteen died for something, that their deaths changed the river, or that the town has learned a lesson in any sense that implies a working mechanism. It must not allow the audience to conclude either that the flood was unforeseeable or that some nameable villain — the commissioners, the developers, the engineers — caused it; the vulnerability belongs to the middle timescale, and I want the speech to say so precisely rather than evasively. It must not treat the third room as decoration: if the long view leaves the night looking like foam on the water, the speech has failed, so the night has to come across as real and irreplaceable without being made the explanation of what happened. It must not personify the river, or fate, or the valley as an agent with intentions. Somewhere it must state plainly what a long-run view cannot do for the bereaved, instead of pretending that the long view is itself a comfort. And it should leave the commissioners and the professors with something to disagree with each other about — a real claim about how the three timescales stand to one another, not the truism that all three matter.

The prose should be plain, spoken, and unhurried: no jargon, no epigraphs, no quotations, no bullet points, nothing that sounds like an essay being read out.

After the speech, add a short note to myself: which sentence is doing the most work, and where a listener who wanted the event-centred story, or the deterministic story, would most reasonably object.
