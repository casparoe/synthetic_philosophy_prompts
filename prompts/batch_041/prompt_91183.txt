I am a doctoral candidate in political theory, preparing materials for a graduate seminar on responsibility and distributive justice. I would like you to write a dialogue between two philosophers who hold opposing views about a distributive scheme I have invented for the occasion. Everything the dialogue requires is stipulated below; nothing should turn on real-world facts, statistics, or empirical findings, and neither speaker should cite, name, or quote any author or appeal to what has been argued in the literature. They should reason from the scheme and from the cases. Please do not let them end by agreeing that the dispute is merely verbal, or that further empirical work is required.

The scheme. Imagine a compulsory national institution — call it the Ledger — whose aim is to make each person's lifetime position reflect only their own choices. Every adult holds an account in which two running totals are kept, one for brute luck and one for option luck. Whenever a person's life goes better or worse than the median in some significant respect — income, health, longevity, capacity to work — the Ledger must attribute the deviation to one of the two totals. Brute luck covers what was brought about by circumstances the person did not choose: illness, accident, inherited disability, the family and neighbourhood they were born into, the talents and temperaments they were born with. Option luck covers what was brought about by a risk the person knowingly took. At settlement, transfers flow between accounts so that the brute-luck totals of all adults are equalized, while the option-luck totals are left exactly as they stand. The intended result is that the only differences surviving settlement are differences people chose. Note that the scheme must therefore answer two questions it cannot avoid: how to sort any given event into one total or the other, and what baseline the phrase worse than the median is measured against.

The two cases, which the speakers should treat as fixed. Marisol has an inherited condition, unchosen by her, that produces episodes in which she makes large, irreversible financial commitments she later regrets. Two further stipulations. First, a cheap daily medication reliably prevents the episodes, and taking it is fully within her control when she is well, but she repeatedly stops taking it, and the stopping is itself part of the condition's pattern. Second, before the episodes began, a subsidized insurance product open to everyone would have covered precisely these losses, and she declined it. Tomás has no condition. He understood a risk, took it deliberately — he put his savings into his brother's business, knowing that most such ventures fail — and lost. In Marisol's case the cause is unchosen but a mitigation was chosen; in Tomás's case the cause is chosen but the outcome was not. Each speaker must say how the Ledger should classify both.

The two speakers. Give them names and keep the attributions unobtrusive. Speaker A defends the Ledger. She holds that the distinction between brute and option luck is the moral core of egalitarianism, that responsibility is properly assessed at the moment of choice rather than traced back to the causes of a person's character, and that the Ledger's classification difficulties are engineering problems rather than defects in the ideal. Speaker B rejects the Ledger. She holds that the classification problem is not a difficulty but an indeterminacy, because whether a shortfall counts as one's own fault is not a fact about the shortfall but a verdict delivered by a practice, so that a state running the Ledger is in the business of conferring and withholding the status of responsible agent; that requiring people to prove they are not at fault is itself a harm and corrodes equal standing; and that the remedy is to stop adjudicating after the fact and instead design background institutions so that the prudent course is the default and the imprudent one is hard to take — the medication automatic and free, the insurance opt-out rather than opt-in — so that the question of who is responsible for what need never be put.

What I need from the dialogue, roughly 1,400 to 1,800 words. Both speakers must be equally competent; neither may be a strawman, and B's positive proposal should be stated strongly enough that A has to work to answer it. The exchange must be real back-and-forth: at least twice, a speaker should take up the other's actual argument and respond to it rather than restating a position, and each should concede something specific under pressure and then say what the concession costs. The dialogue should turn on the features of the Ledger and the two cases rather than on general slogans, and it should end with the disagreement narrowed to a single specific question that both now recognize as the crux, even though they still answer it differently. Neither speaker should win by appeal to authority, and neither should be made to seem foolish for holding their view.
