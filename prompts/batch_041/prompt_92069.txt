You are a philosopher working at the intersection of law, literature, and the theory of consent, and I'd like your help with an essay on Shakespeare's Measure for Measure. I want a reading of the play that is genuinely disciplined by the contemporary debate over what makes consent valid, rather than a reading that treats the play as an illustration of a thesis it never considers. Please think the cases through carefully before you write; I want your conclusions, not your scratch work.

The consent architecture of the play, as I understand it, runs like this. Angelo, governing Vienna in the Duke's absence, sentences Claudio to death for fornication. Claudio's sister Isabella, a novice about to take her vows, comes to plead for his life, and Angelo offers to spare Claudio if she sleeps with him, making clear that the alternative is her brother's death. She refuses. The Duke, who has not really left Vienna but moves through the play disguised as a friar, proposes that Mariana — the woman Angelo was contracted to marry and then abandoned — go to Angelo's bed in Isabella's place, in the dark; Angelo has sex with Mariana believing she is Isabella. He then orders Claudio's execution anyway; the Duke saves Claudio by other means, exposes Angelo, and requires him to marry Mariana. The play closes with the Duke proposing marriage to Isabella, who says nothing.

What interests me is that this is a hard case for every account of consent, and hard in a direction the standard examples do not cover. The person whose consent is defective through deception is Angelo, who is a would-be rapist; the woman who actually has sex is a wronged fiancée who agreed to the substitution; and the woman whose refusal is being circumvented never consents to anything at all. Compare the familiar real-world case in which someone is deceived into sex by a person impersonating their partner: there the deceived party is the one wronged, and here the roles are inverted. The Duke's scheme also appears to assume something strange, namely that consent is substitutable — that because Isabella will not go, someone else may go in her name and Angelo's offer is thereby satisfied. I am inclined to read the play as exposing that assumption rather than endorsing it, and as leaning toward an account on which deception defeats consent only when it bears on what the deceived party took herself to be agreeing to, rather than on something incidental to it. I don't want that conclusion flattered, though; I want to know where the text resists it.

Also in play, and worth weighing: the threat behind Angelo's offer to Isabella, which is coercion or something close to it; the Duke's use of a friar's authority to steer Isabella toward the scheme; Mariana's own position, since her agreement restores the marriage she wants and may be the only route to it; and that silent final proposal, which raises the question of what silence can consent to when the person asking holds every advantage. The old criminal-law line between deception about the act itself and deception about the surrounding circumstances is relevant here, and I would like to know whether the bed trick falls on the side you would expect.

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. Treat each point as a distinct job: the strongest case the play makes about when deception or pressure defeats consent; the moment that cuts most sharply against that case; what the bed trick shows about whether consent can be transferred or substituted between persons; what the play's ending commits it to about consent and power; and what the play adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not.
