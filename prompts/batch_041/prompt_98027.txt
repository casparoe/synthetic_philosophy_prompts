I'm assembling materials for a graduate seminar that pairs Nietzsche's essay on history in the Untimely Meditations — the 1874 one that gives us monumental, antiquarian, and critical history — with Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and I want a reading of the novel that treats it as a serious intervention in the typology rather than a tidy illustration of it. My students can recite the three modes on demand; what they cannot do is say what the novel does to them. So please give me a reading in which the three modes are all present but each is practiced badly, and say precisely how. Stevens, as I read him, is an antiquarian custodian of Darlington Hall who mistakes the preservation of a house and its protocols for fidelity to a past; he is a monumentalist in his conviction that greatness is served by attaching oneself to a great man engaged in great affairs, and that his own dignity is measured by the magnitude of the master he chose; and he is the one thing the essay insists we must sometimes be and he cannot be — critical, willing to bring the past to judgment and condemn it. I am interested in the way the novel performs the critical history that its narrator refuses, and in whether the famous closing scene on the seafront, where Stevens resolves to perfect his banter and serve his new employer well, should be read as the turn toward the present that Nietzsche associates with health, or as one more evasion dressed as convalescence.

The thing I keep circling and cannot settle is whether the novel exposes a fourth relation to the past that Nietzsche's essay does not name. His three modes treat the past as exemplar, as relic, and as defendant; I want you to consider a fourth in which the past is a creditor — something owed, which no quantity of imitation, preservation, or condemnation can discharge. Call it the ledger mode if a name is needed, though I would rather you propose your own. Does Stevens's account, or the novel that surrounds and corrects it, contain such a mode? Is his celebrated professional dignity precisely the device by which the debt is kept off the books — the two housemaids dismissed at Lord Darlington's instruction, the proposal Miss Kenton brings to his pantry door and is turned away, the conferences he serves at while the world tilts toward catastrophe? I suspect the novel is at its most philosophically interesting when Stevens's reliability as a narrator fails, because an unreliable narrator is itself a historiographical position, and I want you to say what position that is.

Keep the reading faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the novel to a case study, and do not soften Nietzsche's typology to make it fit; where the novel resists the framework, say so plainly and say what the resistance shows. No quotations, please — I have the text and I want your account of it, not its words. Point me to the specific scenes you would put in front of the group and say what each is doing. End by telling me whether Ishiguro dramatizes something the essay misses — something about how a life is lived after the history it depended on has been discredited — or whether the apparent fourth mode collapses back into critical history under another name, and I have simply been reading a very English butler into a German problem.
