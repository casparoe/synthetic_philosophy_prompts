I'm a third-year PhD student in epidemiology, midway through a dissertation on treatment-effect heterogeneity, and I keep tripping over a pattern in my data that I can name only by slapping the nearest famous label on it, which no longer feels honest. The pattern: a treatment looks beneficial in the pooled sample but harmful within every stratum I can construct, or the reverse. I understand the bare arithmetic—that pooling can invert a within-group comparison—and I don't need that re-explained. What I can't settle is which of the several things people mean by "Simpson's paradox" I am actually looking at, because two features of my case seem to fall outside the textbook presentations. First, my strata are defined by a lab value measured after enrollment, and I have reason to think the treatment itself moves that value, so I am not sure the stratifying variable is the kind of thing one is permitted to condition on. Second, the apparent direction flips again when I move from the patient level to the clinic level to the region level, so "the effect" seems to depend on the level at which I choose to stop.

A concrete instance, stripped of identifying details: in the full sample, patients on the protocol have lower mortality; within every quintile of the post-treatment biomarker, they have higher mortality; and when I aggregate to clinics and weight by size, the sign flips a third time. Three colleagues give me three diagnoses. One says this is ordinary confounding and I should adjust for everything I can measure; one says I am conditioning on a collider and manufacturing a reversal that is not in the population; one says it is an ecological fallacy and I should never have aggregated in the first place. Each sounds plausible, and they cannot all be right.

What I want is a carving, not a label. Does this configuration have an established name—or, more likely, does it straddle two or three distinct phenomena? If so, draw the boundaries for me: how Simpson's paradox proper differs from the ecological fallacy, from confounding in the modern causal-inference sense, and from collider or selection effects (I have seen "Berkson" attached to that last family but I do not trust my memory of it), and, crucially, what each of those diagnoses does and does not license me to conclude about whether the treatment works. If nothing established covers the exact combination I have described—a post-treatment stratifier together with a level-dependent reversal—say so plainly and coin a term, with a definition and a couple of boundary cases, rather than forcing it into the nearest famous box. Please write in American English, and end with a single final line of the form "Verdict: <the best-fitting name, or your coined term>".
