I teach a seminar on love and marriage and next week we're pairing Ibsen's A Doll's House with Russell's Marriage and Morals. I keep feeling the play is doing something Russell cant see. Russell's whole case, as I read him, is that a marriage is worth keeping only as long as it stays a living companionship of equals, that its real enemy is a sexual morality built on possessiveness and on treating wives as property, and that when the thing dies the honest move is to end it. Nora's door-slam looks like exactly that verdict. But Russell's freedom is basically erotic — love and sex are the goods the marriage exists to house, and jealousy is mostly a social artifact to be got over. Nora walks out with no lover in it and no desire; what she wants is to stop being a doll and become a person, and the play makes that, not love, the thing that matters. So is Ibsen dramatizing a freedom Russell underrates, or am I forcing a contrast Russell would have happily accepted? Give me a reading that's fair to both: where the play vindicates his ideal, where it resists him, and which of the two is the better moralist about what a marriage is actually for. If the contrast I'm drawing is muddled, just say so instead of humoring me. Last line: \answer{Ibsen}, \answer{Russell}, or \answer{neither}.
