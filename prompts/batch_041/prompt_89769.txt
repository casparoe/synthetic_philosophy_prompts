In a seminar on professional ethics a colleague keeps invoking Kant's supposed absolutism about lying — 'one must never lie, not even to the murderer at the door' — to argue that any exceptionless deontological prohibition is fanaticism, since only a monster would refuse to misdirect a would-be killer. I suspect that one-liner is being made to carry more weight than it can bear, but I can't say precisely what it commits Kant to. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making: about what makes lying wrong (the violation of a duty, the degradation of rational agency, the abuse of a practice others rely on), about whom a lie wrongs (the murderer, humanity, the liar), about whether veracity is a duty of right owed to all or only a duty of virtue, about what counts as lying in the first place (false assertion, deception, misleading withholding), and about whether the prohibition is exceptionless in principle or merely in the case at hand. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether it would actually deliver my colleague's conclusion that deontology is fanatical. Present the readings as a menu with verdicts rather than settling on one, and keep the whole answer under 500 words.
