The question as set was:

"Problem 1 (20 marks). Let S = conv{(0,0), (7,0), (3,4), (0,6)} be the set of feasible utility pairs for two players, and let d = (0,0) be the disagreement point. The ideal point of (S,d) is b = (b1,b2), where b1 = max{u1 : u ∈ S} and b2 = max{u2 : u ∈ S}; the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is the point at which the segment from d to b meets the Pareto frontier of S; the Nash solution maximises (u1 − d1)(u2 − d2) over S. (a) [6 marks] Compute the ideal point and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution of (S,d), showing your work. (b) [5 marks] Compute the Nash solution of (S,d) and explain why it differs from the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution of the same problem. (c) [6 marks] The Nash solution is characterised by weak Pareto optimality (the solution lies on the frontier), symmetry (symmetric problems receive symmetric solutions), scale invariance (the solution is invariant under positive affine transformations of each player's utility), and independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA: if S′ ⊆ S contains d and the solution of (S,d) lies in S′, then the solution of (S′,d) is that same point). For each of the four, state whether the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution satisfies it and justify your answer; where you claim a failure, exhibit a pair of bargaining problems that demonstrates it. (d) [3 marks] Now suppose the disagreement point is d = (1,1), with S unchanged. Do the two solution points change? Give them if they do."

The answer as written was:

"Both solutions are invariant under positive affine transformations of the players' utility functions, so we may take the disagreement point to be the origin; here d = (0,0) already, so no normalisation is needed. (a) The ideal point is b = (7,6): player 1's maximum feasible utility is 7, attained at (7,0), and player 2's is 6, attained at (0,6). The ideal point need not be feasible, and here it is not: the frontier reaches u1 = 7 only at u2 = 0. The Pareto frontier of S is the broken line (0,6) → (3,4) → (7,0), with slopes −2/3 and −1. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is where the ray from d through b meets the frontier. The ray has equation u2 = (6/7)u1. On the first piece, u2 = 6 − (2/3)u1, so 6 − (2/3)u1 = (6/7)u1 gives u1 = 126/32 = 3.9375, outside that piece's range 0 ≤ u1 ≤ 3. On the second piece, u2 = 7 − u1, so 7 − u1 = (6/7)u1 gives u1 = 49/13 ≈ 3.77 and u2 = 42/13 ≈ 3.23; since 49/13 lies in [3,7], this is the correct piece. So the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is (49/13, 42/13) ≈ (3.77, 3.23). (b) The Nash solution maximises u1u2 over S. On the first piece u1u2 = u1(6 − (2/3)u1), which increases up to u1 = 4.5 and so is maximal over 0 ≤ u1 ≤ 3 at u1 = 3, giving 12. On the second piece u1u2 = u1(7 − u1), maximal at u1 = 3.5, giving 12.25. So the Nash solution is (3.5, 3.5). The two differ because they use different information: the Nash point is fixed by the tangency between the frontier and a hyperbola u1u2 = c, so it responds to the local shape of the frontier, whereas the Kalai–Smorodinsky point is fixed by the ray to the ideal point and so responds to how large each player's maximum feasible payoff is. Player 1's ideal payoff is the larger one (7 against 6), and correspondingly the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution gives player 1 more (3.77 against 3.23). (c) Pareto optimality: satisfied by both — both solution points lie on the frontier, so at either of them neither player can be made better off without making the other worse off. Symmetry: satisfied by both. Because the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is defined by the ray to the ideal point, it is impartial between the players: it awards them the same utility, which is why it is often described as an egalitarian or equal-sacrifice rule; the Nash product is likewise symmetric in the two players. Scale invariance: satisfied by both. Neither solution depends on the units in which utilities are measured, since both are defined in terms of ratios of gains from the disagreement point; multiplying one player's utility by a positive constant, and the corresponding coordinate of d with it, leaves the solution point unchanged. IIA: satisfied by the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution. Suppose S′ ⊆ S with d ∈ S′ and suppose the Kalai–Smorodinsky point of (S,d) is still in S′. The disagreement point is unchanged, and the only way the new ray can differ from the old one is if the ideal point of S′ is lower in some coordinate than that of S; but rotating the ray downward cannot carry it beyond a point that is still in the feasible set, so the old solution point remains the point at which the new ray leaves S′, and the solution cannot change. IIA therefore holds, and the two solutions cannot be separated by Nash's four axioms; the difference between them must instead be located in the monotonicity axiom of the Kalai–Smorodinsky characterisation, which the Nash solution fails. (d) Both solutions are invariant under positive affine transformations of the players' utility functions, so moving the disagreement point from (0,0) to (1,1) shifts both solution points by the same vector: the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution becomes (49/13 + 1, 42/13 + 1) = (62/13, 55/13) ≈ (4.77, 4.23), and the Nash solution becomes (4.5, 4.5)."
