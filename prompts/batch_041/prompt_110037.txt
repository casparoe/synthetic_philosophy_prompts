I am currently enrolled in an undergraduate seminar on the ethics of suffering, and we have spent the past several weeks on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism and the commensurability of pleasure and pain. The position we have been examining holds that there is a threshold of suffering—call it the threshold of the intolerable—such that any amount of suffering above that threshold cannot be outweighed by any quantity of positive utility, however large, while below the threshold ordinary aggregative reasoning is permitted. The standard objection is that this position is unstable. If pleasures and pains are commensurable on a single hedonic scale, as Bentham's hedonic calculus appears to assume, then no finite amount of suffering can be lexically protected, since a sufficiently large quantity of pleasure will always outweigh it. If the threshold is instead a genuine discontinuity in value, the view seems to abandon commensurability and perhaps collapses into a deontological side-constraint that Bentham would have rejected.

For my term paper, I have been asked to reconstruct how a historical philosopher would have responded to a debate they never encountered. I have chosen Bentham. I would like you to predict, as carefully as you can, what position Bentham would take on lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and to distinguish what follows from his actual commitments from what would be anachronistic.

In particular, please address the following. First, would Bentham accept any lexical threshold at all, or would his commitment to the greatest happiness principle and to the commensurability of pleasures and pains commit him to rejecting it as incoherent? Second, if he could accept a threshold, would he treat it as an axiological discontinuity—a point at which the disvalue of suffering becomes lexically prior to any amount of pleasure—or merely as a decision-theoretic rule for agents who cannot reliably compare vast quantities of utility? Third, how would he respond to the objection that lexical thresholds license the conclusion that a world containing any amount of extreme suffering is worse than a world containing no extreme suffering but an arbitrarily large amount of mild suffering? Fourth, what would he make of the distinction, which my professor has been calling threshold lexicality with a deontic floor, between a threshold that is a natural fact about hedonic intensity and a threshold that is a normative stipulation forbidding certain trade-offs even when the hedonic calculus would permit them? Fifth, would Bentham's hedonic calculus have the resources to represent a threshold without abandoning the single-scale picture—for example, by treating the threshold as a limit case, a discontinuity, or a second-order rule? Finally, how would he handle the related distinction between experienced utility and remembered or anticipated utility, and would that distinction make him more or less sympathetic to lexical thresholds?

I am not asking for a bibliography or for citations to secondary literature; I want a reasoned reconstruction. Please be explicit about where you are extrapolating from Bentham's known commitments, where you are making a judgment call, and where the historical Bentham might simply have had no determinate view. If the most defensible reconstruction is that Bentham would reject lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, please say so and explain what he would put in its place.
