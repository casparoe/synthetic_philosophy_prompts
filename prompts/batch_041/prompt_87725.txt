My roommate is in her third year of a clinical psych PhD and a friend of hers from undergrad — now a postdoc in philosophy of science — crashed on our couch for a week, and the two of them spent most of one dinner going at each other about psychiatric diagnosis while I sat there eating rice and being unable to referee. I'm doing a master's in public policy, so I can follow the words but I definately don't know who had the better of it. It went something like this, as best I can reconstruct it. Her: "The categories in the DSM can't be like the categories in the rest of medicine, because of Hacking's looping effects — when you classify people, they find out how they've been classified and they change. They read about the diagnosis, they meet other people who have it, they learn what the thing is supposed to feel like and how long it's supposed to last, and they start to fit it. So the category feeds back into the thing it was supposed to be describing. That's why the DSM is a set of decisions rather than a list of discoveries the way the periodic table is. And it means you can't point to a diagnosis being useful — treatment works, patients improve — as evidence that there's a real condition there, because the usefulness could just be the loop closing on itself." Him: "That's alot of weight for one phenomenon to carry. Looping shows the classification interacts with the people classified; it doesn't show the classification makes the condition. I don't think Hacking ever says the illness isn't real — my memory is that he says the loop is what makes the category unstable, not that it's fiction. Fugue and multiple personality flared up and then mostly went away. That's a fact about clinicians and about culture, not evidence that the patients weren't suffering."

Her: "But unstable is exactly the problem. If part of what makes someone depressed is that they've absorbed the script of depression — what it feels like, how long you're allowed to have it, what you're supposed to do about it — then take the script away and you've changed the condition. So there's no stable target to validate the category against. That's the difference from chemistry: nobody thinks the periodic table would have come out differently if we'd discovered it in a different century." Him: "Two things. First, partly made by us doesn't mean arbitrary — money and marriage are made by us and nobody thinks they aren't real, or that we can't be right or wrong about them. Second, what you actually mean by 'real' here is 'independent of us,' and I don't see that you've defended that as the test; you've just assumed the natural sciences set the standard and everything else has to measure up. We don't say species aren't real because we drew the boundaries between them." Her: "By that standard a rumor is real. A rumor does causal work." Him: "Sure, rumors are real, they get people killed — that's a consequence of taking social reality seriously, not an embarrassment. And if the label is doing causal work, that's an argument that it's tracking something, not that it's made up. What you want to say is the category isn't mind-independent, and I'm saying mind-independence is the wrong test for a diagnostic manual. The right test is whether it picks out a cluster of suffering that behaves predictably and tells you what will help. Money isn't mind-independent either and it still runs the world." Her: "That's the test the loop contaminates. If the cluster reshapes itself around the label, then the prediction is partly a self-fulfilling prophecy — you'd be using the category to validate itself." Him: "Only if the loop is total. You keep talking as if it's all or nothing, and nobody believes that. Some of what we call depression shows up in every culture and some of it doesn't; that's a matter of degree, and you're treating a matter of degree as if it settled a yes-or-no question." Her: "Fine, but degree matters here. If it's a lot of loop, you should be much more careful about widening the categories, because widening them doesn't just find more cases, it makes more cases. That's a reason to be conservative about adding diagnoses, and it's a reason a lot of the evidence base is worth less than it looks." Him: "That I'll grant. But it's a completely different claim from 'the categories aren't real,' and you keep sliding between them." That's roughly where it ended, with both of them annoyed at each other and me changing the subject.

What I want is a move-by-move adjudication, not a winner announced at the end: for each exchange above, which of them has the better point and why, and where a move is right in one sense and wrong in another — I have a suspicion that the word "real" is doing something sneaky on both sides, but I couldn't say what. Specifically: does her inference from looping to "usefulness isn't evidence of a real condition" actually hold, or is it too strong? Is he right that she's smuggled in mind-independence as the criterion, or was that criterion already built into the comparison she started with when she compared the DSM to the periodic table? Is "rumors are real" a concession that costs him, or a reductio of her — or neither? Is the species analogy fair, or is species realism itself too contested to do the work he wants? And is his "it's only a matter of degree" reply an answer to her validation problem or a dodge? Then tell me what both of them are missing — I'd like to know whether there's a version of each position that survives the other's best shot, and who ends up more right overall. If one of them is misreading Hacking, say which and why it matters, but don't just tell me what Hacking said — I want to know whose moves actually work.
