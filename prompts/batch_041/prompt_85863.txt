I retired a couple of years back after 34 years as a geochemist, and I've been making up for lost time with philosophy, which means I'm now the guy in the book club who won't shut up. We're doing The Count of Monte Cristo next month and I just finsihed Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia, and I can't stop reading one through the other.

My puzzle: Nozick's Chamberlain case seems to say that if every transfer along the way is voluntary, the resulting distribution is just however lopsided it gets — history matters, not pattern. Dantès is the mirror image: a man who was wronged and then spends decades unwinding the holdings of the men who wronged him, by hand. So is the novel an illustration of entitlement theory, or a reductio of it? Two things keep tripping me up. His war chest is the Spada treasure, which if I remember right is a cardinal's hoard with a pretty murky provenence, so the whole rectification is funded by an acquisition nobody would call clean. And he runs the rectification privately — no court, no dominant protective agency, just one injured man deciding who loses what, which is roughly the thing Nozick thinks gets absorbed into a state.

I gather Nozick's own account of rectification is famously thin, so: does the novel show why rectification might be unworkable in practice, or does it show a case where it's actually tractable? Where does the reading strain? And I have a hunch the ending — the doubt about whether he had the right to do any of it, and what he does for the Morrels — is doing work the argument alone can't, but I may just be pattern-matching. I'm a lab guy, so I want the reading that takes both the novel and the argument seriously, not a plot summary.
