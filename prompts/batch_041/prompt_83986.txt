I am preparing for a graduate seminar on metaethics and want to spend an hour being pushed hard on the weakest joint in my own position, so I would like you to play a committed expressivist of the quasi-realist stripe: someone who holds that normative judgments are non-cognitive states — attitudes of approval and disapproval, or plan-laden commitments — and who nevertheless wants to earn the right to all the realist-sounding talk about moral truth, moral knowledge, and genuine moral disagreement. Stay in character until I say stop. You should not concede that the view is refuted, but you should not stonewall either: concede the small points that are genuinely yours to concede, distance yourself from the crude versions of the view on which moral sentences simply mean "hooray" or "boo," and keep each reply to a single substantial paragraph so that it feels like a back-and-forth rather than a lecture. You may ask me to clarify a case, but you may not step out of character to tell me what the "real" position would say.

Here is the ground rule that makes this difficult, and I want you to honor it rather than dodge it. I will grant you minimalism about truth — let every declarative sentence count as truth-apt if that helps you. The problem I am pressing concerns content and validity, not truth. Consider this argument: "If asking my little brother to lie is wrong, then my plan to ask him is a bad one. Asking my little brother to lie is wrong. So my plan to ask him is a bad one." The argument is valid, and it is valid because the antecedent of the first premise and the second premise are the same thought, which is precisely what licenses the inference. Your account, however, seems to assign those two occurrences different jobs: asserted on its own, "asking my little brother to lie is wrong" is a state of disapproval, whereas in the antecedent it is not asserted at all, and what it contributes there is supposedly some relation among attitudes rather than a content that the second premise could share. So either the argument is a four-term equivocation dressed up as modus ponens, or your semantics of embedding must explain, without circularity, how one and the same content can function both as an attitude and as a component of a complex sentence. Do not merely assure me that you have a story about "commitment" or about "higher-order attitudes" — show me how the story earns validity for this particular inference, and be prepared for my follow-up, in which the normative sentence appears in the antecedent of a conditional that is itself the object of a partial credence.

My opening move, then, is this. I claim your view can preserve validity only by quietly becoming the cognitivism it was meant to replace, since anything that can be shared between the antecedent and the asserted premise — and then used in inference — is a proposition by another name. What exactly does the antecedent contribute, and in what sense is it the same thing that the second premise contributes?
