I have no philosophical training — I studied public policy and have spent the last couple of years reading my way into the epistemology of assertion on my own, mostly because I keep running into a case I cannot resolve. The case is Colin Powell's presentation to the UN Security Council in February 2003, laying out the American case that Iraq was hiding weapons of mass destruction. I know the broad shape of what followed: the mobile biological-weapons labs and the aluminum tubes turned out to be nothing, the sourcing behind some of the central claims was thin (for the mobile labs, essentially a single defector), and Powell himself later said publicly that the episode was one he regretted and that it would mark his record. What interests me is not whether the war was justified but the speech act. Powell was insistent that he was not offering the Council a set of assertions but facts and conclusions resting on solid intelligence; he said he could not tell the Council everything he knew; he noted that some of his sources were technical and some were people who had risked their lives to talk. He played intercepted audio and showed satellite photographs and invited the Council to hear and see what he heard and saw. By the accounts I have read, he spent days at CIA headquarters vetting the material and cut large parts of the draft he was handed, and one strand of the case — the aluminum tubes — had been disputed by analysts inside his own department.

I would like you to work through this episode using the competing accounts of the norm of assertion — the knowledge account, the truth account, the belief account, and the various justification- or warrant-based accounts — and tell me what each of them implies about Powell specifically. I want the mapping done carefully rather than gesturally. Who is the asserter here: Powell, the intelligence community whose conclusions he was relaying, or both at once? When he plays a tape and invites the Council to draw the inference, is he asserting the conclusion or presenting evidence and implicating the conclusion — and does that distinction survive once we notice that everyone in the room understood him to be asserting it? If his warrant was that the CIA had told him so, does knowledge transmit along that chain to him, and what becomes of the knowledge account when the speaker is a conduit rather than the knower? Does his explicit hedging shrink what was actually asserted, so that he asserted something weaker than what the Council took themselves to be told? Does his later public regret function as retraction data of the kind usually cited for the knowledge account, given that what he took back was not something he had insincerely claimed but something he had been told and had believed? And on the reception side: if the norm entitles hearers to presume that the speaker knows, were the Council members entitled to rely on him, or does the political setting give them reason to discount in a way the usual entitlement does not anticipate?

My own tentative view, which I would like you to push on rather than simply adopt, is that Powell passes the knowledge account too easily — he believed the intelligence community, the community is institutionally supposed to know, so he knew — and that this is a sign the debate has fastened on the wrong unit of analysis. The epistemically interesting thing is a chain of institutional testimony in which each link can be individually blameless and the whole can still fail. What I want from you is three things: which of the candidate norms handles this case best and which handles it worst; where each of them is simply silent rather than wrong; and whether the episode is best read as a case the norms of assertion handle well or as a case that exposes a gap in them — and if the latter, what a norm built for institutional assertion would have to say that none of the existing ones do. I am not looking for a reading list or citations; I want the analysis itself, and I would rather have you say where a norm runs out than force it to cover everything.
