I teach a second-year seminar on early modern metaphysics, and every year the discussion of the cogito collapses into the same exchange — so does it prove anything? — with students talking past one another because they are running different arguments under the same words. I want a diagnostic instrument that fixes that, rather than another lecture.

Please build me a decision tree that takes a student from the one commitment everyone in the room can adopt — I can doubt that I have a body and that there is a table in front of me, but I cannot doubt, right now, that I am thinking — to a named position on what the cogito establishes. Six to eight branch nodes, each phrased as a question a student can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. No node may name the doctrine at issue, so nothing of the form do you think the mind is immaterial? or are you a dualist? Each node must be a concrete case, contrast or claim on which the positions genuinely come apart.

The distinctions I most want the tree to separate, because they are the ones my students conflate, are these (treat the list as indicative, and add a node if some genuine split is missing):

- whether the step from the premise to the conclusion is an inference at all, or a performance rather than an inference, so that its denial is existentially inconsistent (Descartes himself insisted it was not a syllogism; the performative reading is associated with Hintikka);
- whether the premise contains an I at all, or only an impersonal occurrence of thought — Lichtenberg's objection that we are entitled to no more than it thinks;
- whether the I that is delivered is a particular thing, a bare logical subject or formal condition on the unity of experience (Kant), or a construction rather than a datum (Hume's bundle; and Sartre's claim that the reflective ego is a product of reflection, while the cogito itself is prereflective and impersonal);
- whether the argument yields a nature as well as an existence — Hobbes's objection that from I am thinking one might as well conclude I am a walking thing — or only that something exists;
- whether the certainty is momentary or supports a persisting thinker, since Descartes needs the latter for the remainder of the Meditations;
- whether the certainty is first-personal and non-transferable, or a repeatable argument anyone can run — that is, whether the cogito is a token claim about this thinker now or a type claim about thinkers as such;
- whether the whole thing turns on a feature of first-person present-tense self-ascription, namely that one can be wrong about what one is thinking but not about who is thinking it, and if so whether that feature is a metaphysical discovery or a grammatical one.

Leaves should carry a name where a name exists (the Cartesian substance; the transcendental subject; the Lichtenbergian it thinks; bundle theory; the performative reading; the deflationary grammar-not-metaphysics reading) and the word unnamed where nobody defends the position, each with a single sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, say whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate by any question of this kind, and say why it cannot.

Aim it at students who have read the first two Meditations and the Objections and Replies but none of the secondary literature, and give it to me as an indented text tree, with the question at each branch and the leaf name at the tips.
