I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and this past spring I sat in on a hospital ethics consultation as the lay member of the committee while my uncle was on a transplant list at the same hospital — an arrangement I would not recommend to anyone. Ever since, I've been arguing with friends about what may and may not be bought, and my verdicts refuse to line up. Paying plasma donors seems fine to me, and I would defend a regulated market in eggs for IVF; but I think it should be a crime for a stranger to buy a kidney from a willing adult, and I cannot give a principled reason why, since both involve money for a bodily intervention and the kidney donor stands to gain far more. A hardware store that triples the price of generators during a flood is doing something wrong and ought to be stopped, whereas surge pricing after a ballgame is fine and paying a snow-shoveler double is fine; surge pricing during an evacuation, though, feels like the generators. Paying someone to hold your place in line for a concert or a committee hearing seems fine; paying to jump a visa queue or to buy into a better school district does not, and I suspect those are two different objections wearing the same coat. Selling a vote is never acceptable, even when the buyer's platform would plainly help the seller more than an honest vote would. And paying a child per book read is fine while paying per A is not, which I cannot defend at all.

The tentative principle I have been working with is this: money is objectionable only when the good being priced is one whose value consists in its being shared and non-optional — a vote, a place in a common queue, an impartial hearing — rather than a private benefit the seller can refuse and walk away from. On that view plasma is private and a vote is shared, so some of my verdicts fall out neatly; but it makes my kidney verdict and my per-A verdict look like mere squeamishness, and it may not survive the generator case, where the thing being sold is a generator and not a flood. What I want is the best principle, or small set of competing principles, that actually fits the pattern, precise enough that I can see which clause each verdict depends on and what each one costs me.

Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion. Cover, in turn: the principle or principles you would defend; how they handle the cases I find hardest to reconcile; which of my verdicts they force me to abandon; what they predict about a case I have not mentioned; and finally where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what argument or piece of evidence would change your verdict.
