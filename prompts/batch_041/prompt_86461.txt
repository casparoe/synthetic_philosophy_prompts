The usual way the objection gets run is psychological: utilitarianism supposedly demands that the agent have a certain thought at the moment of action, namely the thought of the general good, even in a case like Williams's man who can save only one person and pulls out his wife. The usual reply is that this is a confusion. Deliberation and justification come apart. The man can act straight out of love, with no maximising thought in his head at all, and it can still be true that the act is justified impersonally. So there's no extra thought, and the objection dissolves.

2. I think that reply is fine and also beside the point, because the interesting version of the objection was never psychological. The real question is whether the fact that she's my wife is doing work in the reason for acting or is merely an input the impartial structure permits. Here's a test I've been calling the residue test: take the complete impartial justification of the act, and subtract it from the agent's actual reason for acting. If nothing is left over, then the relationship was contributing nothing that the impartial justification wasn't already supplying — it's a permissible selection device, not a ground. Williams clearly thinks something is left over. The accommodation reply says: yes, something is left over, and impartial morality can house it as a tie-breaker among options it already permits. Maybe. But being housed as a tie-breaker inside an impartial structure is not the same as being a ground, and I don't think that's a merely verbal difference.
