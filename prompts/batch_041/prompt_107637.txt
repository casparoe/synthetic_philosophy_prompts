I write a monthly column on science and ideas, and there is one argument I keep circling without being able to say what it is actually about: the fight over whether British rule in India was, on balance, a good thing. Put Niall Ferguson's Empire: How Britain Made the Modern World (2003) next to Shashi Tharoor's Inglorious Empire: What the British Did to India (2017, published in India the year before as An Era of Darkness) and you have the two poles. Ferguson's case turns on what outlasted the Raj — railways, the telegraph, a common-law tradition, English as a world language, and the claim that India was pulled into a global economy it would otherwise have missed. Tharoor's turns on what the same rule extracted and destroyed: the collapse of Indian textile manufacturing under imported cloth, the drain of revenue and capital to Britain, the great late-Victorian famines and the relief doctrines that let them run, and the fall in India's share of world output from roughly a quarter to a few percent; his book's summary line is that "Britain's rise for 200 years was financed by its depredation in India."

What strikes me is how little the two sides disagree about what happened. The railways existed; the famines killed millions; the payments to Britain were made. Reviewers of Tharoor have mostly attacked his numbers as secondhand and decades out of date while conceding the force of the charge, and defenders of empire concede the atrocities while keeping the net verdict positive. Two decades of this and nobody has moved, and I have stopped believing that more evidence is what is missing. The column is about the shape of the argument, not the Raj as such.

So do not give me a verdict — I have read enough verdicts. Give me a diagnosis of the disagreement. I want to know which parts of it are genuinely empirical and which only look empirical: how much turns on the counterfactual (and which counterfactual — no colonization at all, a different colonizer, or the paths of Japan, Siam, or a China that was never fully colonized), how much on measurement (is the "drain" a measurable quantity or a category mistake?), how much on the fact that the two ledgers are drawn up for different subjects — Ferguson's for Britain and for a globalized world, Tharoor's for India — and how much is a disagreement about values that no further history could settle. My own hunch, held loosely, is that the deepest problem is the ledger metaphor itself: that "on balance" presupposes the gains and losses are commensurable and can be netted, and that one camp is quietly refusing the framing rather than disputing the arithmetic. Tell me whether that is right, and if not, what the real obstacle is.

Work the way a philosopher of the social sciences would: keep the positive and the normative apart, be explicit about any counterfactual you rely on, and think carefully before you commit — draft a few candidate diagnoses and discard the ones that do not survive contact with what each side actually argues. Do not pad the answer with citations or reading lists, and do not supply figures you cannot stand behind; where a number is contested, say what kind of number it would have to be.

Give me the finished diagnosis as a numbered list of exactly five items, each no more than a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Between them the five should say: what kind of disagreement this is; which sub-disputes are genuinely empirical; what is really doing the work when two writers cite the same century to opposite effect; whether the balance-sheet question is well posed; and what, if anything, would count as progress — a new measurement, an agreed counterfactual, a distinction, or a change of question.
