I've been teaching the Laws for years and I still cant make up my mind about the drinking-party bit in Book II, so I want you to write an essay that settles it for me — or at least takes a stand I can argue with.

Here's the setup. The Athenian proposes that a well-run city should have supervised drinking parties, and he wants to defend them as an educational institution rather than just a licensed vice. He sets ages: no wine at all for the young, wine in moderation for the young man, and a good deal more latitude for the older man, on the theory that wine makes the aging soul soft and young again, like heated iron, so that it can be worked. The party is meant to be run by a sober chairman and sober officers who keep order, and it's meant to function as a kind of gymnasium for the soul: a controlled setting in which the drinker's pleasures, fears, and shamelessness get stirred up so that he can practice — and be seen to practice — the right responses to them.

Now the controversy I want you to adjudicate. It looks like the drinking party is doing two jobs at once, and I suspect they might be incompatible. Job one: it's a test. It reveals what a man is really like once his inhibitions and his social mask come off — the Athenian treats it as a way of discerning men's natural dispositions. Job two: it's a training. It's supposed to make him more temperate, the way a gymnasium makes him stronger, by giving him reps at handling pleasure and fear under conditions of reduced control.

Push on that. If the party is a genuine test, then the drinker shouldn't be able to game it — but the whole thing is a legislated institution with a chairman and rules, so everyone knows exactly what's going on, which sounds more like a fire drill than a fire. And if the party is training, then what exactly is being trained? You don't get better at self-control by repeatedly surrendering self-control. The one thing you might actually be training is orderly conviviality — knowing when to speak and when to keep quiet, what to sing, how to take a joke — which is a real skill, but its not temperance, and it's a skill the sober officers are doing most of the work of enforcing.

There's a further wrinkle I want you to deal with, and I think it's the sharpest one. Wine in this dialogue does two opposite things: it dissolves shame, which is what lets the drinker turn shameless and audacious, and it dissolves rigidity, which is what makes the soul teachable. The Athenian needs the second effect and has to legislate the first one back in — the sober rulers are supposed to reintroduce modesty and shame from the outside, by force of law, into a condition that wine has specifically removed it from. So the city is trying to supply by command a virtue whose whole point is that it can't be commanded. Ask whether that's a fatal flaw or whether Plato has a clever answer to it.

So: write the essay. Weigh the best case for the drinking party as a coherent piece of moral education against the best case that it's confused — or, if you like, that it's a half-serious provocation Plato never fully commits to, which would also count as a verdict, but you'd have to argue it. Reach a conclusion. I'd much rather you commit to something specific and defend it than hedge. The verdict should be about the internal coherence of the proposal, not about whether we'd want such parties today, though a quick gesture at why the modern comparison is or isn't apt is welcome if it does real work for you.

One more thing: don't lean on the Republic's much sterner line on drink as a way of scoring easy points against the Laws, unless you're actually going to do something with the difference. The interesting question is whether the proposal stands up on its own terms, not whether two dialogues disagree.
