I've been going back and forth with a friend about karma and I'd like you to actually take a side and defend it, not just survey what people say about it.

Here's the version I have in mind, which I think is the one you get in a lot of Buddhist-influenced writing: actions leave a trace or a disposition in the mind, and the trace ripens later — maybe much later, maybe in another life — as an experience of a corresponding character. No ledger, no judge, no sentencing, nobody deciding that this particular person deserves that particular thing. Its supposed to be closer to a law of nature than to a courtroom.

My friend says that's still a theory of moral desert with the machinery hidden. The whole point, she says, is that good comes to the good and bad to the bad, and that the ripening is fitting in a way that makes the agent deserve what they get; calling it a law of nature instead of a verdict is just aesthetics.

Argue my side: once you take the no-judge, no-ledger version seriously, it isn't a desert theory at all, and most of the standard objections — that karma is cruel, that it blames people for thier own suffering, that automatic punishment is barbaric, that you can't have deserving without a permanent self around to do the deserving — are objections to a picture the doctrine doesn't actually need.

But be straight with me about where the argument cheats. Does desert really need a judge, or can fittingness alone do the work? If fittingness is enough, my side collapses and I want to hear that said plainly. And if I do win, is what's left a theory of anything — once it stops answering "why did this happen to me," does karma still explain anything, or have I argued it out of a job?

And if I've set this up wrong — if "desert theory" can't do the work I'm asking of it, or the no-judge version is a straw man I invented rather than something anyone actually holds — say so instead of politely arguing with a view I made up. Finish with your own view, how confident you are in it, and what would move you.
