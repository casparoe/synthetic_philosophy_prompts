Ok so I'm a fourth-year in political theory, I'm TAing the big intro lecture on ancient political thought, and every single term some kid in the back asks me what Aristotle would've made of left and right. Every term I punt with a joke about him never having heard of the French Revolution. Which is true, but it's a cop-out, so I want to actually work this out and I'd like you to do the real thinking with me.

To be clear about what I don't want: the lazy version where you tell me he'd see the left as the many and the poor and the right as the few and the rich and then stop. That's maybe a third right and it's boring. I want the machinery actually running, and I want you to tell me where the machinery grinds.

Four things I want you to hit:

1) Is it even a kind, in his sense? He's got that whole apparatus about things that share a definition versus things that only get the same label by analogy or by reference to one thing. Does left/right pick out a kind with a shared essence, or is it just a standing relation of opposition? And if it's the latter, does that mean the distinction isn't real for him? I think I know which way he'd go but I want the argument, and I want the implication spelled out.

2) The equality stuff in Politics V. My reading is that for him faction is always a mismatch between how power is actually distributed and what the parties think they deserve, and that the fight is really over which kind of equality applies -- the numerical kind, or the kind that's proportional to worth. Map the two modern sides onto that. Which side is running the "we're equal and you're not treating us as equal" complaint, and which is running the "we're unequal and you're leveling us" complaint? Then break the mapping, because the modern right isn't just the rich and the modern left isn't just the many, and that's where I think the interesting stuff lives. Do either of them even have a coherent story about what they deserve, or is it vibes all the way down?

3) What holds each side togther. He's got concord, the political friendship thing in the Ethics -- the point being that a city is in concord when citizens agree about their interests and about what to do, not when they merely agree on facts or on everything in general. So: is either modern coalition in concord in his sense, or are they held together mostly by a shared enemy? What does he say happens to people who can't manage concord -- he's pretty brutal about the base not being capable of it. And does his framework have any room at all for a faction organized around identity or recognition rather than class, or is that a case his theory just can't see?

4) Where he thinks it goes. He's got his whole account of regimes sliding into one another and the middle as the stable option. Would he predict anything for us? And is our "center" a real middle in his sense -- a class with its own stake and its own virtue -- or just the absence of the two extremes? If it's the latter, then his remedy isn't available to us, and I want you to just say that plainly if that's where it lands.

Say 1200-1500 words, and please, no citations -- I know where he says it, I don't need a reading list, I need the argument. Also flag anywhere you're extrapolating versus anywhere you think he'd actually have a view. I'd rather get "he's silent here" than a fake Aristotle opinion.
