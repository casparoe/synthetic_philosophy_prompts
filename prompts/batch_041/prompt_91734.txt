I am preparing a seminar paper on statutory interpretation, and I want to work through a case that my professor calls too artificial to be interesting but that I think exposes something the usual textualism-versus-purposivism arguments miss. The setting is a hypothetical jurisdiction, so nothing turns on real doctrine and I am not looking for cases or sources: the constitution requires every statute to be enacted simultaneously in two languages, both texts equally authentic and neither subordinate to the other; a professional drafting bureau is instructed to make each text idiomatic in its own language rather than literal; and courts are barred from treating one language's wording as evidence of what the other "really" means. In practice the two texts often come apart — one states a broad genus where the other enumerates species, one leaves a gap the other fills, one carries a proviso the other omits. The bench is split. One camp resolves every divergence by asking which reading better serves the statute's evident purpose; the other camp insists that a court's job is to enforce the meaning the two texts share, and to fall back on the narrower or more specific text when they share none.

Please argue for the second, shared-meaning camp. The objection I want you to meet head-on is that in the hard cases there may be no shared meaning to enforce, so the method either silently prefers one text to the other or smuggles purpose back in — purposivism with extra steps. I care less about which method better honors the rule of law in the abstract than about what the shared-meaning method can actually deliver once the two texts genuinely conflict, and about what it should direct a court to do in that situation.

Please state your position in the first sentence, and keep the whole answer to no more than 400 words.
