I have drafted the short essay below and would like your comments on it. I am asking about whether the argument holds and whether its central distinction earns its keep; please do not simply endorse it, and please do not send me to further reading — everything needed is in the statement of the problem.

**The absent-minded driver, and a coincidence between two optima**

A driver is travelling a long, straight road. The road crosses two intersections that are indistinguishable from the driver's seat, and — this is the essential stipulation — he cannot remember whether he has already passed one. His information at the first intersection and at the second is therefore identical, so he cannot tailor his behaviour to his location: whatever he does at one intersection he must do at the other. Let p be his policy, the single probability with which he continues past whichever intersection he is at. Turning off at the first intersection leads nowhere and is worth 0. Turning off at the second brings him to his destination and is worth 4. Passing both leaves him in a featureless desert, worth 1.

*1. The ex ante problem.* Evaluated from the start of the road, the policy p is worth U(p) = 4p(1 − p) + p² = 4p − 3p², which is greatest at p = 2/3, where it is worth 4/3. It is worth registering how expensive absent-mindedness is here. A driver who could tell the intersections apart would continue at the first, turn off at the second, and collect 4. Absent-mindedness forbids every plan of that kind, because a plan prescribing different actions at two points the driver cannot distinguish cannot be carried out at all; every deterministic policy is constant, and 4/3 is the best a constant policy can achieve.

*2. The ex post problem.* Consider the driver at the moment of decision. He cannot tell where he is, but he knows his own policy, so he can say how probable each location is. Conditional on being at an intersection, and given policy p, the first has probability 1/(1 + p) and the second p/(1 + p). At p = 2/3 these are 3/5 and 2/5. Turning off is then worth 4 × 2/5 = 8/5. Continuing is worth (3/5)(2) + (2/5)(1) = 8/5, where 2 is what arriving at the second intersection and thereafter playing p is worth — that is, (1/3)(4) + (2/3)(1). The two values coincide, so the mixed policy is not one the driver has any reason to revise at the moment of action.

I dwell on this because a natural alternative computation gives the opposite answer. If one values continuing at the value of the information set itself — 4/3, the value of the whole problem from the start — rather than at the value of the next node, one gets (3/5)(4/3) + (2/5)(1) = 6/5, which is less than 8/5, so the driver appears to want to turn off; iterating that reasoning settles him at a policy of always turning off at the first intersection, worth 0. My diagnosis is that the alternative computation uses the value of the first node where it needs the value of the information set, and that the value of the information set is the belief-weighted average of the values of the nodes belonging to it — (3/5)(4/3) + (2/5)(2) = 8/5 — which agrees with both actions, as an optimum requires. The mistake is nonetheless a tempting one, and I would like to know whether it admits of a defensible reading.

*3. Is the coincidence an accident?* Generalize to n intersections in a line, payoff rᵢ for turning off at the i-th, payoff r₀ for passing all of them, and a stationary policy p. Let Aᵢ be the value of arriving at the i-th intersection and thereafter playing p, so that Aᵢ = (1 − p)rᵢ + pAᵢ₊₁, with Aₙ₊₁ = r₀. Two conditions can be imposed on p. The ex ante condition is that U = A₁ be stationary in p. The local condition is that a driver at an intersection be indifferent between his two actions, the weights on the intersections being the probabilities with which they are reached: Σᵢ p^(i−1)(rᵢ − Aᵢ₊₁) = 0. Because Aᵢ = (1 − p)rᵢ + pAᵢ₊₁ rearranges to rᵢ − Aᵢ₊₁ = (Aᵢ − Aᵢ₊₁)/(1 − p), the local condition is equivalent to Σᵢ p^(i−1)(Aᵢ − Aᵢ₊₁) = 0. Differentiating, dA₁/dp = Σᵢ p^(i−1)(Aᵢ₊₁ − rᵢ), which is the negative of the local condition up to the factor 1/(1 − p). The two conditions are therefore one condition, and every interior optimum of the ex ante problem is a policy from which the driver has no incentive to deviate at the intersection. For instance, with three intersections, payoff 5 for turning off at the third, 0 for turning off at either of the others, and 1 for passing all, the optimum is p = 5/6; the driver's credences at an intersection are 36/91, 30/91 and 25/91, and both actions are worth 125/91.

*4. What, if anything, this shows.* One reading is deflationary. If the ex ante and the local optima coincide, the absent-minded driver is not an example of time inconsistency; there is no gap between the plan and what the driver wants at the intersection, and the problem is an ordinary optimisation under an information constraint whose only casualty is the infeasible plan worth 4. A second reading denies that the coincidence is the interesting thing. What is peculiar to the case is that the driver's credences are functions of his policy: the only way to be at the second intersection is to have continued at the first, so his evidence about where he is depends on what he is going to do. Call this policy-endogenous self-location. If that is right, the driver is not choosing among acts with his beliefs held fixed but among act-and-belief pairs, and expected-value maximisation over policies is best understood as a way of finding a fixed point — a policy that reproduces itself as the driver's own evidence — rather than as a straightforward maximisation. I am inclined to the second reading, but I cannot say what it predicts that the first does not, beyond the observation that the fixed-point formulation survives the loss of stationarity and the maximisation formulation does not.

*5. Where I am least confident.* I have not shown that the equivalence in §3 fails when stationarity or the single-information-set assumption is dropped, and I suspect the second reading collapses into the first if that cannot be shown. I also suspect that the diagnosis in §2 is only a restatement of the fact that an information set is reached with a probability depending on the policy — which is unremarkable in problems of partial observation — dressed up as a thesis about self-location.

I would be grateful for your comments, in particular on the following four points.

1. Is the equivalence in §3 stated correctly, and is it a genuine result or an artefact of the linear, stationary structure? Does it depend on evaluating a deviation as a one-off departure followed by a return to p, rather than as a fresh re-optimisation?
2. Is the alternative computation in §2 defensible under any reading of the driver's situation? If it is not, why is it so tempting?
3. Does the deflationary reading in §4 succeed? If the second reading is to earn its keep, what should it predict that the first does not? Please give me the strongest objection you can to the idea of policy-endogenous self-location rather than a sympathetic reconstruction of it.
4. Which modification of the setup — partial memory, non-stationary payoffs, or more than one absent-minded driver on the road — is most likely to break the equivalence, and what would the break establish?
