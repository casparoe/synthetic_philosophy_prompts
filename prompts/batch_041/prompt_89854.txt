Ok, so I've been drafted into guest-lecturing in a friends third-year philosophy of mind course next month -- 75 minutes, about thirty students, none of whom have written a line of code in their lives, but all of whom sat through the phil sci unit last term so Quine's holism is rattling around in there somewhere (the thing where a new belief can in principle touch anything). The topic I've been handed is the frame problem, and what I want from you is a plan for the lecture, not the lecture itself. Every time I've taught this I've either buried them in situation calculus or waved my hands at "relevance," and I'd like to stop doing both. Here's the skeleton I think it needs, but tell me if the dependencies are backwards, because I suspect they are. First, the mundane fact that when one thing changes almost nothing else does -- I set my mug down and the ceiling doesn't budge -- and that any system that represents the world has to be able to count on that inertia. Second, the formal attempt: for every action, write down what it changes and, as separate axioms, what it leaves alone, and watch the number of axioms blow up as roughly actions times properties; then the non-monotonic patch, where you assume nothing changes unless forced, and the way the patch bites back -- the minimal-change assumption lets the gun quietly unload itself while you're waiting, so the turkey walks away. Third, the move I actually care about: the narrow problem about axioms and the wide problem about relevance are not the same problem, and the narrow one got solved toward the end of the 80s by having the designer specify in advance which properties an action is allowed to touch -- which is precisely the thing the wide problem says an agent can't be handed. That's the pivot I want the whole hour to hang on: solving the narrow problem just relocates the wide one up a level, and the wide one is why this matters for minds and not just for logic programming.

Which brings me to the thing I most need help with: the example that carries it. I keep reaching for the toy case where you paint block A blue and then move it to the garden, and the logic only lets you conclude where it is, not what color it is -- clean, but so clean it makes the whole thing look like a bookkeeping nuisance. My alternative is aggressively boring: I learn the pharmacy closes at six and almost nothing else about my afternoon changes, and the question is how I knew that in advance. Is either of those the right load-bearing example, or is there something better, and what does the right one need to have and to leave out so it doesn't smuggle in a neighboring problem? Because my worry is that anything vivid -- a robot, a bomb, a runaway stroller -- drags in "what conditions does this action need to work" or "what are the knock-on effects," and those are different problems wearing the same coat. I dont want them to leave with the wrong one.

Then the misconception map, with the exact point in the sequence where each one gets killed. My guesses: they'll hear "frame" and think movie frames, or the frames of vision, so the word itself is a trap; they'll come away thinking this was solved in the 80s and is now a history-of-AI curiosity, which is half true and therefore the hardest to dislodge; they'll think it's a complexity problem, about doing too much computation, rather than a problem about not knowing what's relevant in the first place; they'll think it's a problem for machines that we obviously don't have, and that's the one I most want to break and have the least clean answer for; and once I've drawn the frame/qualification/ramification distinction -- what stays the same, what an action needs in order to work, what else it sets off -- they'll over-apply it and start calling everything the frame problem. So: which of those isn't actually a misconception, i.e. which "they'll think X" is just correct and I should stop correcting it, and for the real ones, where in the arc does each get headed off. Last, two things. Give me one question to put to them at the very end that would separate a student who can recite "it's about relevance" from one who actually feels why holism makes it hard. And tell me the two or three things I should announce out loud that I'm deliberately not going to explain at this level, so they don't leave thinking I skipped something by accident.
