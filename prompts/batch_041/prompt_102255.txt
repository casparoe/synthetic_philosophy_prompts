Write a dialogue between two engineers on the same team — one building a risk-scoring model that will allocate a scarce resource such as credit or bail, the other auditing it before release — who disagree about what the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness actually show. The first holds that the impossibility is a fact about the world: once two groups differ in the base rate of the outcome being predicted, no score that is calibrated within each group can also equalize the error rates across groups, so any deployed threshold encodes a choice about whose errors to tolerate, and that choice cannot be made by mathematics. The second holds that this is an artifact of holding the joint distribution fixed: the base rates are themselves partly the output of past decisions and of the model's own feedback effects, and the three criteria are not one demand but claims about three different things — what a score means, how it is used, and what is owed to whom — which no theorem shows to be jointly unsatisfiable in the abstract. Let each press the other's actual argument rather than a nearby one, and have them test the two proposed escapes: a randomized decision rule, and a model retrained after every round of decisions. Neither may simply name a result; each must reconstruct the trade-off from the assumptions. End with the sharpest point each concedes and the question they agree remains open.
