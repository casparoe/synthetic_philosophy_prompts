My mate Dan has been building an arguement in our reading group and it's got right under my skin. He reckons he's just taking Friedman's line seriously — the one where the more important a theory is, the less realistic its assumptions have to be — and following it where it leads. Here's his chain:

1. A model is a model because it leaves stuff out.
2. When what's left out makes a difference to the outcome, the model is, strictly speaking, false of the system it's meant to be about.
3. Anything we can actually work with leaves out something that makes a difference — if it didn't, we'd be computing the world instead of modelling it.
4. So every workable model is strictly false of its target.
5. If a model is strictly false of its target, then its getting the predictions right can't be evidence that it's true of that target; at best it shows the thing is useful for the case in hand.
6. If success only ever shows usefulness, it never shows truth — and nothing changes when you bolt a load of such models together into a theory.
7. So no amount of empirical success can support realism about the unobservable parts of our theories.
8. Now take any two theories that make exactly the same predictions in every situation. Pick your favourite pair.
9. By 5–7, nothing could ever count as evidence that one of them rather than the other is the accurate description.
10. So for such a pair, the question of which one is really true has no content.
11. And if it has no content there, it has none anywhere, since by 4 all our theories are false in that same way.
12. So realism about science isn't merely unwarranted, it's incoherent.

The cases I keep chewing over, in case they help: the ideal gas law, which gives molecules no volume and no forces between them, is still how you get from the gas laws to kinetic theory, and it's fine for air in a room; the Bohr atom has assumptions everyone now knows are false, nails hydrogen's spectrum, and is hopeless for helium; a frictionless plane isn't anywhere; Lotka–Volterra has prey breeding without limit and predators bumping into prey at random, and it's still the first thing anyone reaches for; the Hardy–Weinberg setup assumes no selection, no drift, no migration and no mutation, and the whole point of it is the deviations from it; and in economics you get agents who know everything and never miscalculate, which works for some questions and not others. Phlogiston, meanwhile, was false and did nothing. I keep wanting to say the gas law's assumptions are harmless and the Bohr atom's aren't, even though both are false, but I can't find a slot for that in his argument anywhere.

I don't accept 12, obviously. But I'd rather keep 3 and 5 than just stamp my foot at the end, so: which step goes, why did it look fine while I was reading it, and what does losing it cost me? If it's 2 that's doing something dodgy with 'false', then say what I'm supposed to say about the Bohr atom instead, because I can't just wave it away. If it's 6, tell me what sort of evidence empirical success is meant to be, given that I still think the F-twist has something to it.
