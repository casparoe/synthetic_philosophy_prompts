I serve as policy analyst to a state legislature's technology committee, and I need a piece of analysis I can circulate before next week's hearing.

The bill before us would create a "civic integrity index": one score per resident, computed by merging administrative records the state already holds — property and vehicle registrations, professional and business licensing, court and docket records, benefits enrollment, tax arrears, inspection and permit history. No new data would be collected, and much of the underlying material is already public. The score would set inspection and audit priorities and decide who gets expedited permits. At the sponsor's press conference, when a reporter raised objections about function creep, misclassification, and the absence of any appeal, the sponsor answered that if you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear. That sentence has since been doing a great deal of work in our deliberations: two members cite it to argue the objections are unserious, a third cites it to argue that we have already conceded the principle that innocence is the only thing standing between a resident and the state, and no one has said what the sentence actually claims.

What I want from you is a menu rather than a verdict on the bill: the distinct precise claims that this one-liner could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the radical, and for each, what it would take for the claim to be true, whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and whether it would in fact license the sponsor's dismissal of the objections. I expect the readings to separate along several axes, and I want them separated rather than run together. What does "nothing to hide" quantify over — no illegal act, no act that could be misread as illegal, no embarrassing fact, no fact one would want withheld from some particular audience, no fact that is not already held by someone else? Whose fear is at issue, and of what — coercion by the present government, coercion by a future or hostile government, private parties who obtain the score, aggregation and inference, the chilling effect on lawful conduct? And is the sentence an empirical prediction about consequences, a claim about the very concept of privacy, a rule about who bears the burden of justification, or a move that presumes bad faith in anyone who objects? Be explicit about which readings are trivially true and therefore do no work at all, which are doing the real work when the sentence is used to end an argument, and which of them our colleagues must be relying on for their respective uses of the sentence to go through.

Then a short closing section, because the committee keeps invoking foreign practice. For each of three regimes — the German constitutional treatment of informational self-determination, which grew out of the Federal Constitutional Court's 1983 census judgment and treats no datum as irrelevant to the person; the American third-party doctrine and the broader American default that what one has already disclosed to others is fair game; and the Chinese social credit system as built out from the State Council's 2014 planning outline — say which reading of the slogan that regime's practice presupposes, and whether the slogan is a premise of the practice, a target of it, or simply beside the point there. One colleague insists that the Chinese system is aimed chiefly at firms and financial compliance rather than at individual residents, and that the popular image of a single national score for persons is largely a Western misreading. If that is wrong, say so; if it is right, say what it implies for the slogan.

Three constraints. Do not give me citations, a reading list, or references to particular books and articles — I will not be able to check them, and I gather you will not either. If you need a term of art, define it in the sentence where you use it. And wherever you lean on a contested empirical claim about any of these regimes, mark it as contested.

Finally, since I will be quoting parts of this in a hearing where the stakes are real: tell me how confident you are in each of your main verdicts, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
