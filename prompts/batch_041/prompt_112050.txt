I'm a 3rd-year PhD student in linguistics (my advisor calls it "philosophy of language, badly disguised," which is fair). I'm drafting a chapter and I want your honest comments before I send it to my committee. Below is the argument in note form, not polished prose. Tell me where it's confused, where I'm just restating Hofstadter, and whether the central move actually works or is a bit of a trick.

The setup: Hofstadter's complaint about machine translation (the Google Translate piece in The Atlantic, and the much longer treatment in Le Ton beau de Marot) is usually read as an argument about poetry and wordplay -- puns, meter, the stuff MT visibly mangles. I think that's the weakest version of the argument, and it's also the version everyone repeats. If MT were only bad at poetry, you could shrug: so are most humans, and poetry is a weird edge case. My claim is that the shallowness shows up *more* clearly in the boring cases, and specifically in what I'm calling the audience problem.

Here's the audience problem. Lots of languages force the translator to encode a fact about the social relation between speaker and addressee, or about the referent, that the source language simply doesn't mark. French and German make you pick tu/du vs vous/Sie. Japanese forces a whole keigo register choice. Plenty of languages force grammatical gender on nouns and pronouns where English leaves it unspecified. So an English source sentence can be genuinely silent about something the target *cannot* leave silent. The translator has to commit.

Now the tempting move is to say: MT can't commit because it doesn't know the social situation. But that's not quite right, and I think the more interesting point is this. Modern MT often *does* commit correctly, not because it models the relationship, but because the source text's register, genre, and vocabulary are statistically correlated with the demographic facts of who writes that kind of text to whom. It's reading the corpus's social structure, not the situation. Which means its "correct" choices are inherited priors, not inferences. The tell is what happens when you strip the cues: give it a short, contextless line where the relationship is genuinely underdetermined, and the commitment it makes is just the corpus-wide majority. It isn't guessing about *this* speaker; it's reporting a global frequency.

So my thesis: Hofstadter is right that MT doesn't understand, but he's looking for the wrong symptom. The symptom isn't failing on wordplay. It's that MT is *systematically unaccountable* for its choices. Which leads to the bit I'm least sure about -- the criterion. I want to say a translation is "understanding-laden" to the extent the translator could defend it: could they give a reason, and would a different reason have produced a different output? A reason-responsive translator is one for whom the counterfactual matters. MT has no such counterfactual -- perturb the input and you get a different output, sure, but there's no *reason* being tracked, just probability mass shifting. I think this is what's really behind the "shallow" charge.

Two worries I already have and want you to press on:

(a) Is the criterion circular? If "could defend it" just means "can produce reasons a community accepts," then I've smuggled in a social theory of understanding to define understanding, and it's trivially true that MT fails.

(b) Is the empirical claim even right? Maybe MT *does* infer tu/vous from subtle local cues in a way that's more than global frequency. If so my "tell" collapses.

Can you tell me: (1) is the audience problem a real upgrade on Hofstadter, or am I just renaming his point? (2) does the defensibility criterion survive (a), or do I have to bite a bullet somewhere? (3) anywhere I'm making a factual claim about MT that's just wrong. And if you think the whole framing is a dead end, say so plainly -- I'd rather hear it now.
