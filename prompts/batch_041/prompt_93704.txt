I'm a grad student TA-ing a seminar on reparations, and there's a pattern in the actual practice that I can't make sense of. I want you to act as a philosopher of law and political philosophy — someone at home both in the responsibility literature and in how real legal systems actually handle inherited claims — and please think carefully before you write anything. Start by brainstorming the possible readings, then give me the analysis.

Here's the pattern. On one side, we treat a lot of obligations as fully heritable across generations. If my grandfather dies in debt, the debt comes out of his estate. Mortgages outlive borrowers. States keep servicing the debts of predecessor regimes they'd rather forget about. Britain borrowed roughly £20 million in the 1830s to compensate slave owners for the loss of their human property — a sum equal to something like 40% of the Treasury's annual spending — paid the freed people nothing, and some of the annuities issued to those owners were still being paid off in the 2010s. And when the Nazis stole paintings, the non-binding principles that came out of the 1998 Washington conference on Holocaust-era assets, signed off by 44 countries, don't stop at the original owner: they explicitly invite the prewar owners' heirs to come forward, and say the case should be settled justly and fairly. So a stolen painting can be claimed by the grandchild of the person it was stolen from.

On the other side, we're fiercely resistant to inherited moral claims of other kinds. Criminal liability dies with the criminal; we don't jail the kids of murderers. And when the topic is reparations for slavery in the US or Britain, the standard objection is exactly this one — I never owned slaves, so why should I pay? Meanwhile the reparations programs that actually did get passed tend to pay the directly wronged individual and nobody else: the 1988 Civil Liberties Act paid $20,000 to each surviving Japanese American internee. And when Germany finally settled with Namibia in 2021 over the Herero and Nama genocide, it was about 1.1 billion euros of development aid, deliberately not labeled reparations, with Germany pointedly refusing to accept legal responsibility.

So what does this whole patchwork presuppose — about responsibility, about what a person can inherit, and about what a state is? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Then tell me which parts of the practice it strictly requires versus which parts it merely happens to fit, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. several different theories would license the same pattern.

Two framings I've been kicking around, which I'd like you to assess: (1) a "title theory" — model slavery not as a completed past wrong but as an ongoing deprivation of something never returned, so the claim travels with the beneficiaries of the asset the way the claim on the stolen painting travels with the painting; and (2) a "corporate-continuity theory" — the state is one legal person that outlives all of us, so its liabilities persist while individual citizens' guilt doesnt; citizens are basically shareholders who inherit the polity's balance sheet, assets and debts alike.

Finally: if we swapped the presupposed view for its opposite, what concretely would have to change in how reparations programs, art restitution, and sovereign debt are run? And deal with the netting problem — lots of people descend from both slaveholders and enslaved people, so do these theories even have the resources to say what such a person owes or is owed?
