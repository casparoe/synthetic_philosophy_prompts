Write a short story about a woman who rents her memory. Everything she would otherwise have to hold in her head — appointments, promises, the names of her students, the running commentary she thinks in — is hosted on a subscription service she has relied on for decades, and it is as transparent to her as her own recall. Take the constitutive claim of the extended mind thesis literally in her case: the service is not a tool she consults but a proper part of the cognitive system she is. The tension the story should turn on is one the standard examples leave untouched: her extension satisfies every coupling condition one could ask for — it is constantly and immediately available, consulted without effort, and its deliverances are endorsed without hesitation — and yet it is someone else's property, subject to price changes, terms of service, and a deletion schedule. Let one other character press the obvious objection, that long use no more makes the service part of her mind than her glasses are part of her eyes, and do not let the story settle who is right. Include a scene in which access fails, and let her reaction — grief, irritation, or something she cannot classify — be the crux. Do not resolve the story by revealing that the company has secretly been manipulating her thoughts; the interest lies in what follows from the coupling conditions and the contract alone.
