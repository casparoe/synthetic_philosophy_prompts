For several years I have taught a graduate seminar on the measurement problem, and I am now revising the Everett chapter of a book manuscript — which means I would rather learn the weaknesses of my position now than after publication. The position I have come to hold is this: the Everettian need not, and should not, treat the Born rule as a postulate, because the decision-theoretic strategy inaugurated by Deutsch and developed at length by Wallace — in which the probabilities of quantum mechanics are recovered from the non-probabilistic axioms of the theory together with the representation-theoretic machinery of classical decision theory, applied to an agent who knows the branch structure of the world but not which branch she will turn out to occupy — succeeds well enough that the old complaint that many-worlds leaves no room for probability is no longer compelling. I take this to be more than a technical consolation prize: it is what licenses the Everettian to say that there is a genuine fact about what a rational agent should expect, and so that the interpretation is empirically serious rather than a formalism in search of an epistemology. What I want from you is not a survey of the familiar criticisms but a map of my exposure — which observations, arguments, or formal results should count against the view, sorted by how decisive they would be, and which of them a committed defender ought himself to concede rather than brush aside.

Begin with the assumptions, since that is where I feel least secure. Which of them is doing the real work — in particular, the requirement that a rational agent be indifferent between branches that are physically identical, which appears to be what converts a symmetry of the formalism into a constraint on credence? If that requirement is best read as a disguised physical postulate rather than a condition of rationality, does the argument become circular; and if it is a genuine condition of rationality, what establishes it as one? I would also like the permissibility/mandatory distinction handled squarely. Suppose the derivation shows only that Born-rule credences are rationally permissible among agents satisfying the axioms, and not that they are uniquely correct: how much of what I want to claim survives, and would an Everettian who simply postulated the Born rule be any worse off? A student in my seminar pressed a related point that I have not been able to answer to my own satisfaction — that an argument showing a rational agent should act as though the Born rule holds is not yet an argument that it holds, so the derivation may vindicate Everettian practice while leaving Everettian probability as mysterious as before. Tell me how much weight that distinction between action-guiding and truth-tracking senses of probability can bear, and whether the self-locating-uncertainty treatment of the problem fares any better or merely relocates the difficulty into an account of personal identity across branches.

Finally, play the other side for me. What would a well-informed Copenhagen or Bohmian say is the reason no such derivation can ever work, and which of those objections should I take seriously rather than dismiss as question-begging? Then step back and tell me honestly whether the position as I have stated it has been insulated from refutation — whether any conceivable evidence or proof could count against it — and if so, whether that is a defect of the position or simply a feature of claims at this level of abstraction. I am less interested in a verdict than in knowing what I am actually betting on.
