I write about science and ideas for a couple of magazines, and right now I'm a few hundred words into a piece about the secular mindfulness boom — the stress-reduction clinic Kabat-Zinn started in Massachusetts in the late seventies, the eight-week protocol that got exported into hospitals and law firms, the mega-apps, the corporate wellness contracts, the whole strange arc of a monastic practice becoming a productivity tool. The reporting part is going fine. What I'm missing is a philosophical spine, and a friend in my reading group floated a position she called tranquilism, which I think might be exactly the frame. Let me state it the way I understand it and you can tell me if I've mangled it: on this view the only thing that matters for how well a life goes is the absence of craving and of suffering. Not pleasure, not love, not insight, not achievement — none of that adds anything on top. Someone with no craving and no suffering is already doing as well as a person can do, and everything else is either a means to that or beside the point. (Is that the standard way to put it? I'd rather not build the whole essay on a strawman.)

Here's what I want from you, and I want it run in both directions. Take the modern mindfulness movement as the episode and read it through the theory: who the actual actors are — the clinicians, the app companies, the HR departments buying licenses, the retreat teachers, the people who download the app at 2 a.m. because they can't sleep — and what tranquilism says each of them is really pursuing, as opposed to what they say they're pursuing. Then tell me which features of the movement the theory explains cleanly (I'm thinking of the emphasis on non-striving, the suspicion of pleasant distraction, the way the pitch is always relief from stress and craving rather than bliss or excitement) and where it misdescribes or simply misses things: the movement's own rhetoric of flourishing and compassion, the reports — which I don't want to overstate — that long intensive retreats sometimes leave people anxious or depersonalized, the fact that most practitioners seem to come for relief and then stay for something they'd struggle to describe as the absence of anything. Be honest about the parts where tranquilism just has nothing to say instead of forcing it to speak. Then flip it: what does this episode show about tranquilism as a theory of wellbeing? Does the boom confirm it, embarrass it, or reveal that it's the sort of view that can absorb any evidence you throw at it? I'm especially curious whether the theory has any principled way to distinguish "no craving" from a pleasant numbness, and whether it can make sense of why the industry's own customers keep describing their good states in positive terms.

No citations needed — it's a general-interest magazine and I'll do the sourcing myself, I just need the argument tight. Please write it in American English, my editor is fussy about that and I've already been scolded twice this month for a "realise."
