I am applying for a small four-month pilot grant (the fund is aimed at conceptual work and the deliverable is a two-page proposal), and I would like you to turn a half-formed idea of mine into something defensible. The topic is the symbol grounding problem, and I want the proposal pitched at the general question rather than at a verdict about any particular current system.

The idea is this. Discussions of grounding habitually run together two requirements that can be pulled apart. The first is anchoring: a symbol's reference is fixed by a non-symbolic, causal-informational link to whatever it is about — the kind of link a thermometer has to temperature, or a robot's edge detector to edges. The second is competence: the system can deploy the symbol correctly in novel contexts, notice and repair its own misapplications, and has some grip on what would make a use of it wrong. Most parties in the debate assume that anchoring is what buys competence, or that competence without anchoring is a cheap imitation that will eventually be exposed. I want to take seriously the possibility of competence without anchoring — a system whose corrections come entirely from further symbols rather than from the world — and to ask what, if anything, is actually missing in such a system.

So the question I want the proposal to make precise is: is there a non-question-begging test that separates a representation that is genuinely grounded from an inferentially rich surrogate that is not? And if there is no such test, is the correct conclusion that the problem dissolves, or that it relocates — specifically, that it relocates to a narrower capacity, namely the capacity for one's representations to be corrected by the world rather than only by other representations?

Please write the two-page proposal. It should state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the obvious positions — the sensorimotor response, the functional-role or inferentialist response, and the deflationary response that says there is nothing here to explain — each leave it open rather than settling it; set out the method concretely, as a plan of work by month, including what the test cases would actually be (for instance: perceptual illusion and systematically mislabelled input, terms introduced by stipulation with no perceptual application at all, cases where the world refuses to cooperate, and uses whose correctness turns on which object is at issue rather than on how it is described), and which of those cases would be decisive rather than merely illustrative; say what a positive result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and which sub-question I could abandon without losing the main line. Be honest about the scope: four months, one person, no lab, and tell me what has to be cut.

Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a literature review — refer to positions and arguments if you need to, and I will supply the references myself.
