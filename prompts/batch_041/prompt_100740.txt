I spent the better part of four decades as a working physicist, and since retiring I have been trying, with more enthusiasm than system, to educate myself in moral philosophy. I have reached the point where I can follow the standard debates, but I keep noticing that they were all constructed by and for human beings — creatures of bounded knowledge, limited causal reach, and a strong first-person stake in their own survival. This has led me to a question I would like you to help me settle in writing. Suppose we are designing the decision procedure of an artificial agent that will, in the relevant respects, know vastly more than any human ever has and act with vastly greater precision. Should such an agent reason as a utilitarian — selecting actions by their expected consequences, aggregated impartially across all affected parties — or as a deontologist, treating certain constraints as binding regardless of the consequences of honoring them? I want an essay that takes a position and defends it, not a survey that shrugs at the end. I am particularly interested in the following thread, which I suspect is where the real difficulty lies: many of the classic arguments for deontological constraint seem to presuppose precisely the epistemic and agential limitations that this new kind of agent would not share — the thought being that because we cannot reliably foresee or compute consequences, we do better to bind ourselves to rules. If that is the true source of deontology's appeal, then perhaps it simply lapses for an agent with near-perfect foresight and unlimited patience for calculation. The deontologist's reply, I take it, would be that the core of the position is not epistemic at all — that it rests on something like the separateness of persons, or on the thought that respect for a person cannot be traded against aggregate good — and that this core survives any increase in the agent's power. I would like you to press hard on both sides of that exchange and say which you find stronger, and then consider whether a hybrid is coherent: for instance, an agent whose criterion of rightness is consequentialist but whose decision procedure is rule-bound, so that it behaves as a deontologist without believing deontology. Take a concrete case where a constraint-violation would, on the agent's near-certain projection, avert a large amount of suffering, and say what the agent should do and why.

Please approach this as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of normative ethics and the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and begin by laying out, in a few sentences, the space of positions you intend to consider before you commit to arguing for one — I would rather see your map before your march. Be rigorous about the distinction between a theory of what makes actions right and a theory of how an agent should deliberate, since I have noticed that arguments in this area frequently equivocate between the two, and do not let the essay drift into vague talk about values or alignment without pinning down the normative claim. Because I am an amateur and apt to be persuaded by confident prose, I would also ask you to be explicit about your own epistemic position: state how confident you are in the verdict you reach, and single out the single point in your argument of which you are least sure, explaining what would have to be true for you to abandon it.
