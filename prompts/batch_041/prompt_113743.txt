I teach a second-year undergraduate module on probability and decision theory — eighteen students, one 90-minute seminar a week, and the room has those awful bolted-down chairs so anything involving moving about needs to be planned around a horseshoe of desks. Next week's session is on the Sleeping Beauty problem and I don't want the usual thing where I do a show of hands, announce that some people say 1/2 and some say 1/3, and then talk for forty minutes. I want them to run into the pull of the thirder's answer with their hands, and only hear the labels afterwards. Here's the shape I've half-worked out — please improve it, or scrap it and give me something better if it's broken: the seminar becomes a casino. Each round I flip a coin behind a big dice cup where nobody can see it. Heads, and I hand one "awakening card" to one student chosen at random; tails, and I hand cards to two different students. The card says only that they've just been awakened and that they can't tell whether they're the only awakening this round. Card-holders then write down a credence that the coin was tails, and may take either side of an even-money bet in chips, with stakes fixed before the settlement rule is revealed. Run fifteen or so rounds with a leaderboard. The part I actually care about is the sting in the tail: run a second version of the identical game where the payout is settled per experiment instead of per awakening — one pot for the round, split between the awakenings if there were two — and let them discover that the stake they should take changes. I want the debrief to hook what happened onto the selection-style argument for 2/3, the "nothing new happened when you woke up" argument for 1/2, and the awkward thought that which number is right tracks the reward structure and not the credence on its own. Nobody hears the name Sleeping Beauty until the debrief.

What I need from you: rules that actually work with eighteen people in fixed seats and no real money, a materials list I can assemble from a stationery cupboard (chips, index cards, a dice cup, that sort of thing), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, some roles for the students who aren't holding cards in a given round so they don't just switch off, the exact wording I should use for the two settlement rules so the second one doesn't get given away by the first, the debrief script, and an honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time — I've been burned before by activities where two students immediately spot the trick and the other sixteen spend twenty minutes watching them.
