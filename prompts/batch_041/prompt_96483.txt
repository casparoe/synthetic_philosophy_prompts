Write a short story, somewhere between two and three thousand words, built around a coordination problem rather than a character. The cast: a scattered set of people with no authority over one another and no shared institution, each of whom is privately able to do a single irreversible thing that would matter on a large scale, and each of whom holds evidence that makes doing it look more likely to help than to hurt. The act needs doing only once, by anyone; it cannot be undone or compensated for; and nobody has standing to forbid it. What I want the story to be about is that whoever eventually acts will not be the one with the best evidence but the one whose estimate is worst, since an inflated estimate of the benefit is exactly what makes acting look worth it — and about the group's slow, partial recognition of this, and the half-measure they adopt against it: a shared log on which anyone may declare an intention to act and anyone may object, which turns out to determine who gets there first rather than to filter for error. No character may explain the structure in a speech, and no narrator may name the moral. Give me the texture of people reasoning well on their own and arriving somewhere stupid together, and the particular solitude of the one who acted, or of the one who didn't. I have read enough of this argument already that I don't need it restated; I want the story.
