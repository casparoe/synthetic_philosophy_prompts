I retired some years ago from a career in experimental physics, and I have since been working through the philosophical literature on rational choice on my own. I keep finding that the textbook treatments of the St. Petersburg game leave my own reactions unexplained, and I would like your help in locating the principle, if there is one, that actually governs them. What follows is a report of my verdicts as I genuinely have them, not as I think I ought to have them.

Throughout, let a base game be the following: a fair coin is tossed until it first shows heads; if that first heads falls on the n-th toss, the payout is 2^n dollars.

(1) The unbounded base game. I would pay at most about twenty dollars to play, even though its expected value is infinite.

(2) The base game with payouts capped at 2^20 dollars, roughly a million. Its expected value is about twenty-one dollars, and I would pay about twenty. Here, at least, I am willing to pay the expected value.

(3) The base game with payouts capped at 2^40 dollars, roughly a trillion. Its expected value is about forty-one dollars, yet I would still pay only about twenty.

(4) Twenty independent fair coins, one dollar for each head. Expected value ten dollars; I would pay ten.

(5) A lottery with a one-in-a-million chance at a million dollars. Expected value one dollar; I would pay two.

(6) A variant in which the payout on the n-th toss is (−1)^n times 2^n divided by n. Its expectation, taken as an ordinary series, is finite, but the series converges only conditionally, so that reordering the outcomes changes the sum, and as a Lebesgue integral the expectation does not exist at all. I would still pay a few dollars for this game, though less than my twenty for (1), and I cannot say on what basis.

I am aware that (1) dominates (3) outcome by outcome, which makes my paying the same for both — and paying no more for the one that dominates — look indefensible, and I am aware that (5) runs in the opposite direction from (3): in one I overpay a long tail, in the other I underpay it.

My tentative principle, which I would like you to test and, if possible, repair, is this: price a game by its expected value computed after truncating all payouts at some ceiling, the ceiling being set by the largest sum a counterparty could actually be good for. I can already see that this cannot explain (5), since truncation can only lower a price and never raise it, and that it is not even well defined for (6). But I am more interested in whether something better can be found.

Please give me (a) the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely; (b) for each clause of it, the case of mine that forces that clause; (c) the verdicts it delivers on cases I have not listed — in particular, a version of the base game in which the coin is biased very slightly toward heads, with probability 0.5001, for which the expected value is finite and equal to about five thousand dollars. I find myself torn between paying that five thousand and paying my customary twenty, and I want to know which of my other verdicts commits me to which; and (d) an honest accounting of which of my verdicts (1) through (6) the principle cannot accommodate, and would require me to abandon.
