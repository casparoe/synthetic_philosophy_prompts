I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy, working on the metaphysics of chance, and a colleague has been pressing the following argument on me for weeks. I can no longer tell whether it identifies a real defect in the standard picture or whether it cheats somewhere, and what I want is a diagnosis rather than reassurance.

We both accept the principle usually called the Principal Principle: if you know that the objective chance of a proposition A is x, and the rest of your evidence is admissible — roughly, it bears on whether A only by bearing on the chance of A — then your credence in A ought to be x. My colleague now claims to show that the principle can never constrain anyone.

(1) Objective chances are not brute features of the world; a chance fact obtains in virtue of non-modal facts that ground it or make it true.
(2) On the leading reductive account of that grounding, the best-system account, the chance facts are exactly those delivered by the system of probabilistic laws that best balances simplicity, strength, and fit with the complete history of the world.
(3) The complete history of the world includes what happens after the time at which a chance is ascribed. So a chance fact at t is fixed, at least in part, by events after t.
(4) A chance fact at t therefore could not have obtained unless things after t had gone a certain way. His illustration: if the best system assigns chance one half to heads on a particular toss, that assignment is hostage to the actual run of outcomes; a sufficiently unrepresentative future run would have made a different system fit better, and the chance would have differed.
(5) So if you know that the chance at t of A is x, you know something that constrains how things go after t. You have, in effect, information about whether A and its ilk occur, over and above the bare content that the chance of A is x.
(6) Information about whether A occurs is the paradigm of inadmissible evidence. So the chance fact is itself inadmissible.
(7) The Principal Principle tells you what to do only conditional on the chance fact and admissible evidence. If the chance fact is inadmissible, its antecedent is never satisfied.
(8) So the Principal Principle is vacuous: it never constrains anyone.
(9) If, to rescue it, we stipulate that chance facts are always admissible, the principle then instructs us to set our credence to x even where the chance fact carries information about the outcome — that is, it instructs us to disregard evidence. So the principle is false.
(10) Either way, there is no such thing as rationally deferring to objective chance.

I am unwilling to accept (10). It seems close to a datum that an agent who knows a fair coin's chance of heads is one half, and who has no other relevant information, should be at one half on heads; if that is not rational deference to chance, I do not know what is. Nor do I want to abandon the best-system account lightly, since it is the only account I know that makes chance metaphysically respectable without positing primitive modal facts. My inclination is that the trouble lies in the passage from (3) to (5) — from the claim that the chance fact is grounded in the whole history to the claim that knowing the chance fact is knowing something about the future — but I cannot say precisely what is wrong with it, and I suspect the real difficulty may instead lie at (6).

Tell me which step to reject. If the argument is valid, say which premise is least costly to give up and what the bill comes to: in particular, whether giving it up leaves objective chance a distinctive role in constraining credence, or collapses chance into credence. If the argument is invalid, name the error as precisely as you can and explain why the offending step looked compelling. Finally, say what becomes of the argument if the best-system account is replaced by a different grounding story for chance — say, one on which chances are primitive, or one on which they are propensities of particular setups.
