I'm a second-year PhD student in formal epistemology, and my department is half decision theory, half econ-adjacent philosophy of science, so I spend most of my life in seminar rooms arguing about priors. My flatmate is doing a micro theory PhD and is convinced that Aumann settled all of this in 1976 with the agreeing-to-disagree result — the one that says if two people have the same prior and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge (each knows the other's number, each knows the other knows, and so on up), then those posteriors have to be equal. Everyone in his world draws the obvious moral: two rational peers with a common prior and the same information simply cannot stably disagree. If you're still disagreeing, then either someone is being irrational, or your priors were never really the same, or one of you is sitting on information they haven't pooled. We had this arguemnt again on Sunday night and I've definately had enough of losing it, so I want it written out properly as a dialogue instead — partly because I think my side keeps getting flattened into a slogan, and I want to see whether it survives contact with someone who actually knows the theorem rather than just the folk version of it.

So: write me a dialogue between an enforcer of the Aumann line and a dissenter who thinks the theorem is true but nearly irrelevant to the disagreements real people have — and please make the dissenter at least as sharp as the enforcer, not a vibes merchant. Give them a concrete case to chew on rather than leaving it abstract: the two of them are looking at the same regression table from the same paper, one thinks the coefficient is real, the other thinks it's noise, and neither has any private data the other lacks. The enforcer claims this is impossible; the dissenter claims it's every Tuesday. I want the enforcer to actually state the conditions of the theorem rather than gesturing at it — a common prior, each agent's information a partition of the state space (so you always know what you know and know what you don't, and can never simply fail to have considered a possibility), perfect logical omniscience, and a state space rich enough to include facts about the other agent's information. Then I want the following aired, in whatever order they naturally arise: (i) common knowledge versus mutual knowledge, and the point that weakening common knowledge to near-common belief only gets you near-agreement, so a partial failure of the condition buys you almost nothing; (ii) what happens to the theorem if you drop the partition assumption, or if you let one agent be unaware of a possibility the other is entertaining — I think there's a real escape hatch here and I want to see the enforcer defend the assumption rather than treat it as costless; (iii) the non-constructivity worry, that the theorem tells you the agreed number exists but not how any bounded agent could ever find it, and what that means for what we should actually do in a kitchen at one in the morning; (iv) the betting move — if you really believe your number, why won't you take my odds — and why that move is weaker than it looks; (v) whether applying the theorem to our disagreement about the theorem is circular; and (vi) the gap between the theorem as a result about common knowledge and conciliationism as a norm, since "split the difference with your peer" doesn't seem to follow from it at all.

Craft notes: both of them should be competent, and each should respond to the other's actual sentences rather than restating a position, so real back-and-forth and no talking past each other. Let each win a point, let each concede something specific and unwillingly, and don't force them to converge at the end — I'd rather it close on a genuine impasse than on "well, we both learned something". No reading lists, no citations, no name-dropping of literature beyond what's already in this brief; if a character uses a piece of jargon, they should define it in their own words as they go. Aim for something in the region of 1,500 to 2,000 words, with at least four substantive exchanges each, and include at least one moment where one of them says something the other can't answer straight away — ideally with the enforcer having to admit a real cost of the view instead of retreating to "the assumptions simply don't hold". It's for a reading group, so it needs to be worth arguing with.
