I'm writing a seminar paper for a philosophy of psychiatry course and I want to use a real episode as a test case for Ian Hacking's account of looping effects, but I keep going in circles over whether the episode confirms the account or breaks it. The episode is the rise and fall of multiple personality disorder — later renamed dissociative identity disorder — in late twentieth-century North America. The outline, as I understand it: the diagnosis barely existed before the 1970s, when a best-selling book and then a television film about a young woman with many personalities made it famous; it entered the diagnostic manual as a distinct category around 1980; over the following decade it was diagnosed in thousands of patients, overwhelmingly by a small group of clinicians who specialized in it and almost entirely on one continent; then the recovered-memory controversies of the 1990s discredited much of the surrounding therapeutic practice, and new diagnoses fell off sharply. I want you to run that trajectory through Hacking's framework — looping effects, the contrast between interactive and indifferent kinds, and his thought that we make up people — and to be disciplined about the mapping rather than gesturing at social construction in general. Concretely: what exactly is the kind here, who or what are the classifiers, and which institutions, practices and expectations make up the niche that sustains the diagnosis? Which features of the trajectory does the looping story actually predict, and which does it merely redescribe after the fact? And I'd like you to say where the framework goes silent rather than forcing it to speak. The question I can't get past is how to tell a looping effect apart from the discovery of a genuine condition that had simply been under-diagnosed and under-reported; Hacking's account seems to make the difference turn on whether the classified respond to being classified, and I can't see what observation would settle the matter one way or the other. So tell me what the account can and cannot distinguish here, and whether it is anything more than a self-fulfilling prophecy plus Giddens's double hermeneutic, or whether the extra element — that the classification itself is altered by the people it classifies — does real work.

Once that is on the table, I want the harder test case, which is the declassification of homosexuality by the American Psychiatric Association in the early 1970s: years of protest by gay activists at the association's annual meetings, a decision by its leadership to strike the category, and a subsequent referendum of the membership that upheld the decision, with a narrower residual category about distress surviving for a while and eventually being dropped too. Hacking's picture suggests that when a classification of people takes hold, the people classified are reshaped by it and the kind moves under the classifier's feet. What happened there looks like the reverse: the people classified organized, contested the classification, and got the classifiers to change their minds — and the people did not disappear; the category did. Does that falsify the account or refine it? Is there a principled difference between the two episodes — say, that in one case the classified became classifiers themselves while in the other they accepted and inhabited the label — or does Hacking need an explicit distinction between a loop that runs through the classified and a loop that runs through the classifying profession? I'd also like the payoff for the larger claim that human kinds are moving targets, and therefore that psychiatry cannot hope for the stable taxonomy it wants — for instance in current efforts to ground diagnosis in biomarkers rather than symptom clusters. But push back on Hacking hardest at the point where the account threatens to become unfalsifiable: if the diagnosis spreading, the diagnosis receding, and the diagnosis being repudiated outright can all be narrated as looping effects, tell me what observation would count against the account, and whether either of these two episodes supplies one.
