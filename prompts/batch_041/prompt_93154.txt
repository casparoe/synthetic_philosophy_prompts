I teach a graduate seminar on social ontology, and for two years now the discussion of whether generations are real kinds has gone in exactly the same circle, so I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than one more round of it.

The circle looks like this. On one side stand the generational realists: cyclical theorists in the Strauss–Howe vein, the polling organizations that publish birth-year bands, and the journalists and psychologists who treat a cohort's formative conditions — the media environment it grew up in, the wars and recessions it lived through — as having stamped a durable and distinctive character on its members. Their case is not foolish. Cohorts really do encounter different formative conditions, peer socialization is real, and birth-year differences in attitudes do show up in surveys. On the other side stand the skeptics, mostly demographers and sociologists, who hold that a cohort is a bookkeeping category rather than a collective with a character, and who point out that age, period, and cohort effects cannot be disentangled, since a cohort is simply period minus age; that variation within a birth band swamps the differences between bands; that the band edges are arbitrary and get redrawn whenever a trend is inconvenient; and that a single set of people has been labelled shiftless and then exemplary within one lifetime. Both sides accept the same survey data. Neither has moved.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that it is three disagreements wearing one coat: a verbal one about what the word 'generation' is doing; an empirical one that is partly formal, because the age-period-cohort identity means the quantities the two sides want to compare are not separately identifiable without assumptions that no one is entitled to stipulate; and a meta-ontological one about what it takes for a social kind to be real at all. Tell me where that decomposition is right, where it is wrong, and which of the three is doing the real work.

I want the diagnosis to engage two analogies that I find suggestive and that I suspect neither side has taken seriously enough. The first is the species-as-individuals view: a species is not a kind in the way a chemical element is, but a bounded historical particular whose boundary is fixed by descent rather than by shared properties. The second is the treatment of race as a social kind — not a biological kind, but a classification whose imposition has real causal and self-fulfilling effects on the people classified, so that the kind's reality lies in the classifying practice rather than in any trait of its members. Could generations be real in the first way, as historical particulars with determinate membership and no shared essence, so that the hunt for a generational character is simply the wrong hunt? Or real in the second way, so that whether Millennials exist turns out to be a question about who is doing the classifying and to what end? Say which analogy earns its keep and which distorts, and what follows for each side from your answer.

Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here — a conceptual distinction, an identifying assumption that could be defended rather than stipulated, a study design or comparison that would settle something, a formal result showing that nothing can be settled, or a reason to stop asking the question in this form? Be concrete, and give me your reasoning rather than a reading list; I am asking what you can argue, not what I should go read.
