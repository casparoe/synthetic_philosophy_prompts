So I've been going back and forth with some friends in a Discord server — the kind where everyone has read too much LessWrong and not enough actual philology — about the old Mencius vs. Xunzi thing, whether human nature is good or bad, and I keep feeling like the disagreement is either much deeper than people make out or not a real disagreement at all, and I can't tell which. I'd like you to write an essay that actually takes a side on this and defends it. Don't bother with chapter-and-verse citations, I care about the argument, not the footnotes.

Here's what I want the essay to actually grapple with. Both of them are Confucians, both think ritual, education and self-cultivation are the entire point of being a person, and both seem to agree that a person left completely untutored turns out badly. Mencius has the four sprouts — the twinge you feel watching a kid about to fall down a well, the sense of shame, the deference, the sense of right and wrong — and thinks morality is like a seedling: you don't manufacture it, you water it, and failure is just what happens when the environment mangles it (his image of the mountain that looks bald because people keep going up with axes). Xunzi thinks nature hands you a mess of appetites, love of profit, envy, resentment, and that whatever's good in you comes from deliberate effort (wei) and from the sages' rituals reshaping you the way steam bends a crooked plank or a whetstone sharpens a dull blade. And his dig on Mencius is that if nature were already good, ritual would be pointless — you'd just let the sprouts grow.

Now the twist I want you to take seriously. Run a translation test: read "xing" in Mencius as "the motivational endowment an infant has before any enculturation," and read it the same way in Xunzi. Under that reading, is there any substantive disagreement left, or is it a verbal dispute about whether the word "good" attaches to the raw endowment or to what that endowment reliably turns into under decent conditions? Then make it harder by importing some developmental psychology: if it turns out babies really do have spontaneous comforting and helping tendencies, but those tendencies are super fragile and get shaped past recognition by culture, who gets to claim they were right? Is the disagreement empirical, conceptual, or evaluative, and does that change which one you should endorse? I've absorbed enough of the rationalist canon to reflexively reach for "this is a verbal dispute," but I honestly don't know how to tell when that's the right call versus when it's a cop-out that hides a real disagreement. So please commit: say who you think is closer to right, and about what, rather then ending on "they're both partly right, it's complicated."
