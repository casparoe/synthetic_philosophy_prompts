I've spent the better part of a year reporting on how open platforms, scholarly associations, and a few legislatures are handling organized intolerance in their ranks, and in almost every interview the same figure of speech turns up. An open society, I'm told, is like an immune system. Its health lies not in what it admits but in what it refuses: early on, and largely irreversibly, the body learns to tolerate its own tissue and to reject what is foreign, and it does the sorting before the intruder has done any damage. By this light, a polity that tolerates its declared enemies is not nobly open; it is immunodeficient, or worse, autoimmune — mistaking its own defenders for invaders while ushering the pathogen through the door. Those who reach for the image usually extract a practical lesson from it: that the way to protect a tolerant order is to shut the intolerant out at the point of entry, pre-emptively and by rule, rather than to admit them and fight it out in the open afterward. What philosophers call the paradox of tolerance hovers in the background of all of this.

I'd like you to audit the analogy, not settle it.

Begin with an explicit mapping: for each element of the immune story — self and nonself, the thymus, the antibody, the regulatory cell, the autoimmune reaction, the allergic one, the memory of a past infection — say what, if anything, plays that role in the political case, and flag plainly where nothing does.

Then press on where the comparison gives way. An immunologist I interviewed insisted on a distinction the popular version flattens: central tolerance, in which self-reactive cells are weeded out in the thymus before they ever circulate, versus peripheral tolerance, in which cells that escaped that screening are held in check out in the body's tissues by regulatory mechanisms. Ask whether that two-tier structure has a political counterpart — admission rules versus after-the-fact moderation, say — and whether the biology actually favors the gatekeeping conclusion my sources drew from it, given how much of the immune system's daily work consists of learning to live with harmless foreign things rather than expelling them.

Finally, tell me which of the disanalogies bear on that practical conclusion and which are merely decorative, whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table, and what analogy would do the job better if this one won't.

I don't need citations or a reading list. Please write your answer in American English.
