I write about philosophy and the sciences for a British weekly, and I am partway into a short book on certainty — on the family of views that descend from Wittgenstein's On Certainty, according to which some propositions are not held on evidence at all but instead form the fixed scaffolding against which evidence, doubt and justification become possible. I do not want the book to be a tour of the secondary literature. I want its spine to be the questions that are genuinely live, and I want to be able to defend the claim that these are the live ones.

To keep this from collapsing into a survey, let me put three ideas on the table. I have not seen them treated as a cluster, and I suspect they are where the interesting work now is.

First, hinge drift: a proposition quietly ceasing to function as a hinge without anyone's having doubted it, argued against it, or being able to date the change. Second, hinge collision: encounters between communities — scientific, religious, legal, professional — whose scaffoldings differ, where the obstacle is not disagreement about evidence but the fact that each side's reasoning is only intelligible against its own background. Third, hinge engineering: deliberate attempts, in schooling, professional training, platform design or the deployment of language models, to install or to dismantle a presupposition rather than to argue for or against it.

Please give me a defended agenda rather than a survey. Assume the relevant effort is mine: roughly a year of reading and a book of about 80,000 words, written for an educated general reader rather than for specialists. Rank the open questions you think I should build the book around, and justify the order. For each, I want to know why it is upstream of the others — which questions become easier, or pointless, once it is settled; how tractable it is for someone working from the armchair rather than from empirical psychology, history of science or sociology; whether it is neglected or merely unfashionable; and what argument or development over the next two or three years would move it up or down my list. Tell me which criterion you are weighting most heavily, and where the criteria pull against one another. Then tell me what to leave out, and why leaving it out is safe.

I would also like a short final section on where the field's effort looks misallocated: which questions absorb attention because they are tidy or because they already have an established vocabulary, and which consequential ones are avoided because they are messy. Where you think my three ideas are not the promising ones, say so and substitute better ones.

Two constraints. First, no reading lists, bibliographies or citations: I can find the literature on my own; what I cannot do is work out from the literature alone what deserves my year. Second, please write in British English.
