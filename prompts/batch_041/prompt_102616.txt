I am trying to decide whether to endorse a lottocratic legislature, and I have realized that I cannot evaluate any proposal until I know what a theory of its legitimacy would have to deliver in the first place. So write the desiderata: what any adequate account of why a randomly selected body's decisions carry political authority must get right. Give me the full specification — the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it must be consistent with — each with the consideration that makes it a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp. I am especially interested in the distinctions between procedural and outcome legitimacy, between "everyone had an equal chance of being selected" and "the body mirrors the population," and between selection by lot and selection by rotation. Test the resulting list against a scheme I have been sketching: seats assigned by lot but stratified so that the chamber matches the population both demographically and on some measure of public-reasoning competence, with a technical panel empowered to veto a narrow class of questions. Tell me which requirements that scheme strains or violates, and flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — that is the part I most want to see. Skip the scorecard of existing real-world assemblies; I want the criteria themselves.
