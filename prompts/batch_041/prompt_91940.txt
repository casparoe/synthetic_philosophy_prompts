I was at a small interpetability workshop last month -- not the big flashy one, the kind where people actually argue -- and afterward I ended up at a table with two of the speakers, who spent forty minutes going at each other over whether the network in one of the talks really represented the thing the probe found. I never did figure out who was right, and I write about this stuff for a living, so maybe you can sort it out.

Background, because it matters: the model was a small transformer trained on synthetic sequences in which, half the time, one token was a repeat of something earlier, and the network had to predict a different next token when that happened. It hit 94% on held-out data. The speaker -- call her the prober -- fit a linear probe on the middle-layer activations, and it decoded the repeat flag at 99.9%. Then she went further: on inputs where the flag was absent she added the probe direction to the activations, and the network behaved as if it were there about 70% of the time; and when she projected that direction out, accuracy dropped from 94% to 61%. Her conclusion: the network has an internal representation of the repeat flag and we found it.

The other guy wasn't buying it. First move: decodability is cheap. In an activation space that big you can linearly decode almost anything -- token position, whether the sequence came from the training set, all sorts of things the network isn't using. Her reply was that she didn't just decode it, she steered with it and she broke it by ablating, and if that isn't what it takes for a representation to count as used, nothing could ever meet the bar. He said that shows the direction is causally hooked up to the output, which isn't the same as the network representing the flag. A knob you can turn isn't necessarily a knob the network turns.

Then he said something I couldn't answer on the spot: whatever is going on inside might be something else entirely that just co-varies with the flag -- maybe it's tracking how surprising the current token is, or whether it's in the second half of the sequence, and the flag is correlated with that in this dataset. She said fine, that's empirical, you'd build a dataset where the two come apart and see which one the direction follows. He said sure, but until someone runs that, the finding is consistent with both stories, so the paper shouldn't have said the network represents X.

Then it got weirder, which is the part I actually want help with. She said at some point representation just means there's a state carrying information the network uses, one you can intervene on and predict, and wanting more than that is wanting magic. He said no, there's a real difference between information being present in a state and that state being used as a representation of that content -- and anyway the probe is her construct, a little model she fit on top of the network, so the representation might live in the probe, not the network. She said that's like saying the thermometer has the temperature. He said no, it's like saying a regression you fit to a system's outputs is part of the system. They also disagreed about whether any of this is a factual question at all: he said represents is partly a stance we take because it's useful for prediction, she said that's a cop-out that would make every claim about hidden structure a stance.

Can you go through it move by move -- who's right about the decodability point, the intervention point, the confound point, and the probe-is-my-construct point, and why each one rates the way it does? And then the bottom line: is there a fact of the matter about whether that little network represents the repeat flag, or is this a verbal dispute dressed up as a scientific one? If there is a fact of the matter, tell me what both of them are missing about how you'd settle it.
