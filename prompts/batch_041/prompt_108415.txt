I write literary fiction and have a working craftsman's vocabulary for motive — want, obstacle, scene, reversal, the beat where a resolve breaks — but no formal training in philosophy, and I would rather have a translation than a primer. Take the philosophical debate about weakness of will, including the branch of it that runs through hyperbolic discounting and preference reversal, and restate it in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: the agent's settled verdict about what is best, the intention she forms, the act she performs against it, and the reader's need to find her blameworthy and comprehensible at the same time. Then give me the residue report. What survives translation intact — I assume commitment devices do, since a character who lashes himself to the mast is a scene I can write? What survives only in distorted form — the discount rate, for instance, which for me can only be a kind of person and not a number? And what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I am most curious whether the philosophers' puzzle, that weakness of will looks impossible, is a puzzle my vocabulary cannot even state, or one it never generates because it never assumed that action answers to a single authoritative verdict. Where the translation obliges you to smuggle in an assumption my craft does not have, say so rather than smoothing it over. Finish by telling me how confident you are in each part of the report and singling out the point you are least sure of.
