I serve on the oversight board of our country's National Records and Memory Authority. Twenty years ago the Truth and Reconciliation Commission deposited its working archive with us under a statutory seal; the seal expires in six months, and at our next session the board must decide what becomes of the records. I have to circulate a written opinion before then, and I would like your help drafting it.

The archive is not the clean thing the public imagines. It holds three kinds of material that pull in different directions. First, testimony given to the Commission in confidence by survivors, on the explicit understanding that names would not be published in their lifetimes. Second, statements by low-level perpetrators who cooperated in exchange for confidentiality, some of whom are still living and were never prosecuted. Third, surveillance files: dossiers compiled by the old security service on thousands of people, most of whom were never charged, some of whom are now public figures and some of whom are not.

On top of this — and this is what actually divides the board — there is the problem of what is not there. Large parts of the record were destroyed before the handover, and whole communities, the ones the old state administered orally or refused to register at all, appear in the archive only as the objects of other people's paperwork, if at all. A coalition of descendant communities has petitioned us not to treat these gaps as gaps. They want the Authority to accession the silences themselves: to maintain a standing register of documents known to have existed and been destroyed, of populations the state never recorded, and of questions the Commission never put, and to give that register the same standing as the surviving files, so that anyone consulting the archive is confronted with its absences rather than misled by them. A second petition, from journalists, asks us to publish at least the index of the surveillance files — who was watched, when, by which unit — even if the contents stay sealed, on the ground that having been watched is itself a fact about a person's life, and one the state owes them.

The board is split. One faction says the seal should simply lapse: the public's right to know, and the victims' claim to a public record, outweigh confidentiality promises that were made by a body with no authority to bind the future. The other says a promise is a promise, that releasing informant lists will get people hurt or killed, and that a register of absences is mysticism — you cannot accession what you do not hold, and an archive that fills itself with its own negations stops being an archive.

I need an opinion of no more than three pages that recommends one concrete course of action — full release, continued sealing, or conditional release with criteria stated precisely enough that staff can apply them — together with a timetable the board could actually vote on. It should take the losing side's strongest point seriously rather than waving it away, and it should say plainly what we would be doing, and to whom, if we decide to treat a silence as a holding. I would rather have a decision I can defend in five years than one that merely splits the difference.
