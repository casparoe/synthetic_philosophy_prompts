I spent most of last night in a long argument with a close friend about how we should judge historical figures—specifically Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus—and about the defence that they should be judged by the standards of their own time. My friend pressed an argument that seemed to me to prove far too much, and I could not say exactly where it broke. I would like you to act as a philosopher of history and a moral philosopher, think carefully about the structure of the argument, and help me diagnose it. Before you answer, please brainstorm the possible weak points, then give a focused diagnosis.

The argument, as my friend reconstructed it, runs like this:

1. A person is morally blameworthy for an action only if they could reasonably have known that the action was wrong.
2. Whether a person could reasonably have known that an action was wrong is fixed by the epistemic standards of their time and place.
3. For any historical figure accused of a wrong, the epistemic standards of their time and place did not clearly identify that action as wrong, or positively permitted it.
4. So such a figure could not reasonably have known that the action was wrong.
5. So such a figure is not morally blameworthy for the action.
6. Nothing in steps 1 through 5 depends on the figure being historical. The same reasoning applies to any person whose community did not clearly identify their action as wrong.
7. Therefore no one is morally blameworthy for an action that their community permitted or did not clearly condemn.
8. But we do hold people morally blameworthy for actions that their communities permitted, and we are right to do so in at least some cases.
9. Therefore one of the premises must be false.

My friend says this shows the man-of-his-time defence is either empty or monstrous: it either excuses no one or excuses everyone. I suspect the argument is doing something illegitimate, but I cannot decide whether the fault is in premise 1, in premise 2, in the move from 2 to 3, or in the way the argument treats blame as a single thing. I would rather preserve as much of the defence as I can, because I think there is something to the idea that people are not blameworthy for failing to know what no one around them knew. But I do not want to preserve it at the price of saying that a slaveholder in a slaveholding society, or a colonial administrator in a colonial society, is simply beyond moral criticism.

Please tell me which premise or step to reject, explain why it looked plausible in the first place, and then price the alternatives. In particular, distinguish what the man-of-his-time defence can legitimately do—excuse, mitigate, explain, contextualise—from what it cannot do, namely make an action right or place the actor beyond all moral appraisal. If the argument equivocates between moral blame and moral criticism, or between what was epistemically available and what was normatively correct, say so and show where the equivocation enters. If the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to give up, say that too, and tell me what each rejection costs. Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the diagnosis itself.
