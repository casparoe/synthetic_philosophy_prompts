I'm in a reading group on welfare economics and we keep banging into the same wall: everyone wants to aggregate or compare how well off different people are, and nobody can say what would make one such comparison *correct* rather than just a convention we've settled on. Before we read another paper I want a proper spec for the thing we're actually arguing about. So: write out the desiderata that any theory of interpersonal utility comparison has to meet to count as adequate. I want the full checklist -- the cases it has to get right (two people in very different circumstances, a judgement of who's worse off overall, the weird special case of comparing one person to themselves across time as a kind of fixed point, comparisons spanning different kinds of good like health vs money vs freedom, and the everyday comparisons the cost-benefit and QALY people actually make whether or not philosophers approve), the distinctions it can't collapse (ordinal vs cardinal vs ratio, comparing levels vs comparing gains, the difference between a comparison being vague and it being empty, the zero-point/neutrality problem), and the results it has to stay consistent with (whatever it is that makes some comparisons strike us as obviously true and others as obviously silly). Behind each requirement, put the case or consideration that motivates it, and tell me which of these are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudice of one camp -- the econometric convention that comparison needs a single common unit, say, or the opposing view that with no common unit comparison is meaningless.

The part I actually care about: flag any pair or subset of these desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, and say why. I have a suspicion that the demand for determinate, action-guiding comparisons is in tension with something like respect for first-person authority over one's own good, or with taking incommensurability seriously, but I'd rather you tell me which ones actually conflict than just agree with me. And if one requirement does more than any other to knock out the popular theories -- hedonic, preference-satisfaction, objective-list, and the various extended-preference and hybrid compromises -- say which one it is and what it kills.
