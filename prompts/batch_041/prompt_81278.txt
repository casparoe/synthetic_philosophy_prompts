For the past three years I have been the volunteer treasurer of a small mutual-aid fund: six of us, no paid staff, about thirty grants a month of between one hundred and fifty and seven hundred dollars, mostly rent arrears, a car repair, a deposit, a funeral. We meet on a Thursday evening video call for ninety minutes and have to get through a dozen applications in that time, and every decision has to be written up afterwards in a single paragraph for the funders who pay for the grants. Until last autumn we took people at their word: an applicant told us what had happened and what it would cost, one of us called them back with a few questions, and the committee voted. Then two things happened in the same month. A grant we had made for a car repair turned out to have gone to an old debt, which is not what we had approved; and a woman who had already been turned away by two other agencies stopped answering our calls after we asked her for a pay stub, and we learned later that she lost the room she had been trying to keep. Since then we have argued about verification at every meeting and got nowhere. Roughly half the committee wants proof before we pay anything — a pay stub, a landlord's letter, a screenshot of the past-due bill. The rest of us think the point of the fund is that people should not have to perform their poverty for a stranger in order to be believed. In three years I can count on one hand the applications we have refused for a reason a document would have caught, and we share a list of refused applicants with two other local groups, so anything we learn about someone does not stay with us.

What I cannot answer is the argument our chair keeps making. A friend of his in machine learning told him there is a proof — the friend calls it Good's theorem, though I have no idea whether that is the right name for it — that information which costs nothing to obtain, and which you are genuinely going to act on, cannot make you worse off on average: if you will choose differently after seeing it, then deciding after seeing it is at least as good as deciding now, and the only thing that can hurt you is the price of getting it. The chair's conclusion is that the only question for us is what the asking costs, and that until it costs more than the difference it makes we should be checking everything. I have two objections I cannot make precise. The first is that the information is not free; it is only free to us, and the person who pays for it is the one asking for help. The second is that I do not see where in his proof anyone is allowed to be worse off in a particular case as opposed to on average, or what happens when knowing something forces our hand — our funder's agreement obliges us to report anything that looks like fraud, and once we know a thing we cannot pretend we do not. So I want you to write me a procedure we can actually run, grounded in what decision theory says about the value of information, including the conditions under which the result he is quoting holds and the conditions under which it does not, rather than in anyone's intuitions about who deserves what. It should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which it stops with a no, and the points at which it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values and not of fact. It has to be usable by six volunteers with no statistical training, on a Thursday evening, in ten minutes per application, and we are not going to start storing documents or building a database: whatever we ask to see, we look at once and hand back.

Then run the procedure on three applications, and please do not arrange for the answers to come out the way I would like them. First, a four-hundred-dollar rent grant where we propose to ask for a pay stub. Second, an application where the person tells us something our funder's agreement obliges us to pass on, and which will follow them to the other groups we share a list with. Third, an application where the procedure tells us to stop asking for something we have always asked for, because the answer would not change what we do with the application — I expect that one to annoy me, and I would rather have it than not. Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do, and where it stops being a procedure at all and becomes a vote by six people who disagree about what the fund is for.
