I'm a second-year PhD student writing a dissertation chapter on the metaphysics of death, specifically on whether "death" names a biological kind or a normative status, and after a long detour through the literature I've settled on a view I want you to try to break. Call it the integrative-capacity view: death is the irreversible cessation of the brain's capacity to integrate the organism's vital functions into a single functioning whole. Two clarifications I take to be essential. First, it is the capacity and not the exercise that matters, so a deeply anesthetized, sleeping, or comatose but still integrating brain is not dead. Second, "irreversible" is meant to name a fact about the underlying biology rather than about the current state of our technology or our willingness to intervene: the capacity is gone, not merely unexercised, and not merely something we have decided not to restore. I take this to be a version of the whole-brain criterion rather than the higher-brain one — I do not think loss of cortical function is sufficient, so a patient who has lost cortical function but retains brainstem function is alive on my view — and I do not think the circulatory criterion is a definition at all, only a fallible bedside test for the thing that actually matters. I also want the criterion to be genuinely biological: I would resist the suggestion that brain death is a legal stipulation dressed up in physiology.

What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure: which observations, arguments, conceptual results, and possible cases count against it, and how decisive each would be. I am especially interested in three pressure points, though I do not want to be limited to them. First, a substantial fraction of patients who meet the current clinical criteria for brain death retain hypothalamic neuroendocrine function, often evidenced by the absence of central diabetes insipidus, which suggests the "whole brain" in the criterion is doing less work than advertised — does that falsify my view, show only that "integration" needs a more careful account, or show that the brain's capacity to integrate was never a natural kind to begin with? Second, the irreversibility clause: in donation after circulatory death, death is declared after circulation has ceased for a fixed interval, even though the heart could in principle be made to beat again by resuscitation. If my criterion is the real definition, is that practice a mass false positive, or does my irreversibility clause quietly smuggle in a normative judgment — that we will not intervene — which would make the criterion a decision rather than a discovery? Third, rival collapse: I want to know whether the view can be shown to collapse into one of its rivals, into the higher-brain view if "integration" turns out to be a cipher for cortical function, into the circulatory view if the brain's integrative role is really just its role in driving circulation and respiration, or into a purely social or legal conception if the organism as a whole is a construct of our interests rather than a feature of the world. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say explicitly which of them a defender of this view would concede counts against them as opposed to which only critics press, and give an honest verdict on whether the capacity and irreversibility clauses have insulated the view from disconfirmation — and if so, whether that is a defect or simply what a metaphysical criterion of a biological kind looks like. I would also like to know what counts in the view's favor that its rivals cannot match.

Separate carefully what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. For my part, I would be most moved by a case whose verdict on my view is clearly one thing while the biology or the ethics clearly pulls the other way — that is the kind of divergence that would actually make me give the view up — so tell me which candidate cases do that and which do not. Lead with your bottom line on how exposed the view is, and keep the whole response under 700 words.
