My brother-in-law works in compliance for an engineering firm, and over Thanksgiving we got into a long argument about the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act -- the 1977 US law that made it a crime for American companies to bribe foreign officials -- and neither of us could say what would count as settling it, so I've been chewing on it since. The outline we both had: the law came out of the mid-70s Senate hearings and SEC disclosures that hundreds of US firms had been keeping slush funds and paying 'commissions,' 'consulting fees' and 'facilitating payments' to foreign officials and their local middlemen, the Lockheed payments in Japan and the Netherlands being the ones everyone remebers; at the time, payments like that were legal and often tax-deductible in much of Europe. The US criminalized them unilaterally, mainly for its own companies, then spent two decades pushing everyone else to follow, which eventually produced the OECD anti-bribery convention in the late 90s. One detail I keep coming back to is that the statute itself carves out a narrow exception for small 'facilitating' or 'grease' payments to get routine government action done -- as if the law were quietly conceding that these transfers aren't all the same kind of thing.

Here's the lens I want to test against it, and I honestly don't know whether it survives contact with the case. I've been thinking of political exchange as running on three grammars. (1) Gift logic: the transfer is embedded in an ongoing relationship, reciprocity is diffuse and open-ended, and the point of the transfer is the relationship itself -- you give because of who you are to each other, and keeping a ledger would be a kind of insult. (2) Market logic: arm's-length, priced, one-off -- money buys a specific act, and neither side expects anything afterward. (3) Patrimonial logic: the office is treated as property, so what an outsider calls a bribe is, from the inside, just the officeholder drawing an income off an asset he takes himself to own. My hunch is that 'corruption' as a moral category only really bites in (2), that it misdescribes (1), and that in (3) it isn't a moral verdict at all but evidence that public authority is constituted differently. So run the episode through this and tell me what it can and can't carry.

I want a disciplined case analysis rather than an essay on cultural relativism, and I'd rather you say where the lens goes silent than force it to speak. Structure it like this. (a) 'The episode, in my terms' -- about 150 words, just restating the case the way I have it so I can see whether we're aligned. (b) 'The three grammars, mapped' -- 400 words or so, actually assigning the Lockheed-era payments, the middlemen, and the statute's own grease-payment carve-out to the grammars, and saying who the players were and what each side thought it was doing. (c) 'Where the lens strains' -- 300 words on the features of the episode it misdescribes or can't reach. (d) 'What the episode does to the lens' -- 300 words: does the FCPA's eventual spread vindicate the idea of a universal corruption norm, or is that just coordination and competitive pressure among exporters? (e) 'Verdict' -- 150 words where you actually take a side on whether the FCPA was a moral advance, an exercise in one country legislating its own mores onto everyone else's commerce, or a false choice between those.
