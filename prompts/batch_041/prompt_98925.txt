You are an expert in philosophy of mind and cognitive science who also designs experiential, low-tech classroom activities for seminars. I need a single-session activity designed for me, and I want you to work in two passes: first, brainstorm three or four genuinely different candidate mechanisms for the activity and briefly weigh them against each other; then commit to the best one and develop it in full. Think carefully about whether the mechanism will actually produce the experience I'm after, rather than merely illustrating it.

The setting: an upper-level undergraduate or graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science, 16 to 20 participants, one 90-minute session. The topic is whether a colony-level entity — an ant colony, say — can have a mind: whether there is a real subject of beliefs, desires, or experience at the level of the colony, or only a useful fiction, or only the ants. The participants will have read Hofstadter's ant-colony dialogue (the one in which the colony "Aunt Hillary" figures) beforehand, but the activity itself must not depend on anyone recalling it, and nothing in it should require memorized reading.

What I want the participants to feel rather than hear is the pull of the thought that the colony has a mind. In the first phase, they should each follow only simple local rules, knowing and intending nothing themselves, yet find that the group as a whole behaves in a way that looks purposeful — it appears to "know" something and to "want" something. The force of the idea should come from the inside: they should be surprised by what their collective has done, and notice the gap between what the group exhibits and what any of them individually possessed. In the second phase, the group should be addressed as a single agent — interrogated, asked what it wants and what it knows — so that they run into the question of whether that collective purposiveness is a mind, a fiction, or a description that fits at one level and not another. Ideally the participants should arrive at the disquiet themselves before the facilitator names it.

Deliver, concretely: the rules and materials (paper, sticky notes, tape, a floor or tabletop grid, tokens — no computers, no real money); the setup; a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact local rules each participant follows; how you stage the interrogation of the colony so that it can answer some questions and not others; the debrief; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure; and a frank statement of which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by talking. The local rules must be simple enough to explain in three minutes, and must genuinely prevent any participant from holding a global view of what the system is doing — if someone can see the whole board and steer it, the activity is dead, so build in a safeguard against that.

For the debrief, map specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea: the mechanism by which colony-level behavior arises without any ant's understanding it; the difference between the colony behaving as if it had a belief and its actually having one; and the point at which the question of a colony-level subject of experience comes apart from the question of colony-level information processing. Be explicit about which of these the activity stages well and which it merely gestures at.

Finally, on the ideas themselves: separate clearly what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus. In particular, where you characterize Hofstadter's own position, or the general "emergence" story, or the case for or against colony-level mentality, flag which claims are secure and which are disputed, and say plainly where you are taking a side rather than reporting. If the activity would only make the point vivid under one contested interpretation of the idea, tell me so.
