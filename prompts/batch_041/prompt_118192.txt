You are a philosopher of science whose research concerns determinism, dynamical systems, and the epistemology of prediction, and you have spent years designing inquiry-based lab sessions for philosophy students. Think carefully before you commit to anything, and keep one distinction sharp throughout your own design work: predictability is a relation between a system and an agent with finite information, while determinism is meant to be a property of a system or of a description of one. Do not write a lecture outline. The deliverable is a runnable activity with a debrief.

**Domain.** Chaos, predictability, and determinism.

**Setting.** An upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of science, 16 students, one 90-minute session, an ordinary room with a whiteboard and plenty of paper. No computers, no internet, no calculators required. Assume everyone can do decimal arithmetic and that a handful of students are comfortable with simple probability.

**What participants should come away having felt rather than heard.**

1. That determinism and predictability come apart, and more sharply than the usual slogan allows: the two cannot be placed on a single axis, and the case they will have just inhabited is the interesting one — law fully known, future still unavailable.
2. That the loss is quantitative and brutal. Arrange the mechanism so that precision converts into prediction time only logarithmically: one further digit of information about the present buys a fixed, small number of additional steps, so doubling the prediction horizon requires squaring the precision. They should encounter this as a price, not receive it as a formula.
3. That the system is not thereby lawless. Its aggregate behaviour — digit frequencies, long-run averages, the behaviour of a deliberately coarse-grained description — should be robustly and reliably predictable, so that chaotic is never heard as random.
4. That the same goings-on can be described deterministically or indeterministically depending on how finely the state space is carved, so whether the world is deterministic is not something one can read off the world; it is a claim about a description.

**Design requirements.**

- *Hand-executable engine.* The mechanism must be a deterministic rule that participants can execute by hand in a few seconds per step. I want the family of systems in which each step consumes or reveals a fixed amount of information about the state, so that the horizon is linear in digits and logarithmic in precision. Choose the specific rule yourself and justify the choice.
- *Two phases.* The rule must be hidden at the start, so that the sequence looks lawless and predictions fail, and then be made public partway through, with play continuing. Everything they feel after the reveal must be felt with the law fully in hand. If the rule is never revealed, the activity teaches the opposite lesson.
- *Purchasable precision.* Participants must be able to buy, or spend time acquiring, more information about the present state, at a cost that shows up in the scoring. No real money: points, tokens, or minutes.
- *A statistician.* Include a role, option, or strategy that bets only on aggregate rather than individual futures, and make sure it actually wins.
- *Solvability check.* Give a payoff table showing what a rational participant does at each level of information, with the numbers that make the shallow strategy optimal. Then say what happens if you hand everyone the exact initial condition: the activity should collapse, and the collapse should be the lesson.
- *Timeline* in minutes, and an explicit statement of what participants are told, and not told, at each stage.
- *Debrief* that maps particular moments in the run onto points 1 to 4 above, and replays their own recorded data — their predictions, their errors, the statistician's wins — under the rule.
- *Failure modes.* What predictably goes wrong the first time this is run, and how to handle each.

**Honest limits.** State which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and which you, as facilitator, must supply in the debrief. Address at least the following, if you agree they apply, and add your own. The activity stages only one of the several ways real chaotic prediction fails — measurement precision — and has no model error, no unresolved scales, and no computational cost. The mechanism's future is in a sense already written into information that exists but is unavailable, which makes the hidden-variable picture of the world far too easy; real systems do not come with a pre-written string waiting to be read, and the debrief should say how it handles that. Whether the world is deterministic, and whether that matters for agency or responsibility, is untouched and must be supplied separately. If you raise the question of whether numerical chaos is mere round-off error, you may mention that for strongly chaotic systems there is a technical result, the shadowing lemma, according to which computed trajectories whose one-step errors are small stay uniformly close to genuine trajectories of the system — but flag that this does not restore predictability, and tell the facilitator to check the details before pressing the point in class.

**Constraints.** No reading list, no citations, no references to particular papers or authors: the design must stand on its own for a facilitator who has read nothing on the subject. Do not use dice, shuffled cards, or any genuine randomizer as the source of the participants' uncertainty. Do not put the words chaos, determinism, or butterfly on the board before the debrief.

Begin by brainstorming three candidate mechanisms and rejecting at least one with a stated reason. Then commit to one and give the full design.
