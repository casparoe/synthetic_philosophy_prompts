Over the next three years I have a postdoc's worth of time and almost no teaching, and I want to spend it on the unity of the proposition — the cluster of problems that runs from Bradley's regress about relations through the various attempts to say what makes a proposition one thing rather than a heap of its constituents. My department is half metaphysicians and half formal semanticists, so work that speaks to only one of those audiences will be hard to place, and I would rather aim at questions both of them have to care about. I am not asking for a survey or a reading list; I have read a fair amount of the literature and what I want is an agenda.

What I keep finding is that the discussion returns to the same handful of exits: accept the regress and argue it is benign; posit a special non-relational tie that does the binding; deny that predication is a relation at all and treat unity as primitive; explain unity by the act of judging or asserting, so that a proposition is the type of a certain cognitive or speech act; or explain it by the structure of a representation rather than by anything in the world. My own suspicion, which I would like you to treat as a hypothesis to be assessed rather than as a premise, is that most of these exits answer a question that should not be asked in that form — what is the extra thing that holds the constituents together? — and that the productive work lies in working out what the right question is. Tell me whether that suspicion is worth three years or whether it is an elaborate way of changing the subject.

To make the ranking concrete I want to impose a test of my own, which I will call the single-primitive constraint: a satisfactory account of propositional unity should explain the unity of propositions, of facts, and of the contents of judgment without helping itself to the same unexplained primitive in all three cases, and without presupposing the very unity it is meant to explain in the notion of a "constituent." I suspect that no extant account passes it, but I want to know which open questions are the ones whose answers determine whether anything could pass it, and which are merely downstream of that. In the semanticists' idiom the same worry shows up as the gap between a derivation of a meaning and the meaning itself — whether the composition rule belongs to the content or is only a fact about how we compute it — and that seam is where I would most like to be able to say something.

So: which open questions in this area most deserve the time, in what order, and why? For each one I want to know whether it is upstream of the others or a local skirmish, whether it is tractable with the tools philosophers already have or is waiting on something else, and what would count as real progress on it within three years — a distinction, an impossibility proof, a counterexample to a whole family of views, a translation of one vocabulary into another. I also want an honest account of what I should not work on, including anything that looks central but whose answer would not change much elsewhere, and of the strongest reason to think the area is a dead end and my suspicion is simply a way of avoiding the hard problem.

Please write this as a letter to me in continuous prose. No headings, no bulleted or numbered lists, no bold text, no tables — just paragraphs, the way you would write to a colleague whose time you were actually allocating.
