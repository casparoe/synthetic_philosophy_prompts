Essay: "Superrationality Is Not a Decision Theory."

Setup. A lottery's total prize is a fixed sum divided by the total number of entries received, each entry receiving that quotient; you may submit as many entries as you like. If all N players submit n entries, each player personally receives the same amount whatever n is — the total prize divided by N. If you alone submit far more than everyone else, you gain a great deal. The superrational argument says: submit exactly one entry, because any rational agent in my situation reasons exactly as I do, and so will also submit one.

1. That argument presupposes determinism. The step from "I will do X" to "the others will do X" holds only if my action is a deterministic function of a situation we share. If my choice is genuinely undetermined, my action carries no information about theirs, and the argument collapses into ordinary expected-value reasoning.

2. But under determinism my action was fixed by the laws and the past before I ever deliberated, so there is nothing left to decide and the deliberation is epiphenomenal. The argument is therefore either unavailable (indeterminism) or idle (determinism).

3. The escape is to stop reading superrationality as a theory of what to do and read it as a theory of which procedure to run: I should want to be an agent whose procedure maps this situation to "submit one," because a population of such agents outperforms a population of inflators. Superrationality then constrains the rationality of procedures, not of acts.

4. On the procedure reading, the lottery has no unique solution, and its appearance of one comes from an arbitrary normalization. Every symmetric profile gives each player the same payoff, so "submit one" is singled out only by being the smallest positive integer — a fact about notation, not about rationality.

5. The only principle doing real work here is a symmetry constraint: agents in identical situations act identically. This is not a principle of rationality but a constraint on how agents may be described. It earns its keep in the Prisoner's Dilemma, where it selects mutual cooperation over the Nash equilibrium, and falls silent in the lottery, where all symmetric profiles are equally good.
