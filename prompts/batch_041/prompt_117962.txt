I help run a public deliberation series, and at the most recent session two invited speakers argued over a policy question that is part empirical — whether the measure reduced the harm it was meant to reduce — and part evaluative — whether the reduction was worth what it cost. One speaker repeatedly attributed to the other a position the other had explicitly disavowed, and slid between the empirical and the evaluative half of the question whenever it suited him. When I asked the moderator afterwards whether the exchange had met the series' stated standard of good-faith argument, he declined to make any finding at all: the maxim that there are two sides to every question, he said, means that a moderator's office is not to adjudicate between them. Pressed, he glossed his own stance as equal standing rather than equal merit. A colleague defended him on different grounds, saying that nothing counts as settled until each side has been heard at its strongest.

I am not asking you to judge the exchange. I want the maxim's possible contents separated and graded. Distinguish at least: the near-logical reading, on which a question admitting two answers has two sides and nothing follows about their merits; the descriptive reading, on which live disputes have two parties; the symmetry reading, on which the two sides are roughly as likely to be right, or the truth lies between them; any reading on which the maxim is a thesis about justification — about what it takes to dismiss a claim; any reading on which it concerns the standing of the parties rather than the merits of their cases; and any relativist reading, on which each side is correct relative to its own framework. For each, say whether it is empty, contestable but defensible, or false, and flag those that undermine themselves when asserted. Then say which readings would actually license (a) the moderator's refusal to find bad faith, (b) his standing/merit distinction, and (c) my colleague's claim about when a question is settled, noting in each case the further premise the reading needs, and what becomes of that support when one party's argument is demonstrably unresponsive to evidence. Close with your best guess at which reading the maxim's habitual users intend, and whether the maxim is better understood as a procedural rule for forums or as a claim about the shape of truth.
