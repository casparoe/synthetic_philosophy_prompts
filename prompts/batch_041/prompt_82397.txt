I am a novelist, and for three years now I have been writing a book set in the 1620s, whose central figure is a man obliged to hold two books at once: one that tells him what the heavens do, and one that tells him what he is to believe. The research has led me back repeatedly to Galileo's long argument that scripture and nature cannot contradict one another, since both proceed from a single author; that where a demonstrated conclusion about the natural world appears to conflict with a passage of scripture, the fault must lie with the interpreters rather than with the text; that scripture was written for ordinary people and speaks in accommodation to their habits of speech and thought, its purpose being to teach what is needed for salvation rather than to instruct anyone in astronomy; and that the sacred writers, when they touch on the natural world, follow the common understanding of their age rather than asserting anything about it. I find the argument at once compelling and slippery, and I suspect the difficulty is that I am reading it with the wrong toolkit. Mine is the novelist's: voice and register, the implied reader, what a narrator presupposes as against what he asserts, the difference between writing something false that an audience can bear and writing something true that they will misread, scene against summary, and the peculiar unreliability of a narrator who is honest but limited. That vocabulary I know cold; theology and the history of biblical interpretation I know not at all.

I would therefore like you to do two things. First, restate Galileo's position as faithfully as you can in that vocabulary — not as a summary of what he said, but as a genuine translation, conducted in the concepts a working novelist uses when thinking about how a text can be true without being literal and what it means for a narrator to write down to his audience. Second, give me the residue report, and be scrupulous about it: which parts of his position survive the translation intact, which survive only in a distorted form, and which cannot be said in my vocabulary at all. My working suspicion, which I would rather you test than flatter, is that accommodation is where the translation does its quiet damage. My accommodating narrator is permitted to say what is false because it suits his story, whereas Galileo's accommodating author appears to be constrained to say what is true but not literal, and I cannot tell whether my vocabulary has any means of marking that difference or whether it simply collapses the two. If the collapse is real, tell me whether it is a defect of my framework — a novelist's vocabulary being too permissive, since a novelist's reader is never bound by anything — or a sign that the load-bearing element of Galileo's argument is not an interpretive claim at all, but a premise about what God intended, which no account of texts, however sophisticated, could supply. I should add that I have no use for citations, titles or reading lists; I want the argument clean.
