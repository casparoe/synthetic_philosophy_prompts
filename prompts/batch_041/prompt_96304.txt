I am a graduate student in a decision theory reading group, and this term we have been joined by a visiting fellow, Dr. S., whose research is on the foundations of rational choice. She has a habit of issuing crisp verdicts on whatever puzzle the group is discussing, and I have been keeping a careful record, because I want to understand the rule she is actually running rather than the theory she professes. Next week it is my turn to present a case, and I would like your help predicting what she will say and pinning down what her record does and does not settle.

Every game below uses the same chance device: a fair coin is flipped until it first lands heads, and n is the toss on which that happens, so the probability of any particular n is 2^{-n}. Her recorded verdicts are as follows. On the "Doubling" game, which pays $2^n, she says the expected value is infinite but that the infinity is an artifact of the formalism rather than a reason to expect a fortune; offered a straight choice between the game and a sure $50, she takes the $50, and she says she would not pay more than about $20 for it. On the "Harmonic" game, which pays $2^n/n, she gives the same verdict: the expected value is infinite, but she takes a sure $50 over it and would not pay more than about $20. On a plain lottery that pays $10 with probability one half and $0 otherwise, she says the value is exactly $5 and would pay $5 for it. On the "Alternating" game, which pays (-1)^{n+1} 2^n/n, she observes that the expected value is undefined: the positive and negative parts of the expectation series each diverge, so the sum over outcomes is not absolutely convergent and depends on the order of summation; she does not, however, say whether this means the game has no value or merely that the standard theory cannot assign one. On the "Alternating-plus" game, which pays (-1)^{n+1} 2^n/n + $1, she says it is straightforwardly better than the Alternating game, since it pays $1 more in every outcome.

Next week's case is a straight choice between the Alternating game and a sure $0.10. I would like a rigorous, professional treatment. Think carefully and brainstorm the candidate rules that fit her record before you commit to anything; then give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the candidate rules her record is consistent with, stated precisely enough to generate predictions; (2) what each of those rules predicts about the new case; (3) your best guess at her verdict, with the reason you prefer that rule over its rivals; (4) a further case, different from the new one, that would best discriminate between the surviving rules; (5) the strongest objection to your best guess, and what would have to be true of her for it to be wrong.
