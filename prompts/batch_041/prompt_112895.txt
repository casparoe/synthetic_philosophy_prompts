I'm a novelist, and I'm deep into a book set in the back rooms of a mid-sized city's mayoral race — school closures, a transit bond, a recall petition, the whole municipal weather. One of my characters, a former campaign manager named Delia who has drifted into adjunct teaching, is supposed to deliver a monologue explaining why she has stopped believing in the phrase "the will of the people." I want her to be clever rather than merely cynical, and I'd rather her skepticism rest on a real argument than on a slogan, so I've been reading around in popular political science. A friend of mine, who finished a doctorate in the field before decamping to data journalism, gave me the following gloss, which I have been treating as my crib sheet: "The paradox of voting is one of the oldest results in social choice; it goes back to Condorcet. Since the chance that any single ballot decides an election is vanishingly small, a rational citizen has no instrumental reason to vote at all — the expected benefit is a fraction of the cost of getting to the polls. That's the paradox: millions of people do the one thing rationality forbids. Political scientists resolved it by showing that voters are expressive rather than instrumental, voting the way fans sing an anthem, and, more radically, that the paradox isn't really about individual voters at all. Because majority rule can cycle, there is in general no such thing as the majority's preference, and therefore no such thing as the will of the people that populists keep invoking. Arrow proved that no voting system can satisfy a short list of obviously desirable conditions at once, which is why the more sophisticated people in the field treat majority rule as mathematically impossible and democracy as valuable for reasons other than its ability to register a popular will." I suspect this is a mixture of things I should and shouldn't put in Delia's mouth, but I can't locate the seams, and I would rather not have a reviewer who knows the literature wince at every other line.

Would you audit the gloss for me, sentence by sentence? For each claim, tell me what a working political scientist would accept without qualification, what they would wince at as a distortion or an overstatement, and what is simply wrong — and, in particular, flag any place where the gloss has quietly swapped one idea for another that happens to travel under the same name. I'm interested in which of the claims are load-bearing for the punchline, and whether the punchline survives their correction: if the argument about the incoherence of a popular will turns out to be independent of the argument about turnout, does that leave Delia's position stronger or weaker than the gloss makes it sound?

Finally, since I need her to actually say something on the page, propose two or three sentences she could deliver that make a defensible version of the point with comparable bite — punchy enough for dialogue, but not something an expert would have to disown. Please write your answer in American English, and don't bother with citations or a reading list; what I need from you is the reasoning, not the bibliography.
