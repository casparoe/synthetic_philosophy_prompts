ok so I'm a science-and-ideas journo, and next Thursday I'm on a panel at a small workshop on tech and society. The room is mostly STS types — history and sociology of technology people — plus two engineers who wandered in and one person from a policy shop. I've been asked to stake out a position, and I picked the fight I actually believe: that the whole guns-dont-kill-people-people-do line is not just lazy but backwards, and that some technologies are political in their bones — not because of who ends up using them but because of what they demand in order to work at all. Think of a system that only runs smoothly if you centralize control somewhere, or one whose default settings quietly make certain ways of living easier and others a constant uphill slog. I'm the one arguing that artifacts have politics, and the strong version of it, not the watered-down "well, design has consequences" version.

What I need is a Q&A prep sheet — not a defense of my view, the ammunition coming the other way. Give me the objections I'll actually get from THIS room, phrased the way they'd phrase them, not the strawmen. Order them by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do. For each one: what I should concede without a fight, what I should hold onto, a roughly two-minute reply, and a one-line flag on where that reply is weakest. I especially want the objection from the constructivist corner — they'll say I'm smuggling technological determinism back in, or treating the artifact as a little agent with intentions — and the one from the engineers, who'll say I'm moralizing choices that were just boring engineering constraints or cost tradeoffs at the time. And finish with the question I'm praying nobody in the room thinks to ask.

Dont worry about being fair to me. If my framing has a hole in it that this crowd will drive a truck through, I'd rather see it in the prep sheet than from the front row.
