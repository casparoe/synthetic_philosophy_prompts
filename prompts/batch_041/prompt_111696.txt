I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend who works on counterfactual theories of causation, and I came away with an argument I cannot shake. I am sympathetic to the interventionist account, and she offered this as a reductio of it. The argument runs as follows. (1) On the interventionist account, X causes Y just in case there is a possible intervention on X that changes Y, where an intervention is a process that changes X, is not caused by X or by Y's other causes, and changes Y, if at all, only through X. (2) In a deterministic world, take any two variables X and Y related by a bijective law, so that Y = f(X) and X = f^{-1}(Y). (3) An intervention on Y that changes Y will, by the inverse law, change X. (4) That intervention satisfies the interventionist's conditions with respect to X: it changes Y, it is not caused by Y's other causes, and it changes X only through Y, because the law is deterministic. (5) So by (1), Y causes X. (6) By the same reasoning, X causes Y. (7) So in any deterministic bijective system, interventionism makes causation symmetric. (8) But causation is asymmetric. (9) Therefore interventionism is false.

She used a thermometer as an illustration: the temperature H and the reading T are related by T = h(H), and she said that if we intervene on T by heating the thermometer, the inverse law tells us H changes, so the reading causes the temperature. I said that was absurd, but she said the absurdity is the point. I would rather keep (1) and (8) than (3), but I want to know what I am paying. Which step should I reject, why did it look so plausible, and what does rejecting it commit me to about the relation between deterministic laws, structural equations, and the asymmetry of causation? If the argument is valid, which premise is least costly to give up, and what would the interventionist have to say about the thermometer case to make that cost acceptable?
