I am completing a novel whose central character, Dr. Vera Lindqvist, sits on a fictional advisory body called the Commission on Custodial Innovation. The commission reviews proposed changes to prisons, schools, hospitals, and workplaces in a midsized European city. I have given it a track record, and I need its next ruling to follow from that record rather than from my convenience. Its decisions so far are these.

It approved replacing public floggings and execution days with private, scheduled imprisonment, a written case file for each inmate, and periodic examinations by experts. It reasoned that punishment should cease to be a spectacle addressed to the crowd and become a technique addressed to the offender.

It rejected a proposal to install continuous two-way mirrors in school corridors and locker rooms to deter bullying and theft, even though the pilot reduced both. It held that being seen without any relationship of guidance or care does not reform anyone; it only teaches people that they are always suspect.

It approved a factory’s replacement of arbitrary fines and public reprimands with continuous biometric monitoring, shift-ranking, and automatic adjustment of privileges, but only after the factory added mandatory weekly one-on-one coaching sessions in which the data were discussed as a basis for self-improvement.

It rejected a public-health proposal to brand or tattoo persistently non-compliant patients, despite strong evidence that it improved treatment adherence, because it restored a spectacular, bodily, sovereign mark.

It approved an indeterminate sentencing scheme in which prisoners receive individualized treatment plans and release depends on expert assessment of their normalized conduct, even though some prisoners will serve longer than under the old fixed-term regime.

It rejected a proposal to give prisoners access to their own files and a formal hearing before any transfer, reasoning that such juridical procedures would reintroduce a rights-based adversarial logic into what should be a therapeutic and disciplinary process.

Now the commission must decide on a new proposal: replace the parole board with a predictive system that continuously scores each prisoner on two hundred behavioral, physiological, and social signals; adjusts privileges in real time; releases prisoners whose scores fall below a threshold; and offers no human hearing, though prisoners can contest their scores through an automated chatbot that explains which signals pushed them up or down. The system has reduced recidivism in a pilot, but prisoners report that they feel perpetually observed and cannot tell what they are being judged for.

I need you to predict the commission’s verdict. More important, identify the competing rules that fit its earlier decisions equally well, say what each rule predicts about this case, give your best guess with reasons, and name the further case that would best distinguish the rules. I am less interested in a summary of Foucault than in the commission’s internal logic.
