Take the CIA's enhanced interrogation programme after 9/11 and use it to stress-test the ticking time bomb argument — but not the usual does-torture-work debate. I want the question of whether the hypothetical even describes the situation those interrogators were in. The standard hypothetical hands you three things: you know there's a bomb, you know the prisoner knows where it is, and you know the clock is nearly out. The real thing ran for years, on people whose knowledge was itself in doubt, with no bomb anyone could point to. So do a proper mapping — who plays the interrogator, what each of the three conditions becomes once you strip the certainty out, and whether the programme is a case the hypothetical predicts, a case it never anticipated, or a case its defenders should just say doesn't count. Call the stripped-down version the fog case if it helps: no known bomb, only a suspected one. Then the bit I actually care about. Does the historical record confirm the ticking-bomb defence, refute it, or show that it was never really an argument about cases like this at all — that the hypothetical was doing rhetorical work no real episode can do? And the deeper one: is the defender and the critic even answering the same question, or is one talking about the individual act and the other about the institution that authorises it? Take a side, dont just lay out both. Finish with whether applied ethics should keep a hypothetical that can't be institutionalised without collapsing into the fog case, or bin it. British English, please.
