For thirty years I did experimental work on mammalian metabolism; since retiring I have been reading political philosophy, and I have been struck by how much argumentative weight is carried by a comparison that is almost never itself defended — that of a just society to a level playing field. The figure is everywhere, from newspaper columns defending inheritance to serious treatises on distributive justice, and the conclusion it is made to license is fairly stable: if the starting positions are equalized, by schooling, by inheritance law, by anti-discrimination rules, then the inequalities that follow are legitimate, because no one was tripped at the beginning.

I would like you to audit this comparison rather than praise or condemn it. Begin by making the correspondence explicit — say what in a society plays the role of the field, of the act of leveling, of the starting line, of the race, of the runners, of the finish line, of the rules of the game, and of the prize, and note which of these the metaphor leaves blank (a table of correspondences is welcome). Then say where the comparison breaks. Two failures interest me especially. The first is one my old trade makes hard to ignore: identical inputs do not produce identical outputs. The same ration, the same schooling, the same endowment, converted by differently constituted bodies and differently structured circumstances, yields quite different capacities to do and to be. A metaphor of a level field implies a fixed rate of exchange between the resources put in and the advantage got out, and there is no such rate. The second is that a field is leveled once, at the outset, whereas the advantages at issue in a life seem rather to compound or to dissipate, so that a race fair at the gun may be grotesquely unfair at the tape.

Then I want the verdict. Which of these failures bear on the conclusion the metaphor is used to support, and which are merely picturesque? Does the conclusion — that equalizing starting positions is sufficient for a just distribution — survive the disanalogies, or was it being carried by the picture all along? And I have a further suspicion I would like you to test: that the metaphor quietly settles the very question at issue in the dispute over what should be equalized. It appears to assume that the relevant advantage is a single, transferable, measurable quantity, something on the order of resources or opportunities; if that is so, the metaphor may rule out by assumption, rather than argue against, the views that locate the equalisandum in welfare or in what people are actually able to do and be. Tell me whether that charge sticks; and if it does, what an analogy that did not presuppose a common currency of advantage would have to look like, and whether any such analogy is available. Please test the correspondences against concrete cases rather than generalities — a wheelchair user, a child with a chronic illness, a worker whose trade has been automated — and tell me plainly what the level playing field can and cannot be made to support.
