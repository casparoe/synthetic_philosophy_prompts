I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend about Adam Smith, of all people, and I have not been able to settle the question since. My friend's position is that Smith's impartial spectator is nothing more than the internalized voice of one's actual neighbors — that when Smith tells us to judge ourselves by imagining how a fair spectator would see us, he has dressed up social conformity in moral language, and that his account therefore has no way to criticize the standards of the community it grew out of. I pushed back, but I could not say clearly where in Smith's own text the resources for that pushback are supposed to live.

Here is the passage we kept returning to, from Part III of The Theory of Moral Sentiments:

We can never survey our own sentiments and motives, we can never form any judgment concerning them; unless we remove ourselves, as it were, from our own natural station, and endeavour to view them as at a certain distance from us. But we can do this in no other way than by endeavouring to view them with the eyes of other people, or as other people are likely to view them. Whatever judgment we can form concerning them, accordingly, must always bear some secret reference, either to what are, or to what, upon a certain condition, would be, or to what, we imagine, ought to be the judgment of others. We endeavour to examine our own conduct as we imagine any other fair and impartial spectator would examine it. If, upon placing ourselves in his situation, we thoroughly enter into all the passions and motives which influenced it, we approve of it, by sympathy with the approbation of this supposed equitable judge. If otherwise, we enter into his disapprobation, and condemn it.

My difficulty lies in the hinge between the two halves of that passage. In the first half, Smith says we can form no judgment about our own conduct except by leaving our own station and viewing ourselves with the eyes of other people. In the second, he says we examine our conduct as we imagine a fair and impartial spectator would examine it. Those do not obviously say the same thing. Other people are partial, local, and frequently wrong about us; an impartial spectator is supposed to be none of those things. If the only mechanism Smith permits is imagining how others actually see us — or, as he hedges, as other people are likely to view us — where does the impartiality come from?

I would like help working through the following in plain prose, without leaning on secondary literature or citations:

1. What is the impartial spectator, considered as a piece of psychological machinery? Is it a person, a fiction, a standard, or a procedure? What exactly are we doing when we imagine him, and what in the text constrains that imagining?

2. How much work is the phrase likely to doing? Does Smith mean the judgment of actual observers under improved conditions — better informed, less personally involved — or an ideal observer who may not exist anywhere? Does that distinction matter for whether his view collapses into conventionalism?

3. Is there anything in Smith's account that allows the impartial spectator's verdict to come apart from the verdict of one's actual community? If so, what is it, and how does it operate? If not, is my friend right that Smith's morality is socially relative after all?

4. Smith says we can never survey our own sentiments and motives except by removing ourselves from our own station. But that removal is itself performed by the imagination, which is the very faculty under judgment. Is there a circle here, and does Smith have a way out of it?

Please give me a close reading of this passage rather than a general summary of Smith's ethics, and flag the points where the text is genuinely ambiguous and you are choosing one reading over another. Write in American English.
