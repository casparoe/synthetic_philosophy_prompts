I am a professor of philosophy, and I am assembling the final examination for an upper-level seminar in epistemology. The latter half of the course is devoted to higher-order evidence, the rationality of epistemic akrasia, and the relation between first-order and higher-order justification. I would like you to act as an expert epistemologist and a highly experienced examiner, and to produce a single, self-contained exam item that I could hand to a grader: one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

Please proceed methodically. Before drafting, brainstorm briefly — in a short planning section you may then set aside — the distinct positions the item is meant to probe, the crux that separates a student who genuinely understands the problem from one who has merely memorized the labels, and the specific, identifiable mistakes that ought to cost marks. Then write the item itself.

Design constraints. The question must not be answerable by reciting the standard material on conciliationism, steadfastness, or level-splitting; a student who reproduces the usual summaries should not score well. At the same time, it must be answerable by a student who has understood the course and is reasoning on the spot, with no internet access and no library. It should therefore lean on no citations, no reading list, and no unstated empirical findings, and it should not depend on recall of any particular author's example or terminology. The difficulty should come entirely from the structure of the case and the argumentative work it demands. To that end I have constructed the following case; treat it as the spine of the item and do not replace it with a textbook example.

The case. Amara has spent a year developing an argument, using her field's standard method M, and it has led her to accept hypothesis H with credence 0.9. She is competent with M and has used it for years. She then encounters a large methodological study — call its conclusion the Calibration Finding — which reports that claims of exactly this type, advanced by researchers using M, have turned out true only about 60% of the time when their authors reported credence 0.9. Amara has no reason to think her own case is atypical, and she accepts the Calibration Finding. But she notices two further things. First, the Calibration Finding was itself produced by researchers using M, and the study reports its own conclusion with credence 0.9. Second, its content is not a first-order claim about her subject matter but a statistical claim about the track record of M-users. Let us say that higher-order evidence is endogenous when it is produced by the very process whose reliability it concerns; the Calibration Finding is, in this sense, endogenous.

The question you draft must force students to address, in a connected argument, three things: first, whether and how the Calibration Finding defeats Amara's confidence in H — in particular, whether it functions as an undercutting defeater, and whether its endogeneity alters its defeating force; second, whether it would be akratic for her to retain credence 0.9 in H while accepting the Calibration Finding, and whether such akrasia is rationally permissible; and third, whether endogeneity supplies a defeater-defeater that restores her original confidence, or whether it instead leaves her in a genuine rational tension for which there is no clean resolution. The question should require a definite verdict, defended against the strongest objection the student can construct, and it should be pitched so that a merely competent summary of the surrounding debate fails to answer it.

Deliverables. (1) The essay question, suitable for a seventy-five-minute in-class final, with an expected length of roughly 900 to 1000 words, worth 25 marks. (2) A model answer of about 750 words, written as a strong student would write it: it should take a definite line, and it should be candid about where the argument is under pressure. (3) A marking scheme totaling 25 marks, decomposed into named must-hit moves with a point value for each — for example, correctly classifying the evidence and the kind of defeat at issue; handling the self-undermining structure of an endogenous defeater, including the difference between an argument that is self-undermining and one that is self-defeating; framing the akrasia question rather than merely asserting a verdict about it; giving a definite, defended answer to the defeater-defeater question; and engaging the strongest objection to the student's own view. The scheme should also list the specific errors that should cost marks — for instance, treating the case as ordinary peer disagreement and applying an averaging formula mechanically, concluding that endogeneity simply voids the Calibration Finding, declaring akrasia rational or irrational by fiat, confusing undercutting with rebutting defeat, or overlooking that a statistical claim about M's error rates may not inherit the unreliability it attributes to first-order M-outputs — and should end with a short paragraph distinguishing an A answer from a B answer by naming the moves the former contains and the latter lacks. Reward the quality of the argument, not the particular verdict reached.
