I am a novelist, and I have spent much of the past year reading about the ethics of infinite populations for a book in which one character — a demographer who has come to believe the long-run future dwarfs everything — argues with another about whether the far future can be weighed at all. The picture that keeps surfacing in that literature, and that a philosopher friend of mine leans on whenever I press her, is this: to compare two worlds containing infinitely many people is like comparing two infinite series of numbers. You can pair the elements of the two off against each other one for one, exactly as you can pair the even numbers with all the counting numbers; and so, she says, a world of infinitely many happy lives is no more valuable than one with only 'half' as many, and neither is better than a world of infinitely many miserable ones. Her conclusion is not that the question of which infinite world is better is merely difficult but that it is empty — there is no fact of the matter — and that we may therefore set the whole matter aside and attend to finite goods.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on her politics. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the series, the terms, the pairing, the sum, and the 'size' of the set. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. I suspect several joints are loose — the terms of a series are interchangeable placeholders while persons are not; the picture seems to have no room for the difference between adding lives and improving the lives already there, or for any question of distribution; and rearranging a series is costless in a way that redistributing welfare is not — but I would rather you find the breaks than confirm mine. Say which of them matter for the use she is putting the analogy to and which are idle, and then tell me whether her conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table.

One thing I would especially like you to consider, because it seems to cut against her rather than for her: some infinite series can be rearranged to converge to any sum you like, while others cannot, and the difference turns on whether the series is absolutely or merely conditionally convergent. If welfare is at all like the first kind, then the one-to-one pairing she treats as decisive is precisely a rearrangement, and rearrangements can change the total — so the analogy, far from showing the question is empty, would show that the order of lives matters. Is that a fair use of the analogy, or does it break the mapping somewhere else? And if the series picture is beyond repair, what would be a better one for the point she is actually trying to make?
