I spent my working life in the natural sciences—experimental physics, if it matters—and have only recently begun to read philosophy and historiography. I have been reading about the Fischer controversy, the debate over German responsibility for the First World War, and I find the conceptual question more interesting than the archival one: what does it mean, exactly, to hold a nation responsible for a war? I have been trying to formulate a criterion, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it.

The criterion, roughly, is this. A nation N bears principal responsibility for a war W when: (1) some decision-making body within N occupied a pivotal node in the causal network leading to W—there was a feasible alternative decision that would have made W substantially less likely; (2) the decision was taken with foreseeable awareness of the risk of general war, or with culpable indifference to that risk; (3) the decision was not itself the product of coercion or of a threat that left N no reasonable alternative; and (4) N's internal political order gave the relevant decision-makers the capacity to choose otherwise. I do not mean sole responsibility. I mean something like primary or principal responsibility, compatible with other powers having contributed and compatible with the war being overdetermined in various respects. I have also been trying to carry over two ideas from outside history: the NESS test from tort law, on which a condition is a cause if it is a necessary element of a sufficient set, and the sufficient-component cause model from epidemiology, which treats causes as components of sufficient sets rather than as necessary conditions.

On this criterion, I am inclined to think that the German leadership bears principal responsibility for the First World War. But I am not confident that the criterion is doing the work I want it to do. What I want from you is a map of its exposure. What findings, arguments, intuitions, or formal results would count against the claim that the German leadership was principally responsible on this criterion? Which of these would be fatal, and which merely awkward? Which would a defender of the criterion concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss? Does the criterion collapse into a tautology, or into a restatement of the fact that the losers were blamed? Does it blame too many parties—Austria-Hungary, Russia, France, Britain—and if so, does that show that principal responsibility is the wrong concept, or only that it is distributed? What does the criterion predict that rival accounts—a balance-of-power account, a structural account, an account centered on the alliance system—do not? If it predicts nothing distinctive, is it a substantive position or a redescription? What would the world have to look like for the German case to fail the criterion: for the pivotal decision to be elsewhere, or for the German decision to be coerced, or for the relevant decision-makers to lack the capacity to choose otherwise? And how much of my position is empirical, how much conceptual, and how much moral?

I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or citations. I am asking for the conceptual shape of the defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything at all or has been insulated by its qualifications.
