I'm prepping an episode for a small epistomology reading group's podcast, and next week's guest works on the dogmatism paradox -- roughly, the argument that if you know that P, then you know that anything that turns up against P would have to be misleading, so it looks like you're entitled to just ignore it, which makes knowledge dogmatic on its own terms. His spin is that this is a decision-theoretic problem wearing an epistemology costume: the real clash is between what to believe and which inquiries are even worth running, and once you split those two apart, the only residue left is a question about how far you should trust your own future self to handle evidence well. He calls that diachronic self-trust and insists it's the crux.

Draft me 8-10 questions that don't let him just restate that. I want at least one pressing on whether his move is just the old reply that misleading evidence is still evidence and that you can't know in advance that it'll be misleading; one on what happens when the counter-evidence has already arrived rather than sitting off in the hypothetical future; one on the case where the counter-evidence comes from someone as reliable as he is; and one that pushes on whether his self-trust story ends up licensing exactly the dogmatism the paradox was supposed to expose -- say, someone who treats any doubt about their own competence as itself a sign that the doubt is unreliable. Give each question a follow-up keyed to the answer hes most likely to give, and close with one about what would actually count against the view.
