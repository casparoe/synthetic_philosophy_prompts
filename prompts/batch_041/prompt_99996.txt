I sit on my university's animal research ethics commitee — we're the ones who sign off project licences, and we can refuse them. Next Thursday we're deciding one I can't stop thinking about. A lab wants to breed a line of mice with a mutation that leaves them in a state a lot like severe nerve pain: constant, agonising, for roughly the last third of their lives. The point is to test a new analgesic. If it works it would bring real relief to a very large number of people and animals with chronic neuropathic pain. A few hundred mice a year, enormous benefit downstream. Our normal framework is a plain harm–benefit balance, and on that it goes through easily — the numbers are ridiculously lopsided.

The complication is one of our members, a philosopher, who has been pushing something she calls 'lexical threshold negative utilitarianism' and wants us to adopt it as our standing framework. As I understand her: what matters is the reduction of suffering, not the production of benefit; and suffering is lexically prior to benefit once it crosses a threshold. Below the threshold, pain and benefit trade off more or less normally; above it, no amount of benefit to anyone else, however vast, can outweigh that suffering. She says these mice are over the threshold, so we refuse, full stop — and she's honest that this means refusing a lot of things we currently wave through, while waving through things that would give the rest of us pause (she's already said she'd approve a different application that keeps thousands of animals in mild chronic discomfort for years, because none of it crosses her line).

I have to draft the committee's written opinion before Thursday. Please write it. I need a clear recommendation — approve, refuse, or approve with conditions, and if it's conditions, make them concrete — plus reasoning we can defend when the minutes are read back, and a genuinely fair statement of the strongest case against my recommendation, because the committee is split and I don't want to win on numbers alone.

Two things I'd especially like you to sort out. First, whether her view really commits her to what she says it does: I have a nagging feeling the word 'threshold' is doing more work than she lets on, and I want to know which of her conclusions actually follow from the view and which are just her gloss — and, relatedly, whether pain and benefit are even the sort of things that can be traded off against one another at all, or whether the threshold is secretly a way of admitting they can't. Second, how we give reasons that the non-philosophers on the committee can follow, without quietly turning her view into a softer one she'd disown. And if adopting her framework would commit us to something that looks mad — say, that one mouse in agony outweighs any amount of human benefit, or that we should spend the whole research budget on painkillers and none on cures — please say so plainly rather than tidying it up. I'd rather have the awkward version.

British English, please.
