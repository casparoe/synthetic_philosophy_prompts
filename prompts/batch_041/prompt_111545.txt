I've been persuaded by the lottocracy case: that legislative bodies should be filled by stratified random selection rather than election, with deliberation among those selected, and elections kept at most for executives and agenda-setters. I want to know what would count against it — not the usual grumbles about voter competence or apathy, but the things that should actually move someone who already accepts the argument. Rank them by how decisive they'd be, distinguishing defeaters that would kill the view from ones that would merely force a qualification.

In particular: what would the evidence have to look like to show that the deliberative-minipublic results don't survive scale-up, or that stratified sampling is quietly undermined by which people actually agree to serve; whether the real work is done by deliberation itself or by the small-group and facilitation features a full legislature wouldn't reproduce; what the Irish citizens' assemblies and the permanent citizens' council in the German-speaking Community of Belgium actually demonstrate versus what lottocrats read into them; whether removing re-election does more to kill pandering or to kill responsiveness; and whether the agenda-setting and lottery-administration roles simply relocate power to whoever runs them. Then tell me which of these the committed lottocrat would themselves concede as counting against them, and whether the view has been insulated by qualifications — and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. For what it's worth, the scale-up objection is the one I'd find most persuasive if it held up.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
