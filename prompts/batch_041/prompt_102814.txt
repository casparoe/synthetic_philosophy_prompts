I am in the final term of an upper-level metaphysics seminar, and I am preparing for a take-home final. The final is unusual: rather than asking us to summarise the familiar menu of answers to the special composition question, our instructor has given us an unfamiliar candidate principle and wants us to work with it. She introduced it in the last lecture as a deliberate puzzle, calling it the Persistence-Dependence Principle (PDP). Stated roughly: some things compose a further object just in case the plurality has persistence conditions of its own — conditions under which it, as a single thing, would continue to exist — and those conditions are not fixed by the persistence conditions of its members. She has warned us explicitly that she will not accept an answer that simply labels PDP a restricted view and then recites the standard positions, and that the exam will be marked down for name-dropping without engagement.

Please draft one essay question, in the register of a final-exam prompt, that would force a student to do real work with PDP rather than recall the literature. At a minimum the question should require the student to: (1) say what answer PDP gives to the special composition question and where that answer sits relative to the standard options the course has covered, including nihilism, universalism, and the van Inwagen-style view on which only living things compose; (2) test PDP against a small set of cases chosen to pull in different directions — I would suggest at least a living organism, an ordinary artifact, a heap or pile, and a gerrymandered plurality such as the moon together with one of my thumbs; (3) address whether PDP is an intrinsic or an extrinsic criterion of composition, and why that matters; (4) press the strongest structural objection to PDP, which I suspect is that it may be circular, since speaking of the persistence conditions of a plurality may already presuppose that the plurality is a single thing, i.e. that composition has occurred; and (5) say whether PDP collapses into one of the views it was meant to improve on, and if so which.

Then give a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points worth 20 marks in total, with the marks for each and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs candidates the point. Please make the marking scheme separate a genuinely good answer from a competent but superficial one, and do not let it depend on citations, dates, or reading lists, since the exam is closed-book and offline.
