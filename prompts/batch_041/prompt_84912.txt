I am a high school student writing a paper for a philosophy of science elective, and I have run into a passage that I cannot get past. It comes from an old English translation of Newton's Principia (Motte's translation), in his discussion of the rotating bucket of water:

"If a vessel, hung by a long cord, is so often turned about that the cord is strongly twisted, then filled with water, and held at rest together with the water; after, by the sudden action of another force, it is whirled about in the contrary way, and while the cord is untwisting itself, the vessel continues for some time this motion; the surface of the water will at first be plain, as before the vessel began to move; but the vessel by gradually communicating its motion to the water, will make it begin sensibly to revolve, and recede by little and little, and ascend to the sides of the vessel, forming itself into a concave figure..."

Newton then remarks that from this experiment one can find out and measure the water's true and absolute circular motion.

What I cannot follow is how the shape of the water is supposed to license that conclusion. As I read the passage, the two stages seem to be arranged as a trap. Early on, the water and the vessel are moving very differently from each other, and the surface stays flat. Later, the water has been dragged along until it is at rest relative to the vessel, and now the surface is concave. So the concavity does not track the water's motion relative to the thing immediately around it. I can see that much. What I do not see is how we get from "not motion relative to the bucket" to "motion relative to space itself." It looks to me as though Newton has subtracted the bucket from the picture and given the remainder a name, and I cannot tell whether the name is doing any work or is just standing in for a blank.

I also tried to invent a case that might break the inference, and I would like to know whether it is a real objection or just my confusion. Suppose I spin a wide vat of wax or gelatin and let it set while it is still spinning, then stop it. The set object keeps its dished, flattened shape even though it is no longer rotating at all; the deformation is stored in the material, as a kind of memory of having spun. Its shape, read the way Newton reads the water's shape, would tell me it is rotating when it is not. Does this show that shape by itself never licenses the inference Newton makes, and that he needs an extra premise — something like "the water is a fluid, with no memory of its past motion, so it must be deformed by something acting on it now"? And if he needs that premise, has the argument quietly turned into a claim about which materials make good rotation detectors, rather than a claim about space? Or would you say the set wax is still "rotating" in some extended sense, because its parts are stressed as though it were, and that this is not a counterexample at all?

Here is what I would find most helpful. First, restate the argument in ordinary language and tell me exactly which premise is carrying the weight, since I suspect it is not the one I have been staring at. Second, tell me what a relationist would say in response to this passage — someone who thinks that all motion is motion relative to other bodies, and that there is no such thing as being in motion relative to space itself. Third, assess my wax objection on its merits; if it fails, I would rather know why than have it politely set aside. Fourth, and most importantly for my paper, tell me whether the passage actually requires absolute space to be a thing that exists, or whether it only requires there to be some distinguished frame of reference against which rotation shows up — and if those two come apart, explain how, because at the moment I cannot see the difference. If you use terms like "inertial frame," "substantivalism," or "relationism," please define them as you go; I have met them only in passing. You do not need to give me reading lists or citations; I am trying to understand the reasoning, not to assemble a bibliography.
