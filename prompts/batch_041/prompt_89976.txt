I'm a TA for a grad seminar on rationality, and the prof wants each of us to bring in something for the first session of the unit on reasons for attitudes — something that isn't philosophy, that makes the students feel the problem before she hands them the taxonomy. I said I'd write a poem, so now I actually have to, and I want you to write it.

Here's what it has to be about. The thing that trips everyone up is that a consideration can be a perfectly good reason to want to have an attitude, or to do something about it — take a pill, pay a hypnotist, be pleased with yourself if you wake up with it — without being any reason at all to have it. The stock cases are a demon who says it'll destroy everything unless you admire it, or someone offering you a fortune to believe the moon is made of cheese, or a parent who says there'll be no dessert unless the kid feels properly grateful. It's not just belief, either — admiration, gratitude, resentment, all of them. The offer is obviously relevant, and that's the problem: it's relevant the way a bribe is relevant, not the way evidence is. If you did come to believe it because of the money, something's gone wrong that "but the money was real" doesn't fix. And I don't want the poem settling it by saying the offer is just irrelevant, because it isn't — that's the bit everyone has to get past.

Here's the twist I want in there, since its the part my students never see coming: sometimes the wrong-kind reason is also a right-kind one, because the offer itself carries information. If someone's willing to pay me a fortune to believe the moon is cheese, that tells me something — about him, about why he cares, maybe about the moon. So a single fact is doing two jobs at once, and I want the poem to make you feel the two jobs coming apart even when one fact is doing both.

Form: a pantoum. If you know it, great; if not: quatrains, where lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last stanza loops the first stanza's opening lines back in so the whole thing closes on itself. Dont be precious about the rhyme if it's fighting the sense — the looping matters more than a clean abab. The repetition is the point: I want the same line doing right-kind work the first time it shows up and wrong-kind work when it comes back, or the other way round, so that by the end the reader genuinely can't say which job it's doing — not because the poem's being coy, but because that's the actual state of the debate. And the loop should feel like the reason you can't get out: any attempt to say okay, forget the reward, just ask what's true is itself the sort of thing you can be rewarded for doing.

Four to six stanzas, first person — the speaker is the one the offer or the threat is aimed at. Go easy on the word reason, and don't use the phrases right kind of reason, wrong kind of reason, or fitting anywhere in the poem. Don't resolve it, and don't end on a tidy line saying the distinction is really about X. Someone in the room who knows this literature should recognize exactly which puzzle it is, and someone who doesn't should leave with the itch. It's going on a handout, so keep it under a page.
