I sit on the ethics/transplant panel at a big hospital — we're the ones who get paged when the ICU and a family can't agree on whether someone has actually died, so over the years we've built up this weird little body of case law. Three rulings I'd want you to treat as the record:

- A motorcyclist after a catastrophic crash: no brainstem reflexes, no respiratory drive whatsoever, flat EEG, but still on a ventilator with his heart beating. The family wanted everything continued. We said he could be declared dead and the ventilator stopped.
- A woman in an irreversible vegetative state after a near-drowning: cortex essentially gone, brainstem completely intact, breathing on her own. Her family asked us to declare her dead so they could stop tube-feeding. We refused — said she's alive.
- An elderly man in asystole with a DNR order; nobody attempted CPR. Five minutes later, no circulation, no spontaneous return, and no one was going to try. We said he could be declared dead right then, even though the heart might in principle have been restartable if someone had gone for it.

Now the new one, and the room is definately split. 40-year-old, massive brainstem hemorrhage. He meets every standard brainstem-death criterion you could name — no reflexes, no drive, positive apnea test. But our newer neuromonitor is picking up intermittent low-voltage activity over the cortex, and he's also still regulating his temperature and concentrating his urine. The family wants him declared dead so they can go ahead with organ donation. Half the ICU team says "he's brain dead, call it"; the other half says "there's stuff lighting up up there, we can't."

I want to know what our own record actually commits us to. Don't bother with citations or what other hospitals do — I want the reasoning. Give it to me in these sections, in this order:

1. "What the record commits us to" — the two or three rules our three rulings are all jointly consistent with, and why more than one fits rather than just one. (~150 words)
2. "What each rule says about the new patient" — go rule by rule, including any rule our own precedents seem to rule out, and say why. (~200 words)
3. "My call" — your best guess at what we should decide and why, which earlier ruling is the closest analog, and honestly what principle we'd be adopting if we went the other way instead. (~150 words)
4. "What would settle it" — the fact or the further case that would tell the surviving rules apart, if this one doesn't already. (~100 words)
