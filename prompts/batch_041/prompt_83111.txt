I sit on the board of a housing cooperative — 214 units across three buildings, governed by a one-unit-one-vote general meeting — and we are preparing to vote on whether to absorb the 38-unit building next door, currently a separate cooperative in financial difficulty. Our solicitor's advice is crisp: only our own members may vote, because the question is whether we take on the debt. A residents' group in the neighbouring building has begun arguing the opposite, namely that they are the ones whose homes, rents and community would be most affected, and that a vote taken without them is not a decision about them but a decision over them. In the middle of this I have been reading about what political theorists call the boundary problem: roughly, that no democratic procedure can by itself determine who the demos is, because every procedure presupposes an already-constituted electorate, and any attempt to settle membership democratically merely pushes the question back one level — who votes on who votes? I would like you to set out the most important implications of that problem for our situation, treating it as a structural feature of the decision rather than as a debating point to be waved away.

Please be concrete, and distinguish the levels at which the problem bites. In particular: what follows for the legitimacy of a vote from which the neighbouring residents are excluded, and does the answer change if the merger would leave them materially worse off as opposed to merely differently off; whether the familiar all-affected-interests test can do any real work here, given that it threatens to expand the electorate without limit (our suppliers, our lender, the city's tax base, the neighbours' children who might one day want a flat), and how it compares with the narrower thought that only those subjected to a decision's coercive enforcement have a claim to a say; whether the fact that we are ourselves a demos nested inside a larger one — a cooperative inside a municipality inside a state — makes the boundary problem easier or harder to live with; and what the problem implies about exit and voice, that is, whether a right to leave does any of the legitimating work that a right to vote cannot.

Finally, we floated one proposal that I would like you to assess as an implication rather than as a design: an affectedness weighting, under which non-members who would bear a material share of the consequences receive a fractional vote, capped so that the existing membership can never be outvoted on the transfer of its own assets. Does the boundary problem show this to be a principled compromise, a fudge that gets the worst of both principles, or an honest acknowledgment that every demos is drawn by a decision that cannot be democratic all the way down? I am less interested in a survey of positions than in what the problem actually forces us to concede, and what it does not.
