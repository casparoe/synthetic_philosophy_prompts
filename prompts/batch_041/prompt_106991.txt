I am a retired field geologist who, since putting down the hammer, has been reading philosophy with the enthusiasm of a late convert. I have lately been preoccupied with Kierkegaard's diagnosis of the press and of the public: his contrast between the public as a phantom and the crowd as a place where truth is lost, his account of leveling as the dissolution of qualitative distinctions into an abstract equality, his notion of chatter as talk that talks about everything and commits to nothing, and his insistence that the single individual must become contemporary with his own life rather than live it at second hand. These ideas have returned to me with some force because I have been following current proposals for what their designers call epistemic infrastructure—platforms that combine large language models, persistent pseudonymous accounts, engagement-optimizing recommenders, and lightweight proof-of-personhood checks. I would like you to imagine Kierkegaard alive today, with his psychology and commitments intact, and to reconstruct what he would say about one such proposal.

The proposal is this: a public-facing science and culture platform on which every article is drafted by a large language model from a human-supplied prompt, signed with a persistent pseudonym, and ranked by a recommender trained to maximize engagement; users may vote, share, and reply, and their replies are themselves ranked and sometimes rewritten by the same model; a separate verification layer certifies that each account belongs to a distinct human being, but does not reveal who that human is; and the platform's stated aim is to make expert consensus legible to a mass audience by translating every finding into a short, emotionally salient story. What would Kierkegaard make of this arrangement? I do not want a generic denunciation of social media. I want a disciplined reconstruction: which of his concepts carry over and which break? Would he see the algorithmically amplified public as a more radical version of the phantom he described, or as something qualitatively new? Does verified uniqueness of personhood answer or evade his worry about anonymity and irresponsibility? What does large language model authorship do to his distinction between chatter and authority—does it intensify the former, or create an unexpected new form of the latter? And is there any redemptive possibility here, or would he counsel the single individual to withdraw into edification? Please be explicit about where you are extrapolating from his views rather than reporting them.
