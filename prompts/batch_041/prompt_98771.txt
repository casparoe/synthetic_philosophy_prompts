Ok so context: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background beyond one intro course, and this has been bugging me since my team started shipping an anomaly-detection thing that can auto-remediate production incidents. Here's the setup. Nobody wrote a rule that says "do X." The system learned a policy from a pile of historical incidents. Say it takes down a service in a way that no one on the team can trace back to a decision anyone made — nobody intended it, nobody could've predicted that specific action, and everyone involved did their job by the standards of the time. Something bad clearly happened and something that looks like an agent did it.

I want your credence that there's a *real* responsibility gap in cases like that — not a legal loophole, an actual gap where it's true that nobody is blameworthy. Give me a number, but mostly I want the decomposition, and I want you to price the pieces rather than hide behind "it depends on your theory of responsibility." Here's my first pass at the terms, rip it up or replace it:

1. Does blame require the agent to have been aware of, or in control of, the specific act? And how much does that bite here, versus the fact that we blame people for unforeseeable outcomes all the time — negligence, strict liability, you built the thing so you own the risk?
2. Does the system itself count as a moral agent? If yes, seems like the gap closes (someone's home). If no, we're back to pricing the humans. Which way do you lean and how confident?
3. Is this actually new, or is it just the normal many-hands problem — nobody goes to jail for a bad corporate decision either, and we mostly decided that's fine and parked the blame on the org or the process?
4. Can responsibility be smeared across designers, deployers, operators, and whoever supplied the training data in proportion to contribution, or does proportional smearing just give everyone a sliver that rounds to zero?
5. Is the gap closed by blaming people for the *risk* rather than the *outcome* — you're culpable for putting an opaque system in a high-stakes spot, full stop, regardless of what it does?
6. Or is the individual-blame frame itself the problem, and the right answer is something collective or institutional we just don't have decent concepts for yet?

Rough number for each, how they combine into the final one, and then tell me which single sub-question my answer is most sensitive to — flip that one and the whole thing moves. Also: what evidence or argument would actually shift your number? Assume you can't look anything up, so I mean arguments, not studies. And please don't just tell me it's a matter of definition, that's the thing I want you to price.

One more thing, since I suspect this is where it gets interesting: does your answer change if the harm is trivial (a few hours of downtime) versus catastrophic (someone definately dies)? If the gap is only "real" at one end of that spectrum, say which end and why.
