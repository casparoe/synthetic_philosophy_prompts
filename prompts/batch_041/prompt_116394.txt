I chair the governing body of a large state secondary school in a city with a substantial and growing Muslim population, and on Thursday the full governing body will debate a new policy of neutrality in dress that the trust which runs our school has proposed for all its schools. The policy would forbid pupils to wear conspicuous religious symbols at school — headscarves, kippot, turbans, visibly worn crosses — on the ground that a state-funded school should be neutral between religions, should not ask children to advertise their community's allegiances, and should prefer one rule for everyone over a patchwork of exemptions. It is openly modelled on the French conception of laïcité, and several of my governors say so with approval; others reply that it imports a French quarrel into an English school, that our own tradition has been to accommodate religious difference rather than to push it out of sight, and that a rule aimed at conspicuous symbols will in practice fall on one group, since a headscarf cannot be tucked under a collar as a cross can.

The case that has forced the issue concerns a Year 10 girl from a family that has not hitherto been especially observant; she began wearing a headscarf after an older cousin came to live with them. Her mother has asked the school to exempt her. Her form tutor reports that the girl has told her, privately, that she would rather not wear it. Two members of the parent council, both Muslim women, have written in support of the policy: they argue that in the absence of a school-wide rule, girls whose families would not otherwise insist will come under a pressure the school cannot see, and that an exemption regime leaves precisely those girls exposed, since the girls who most need protection are the least able to ask for it.

Draft the written opinion I shall circulate before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — adopt the policy as drafted, reject it, or adopt it with specified amendments — with a time frame for whatever we decide, and with reasons the governing body can act on; it should give an honest account of the strongest argument for the course I do not take, rather than dismissing it. It should also assess the following test, which one governor proposes as a way through, and say whether it can bear the weight he places on it: accommodate a religious practice so long as (a) the practice does not itself impose a cost on non-adherents, and (b) permitting it does not make it harder for any member of the same community to decline the practice. Please write in British English.
