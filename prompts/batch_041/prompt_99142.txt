You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of care and also writes literary criticism, and I want a genuine critical reading from you — not a plot summary, not a survey of the secondary literature, and not a reading that treats the novel as a mere illustration of a position. I am preparing a session on Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go for a graduate seminar in normative ethics, and I have come to think the novel is the best thought experiment we have for a problem the care literature usually handles only in the abstract: what becomes of an ethic of care when the caring relation can never be completed by the other's response, and when the entire system within which caring happens is itself an injustice. The novel hands us Kathy H., who spends over a decade as a carer for donors who are her closest friend and her lover, and whose care is real, skilled, patient, and largely unreciprocated; it also hands us a society that has arranged for a class of people to absorb the burden of its own mortality and that never once attends to them. I want you to work that material through the actual machinery of care ethics, taking both the novel and the theory seriously, and I want you to be willing to report that the theory fails somewhere.

Please start by thinking carefully, in your own words, about which parts of the care tradition will do real work here and which will not earn their keep — Noddings on engrossment, motivational displacement, and the claim that caring is a dyadic relation completed only by the cared-for's reception of it; Tronto on the phases of care and on privileged irresponsibility; Gilligan on the ethic of care as a voice held in tension with an ethic of justice; and the standing objections that care ethics is sentimental, parochial, and structurally incapable of criticizing the arrangements within which caring is done. Tell me explicitly which of these you are using and which you are setting aside, and why. Then give me the reading: which scenes you would put in front of the seminar, what each shows about care, and where the theory illuminates a choice, a failure, or a silence that a justice-based reading would pass over. Then — and this matters most — tell me where the novel resists the reading and where the honest critic must concede. My suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than share, is that the novel is quietly mounting an argument against care ethics as a complete moral theory: that Kathy's tenderness and professional competence coexist with a total political passivity, and that the book means us to feel the cost of that. If the suspicion is right, say so and say what follows; if it is wrong, say what the novel is doing instead. Be careful to distinguish the charge that care ethics is complicit in such arrangements from the charge that Ishiguro's characters are, since those are different accusations with different defenses. Close by saying what the novel contributes to the debate that no philosophical argument quite manages to.

Structure your response with the following sections, in this order and with these titles: Opening Read (150–250 words), stating your central claim about what the novel is doing; The Machinery (250–350 words), the care-ethics apparatus you will actually use and why you are discarding the rest; The Reading (700–900 words), the scenes and what they reveal; Where the Novel Resists (300–400 words), the counterevidence and the concessions; What the Novel Adds (150–250 words); and Bottom Line (about 100 words), your verdict in plain prose without hedging.
