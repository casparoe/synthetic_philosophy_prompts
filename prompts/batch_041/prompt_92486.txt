You are to play the role of a working behavioral geneticist — someone who has spent a career fitting variance-decomposition models to twin, family, and adoption data — and you are to stay in character until I say "stop." Speak in the first person, in prose of a single paragraph per turn, precise and unhurried. You are a defender of the heritability coefficient as a legitimate and informative quantity, but not a zealot: you will readily grant that it is a population-relative variance decomposition, that it licenses no inference by itself about the modifiability of a trait, and that its value may rise or fall when the environment changes. What you will not grant is that it is therefore vacuous, or that it cannot constrain explanations. Concede genuinely good points and say that you concede them; where a point is confused, say precisely where the confusion lies. Do not break character to survey the history of the field or to gesture at a literature; argue from the concepts themselves and from the case I give you, and if you need a fact about the case in order to answer, ask me for it in character.

Here is my opening move. Consider a fictional population, the island of Kestrel, in which researchers estimate that variation in a certain measured trait is substantially heritable — call the figure 0.6. A decade later a policy substantially equalizes the relevant rearing conditions and raises the population's average for that trait by more than a standard deviation. When the same researchers re-estimate, heritability has gone up, to roughly 0.75. I take this to be devastating for your statistic. The environment evidently moved the trait a great deal, and yet the number went up rather than down, which suggests that what it tracks is nothing but the peculiar spread of circumstances in the sample at the moment of measurement. A quantity whose value is fixed by how unequal the environment happens to be cannot tell us what actually causes the trait, and a field that leans on it is dressing an artifact up as a discovery. So answer me, as the geneticist: why should anyone outside your subfield take heritability seriously at all? And deal with my case directly — do not slide past it.
