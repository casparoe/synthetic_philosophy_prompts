I've more or less adopted predictive processing as my default way of thinking about minds — the brain as a prediction machine, perception as inference, action as a way of making the world fit the prediction, and the free energy principle as the thing that supposedly ties perception, action, learning and motivation into one story. I'd like you to argue the other side properly: what would actualy count against it?

The bit I care most about is that this is really several claims stacked on top of each other, and I don't think they stand or fall together. There's the free energy principle as a formal claim about any system that manages to keep existing; there's predictive coding as a claim about the actual machinery — error signals, precision weighting, message passing up and down; and there's active inference as a claim about behaviour and motivation. I want to know where the weight actually sits, and whether a defeater at one level touches the others. Related: if the formal principle turns out to be true of anything you can draw a boundary around, is that vacuity, or is a principle of that kind allowed to be that way?

Specifically:

- what result, experiment or model comparison would make you say predictive coding had lost to a rival account of the same data? Not "it's vague" — an actual discriminating prediction, and which rival it discriminates against.
- is the treatment of motivation a real unification or a relabelling? I mean the move where wanting something becomes a matter of high-precision priors over outcomes, so desire and expectation turn out to be the same kind of thing handled by different machinery. Is there a case — motivated reasoning, wishful thinking, self-deception, grief, something around addiction — where that predicts a pattern we don't observe, or misses one we do?
- the dark room. I know the standard escape; what I want to know is whether the escape costs the theory anything, and what a principled version of it has to say about systems that really do nothing at all.
- what a comitted defender would themselves concede counts against the view, as opposed to what critics say from the sidelines.
- and finally: has the framework been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, is that actually a problem for a principle like this, or just the price of a unifying framework? If you think it's well insulated, say so, and say whether that's a bug or a feature.

I'd be most moved by a case where the framework commits itself to some specific odd pattern of behaviour and the pattern doesn't turn up, so weight things that way rather than giving me a survey. If some of the defeaters only bite for someone who already accepts a rival framework, flag that — that's useful to know too. And don't give me citations or a reading list, you'd be guessing and I can't check them anyway.

British English please.
