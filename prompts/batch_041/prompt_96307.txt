I am a novelist, three years into a book set in a provincial capital where one family has, for four decades, placed its people in every office that matters — the water board, the licensing bureau, the land registry — and where, at every wedding in that city, a share of the room's envelopes finds its way to the family's fixer. The book has a journalist in it, and an anticorruption prosecutor, and I have found that I cannot write either of them honestly until I settle a question I keep getting wrong. So let me state the position I have arrived at, and ask you to tell me what should move me off it.

The position: corruption is not a natural kind but a normative one, and its content is fixed by the norms of office that a given polity actually holds, rather than by any trans-contextual standard. The definition that circulates in the governance literature — corruption as the misuse of entrusted power for private gain — is not a neutral description but one local normative order's self-understanding, promoted to a yardstick and exported. It follows that there is no context-independent answer to whether a gift, a favor, a job for a kinsman, or a payment for a permit is corrupt: the answer depends on what the relevant office, in that place, is understood to be for. Call this normative localism about corruption.

What I want is a map of its exposure. Take a position — I would rather have a verdict with its reasons than a balanced menu of options.

1. Say which of my commitments is doing the real work. In particular, tell me whether the view collapses into the truism that evaluation is context-sensitive, or whether it claims something a universalist must deny. If the honest verdict is that it has been qualified into something nobody would dispute, say so, and tell me whether that is fatal or merely a rebranding.

2. Give me what would count against it, ranked from decisive to merely awkward, and distinguish conceptual defeaters from empirical ones: cases, findings, or developments that would show I am describing something real but calling it by the wrong name, or that the trans-contextual standard has independent purchase of its own.

3. Say what a committed localist has to concede. I want the costs the view's own defenders would own, not only the critics' bill of particulars.

4. Work through the cases that worry me most. The boundary between a gift and a bribe, and whether anything in my framework draws it non-arbitrarily — openness, diffuseness, timing, reciprocity, the absence of a specific quid pro quo. Clientelism as a form of representation where programmatic parties are weak, and whether that counts as a partial vindication of localism or simply changes the subject. Conduct that is corrupt in one jurisdiction and lawful in another, such as lobbying, influence, and revolving doors, where I need to know whether I am committed to the moral property differing or only the legal one, and whether conceding the latter collapses the view. And the rotten office: what the localist says about an office whose own norms are predatory, and about colonial and authoritarian administrations.

5. Tell me what localism predicts that universalism does not, and whether the cross-national perception indices are, on my view, merely biased — a complaint I can accommodate — or category-incoherent, which is a prediction I am making and which could be tested. I want to see whether my novel is placing the localist's bets or quietly hedging them.

One thing I would find most persuasive, if it is true: evidence that the people closest to a practice are its harshest judges, and that the condemnation is native rather than imported. If that is the rule rather than the exception, say so, and tell me what it does to my position.
