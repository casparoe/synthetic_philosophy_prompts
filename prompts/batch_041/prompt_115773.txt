For a 90-minute session of a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind and cognitive science — about 20 students, most of whom have read the standard material on the intentional stance and can recite the three stances but have never felt the argument — design an in-class game that makes them feel why attributing beliefs and desires to a system is a predictive strategy rather than a claim about its innards, and then makes that lesson strain when the system in question is a language model. The game must work like this: participants forecast a hidden system's next move among three options each round and score points for being right (tokens, nothing of real value), with the rules arranged so that the only tractable way to score is to attribute a goal to the system, and so that participants arrive at the goal hypothesis themselves rather than being told it. At least one round must make the system's behavior depend on the group's own past choices, so that the best predictors are the ones who model the system as modeling them. One later round must reproduce an earlier round's output sequence exactly while the facilitator generates it from a mechanism with no goal in it at all, so that the group discovers the transcript underdetermines whether anything is being represented. Build in a compression test: once per round a team may submit one sentence saying what the system wants, and it earns a bonus only if a participant who has seen nothing but that sentence can predict the next three moves from it. Give me the materials, the hidden rules stated exactly, the payoff table, a round-by-round timeline, the roles (including how to keep the students who never guess right engaged), and a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the difference between the stance's predictive payoff and any claim about inner representation; onto the underdetermination the twin round exposes; and onto the further question of whether the stance stays stable when the target's behavior is a function of the prompt it is given rather than a fixed disposition, which is where the analogy to a language model has to be handled carefully. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — at minimum the round that collapses into a coin flip and the round where everyone simply copies the loudest player — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: in my setup there is always a fact about the mechanism, so the game can make the instrumentalist reading vivid but cannot touch the question of whether there is genuine aboutness rather than merely a description that compresses well, and I want the debrief to say so and to supply that part by argument.
