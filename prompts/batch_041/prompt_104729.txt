I am assembling the assessment material for a graduate seminar on the classical American pragmatists, and I need one examination item that makes a particular point unmissable: that the Peircean and the Jamesian accounts of truth are not two formulations of a single criterion, and that the gap between them is not a quibble. Please design the case for me rather than merely illustrating the contrast.

What the case is for: it will be graded on whether the scenario actually generates the two verdicts from the two theories. A story that stipulates the answers, or that a careful reader could accept while denying that either theorist is committed to the verdict you assign, fails the item.

Specifications:
- The item must be a single scenario, built to order, in which the two theories deliver different verdicts on the truth of one belief.
- The belief must concern a matter of public fact, empirical or historical, with a determinate truth value on any realist reading. It must not be a moral, religious, or metaphysical hypothesis, and not a claim about another person's private mental life. Those are the stock examples; I want none of them.
- The belief must not be self-fulfilling, and its truth must not depend on its being held. The world must settle the matter independently of anyone's attitude toward it.
- The believer is one ordinary person who weighs the evidence about as competently as anyone could. The case must not work by making them credulous, self-deceived, or careless.
- I need the direction that is usually taken to be the harder one to construct: the belief must come out true on Peirce's account and not true on James's.
- To earn the Peircean verdict, the proposition must be one on which an indefinite continuation of inquiry would converge, and it must be genuinely determinate rather than vague. But no one in the story ever actually verifies it, and the reason must be structural: the evidence is unrecoverable, or the question is too remote ever to be settled in practice, not that anyone is lazy or unlucky in a trivial way.
- To earn the Jamesian verdict, holding the belief must yield no benefit, experiential or practical, to the believer or to anyone else, at any point in the story; if anything it should cost them. It must also fail to pay in the broader currency a sophisticated Jamesian might invoke, such as coherence with the rest of what is believed or economy of thought. If it pays there, the case collapses.
- The case must be realistic and compact: an ordinary person in an ordinary setting, no time travel, no immortal observers, no science fiction, no possible-worlds machinery, no appeal to beings smarter than we are.

Then deliver the following, under these headings, in this order:
- The scenario (about 250 words): the story itself, in everyday terms, with the belief and the believer's situation clear but the diagnosis left implicit.
- Specification check (about 100 words): confirm point by point that the scenario satisfies each constraint above, and flag any place where it strains.
- Peirce's verdict (about 200 words): state the belief, then show why Peirce's criterion makes it true. Say explicitly why Peirce would refuse to let any actual consensus, even a unanimous one, settle the matter, and how the ideal limit does the work instead.
- James's verdict (about 200 words): show why James's criterion withholds truth here, and be precise about which sense of working or paying off you are relying on.
- What does the work (about 150 words): name the single feature of the case that separates the two verdicts, and explain why no other feature is doing the separating.
- Stress test (about 150 words): alter or remove that one feature and show what happens, whether the verdicts converge or one of them flips.
- The Jamesian's best reply (about 200 words): state the strongest objection a defender of James could make to your case, and say honestly whether the case survives it.

Keep the prose plain and the reasoning explicit, and do not supply citations.
