I have spent most of this term arguing about forgeries with two friends — one of them collects prints, the other works in a conservation lab, so every dinner has turned into a seminar — and I have come to notice that my own reactions follow a pattern I cannot put into words. I would like you to find the principle, if there is one, and to tell me which of my reactions it forces me to abandon.

Here is what I think, case by case. A forger paints a canvas indistinguishable from a lost Vermeer; hung in a gallery, it moves me exactly as the Vermeer would, and I think it would be wrong to say that my experience of it is worth less than my experience of the genuine article. The same canvas, once the forgery is exposed, is still beautiful and I still want galleries to show it — but I no longer want it in the room labelled Vermeer, and I would not cross a city to see it. A painting long catalogued as the work of an unknown follower is reattributed to the master himself; nothing about the object changes, yet I suddenly find it more worth looking at. A restorer replaces a third of a damaged altarpiece with new wood and pigment; I think that what hangs there is no longer quite the painting, though I cannot say which third of my experience is missing. A cliff face weathered into a shape a sculptor would have had to labour for months to carve moves me as much as a sculpture does, and yet I resist calling it art at all. A brilliant novel published anonymously, its author unknown, seems to me no worse as a novel than the same book once the author is identified and turns out to be someone I dislike — though I notice my estimate of it shift anyway. And a machine carves a marble figure from a design no human being ever conceived; the figure is lovely, and I find I cannot decide whether to say it was created or merely produced.

My own guess is that a work's value splits into two parts, one carried by the object itself and one carried by its causal history, and that every verdict above is explained by which part the case draws on. But I can feel this going wrong somewhere, and I would rather you tell me where than let me paper over it. So please state the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that actually fits these verdicts; identify the case that pulls against the rest; say what each candidate predicts for cases I have not mentioned — a copy that the artist made of his own earlier painting, an honest replica sold as a replica, a performance by a tribute band, a signature added to a painting by someone other than the painter — and be candid about which of my verdicts the cleanest principle cannot keep.
