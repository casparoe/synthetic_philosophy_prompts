I've been roped into covering a colleague's intro epistemology class next week — 80 minutes, about 25 students, mostly second-years with a handful of majors. They've done correspondence and coherence already, so truth as a topic isn't new, but none of them have seen a deflationary view of it, and my guess is most of them think 'deflationary' means the view that truth doesn't matter or is just whatever we decide. Don't explain deflationism to me; I know it well enough. I need a plan for teaching it, not the teaching.

First, the order. What has to come before what, and why that order rather than some other one? Which single example should carry the whole session — and what does that example need to have, and need to not have, for the payoff to land? I'm tempted to build it on the plain equivalence between a sentence and the claim that it's true, but that feels too thin to hang eighty minutes on; if you agree, tell me what to use instead. Also tell me why I shouldn't open with the liar paradox, which is what half the internet does, and what opening with it would cost me.

Then the misconceptions. I can guess a few — that the view makes truth subjective, that it says the word true is meaningless, that a deflationist has no way to say lying is wrong, that it's the same thing as pragmatism — but I want the full list in roughly the order they'll actually surface, and for each one the precise point in the sequence where I should head it off rather than waiting for it to come up on its own. Some of these I'll want to preempt before the idea is even on the table; say which, and say why.

Two things I'm unsure how to place: the cases where you can't just delete the truth predicate and walk away (blind endorsements and the like), and the objection that truth does some kind of normative work, that we aim at it — whether the deflationist can answer that or only dodge it. Tell me where each belongs, or whether either is too far in the weeds for this group.

And what should I refuse to go into at this level, out loud, so they know there's more without thinking I'm hiding it?

Finally, one question I can ask at the end that will tell me whether they've actually got the view, as opposed to just nodding along.

One more thing throughout: seperate what's actually settled in the literature from what's genuinely contested among people who work on truth, and don't sell me a contested line as if it were the consensus. I'm the one standing in front of them, and I definitly don't want to hand them a live fight as a fact.
