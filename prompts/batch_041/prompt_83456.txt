For some years I have been turning over a small puzzle I met in a popular account of decision-making, and I have not managed to settle it by thinking it through on my own. Two jars of marbles are on a table. I am told the exact composition of the first. Of the second I am told only that it holds red and black marbles in some proportion, and that it holds the same number in total. I am invited to bet on a colour drawn from one of the jars. Most people choose the jar whose contents they know, and, more interestingly, they choose it a second time when the bet is switched to the other colour — even though nothing they were told gives them any reason to think the unknown jar holds fewer red marbles than the known one, and nothing gives them any reason to think it holds fewer black ones either. The usual moral is that people are muddled about what they do not know.

I am not sure the moral is that simple, and I want a case that tests it rather than merely illustrates it. Please build one to the specifications below.

First, it must be realistic. No jars, marbles, dice, coins, cards, or lotteries with stated odds. It should be a situation an ordinary adult could actually be in — a purchase, a repair, a hire, a tenancy, a course of treatment — described in plain language, with no numerical probabilities attached to anything.

Second, it must contain two decisions, made by the same person and presented as a pair, of the same general kind and with the same amount of money or wellbeing riding on each. In each decision one option's odds are known to the person and the other's are not, and the known odds must be the same figure in both decisions.

Third, in both decisions the person must choose the option whose odds are known. I want the behaviour to be identical across the pair, so that an onlooker would describe the person as consistent.

Fourth, the two decisions must differ in exactly one feature of the situation. That feature must not be the person's mood, appetite for risk, tiredness or haste, nor the size of the stakes. It must be a feature of the situations themselves, and it must be the thing that makes one of the two choices sound and the other unsound. It must also be something the person could give as a reason if asked, not merely a cause of what they did.

Fifth, nothing may be settled by hindsight. At the moment of choosing, the person has everything needed to make the better choice in both decisions, and which choice is better is knowable in advance.

Then, in this order: set out the two decisions in enough detail that I could picture myself in them; say what the person chooses in each and why; name the single feature that makes one choice sound and the other unsound, and explain exactly how it does that work; tell me what the case shows about the original puzzle, whether it supports the usual moral, undermines it, or shows that the question has no general answer; remove the feature and tell me what becomes of the person's uniform preference, whether it turns uniformly sound, uniformly unsound, or the case loses its point; and finally, put the strongest objection available on behalf of someone who still holds the usual moral, and say how much of the case survives it.
