I'm a novelist — fourth book, a campus novel set on a hospital ethics committee, so for the last few months I've been reading moral philosophy at the trade-paperback level plus a couple of recorded lecture series my brother-in-law sent me (he teaches intro at a state school). Last weekend I was at a dinner and a friend who did a PPE degree and never lets anyone forget it held forth on Scanlon's contractualism for what felt like half an hour. I'm reconstructing from memory so the wording is mine, but the content is his: contractualism is the view that an action is wrong if anyone could reasonably reject a principle that permits it; "reasonably" there basically means "in your own interest," so it's a bargaining model, which is why it counts as social contract theory — Hobbes with better manners, morality being whatever we'd all agree to; and the crucial bit is that it's strictly anti-aggregative, meaning numbers never count for anything, so a contractualist has to say it's exactly as bad to let one person drown as to let a thousand drown. Which is why, he said, it's useless for real policy and why Parfit and the consequentialists (Parfit, I think? someone like that) tore it apart. He also said Scanlon's whole point was that we must never treat anyone as a means, which he lifted from Kant, so the theory is basically the categorical imperative with a bit of maths about rejectability bolted on.

Is any of that fair? I have a scene where my bioethicist character has to defend a triage call to a furious parent, and I want her to sound like someone who actually knows the position rather than someone doing the TED-talk version of it.
