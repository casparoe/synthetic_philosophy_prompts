I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years in condensed-matter work, mostly spectroscopy — and since retiring I have been reading my way into moral philosophy with more enthusiasm than method. I have recently finished Sidgwick's Methods of Ethics and have also read a fair amount about his long involvement with the Society for Psychical Research, which he helped found in 1882 and served as its first president. I have written the essay below and would be grateful for detailed comments, especially on accuracy and on whether the central interpretive claim is defensible or whether I have made an amateur's error.

Sidgwick's Two Investigations and the Same Discipline of Belief

Everyone notices the juxtaposition: the most rigorous of the Victorian utilitarians spent enormous energy on thought-transference experiments and on mediumistic sittings, and died unable to affirm the survival he hoped for. The usual treatment is either apologetic (a great philosopher had a blind spot) or romantic (a courageous freethinker followed the evidence wherever it led). Both seem to me to miss what is philosophically interesting.

My claim is that Sidgwick's psychical research and his ethics are governed by one and the same epistemic discipline, and that this discipline is best described not as scepticism but as a refusal to let a desired conclusion do any work in the argument that establishes it.

Start with the ethics. In the Methods he finds two apparently self-evident principles — egoism and universal benevolence — and cannot show that they coincide in every case. The reconciliation, he thinks, would require something like a postulate about the moral government of the universe, and for him that was bound up with the question of a future life. So the question of survival was not a hobby sitting alongside the ethics; it was the empirical shadow of the ethics' central unresolved problem. That gives him a powerful motive, and he knew it.

Now the discipline. Precisely because he had a motive, he set a higher evidential bar for survival than for telepathy. Telepathy, he thought, could be established by controlled experiments in which fraud and sensory leakage could be excluded, and he and his wife ran such experiments and defended them against the objection that the agents were unconsciously whispering or sub-vocalizing cues that the percipient overheard. Survival, by contrast, reached him only through mediumistic phenomena, where the same controls were unavailable, where the medium had every incentive to produce results, and where communication among the living was always a rival explanation. So he became convinced of telepathy while remaining agnostic about survival — not because the survival evidence was weaker in some generic sense, but because the rival explanations could not be eliminated.

There is a corollary he would have appreciated. The sealed-letter test that his friend Myers arranged, and that could only be adjudicated after both men were dead, was reported as a failure. The method by which Sidgwick protected his ethics from wishful thinking also insulated him from the consolation he wanted. That is the tragedy, if it is one.

A second and more speculative claim: the same discipline explains why his ethics ends in a dualism he could not resolve. He would not let the practical need for a moral order count as evidence for one, and so he left the contradiction standing rather than dissolve it by postulate. In this respect he is the opposite of Kant as usually read: Kant postulates what he cannot know, whereas Sidgwick refuses to postulate and reports the contradiction. The psychical research is then the empirical attempt to obtain, by investigation, what the postulate would have handed him for free.

What I would like from you:

1. Is the central claim — that the ethics and the psychical research are continuous rather than merely coexisting, and that both are governed by a single norm about desired conclusions — a fair reading, or does it overstate the connection? I have a nagging worry that I am reading the biography back into the philosophy.

2. Is my account of the dualism accurate? In particular, have I got it right that he thought the reconciliation would require something like a postulate about the moral government of the universe, and that he declined to make that postulate within the ethics itself?

3. On telepathy versus survival: is the asymmetry I describe — controlled experiment on one side, non-eliminable rival hypotheses on the other — the right way to characterize his actual position, or was he more ambivalent than that?

4. Is the Kant contrast in the last paragraph defensible, or is it the kind of amateur pattern-making that a Kant scholar would wince at?

5. Is the framing interesting to a philosopher, or is it a physicist's rediscovery of something that already has a standard name and a standard refutation?

Please be specific, and where I am wrong tell me what the more defensible formulation would be. I am not asking for reading lists; I am asking whether the argument as stated survives.
