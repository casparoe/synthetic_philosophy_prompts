I'm writing a short position statement for a reading group on intentionality and would like a consistency check before I circulate it. Here is where I've ended up.

(1) The two-bitser case settles the question for machines: a coin-detecting device's inner state can be described equally well as registering a quarter, a disc of a particular weight and alloy, or a voltage, and nothing in the device decides which description is correct. Its content is ascribed relative to our purposes, not possessed.

(2) For organisms it is otherwise. When a honeybee's dance carries information about the direction and distance of a nectar source, there is a fact of the matter about what the dance represents, and it is not up to us.

(3) What fixes that fact is function. The dance was selected for carrying that information, and functions are real, mind-independent facts about the world.

(4) I am a physicalist, and I take this to be a fully naturalistic story.

(5) Searle's point about symbol manipulation is right: no amount of formal structure adds up to meaning.

(6) The intentional stance is a predictive strategy rather than a discovery; its ascriptions are instruments for forecasting behaviour, not claims about what a system really represents.

(7) One verdict I want to keep: a trap built to catch codling moths, deployed where the only matching insect is a look-alike species, represents the look-alike, because what a state represents is fixed by what it now reliably tracks.

I suspect these do not all sit together, though I can't see where the pressure is. Tell me which of them pull against each other and which pairs only appear to conflict, and for each genuine conflict give the cheapest repair and say what that repair costs me elsewhere. Please order the conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad.
