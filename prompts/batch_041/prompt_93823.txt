I recently joined the model governance committee at the hospital system where I work. I am a nurse there and also a patient there, and I happen to be in one of the groups that the tool in question flags more often, which is how a committee assignment turned into something I think about at night. In a few weeks we are supposed to approve a risk score that flags patients for follow-up outreach, and I had assumed the hard part would be statistical. Instead, nearly every meeting now turns on the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness. As I understand them, a score cannot be calibrated within groups and at the same time have equal false-positive and false-negative rates across groups when the groups' base rates differ, unless the prediction is nearly perfect. The committee has drawn what looks to me like the obvious conclusion: no fair score is available for what we are doing, so what we are really choosing between is a tool that over-flags one group and a tool that misses patients in the other. On that reading, fairness is not a technical property we can secure but a political choice about whose interests to trade away, and the only honest thing is to make that choice openly.

That is where I need help, and I would rather have the reasoning than a verdict, since I will have to defend it in front of people who are already skeptical of the whole project. If a fair score is off the table, what is the most defensible way to decide which group's interests to subordinate? And how should we think about the accuracy we would be giving up in order to shrink the disparity — is there a principled stopping point, or is that number simply a judgment call we should own as such?

Two features of our situation may matter. First, the score is not the decision: it goes to a nurse who weighs it alongside everything else she knows, so the group-level disparity in who actually gets called is partly the model's doing and partly ours, and we cannot currently separate the two. Second, the vendor's score is being validated for us against two different targets, missed appointments and unplanned readmission, and the gap in base rates between groups is large for one and nearly absent for the other; a colleague says that means the impossibility result applies to only one of them. If either detail changes the analysis, I would like to know why, because at the moment they feel like complications the theorem simply ignores.
