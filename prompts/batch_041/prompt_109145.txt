I teach the last chapter of Principia Ethica every other year, and this term a colleague in the department — a sympathetic reader of Moore rather than a critic — put an argument before our reading group that I have not been able to answer to my own satisfaction. It turns on the doctrine of organic unities and on the method of isolation, and it appears to establish something I am not prepared to accept. Each of its steps looks plausible to me taken on its own, which is precisely what makes it intolerable; I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

The argument, in the form in which he gave it: (1) The intrinsic value of a whole is not the sum of the intrinsic values of its parts — this is the doctrine of organic unities. The consciousness of a beautiful thing, or the love of a friend, is worth far more than the bare existence of the object plus the mere occurrence of the feeling; on Moore's account the components, taken severally, have little or no value. (2) That value is intrinsic: it is the value the whole would have if it existed quite alone, which is what the method of isolation is meant to bring out, and nothing outside the whole can add to it or subtract from it. (3) So if one world contains such a whole and another does not, the second containing nothing else of value, the first world is better. (4) If one world contains two such wholes and another contains only one, the first is better, since each whole carries its own intrinsic value with it and, by (2), the presence of the other cannot affect it; the two values simply add. (5) The same reasoning iterates: for any number n, a world containing n+1 such wholes is better than a world containing n. (6) Nothing in the argument depends on how great any particular whole is, so long as the cognition is accurate and the feeling appropriate — a being taking a correct but faint pleasure in a well-made teacup, two people sharing a mild and unremarkable fondness. It follows that for any world containing a few great goods, such as one profound and lifelong friendship, there is a better world containing an enormous number of small ones; and iterating, a universe of countless trillions of mild, accurate pleasures in pretty objects comes out better than a universe containing that single great friendship. (7) He adds a corollary: since bringing into existence a being whose life contains such small goods is itself to add a valuable whole, we have reason to produce such beings without limit.

I accept neither (6) nor (7). But I am reluctant to give up (1), which I take to be both true and Moore's most durable contribution to the theory of value, or (2), which states what it is for a value to be intrinsic rather than instrumental. Tell me which step or steps to reject, why each of them looked as though it could not be the culprit, and what rejecting them costs. If the argument is in fact valid, then say which of Moore's commitments has to go and what becomes of the rest of his position — in particular, whether an ethics that identifies the good with the greatest possible amount of intrinsic value can survive the loss. I would rather keep (1), (2), and (7) than (6), but I want to know what I am paying.
