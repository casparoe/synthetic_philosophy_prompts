For a graduate seminar on decision theory I've been given leave to write my term paper around a film, and I've chosen the Safdie brothers' *Uncut Gems* (2019), with Adam Sandler as Howard Ratner, read through the theory of risk-weighted expected utility. As I understand the view — Buchak's version, in *Risk and Rationality* — the agent's attitude to risk is a separate ingredient in her preferences from the utility she assigns to outcomes. The risk-averse agent does not act as though the better outcomes had less value for her (diminishing marginal utility); she acts as though the chances of the better outcomes were worth less than they are, and the reweighting is applied to the cumulative probability of each outcome, taken in order from worst to best. Risk aversion and risk seeking thus become transformations of probability rather than features of value, and — the part I actually care about — they are supposed to be a legitimate exercise of instrumental rationality rather than a lapse from it, which is where this departs from the reference-dependence and loss aversion of prospect theory. Howard looks to me like a gift of a case. He has an opal smuggled in from Ethiopia, lends it to Kevin Garnett as a good-luck charm and keeps Garnett's championship ring as collateral, pawns the ring and puts the money on a six-way parlay and wins; he turns down Garnett's offer of $175,000 for the opal because he expects the auction to do far better; and at the end, holding cash that would clear the debt he has been running from for the whole film, he sends Julia to the casino to put it all on a three-way parlay rather than settle, refuses to cancel the bet when Arno demands it, and is euphoric for a few seconds when it comes in before he is shot.

Two things I keep running into and cannot settle on my own. The first is that the film seems to supply evidence for the risk-weighting story and for its chief rival at the same time. On the rival story Howard is just an ordinary expected-utility maximizer whose utility function happens to attach value to the act of betting itself — the action, the sweat, the watching — in which case no transformation of probabilities is needed at all. Is there a scene that lets us tell these apart, or does the film, by making the gamble feel like its own reward, show us that the two hypotheses are not distinguishable from choice behaviour? The second, which I suspect is worse for my use of the theory, is that almost nothing in Howard's world has a known probability: whether Garnett will play well, what the opal will fetch, whether Arno will actually hurt him. That is ambiguity rather than risk, and risk-weighted expected utility as I understand it has nothing to say about it. So I'd like you to say where the risk-weighting lens genuinely illuminates a choice or a scene, where the film resists it — including whether Howard's choices are consistent enough over time for any single risk function to represent them, or whether what we are watching is an addict whose preferences shift from moment to moment, which would put him outside the theory's domain altogether — and, finally, what you take the film to be saying about whether an attitude to risk can be called rational or irrational at all. I don't need a reading list, and I'm not after citations; I want the argument, anchored in particular scenes, plus a verdict on whether the film is a good case study for this theory or whether the honest conclusion is that it dramatizes the theory's limits. If the film adds something to the debate that a counterexample in a journal article couldn't, say what that is.
