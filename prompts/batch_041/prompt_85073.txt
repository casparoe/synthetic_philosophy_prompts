You're a constitutional law academic who works on federalism and subsidiarity — think carefully, and start by brainstorming the possible rules before you commit to one.

I sit on our federation's subsidiarity comittee. We decide whether a bill from the centre gets struck down as a matter for the regions; our default is that the regions keep a competence unless the centre can show it needs it, so nearer the EU's subsidiarity test than the US's enumerated powers, if that helps.

What we've done so far. Upheld the centre on: a ban on an industrial solvent that leaches into a river crossing three regional borders; minimum staffing levels for hospitals, on the grounds that patients cross borders for treatment and the medical labour market is shared; a minimum income floor, even though every region could fund its own, because otherwise regions would compete to be the least generous and people would move; and a ban on parents physically disciplining their children, where two regions had deliberately kept a defence letting parents argue it was reasonable — we upheld that one too, while admitting there was no cross-border effect whatsoever. Struck down: a cap on homework hours in primary schools; rules on the height and materials of buildings in historic town centres; and central procurement of school meals, which the centre said would simply be cheaper.

Next month's case: a single federation-wide minimum staffing ratio for residential care homes — something like one carer per four residents overnight — with the centre topping up the funding. The centre's pitch is equality rather than cruelty: a frail 90-year-old shouldn't get a different standard of care depending on which region she happens to live in. Nobody's claiming residents move between regions for care, and nobody's alleging abuse or neglect. The regions say rural labour shortages, wage costs and their own mandates all differ, and that they were elected to decide this.

What should we do if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I can see at least two rules that fit everything above and split on this case — tell me what they are, which of our earlier decisions are ambiguous between them, and which is the nearest analog to the care homes case. Give me your best guess and the reason for it, and if the record genuinely underdetermines it, be honest about which principle we'd be adopting either way. Last thing: if I can get one more case onto the docket that would settle it, what should it be? British English, please.
