I'm applying for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship and need a short research proposal: roughly 1,500 words, one project, sized to a single semester of work. What follows is a half-formed idea that needs to be turned into something defensible, so I'd like you to do the framing work rather than fill in a template.

The starting point is a family of results in the economics of information, usually called no-trade theorems. Roughly: if two agents share a prior over the relevant states, each updates by Bayesian conditionalization, and it is common knowledge that a trade would be mutually beneficial given what each of them knows, then no such trade occurs — because the bare fact that the other party is willing to take the opposite side is itself evidence about what she knows, and that evidence cancels the apparent gain. A closely related result is that agents who share a prior cannot agree to disagree: once each knows the other's posterior, the posteriors coincide. The moral usually drawn is that speculative trade, trade motivated only by a difference in information, requires either heterogeneous priors or a failure of common knowledge — that is, it requires something the theory treats as a defect.

Put that beside the way philosophers and economists habitually talk about markets as epistemic devices: prices as a summary of dispersed information, the price system as a way of making private knowledge publicly usable. There is an obvious tension here, but I do not think it is the familiar one about incentives, according to which perfectly informative prices would pay nobody to gather the information that makes them informative. My worry is more basic, and I suspect less discussed: the no-trade results are results about trades, and prices are not trades. A price can be informative in an equilibrium with no trade at all, and much of what is supposed to make a price informative is public in a way that the act of trading is not. So the question is whether the informational role of prices survives these results at all, or whether it is quietly parasitic on the results being about something else.

The angle I want the proposal built around is a distinction between two ways a price can carry information. Revelation: the price encodes information because the people who set it knew things, so the price is a window onto private knowledge. Aggregation: the price is a device that makes dispersed information usable by people who never had it and never learn whose it was, the informational work being done by the publicness of the price rather than by anyone's willingness to bet. These come apart. Revelation is what the no-trade results threaten, since it requires someone to trade on a difference in information. Aggregation may be untouched by them, since it requires only that a price exist and be public. But then a second question arises: for a price to do aggregative work it is not enough that it be informative, it must be common knowledge that it is informative, or nobody will treat it as a signal. And whether a price is informative depends on whether the trades behind it are informative, which is precisely what the no-trade results say cannot be taken for granted. So the aggregative story looks self-referential — a fixed point rather than a straightforward mechanism. Whether that fixed point is benign, a stable equilibrium in which prices are informative because everyone treats them as informative, or vicious, an infinite regress in which the information never gets in, is in my view a genuine open question. A third thread is the difference between a market and a conversation. In a conversation the informative event is an utterance by an identified speaker, so the epistemology of disagreement becomes an epistemology of speech. In a market the informative event is a number, and the identity and motives of the traders behind it are typically invisible; anonymity may be exactly what licenses the informational externality to do its work, or exactly what blocks it. I want the proposal to take a position on which.

What I need from you is a proposal containing: (1) the central question stated so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a survey topic; (2) why the existing positions — the rational-expectations treatment of prices, the Hayekian treatment of dispersed knowledge, the moral drawn from the no-trade results, and the conciliatory side of the epistemology of disagreement — leave it open, without pretending any of them has already settled it; (3) the method, meaning what I would actually do, which should be conceptual with only light formalism: reconstruct the arguments premise by premise, identify which premise the market epistemologist must abandon and at what cost, and work out the fixed-point condition rather than reviewing literature or running regressions; (4) what a result would look like, both what would count as showing that the informational role of prices survives and what would count as showing that it does not, and what would count as the project failing; and (5) a realistic scope for one semester, including an honest statement of which part is likely to be too big and what I should cut. Use concrete cases where they earn their place: the small share of trading volume plausibly driven by information as against liquidity and hedging, the prohibition of insider trading as the purest case of a trade that is information-driven and that we forbid anyway, and prediction markets, where prices are supposed to be informative even though participants may be trading for reasons other than being right.
