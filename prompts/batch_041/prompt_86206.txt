I've been having a long, slightly wine-fuelled arguement with two friends about whether soldiers on the two sides of an unjust war are morally on a par, and I keep landing on verdicts that I suspect can't all be true at once, so I'd like you to work out what principle, if any, I'm actually operating with. Here's where my gut goes. Two conscripts, neither of whom volunteered, each told by his own government — plausibly, with maps and forged intercepts and all the rest of it — that the other side crossed first, both of them just holding the line in their own country's fields: I can't see that one of them is more killable than the other, and I don't think either is a murderer for shooting the other. Now swap in a volunteer on the aggressor's side, well informed, who signed up for the pay and the chance to loot, and who is shelling a city: it seems to me plainly permissible to kill him, and I don't feel his death as the same kind of tragedy as the conscript's. Then a reservist on the aggressor's side — a farmer who does three weeks a year, called up by a government he voted against, driving an ammunition lorry to the front, who has been told by his brother that the war is a lie and drives anyway because refusing means prison and his family's ruin: I feel queasy. I don't want to say shooting him is murder, but I can't quite say he's on a par with the two conscripts either. Meanwhile the bloke who makes the shells in a factory back home, well paid, fully informed, under no coercion, I think it's wrong to kill, even though I'd call him more culpable than the lorry driver. A fourteen-year-old pressed into the aggressor's army at gunpoint: wrong to kill him, full stop. And when the just side pushes into the aggressor's own territory and those same conscripts from my first case are defending their own villages, my verdict definately shifts — killing them then feels much closer to murder, even though nothing about their side's cause has changed.

My friend says the thing that matters isn't culpability but who is 'the last person who can stop the threat at a cost we could reasonably ask him to bear' — the one nearest the gun — and that this explains why the lorry driver is liable and the factory worker isn't, and why the child isn't; my other friend says it's just 'contributing to an unjust threat while able to understand what you're doing', which makes the conscripts and the volunteer all liable, and she thinks that's fine and I don't.

What I'd like from you: the principle, or a short list of candidate principles, that gets as many of my verdicts as possible; for each clause, the case that buys it; what the principle says about cases I haven't mentioned — a military doctor on the aggressor's side treating wounded soldiers, a civilian who voted for the war and cheers it on, and a soldier on the just side defending his own town but firing from a school; and then an honest statement of which of my verdicts has to go. I'd much rather have a simple principle with one casualty than a baroque one that saves everything. Please finish with a line of the form \answer{...} giving a short name for the principle you'd go with and the one of my verdicts it costs me.
