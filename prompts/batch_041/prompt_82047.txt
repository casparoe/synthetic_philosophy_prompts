I'm a doctoral student in public administration, and for the past year I've been observing — and occasionally facilitating — a citizens' assembly that my city convened on climate and housing policy, and I've been trying to work out what we have actually committed ourselves to. The design is roughly this: one hundred residents are selected by lot, stratified by ward and age; they are paid a stipend for six weekends; they hear from invited experts and from stakeholder advocates; they deliberate in facilitated small groups of eight; and at the end they vote on a short list of policy options. The short list, however, is not theirs. Before the assembly ever meets, the city's technical staff screen every proposal for legal and budgetary feasibility, and the assembly may choose only among the surviving options — a member who proposes rent control or a municipal bank is told the item is out of scope because staff have already ruled it infeasible. The council then receives the assembly's recommendation, is not bound by it, and has twice amended it substantially. Everyone involved, from the mayor's office to the facilitators' manual, describes the whole exercise in Deweyan terms: democracy conceived as inquiry, a public working out its own problems experimentally rather than merely registering antecedent preferences. I find this both apt and false, and I can't tell whether the falseness is in the rhetoric or in the design.

What philosophical commitments does this institution actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of democracy and of inquiry that the design encodes: does it treat deliberation as a method for discovering better policies or as a fairer way of aggregating preferences that were already there; does it treat the assembled public as a knowing subject or as a legitimating sounding board for decisions made elsewhere; and, most tellingly, does the expert feasibility screen presuppose a division of labor in which citizens supply ends while technicians supply means — exactly the dichotomy Dewey spent his career denying? I want to know which features of the practice strictly presuppose the theory you identify and which merely sit comfortably with it, where the design is philosophically underdetermined so that several rival theories would license the very same institution, and what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the presupposed view with a genuinely experimentalist one — including whether any of those changes could survive contact with a real city budget and a real city council.
