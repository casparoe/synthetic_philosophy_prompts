I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I would like help with a puzzle about a colleague. She is a former physicist who now runs probabilistic forecasting for our team, and over the past year she has given me a series of firm and, as far as I can tell, internally consistent verdicts about when a person ought to revise her present credence to match a credence she knows she will hold later. When I described the pattern to a friend who studied philosophy, he said it sounded like the territory of a principle about credence and future selves, which he attributed to van Fraassen and called the Reflection principle. I have not read the source material and am relying on your ability to reason about the cases directly, so please do not send me to a reading list; I want your analysis of the cases themselves.

The verdicts are all of the following form: a person now holds credence p in some proposition, knows with certainty what credence she will hold at a later time, and must decide whether to move to that later credence now. My colleague's verdicts, as she has stated them to me:

1. She knows that tomorrow, having reviewed the same evidence and also examined a new dataset, she will estimate a 0.8 probability that a certain hardware defect caused an outage. She now estimates 0.6. Her verdict: she should move to 0.8 now.

2. She knows that tomorrow, after a minor medical procedure and its anesthetic, she will feel 0.9 confidence that the same defect caused the outage, with no new evidence of any kind. She now estimates 0.5. Her verdict: she should stay at 0.5.

3. She knows that tomorrow, in a fit of irritation at a colleague, she will estimate 0.95, and that the irritation will have passed by the following day. She now estimates 0.5. Her verdict: she should stay at 0.5.

4. She knows that tomorrow she will consult a domain expert she regards as reliable and will move to 0.2, even though she cannot now reproduce the expert's reasoning and will never have independent access to the expert's evidence. Her verdict: she should move to 0.2 now.

5. She knows that tomorrow she will have forgotten a detail she now remembers and that, for that reason alone, she will estimate 0.4. She now estimates 0.7. Her verdict: she should stay at 0.7.

6. She knows that tomorrow she will read a brief that she already knows to be one-sided and misleading, and that reading it will move her to 0.3. She now estimates 0.7. Her verdict: she should stay at 0.7.

The new case, which she posed and then declined to answer, is this. She is about to spend a long day re-deriving the same calculations from the same data she already has, with no new evidence arriving. She knows that by tomorrow evening this process will leave her at 0.4 rather than her present 0.6. She also knows that, on past occasions, this kind of extended re-derivation has sometimes improved her estimates and sometimes degraded them, and that she has no way, in advance, to tell which is happening this time. She asks: should she move to 0.4 now, stay at 0.6, or land somewhere in between?

What I want from you is the following. First, your best guess at the verdict she would give, and the verdict you think she should give for consistency with her own record. Second, the set of rules that fit all six earlier verdicts equally well — I expect more than one, and I want each stated precisely enough to be tested, including any that differ only in what they treat as a distorting influence, as an informational gain, or as a change in reliability. Third, what each of those rules predicts about the new case, and which of them the new case actually separates. Fourth, your reasoned preference among the rules, and the case or argument that would best tell them apart. Finally, since this is a matter where I would rather know the shape of your uncertainty than get a confident-sounding answer: tell me what would change your mind about which rule she is running, and where your reasoning seems to you most likely to fail.
