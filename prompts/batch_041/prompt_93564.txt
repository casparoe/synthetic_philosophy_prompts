Quick version of the setup so we're on the same page: take two agents who share a prior over everything, see all the same evidence, and it's common knowledge between them that each is rational and what credence each currently assigns. The theorem says they can't agree to disagree — their credences have to match. Everyone I talk to accepts the math. What nobody agrees on is what the math is *for*. One camp says it's a deep constraint on rationality: any persisting disagreement between genuine peers, once it's out in the open and mutually known, is a rational failure somewhere, full stop. The other camp says it's a curiosity about an idealized model that no real disagreement ever violates because the assumptions never hold, and that treating it as a norm is a category error.

Both are ideal Bayesians, both update correctly, both know all this about each other, and they still land at 70/30 and 40/60. Now the wrinkle I actually care about: suppose they don't differ in their prior over the world's state at all. They differ in their prior over *each other's reliability* — Ada treats Bo as a slightly noisier channel than Bo thinks he is, and Bo does the same in reverse. Neither is being sloppy; each is just reasoning from a different background theory of who's trustworthy. I want you to take that wrinkle seriously and not dissolve it by stipulation. Does this scenario show the theorem's conditions are empirical idealizations that fully rational agents can legitimately fall short of, so that persisting disagreement is sometimes perfectly reasonable?
