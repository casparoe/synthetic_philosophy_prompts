I'm working through Kant's Critique of Pure Reason with a reading group on transcendental arguments, and I've been handed the session on the chapter on the schematism of the pure concepts of understanding, in the Analytic of Principles. I thought I had the Deduction more or less under control, but the opening pages of this chapter have stopped me. The text I have is Meiklejohn's 1855 translation, and the passage runs as follows:

"In all subsumptions of an object under a conception, the representation of the object must be homogeneous with the conception; in other words, the conception must contain that which is represented in the object to be subsumed under it. For this is the meaning of the expression, An object is contained under a conception. Thus the empirical conception of a plate is homogeneous with the pure geometrical conception of a circle, inasmuch as the roundness which is cogitated in the former is intuited in the latter."

Kant then observes that pure concepts of the understanding are heterogeneous with any intuition, and asks how their application to appearances is possible at all, before continuing:

"Now it is quite clear, that there must be some third thing, which on the one side is homogeneous with the category, and with the phænomenon on the other, and so makes the application of the former to the latter possible. This mediating representation must be pure (without any empirical content), and yet must on the one side be intellectual, on the other sensuous. Such a representation is the transcendental schema."

And then, of time:

"Now a transcendental determination of time is so far homogeneous with the category, which constitutes the unity thereof, that it is universal, and rests upon a rule à priori. On the other hand, it is so far homogeneous with the phænomenon, inasmuch as time is contained in every empirical representation of the manifold. Thus an application of the category to phænomena becomes possible, by means of the transcendental determination of time, which, as the schema of the conceptions of the understanding, mediates the subsumption of the latter under the former."

Three things in this leave me stuck, and I suspect they are connected. First, homogeneity: in the plate-and-circle case it looks like a matter of shared content, the concept containing what the intuition presents. But when Kant explains why a transcendental determination of time is homogeneous with the category, he appeals instead to the schema's being universal and resting on a rule. That is not the relation he set up a page earlier, and I cannot tell whether "homogeneous" is being stretched to cover two different relations, or whether the second is meant to be the general form of the first. Second, if the need for a third thing follows from the very idea of subsumption, the same need should arise for the schema itself: something must make the schema homogeneous with the category, and so on. Why does the regress stop where it does, and does the answer depend on treating the schema as a rule of application rather than as one more representation? Third, the chapter goes on to say that after the deduction of the categories nobody should hesitate over whether the categories may be employed beyond experience, which makes it sound as though everything has already been settled. If the deduction has already established that the categories are conditions of possible experience, what work is this "third thing" doing, and does the need for it show that the deduction proved only that we must think objects under the categories rather than that intuitions are subsumable under them? If so, I want to know what that does to the conclusion the deduction was supposed to deliver.

What I need from you is a reading of the passage that holds these three points together: what exactly "homogeneous" is doing in each of its occurrences, why the demand for mediation does not simply regenerate itself, and how the schematism stands to the deduction, whether as an addition to it, a presupposition of it, or a restriction of its result. Please also say where your reading is most likely to fail, and what would change your mind about it: which textual detail, or which interpretive commitment about how the Deduction is to be read, would push you to a different verdict, so that I can see what the reading is resting on rather than taking it as settled. Write it as continuous prose, without headings, numbered points, bold text, or tables, the way you would set it out in a letter to a colleague.
