I spent thirty years as a geochemist before retiring, and I have spent the years since reading philosophy on my own — more enthusiastically than systematically. I keep meeting a case where an institution's settled practice seems to commit it to a substantive view about identity, and I would like you to reconstruct that commitment for me.

Consider how museums, conservators, and the art market treat works of art as objects that persist. A painting is lined, its canvas replaced, its losses filled and inpainted, its discolored varnish removed; a marble figure loses an arm and is given a new one; a bronze cast from the artist's own mold is sold and displayed as the artist's work. In each case the institution carries on as though it still has the very same work: one catalogue entry, one attribution, one valuation, one object of care. Conservators even work under norms of minimal intervention and reversibility, which would make little sense unless they took the work to be something that survives its present material state. Yet the same institutions treat a well-made replica as not the work at all, even where no one can tell the two apart by inspection; they label reconstructions as reconstructions and will not insure them as the original. Heritage practice adds a third wrinkle: a building gutted and rebuilt after a fire is frequently still listed, and spoken of, as the same building, though none of its fabric survives.

What theory of material constitution and identity across change does this cluster of practices presuppose? Which parts of it strictly require that the work not be identical with the stuff that currently composes it, and which parts merely sit comfortably with that? Is the practice committed to the work being a form, a type rather than a particular, an abstract object, or a historical individual whose origin rather than whose appearance fixes its identity — or is it compatible with several of these? Does it rule anything out? I am especially puzzled by whether the two halves of the practice pull in opposite directions, since the first half tolerates drastic replacement of matter while the second half refuses to accept an indistinguishable substitute. Tell me too where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, so that rival theories would license it equally, and what would have to change in museums and in the market if we replaced the presupposed view with the thesis that a work is identical with its material parts, or with the thesis that two things are the same work whenever they are perceptually indistinguishable.
