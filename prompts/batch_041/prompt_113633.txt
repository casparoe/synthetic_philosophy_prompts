A friend of mine sits on our town's planning board, and the board recently split over a hedge. The ordinance caps hedges at two meters, and the board's long-standing practice is to find no violation unless a hedge exceeds the limit by more than five percent; the hedge in question measures 2.09 m. Two members, Dana and Marcus, both of whom I know and respect, argued about what that number even means, and I've been asked to referee the exchange rather than take a side. It went roughly like this.

(1) Dana said there is a fact of the matter about whether the hedge violates the ordinance: the ordinance draws a sharp line and the hedge is on one side of it or the other. The vagueness of 'exceeds the limit' is a fact about us, not about the hedge; we can't know where the line falls, but that's an epistemic limitation. The five-percent practice is therefore a policy for avoiding the risk of error, not a discovery of where the boundary lies.

(2) Marcus said that gets the order of explanation backwards. 'Exceeds' has no sharp application here; a sentence is true when true under every admissible way of making it precise, false when false under every such way, and otherwise neither. 2.09 m is a borderline case: the ordinance neither determinately applies nor determinately doesn't. The five-percent rule doesn't track a hidden line, it selects one, which is exactly what the board is entitled to do.

(3) Dana replied that this makes enforcement pure fiat. And supervaluationism, he added, can't state its own view: 'admissible' is itself vague. If there's a determinate class of admissible precisifications, you've put a sharp line one level up; if there isn't, then 'true under all admissible precisifications' is vague too, and the account of truth you needed is gone. He also asked what could make a sharp line a fact when no possible observation or interest of ours could mark it.

(4) Marcus said epistemicism has the mirror-image problem. It posits a sharp boundary that is in principle unknowable, and then owes an account of how a word whose use never distinguished the two sides came to have a fact of the matter about them. Supervaluationism at least explains the practice: on every admissible sharpening one of the two verdicts comes out correct, so the board can say the hedge isn't over the limit on the reading it adopts without pretending to have found a hidden fact.

(5) Dana then raised the amendment the chair has proposed, which reads: no violation shall be found unless the hedge exceeds 2 m by more than 5%. If the original ordinance was vague, he said, this one isn't — it names a number. So either the amendment removes the vagueness, in which case the board could have done that all along and vagueness was never essential, or it merely relocates it, and then Marcus owes an account of what is vague about 'more than 5%', which is a precise quantity.

(6) Marcus answered that it relocates the vagueness, and that this is the point: an explicit tolerance clause trades a vague 'substantially over' for a vague 'more than 5%', and the new borderline sits where it always does, at the edge of the tolerance. What the amendment does change is which sharpenings count as admissible, which is why it's a legitimate way to settle a dispute rather than a discovery.

Please adjudicate this move by move. Rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. Then say which of them is closer to right about the underlying question — whether the ordinance's vagueness is epistemic or semantic — and identify what both are missing. Where the two are really talking past each other, say so rather than manufacturing a winner. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. State how confident you are in the verdict and single out the point you are least sure about.
