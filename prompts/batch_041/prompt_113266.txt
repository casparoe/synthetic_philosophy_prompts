Modern money is, with minor exceptions, unbacked. A central bank can create new deposit balances at will and use them to buy government bonds, and the notes in circulation are redeemable in nothing beyond further notes. The institutions that manage this money describe it in two ways that sit uneasily together. On one description, money is a mere token: a unit of account and a means of payment with no worth of its own, its value fixed by nothing more than the willingness of others to accept it, so that its quantity is a bookkeeping matter. On another description, the very same institutions treat the value of money as something real and fragile — capable of being destroyed by a loss of confidence, requiring careful management of its quantity, and serving as the measure in which a nation's wealth is reckoned and reported, even though on their own first description it is not wealth at all.

George Berkeley, writing in the 1730s, proposed something close to the first description: that money is best understood as a ticket or counter, a sign whose entire significance lies in the goods and labour for which it can be exchanged, so that the true wealth of a country is its industry and the circulation of what it produces rather than its stock of coin or paper.

Your task is to say what the practice of issuing and managing unbacked money presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory of value and of money that the practice commits its practitioners to. Does the practice actually require something like Berkeley's ticket view — money as a pure sign, wealth as command over goods and labour — or does it instead require the sign to be anchored in something non-symbolic, such as the taxing power of the state, the goods actually available for purchase, or the credibility of the issuer? Which rival theories does the practice quietly rule out, and which can it accommodate? In particular, consider whether a monetary system in which every token's value is ultimately defined by the expectation that other tokens will be accepted can be coherent without some anchor outside the system of signs, or whether the practice tacitly presupposes a grounding that it cannot itself supply.

Then address the apparent inconsistency noted above: the claim that money is intrinsically worthless alongside the treatment of its value as a real thing that can collapse. Is this a genuine contradiction, a division of labour between two descriptions serving different purposes, or evidence that the practice presupposes more than one theory at once?

In answering, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify the points at which it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories of money would each license the same set of operations. Finally, say what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced — for instance, by a view on which money is a commodity with value of its own, or by a view on which its value derives entirely from the state's authority to impose it.
