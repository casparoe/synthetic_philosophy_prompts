A colleague of mine has, over the years, given me a remarkably consistent set of verdicts about what one ought to do upon finding oneself in disagreement with someone one regards as an equal, and I have been cataloguing them. I want to know what she will say about a case that has just arisen.

Her verdicts so far. When she and a peer split a restaurant bill, work the same arithmetic over the same evidence, and arrive at different totals, she says the matter is genuinely unsettled: each of them should move roughly halfway toward the other until someone locates the error. When the same thing happens with a colleague who has slipped twice in the last ten such calculations while she has slipped not at all, she keeps her own total and marks her confidence down only slightly, in proportion to what the record does to the odds. When the person who disagrees has never done this kind of calculation, nothing in her own thinking changes; she will recheck, but not on his account. When she has read a source that the other person has not, she treats the disagreement as not yet real: she owes him the source, and until he has it her own view stands. When she and a peer disagree about whether a proof is valid, and she has derived it twice while he reports that he cannot follow the fourth step, her verdict on the proof is untouched; the disagreement is evidence about her exposition, not about the theorem. When a peer who is on the whole more accurate than she is — better at statistics, worse at judging whether a source can be trusted — disagrees with her about whether a particular source can be trusted, she gives his dissent almost no weight, on the ground that the comparison that matters is reliability on this kind of question, not overall standing. And when five people disagree with her but all five formed their view from the same blog post, she counts the apparent majority as a single opinion.

The new case. She and a peer are given the same data and the same question, and come out on opposite sides. She has worked the problem three ways and reached the same answer each time. He reports the opposite conclusion by a route she cannot check — a long computation, or a body of technique outside her competence — and she can neither find the error in it nor confirm it. Neither of them has evidence the other lacks, and their records on questions of this kind are indistinguishable.

What will she say she ought to do? Set out the rules that fit her earlier verdicts — the equal-weight idea that a genuine disagreement between equals over shared evidence should move her about halfway; a steadfastness on which she keeps her answer whenever she has handled the reasons properly, while lowering her confidence that she is the one who is right; a rule that weights the disagreement by how independent his route to the answer is from her own; a division-of-labour rule on which she should defer precisely where the route runs through a competence she does not have — and say what each predicts about the new case, give me your best prediction of what she will actually say with the reasons for preferring it, and name the single further case that would most sharply separate the candidate rules. Her verdict about question-specific reliability seems to cut the other way here, since his route passes through a competence she cannot assess; say whether that pushes her toward deference rather than steadfastness, and whether that is consistent with everything else she has told me.
