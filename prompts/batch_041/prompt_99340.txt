I retired from physics a few years back and have been mainlining philosophy ever since — logic and philosophy of maths mostly, since that's the part my brain still has the muscles for. There's one move I keep running into that I can't decide whether it's a genuine insight or a bit of a shell game, and I'd like you to take it apart.

Here's the move. People sympathetic to intuitionism — Brouwer, Heyting, drop the law of excluded middle, proofs as constructions, the whole program — keep saying that intuitionistic logic stands to classical logic the way non-Euclidean geometry stands to Euclid. You take the parallel postulate away, and instead of collapsing you get a whole family of consistent geometries, and nobody goes around saying the hyperbolic plane is simply false or that Lobachevsky was doing pseudo-geometry. So, the argument runs, take excluded middle away and you get perfectly respectable logics; the classicist who insists there's only one Logic is in the position of the guy who thought only Euclidean geometry was real. I've seen this used to make intuitionism sound like it's already won on points, and I've also seen it flipped around and used to defuse the whole dispute — as if choosing your logic is like choosing your coordinate system, a matter of convenience, and nobody's more right than anybody else. I don't trust either use and I cant tell if the analogy is doing the work or just decorating it.

So: lay out the mapping properly. What plays the role of the parallel postulate, of the other axioms, of the models, of "true of physical space"? A table of correspondences is fine. Then tell me where it breaks. Some things I already suspect matter but don't know what to do with: geometry is *about* something, whereas logic is supposed to be topic-neutral, so does the analogy quietly assume logic has a subject matter it can be right or wrong about? And the intuitionist isn't just saying excluded middle is optional — he's saying the classicist's sentences don't even have the meaning he thinks they do, which sounds like a much stronger and stranger claim than "different axiom set, different structure." Also, I've heard that classical logic can be embedded in intuitionistic logic by some double-negation trick, and I believe Euclidean geometry can be modelled inside hyperbolic geometry — if those two facts line up, does that strengthen the analogy or undercut it?

And then the part I actually care about: what does the geometry picture smuggle in that the logic case doesn't have? For instance, does anything in the geometry support the "no one is more right, it's just taste" conclusion, or is that conclusion riding entirely on the analogy and not on the logic at all? Give me a verdict on whether that relaxed conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the analogy can't carry it, say what a better one would be — or whether there's no good analogy here and people should just argue it out on the merits.
