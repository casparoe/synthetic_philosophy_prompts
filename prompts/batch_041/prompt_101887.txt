I'm putting togehter a scoring rubric that grades how genuinely joint an interaction is — call it a jointness score between 0 and 1 — and I want to run it on borderline cases: two people wordlessly carrying a sofa, a pair of traders who each follow the same public signal, a human and an AI assistant co-writing a document, a crowd that surges through a gate together.

Act as a philosopher of action and social ontology who's spent real time on shared intention, we-intentions, and joint commitment. Think carefully and start by brainstorming, in a few lines, what a jointness score is even supposed to be measuring and what tends to go wrong when people try to reduce this to one criterion. Then do the actual work.

Here are the candidate criteria I've collected, in no particular order:

1. Mutual responsiveness — each party's contribution is conditioned on what the other is actually doing, in real time.
2. Shared goal representation — each party has an intention whose content is the joint activity itself, not just their own part in it.
3. Commitment — each party is bound to the other in a way that makes abandoning the joint activity wrong the other and call for excuse or apology.
4. Common knowledge — the responsiveness and the shared goal are out in the open, each knows the other knows, and so on.
5. Subplan meshing — the parties' individual plans fit together at the level of means, not just ends.
6. Repair readiness — when the joint plan breaks, the parties renegotiate or improvise together instead of each quietly sliding into their own private fallback.
7. Stake asymmetry tolerance — each party will absorb more than their fair share of the cost to keep the joint activity going, up to some limit.
8. Emergent lock-in — the pattern persists because each party's best response to the others keeps it going, with no representation of the joint goal required at all.

Now:
(a) Rank these eight from most to least load-bearing for a jointness score, and say in a sentence or two why each lands where it does.
(b) Give each a weight so the whole set sums to 1.0.
(c) Apply your weights to these three cases and give me a number for each: (i) two strangers each independently choosing to walk through the same door at the same moment because it's the only exit; (ii) a long-married couple cooking dinner where each just does their usual half without any discussion; (iii) two firms in a price-fixing arrangement that both keep to because each fears the other will defect.
(d) Tell me which single criterion, if I deleted it, would move the scores the most, and whether that's a reason to keep it or a sign it's doing illegitimate work.

Be opinionated and take a position. I care way more about the reasoning than the numbers, and I'd rather you flag a criterion as confused or doing no work than force it into the ranking. Also tell me if I'm missing a criterion that should definately be in there.
