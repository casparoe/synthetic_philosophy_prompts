Write an essay on the following controversy in the theory of convention. On David Lewis's account, a convention is a regularity in behaviour that holds because each party expects the others to conform — and knows that they expect the same of everyone — and prefers to conform on condition that the others do. On this picture the authority of a convention appears purely instrumental: one ought to conform because conforming is the best way to get what one wants from others, and only for so long as it is. Yet the conventions we live by are not merely predictable; breaches of them are resented and criticised, and the criticism does not sound like advice about prudence. Evolutionary and game-theoretic stories about how norms emerge sharpen the difficulty rather than ease it, for they explain why a regularity would become stable without saying what is owed to those who have come to rely on it. Weigh the considerations on both sides and reach a verdict: is the normative 'ought' of a convention reducible to the instrumental 'ought' of someone who expects others to conform, or does it require something further? In defending your verdict, address at least these three matters: whether the arbitrariness of a convention — that either side of the road would have done — tells against its having genuine normative weight; whether the prior conformity of others gives one a reason to conform that is not merely the reason of continuing coordination; and whether a regularity that holds only because breaches are sanctioned is a convention at all, or something else wearing the name. Argue in your own terms; no survey of the literature or list of references is wanted.
