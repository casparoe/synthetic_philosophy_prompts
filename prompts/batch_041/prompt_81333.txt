I'm a novelist — literary fiction, three books, the last one about a family that keeps rewriting its own history — and I've ended up in an argument with a friend who reads a lot of philosophy. I need someone to referee it, and the ref work is basically translation.

Here's the thing we're arguing about. There's an old perception demo where you flash a colored dot on the left and a differently colored dot on the right, less then a second apart, and people swear they saw a single dot travel across the gap and change color partway through — it starts green on the left, say, and it's red by the time it lands on the right. The wierd part is that the only place the red information comes from is the second flash, but subjects see the red arrive mid-flight, i.e. before the second dot has even appeared. So the brain has apparently used the ending to write the middle. There are two ways to tell that story. Either the brain takes in both flashes, sorts everything out, and only then hands you the finished version — in which case the "mistake" was never actually experienced, it just got settled offstage. Or the brain really did hand you the first version, and then went back and quietly rewrote the memory, so that what you recall having seen isn't what you saw. Dennett calls these the Stalinesque and Orwellian options — show trials where the verdict is fixed before the public sees anything, versus the Ministry of Truth quietly editing old newspaper back issues — and I always mix up which name goes with which. His point, the way I understand it, is that you can't possibly tell which one happened, and that wanting to know is itself a sign you're still secretly picturing a little theater in the head where the finished show gets played to an audience of one.

So here's my problem, and it's the same problem I have when I revise. Draft one, my character walks into a room and the window is shut. Draft four, I need it open. Two ways to fix it. I can rewrite the earlier scene, so the window was always open and no version with a shut window survives into the book. Or I can leave the scene and add, forty pages later, a line where she remembers the window being open, and the reader never spots the seam. Either way the published text is identical, no reader on earth can tell, and yet I feel like there's a real difference, because I know which one I did.

What I want from you: translate this whole debate into the vocabulary I actually work in. Story time versus telling time, drafts and revisions, retconning, continuity, unreliable narration, the order of composition versus the order of the text, what's in the manuscript versus what's in the reader's head, omniscient versus limited — whatever genuinely maps. Do it as faithfully as you can, and don't just spray fiction words over the philosophy to make it sound pretty; if a piece of the argument has to get distorted to fit my vocabulary, I want to see the distortion. Then the part I care about most: the residue report. What in the philosophers' argument has no counterpart in how novels work at all? And where something doesn't translate, is that because my vocabulary is missing something, or because they're chasing a fact that isn't there — insisting there must have been a moment when the "real" version existed, when the only thing that ever exists is the finished book? If that last move is too glib, push back and tell me why. I know there's one obvious asymmetry (I have drafts, and the brain doesn't have a me standing outside it, red pen in hand) and I'd like to know how much work it's doing.

American English, please. Headers are fine, I'm not picky about formatting.
