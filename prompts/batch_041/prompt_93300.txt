I'm preparing for a qualifying exam in the philosophy of computing, and I want to stress-test a popular summary of computational complexity that I suspect is unreliable. A well-regarded science writer put it to me this way:

"The P versus NP problem is the deepest open question in computer science. P is the class of problems a computer can solve quickly, and NP is the class whose proposed solutions can be checked quickly. No one knows whether the two are equal, though nearly everyone believes they are not. What gives the problem its weight is NP-completeness: Cook and Levin showed that thousands of natural problems -- scheduling, protein folding, breaking codes, proving theorems -- are really a single problem in disguise, so a fast method for any one of them would instantly yield a fast method for all the rest. That is why complexity theorists call these problems intractable: an NP-complete problem simply has no efficient algorithm, and the only way to solve one is to search through the possibilities. The philosophical payoff is that the world contains a fundamental asymmetry between finding and checking -- one we could never hope to prove, since Baker and Gill showed in the 1970s that the question lies beyond the ordinary methods of mathematics."

I don't trust this, but I can't locate the problem. Please work through it claim by claim, distinguishing what a complexity theorist would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification, and what is genuinely mistaken; then say whether the closing claim about a fundamental, unprovable asymmetry follows from anything true in the summary. Tell me how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the point you are least sure of.
