I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about behavioral criteria for mentality — not the canonical objections, which I can recite in my sleep, but the verdicts I catch myself giving on particular cases. I'm meant to lead a session on this at our work-in-progress group next month, so I'd like to arrive with something more defensible than a pile of intuitions. Here is my scoring rule and the cases I've been running through it. A candidate passes when a competent interrogator, restricted to text, cannot reliably pick it out as the non-human member of the pair. A lookup table that contains, for every exchange the interrogator might initiate within the allotted time, a canned but human-sounding reply: it passes, and I'm not comfortable with what that implies. A system that passes only because the judge is careless or hurried: it passes, but its passing is evidence of nothing — and I want to say the same about a machine judge that cannot tell a human from a machine, though I can't articulate why the species of the judge should matter when all that is being measured is discriminative competence. A system that passes five minutes and would plainly collapse at five hours: it passes, and I do think that is evidence of something, though I can't say what the five minutes bought me that five hours would have destroyed. A system that survives by charm and evasion — deflecting, joking, refusing to answer, which is of course what a person might well do: I want to say it fails, and I can't give a principled reason, since being unhelpful isn't a failure of mindedness. A system that answers fluently and then, asked outright, truthfully says it is a machine: it fails the test as standardly run, and yet I would rank it above the evasive one. And a human who is picked as the machine — the non-native speaker, the pedant, the one who insists 'I'm a person' and is disbelieved: this tells me about the test's error rate and nothing about their minds.

The principle I keep reaching for is that a candidate has a mind just in case no interrogation, however long or ingenious, would expose it — indistinguishability in principle rather than in practice. That accommodates the misidentified human, since the error is the judge's and a better interrogation would not have made it, and it explains why the careless-judge pass leaves me cold. But it hands a mind to the lookup table, which I take to be absurd, and it ranks the honest confessor and the charming evader together, which I don't. So: is there a single criterion that captures all six verdicts, or must some go? If some must go, which, and what does the survivor predict for a case I haven't listed — a system that passes any single session but, across sessions, presents as a different person each time, with no continuity and no memory of the previous ones? I would much rather be told that the way I've set this up is confused, or that no principle can do the work I'm asking of it, than be handed a tidy criterion that quietly drops a verdict I care about.
