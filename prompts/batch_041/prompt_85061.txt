I am designing a single sixty-minute session for an adult-education series on medieval Jewish thought. The topic is Maimonides' treatment, in Part III, chapter 32 of the Guide of the Perplexed, of the reasons for the sacrificial commandments: his suggestion that sacrifice was not commanded because God required it, but because it was the mode of worship to which the Israelites and their neighbours were already habituated, so that the Law redirected a practice it could not have abolished outright. My attendees have not read the Guide; some are attached to the commandments and some are not; nearly all arrive with the prior that a thinker who supplies reasons for the commandments is well on the way to discarding them. Do not explain the thesis to me. Plan how I should teach it.

Give me the order in which the components must be introduced and why the dependencies run as they do — what has to be in place before the thesis can be stated at all without sounding like a debunking, and where the question of the commandments' permanence belongs in the sequence. I want one carrying example, and I want to know what it must have and what it must lack; my own candidates (a parent weaning a child from a habit; a physician who tolerates a patient's vice) look to me like failures, and I want to know whether they are, and why. I propose to hang the session on a distinction between a law's justification and its occasion — why worship of this form, for these people, at that time — and I want that distinction to bear the weight, since I take the central confusion to be the collapse of the two. Tell me whether the spine will hold.

Then map the misconceptions in the order in which they will surface, and say precisely where in the sequence each is to be headed off: that the thesis makes the sacrificial cult a divine concession to weakness; that it makes the commandments historically contingent and so collides with their permanence; that exposing a practice's origin in the surrounding culture debunks it; and that Maimonides must therefore have regarded the sacrificial form as dispensable. Note that he raises an objection of this shape himself, in the same chapter, and answers it — I want the room to feel the objection before they hear the answer. Finally, tell me which rival reading to put on the table so that the session ends with the objection rather than the thesis; what I should refuse to explain at this level, and say openly that I am refusing; and one closing question that would show whether they hold the justification/occasion distinction or can merely recite the thesis.
