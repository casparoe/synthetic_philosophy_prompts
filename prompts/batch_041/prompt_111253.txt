I've been defending the idea that political office can require someone to do a genuine wrong and to carry the guilt for it. A colleague has produced an argument meant to show that this idea proves far too much, and I can't find the flaw:

(1) An officeholder is sometimes required to do a wrong act, because refusing would foreseeably bring about a much greater harm.
(2) What makes the act required is that it averts a much greater harm; the act's wrongness is outweighed by that, but not erased, and this is why guilt is owed.
(3) For any act a person might do, there is some harm greater than the act itself that can be described as the harm its omission would leave standing — if not the harm the person had in view, then some slightly larger one they could have foreseen.
(4) So by (2), every act a person performs has its wrongness outweighed but not erased.
(5) So everyone is required to do wrong, and guilt is owed for everything they do.
(6) That is absurd: not every act is required, and guilt is not owed for everything.
(7) Therefore (1) or (2) is false, and there is no distinctively political dirty-hands predicament.

The case that got us here: a health minister, mid-outbreak, publicly denies a real but rare risk from a vaccine, knowing the denial is a lie to the public, because the truth would collapse uptake and let the outbreak run. I would like to keep (1), and I am attached to the thought that the minister is right to feel guilty; what I cannot tell is whether the trouble lies in the generalizing move or in what (2) says about wrongness being outweighed rather than erased. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked innocent, and what the repair costs.

Answer in four sections, in this order: "The step that breaks" (200–300 words); "Why it looked innocent" (150–250 words); "The price of rejecting it" (250–350 words), saying what happens to (1), to (2), and to the claim that guilt is owed; and "The best surviving version" (150–250 words), stating the most defensible version of the original idea and what it can no longer claim.
