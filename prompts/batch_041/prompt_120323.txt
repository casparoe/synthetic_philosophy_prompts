I am putting together a handout for a graduate epistemology seminar on the lottery and the preface paradox, and what I want is a decision tree that maps the logical space of responses, not a list of labels with summaries attached. Please construct the instrument as follows.

Build the tree from the space of possible positions, not from a roster of authors. Each branch node should be a question about what a position says — phrased so that a student who understands both paradoxes but has never heard a name for any of the views can answer it, and never phrased as a question about what someone would call themselves. Nodes I expect to be in play, in whatever order your argument for the tree's shape dictates: whether there is an attitude of outright acceptance over and above graded credence; whether the set of propositions one accepts must be consistent; whether acceptance is closed under conjunction and deduction; whether acceptance is governed by a threshold on credence or by a non-probabilistic role, such as being usable as a premise; whether any threshold varies with context, stakes, or the question under discussion; whether the agent's knowledge of how the lottery is set up counts as part of the accepted set; and whether the item under assessment is belief at all, rather than assertion or the norms governing testimony. For each split, add one sentence on why the tree branches there rather than somewhere else.

Render the result as a numbered outline with explicit answer branches and lettered leaves. Each leaf should be a position, labelled with the conventional name where one exists and a descriptive label where none does. Do not attribute views to individuals, and include no citations, reading lists, or references of any kind — the handout has to work without a library. For each leaf give: the sequence of answers that reaches it; what it implies about the thousand-ticket lottery; what it implies about the book whose preface concedes that one of its claims is false; the case or question that most clearly separates it from its nearest neighbour on the tree; and, in one sentence, its most awkward commitment.

A few constraints on coverage. The tree must be able to reach at least these positions: (i) there are no outright beliefs, only credences, so the paradox dissolves; (ii) acceptance is threshold-governed and the threshold is fixed; (iii) the threshold is context-sensitive; (iv) acceptance requires consistency but not closure, so one may accept each of the lottery propositions while declining the conjunction; (v) acceptance requires both consistency and closure, so the correct response is to accept none of the individual lottery propositions (or only the disjunction that one ticket wins); (vi) rational acceptance may itself be inconsistent, so the paradox is a genuine result about rational inconsistency; (vii) only certainty, in the sense of credence one, licenses acceptance; (viii) the paradox concerns assertion or what one may offer as testimony rather than belief; (ix) something about the preface makes it different in kind from the lottery, so the two are not instances of a single problem. If you judge that one of these is not a distinct position, say so and merge it instead of padding the tree.

Three further requirements. First, use both a large-scale case (a thousand tickets; a long book with one claim per page) and a small-scale calibration case (say, five tickets; a five-sentence note conceding that one of its sentences is false), since the small case is where a threshold does visible work. Second, wherever the tree generates a combination of answers that no position occupies, mark it unoccupied and say in a clause whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. Third, where two leaves cannot be separated by any node in the tree, flag the pair explicitly rather than pretending the tree distinguishes them — in particular, tell me whether your tree separates the fixed-threshold view from the context-sensitive one, and whether it separates the premise-role account of acceptance from the threshold account.

Keep the instrument to roughly ten to twelve branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves, and close with a short paragraph identifying the single question in it that would move a student furthest across the tree.
