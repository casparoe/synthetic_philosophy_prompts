I run a fortnightly ethics reading group and we're doing population stuff this term — whether more people is better, pronatalism, that whole area. For the first session I want to open with a sorting exercise rather than a paper, so people find out what they actually think before they find out what the views are called.

Can you build me a decision tree? Start from something anyone can answer — say, 'you could add a billion people whose lives are just about worth living; does that make things better, worse, or neither?' — and let each branch be the claim that actually splits the positions apart. I don't want a glossery of -isms with arrows drawn between them. Each node has to be a real question about a case or a commitment, answerable by someone who's never heard of any of these positions, so it sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by the label they'd pick. Build it from the questions outwards: work out what combinations of answers are even possible first, then say which combinations correspond to views people actually hold.

Please make sure these all turn up as leaves somewhere: the view that more happy people is straightforwardly better; the view that only average welfare matters; the view that adding lives is good but only above some quality theshold; the view that adding a happy person is not in itself any better at all — I suspect this one is the real crux and I want the tree to make it visible rather than smuggle it in; the view that coming into existence is a harm; and a properly pronatalist leaf, one that thinks more people is good for reasons that aren't about anyone's welfare — duty to the species, natality as a value, whatever it turns out to be. Give the leaves their standard name where one exists and write 'unnamed' where it doesn't.

Use concrete cases as far as possible: a baby bonus funded by a tax that leaves every existing person slightly worse off; a world that's larger and less happy on average but where every single person in it is better off than their counterpart in the smaller world; two worlds with the same total welfare but different numbers of people. Flag any pair of leaves the tree genuinely can't separate, and note which combinations nobody occupies — and whether that's because the view is incoherent, or just because nobody's tried it.

British English please, and keep the whole thing under about 900 words.
