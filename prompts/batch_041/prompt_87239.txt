I've been roped into giving the keynote at our county historical society's 150th anniversary dinner next month, and I'm stuck. The room will be mostly old families and amateur geneologists who've spent decades tending the cemetery records and the one-room schoolhouse museum, plus a handful of actual historians, the board who just paid for a bronze restoration of the founder's statue, and about a dozen high schoolers who won the local history essay contest. The founder's great-great-granddaughter is on the board and she's the one who organized the restoration, which is part of my problem. While I was going through a box of unprocessed ledgers for my own talk, I found evidence that the founder's original fortune came from a milling operation that depended on indentured labor, and that the woman whose diary the society acquired last year—the one everyone calls the town's first schoolteacher—was written out of the official town history by her brother-in-law after she backed a strike. I don't want to ambush the room, but I also don't want to stand up there and do the usual smooth anniversary thing. I'm not a Nietzsche scholar—I read his essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life in a seminar years ago and it's been rattling around my head—but I want the speech to actually work through the monumental, antiquarian, and critical modes rather than just name-drop them.

Can you write me a ten-minute speech? It should be warm enough that the genealogists don't feel mocked, serious enough that the historians don't feel it's a Hallmark card, and honest enough that I don't have to pretend the founder was a saint. The three modes should do real structural work: show what each one can give this room and what it costs, maybe use the unhistorical/suprahistorical distinction as a pressure valve if it fits, and make the tension between piety toward the past and the need to break with it the spine of the thing. What would it mean for this society to have enough plastic power to absorb all this without either romanticizing or self-flagellating? End with a concrete, non-preachy proposal for what the society might do with the statue plaque and the diary—something that doesn't resolve the hard question but gives people a way to keep arguing about it together. Avoid quoting Nietzsche directly, since I'll be speaking and don't want to misattribute anything; paraphrase is fine. Also, please don't make it a lecture about Nietzsche—the philosophy should be the load-bearing wall, not the furniture.
