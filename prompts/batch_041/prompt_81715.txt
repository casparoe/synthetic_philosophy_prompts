I'm an undergraduate in a seminar on the deep history of inequality, and one session — whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake — has produced the same standoff three times without anyone moving. I'd like to understand the disagreement rather than hear it rehearsed once more.

The standoff is roughly this. One side treats the transition to farming as a catastrophe: it points to skeletal evidence of smaller bodies, more dental decay and more markers of nutritional stress, together with the earliest archaeological signatures of institutional hierarchy and inherited property, and concludes that a large share of humanity traded a relatively secure and egalitarian existence for drudgery, disease and domination. The other side denies that there is a single event to evaluate: cultivation was adopted piecemeal and regionally, sometimes abandoned and later resumed, foraging and farming were mixed for millennia, and the real comparison is not forager versus farmer but a spread of intermediate subsistence strategies; on this view, calling the outcome a mistake presupposes an agent and an alternative that never existed. In discussion, the first side reads the second as evasion and the second reads the first as committing a category error.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict, and I'll put my own hypothesis up so that you have something to push against. My hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under a single heading, and that the crux is which counterfactual is in play: nearly all of the evidence is cross-sectional — foragers compared with early farmers — while the moral heat comes from a historical counterfactual about what would have befallen the particular populations that took up farming, which no cross-sectional comparison can settle. I also suspect that affluence is doing double duty in these arguments, meaning in one usage that wants were modest and easily satisfied and in another that means were abundant, and that the two readings do not pick out the same people or the same century.

Please tell me whether that diagnosis holds up. If it does, say which parts of the disagreement survive once the two questions are separated, and whether the remainder is empirical (and what evidence could move it), conceptual, or evaluative. If it is wrong or half-right, correct it rather than accommodating it. Two further suspicions you should test rather than assume: whether the skeletal evidence can bear the weight the first side puts on it at all, given that a skeleton showing stress is the skeleton of someone who survived long enough to show it, and whether the comparison class is contaminated, since the foragers anthropologists actually describe often occupy marginal environments and post-contact conditions rather than anything resembling the Palaeolithic.

Finally, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a change in the unit of analysis, a body of evidence, or nothing — that is, whether this is a hard question or an ill-posed one, and whether a seminar that keeps arguing about it is arguing about something. Give me a diagnosis you are willing to commit to rather than a survey with a hedge at the end, mark clearly which parts of it you take to be well supported and which are speculative, and if the way I have posed the question makes a decent answer impossible, say so plainly instead of working around it.
