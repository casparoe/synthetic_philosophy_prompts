I run the monthly lunch-and-learn at a mid-sized applied-AI company, and next month the topic that has fallen to me is the one everyone has been circling for a year without quite naming: whether a large language model understands what it is doing. The room will be about forty people — machine-learning engineers, a couple of product managers, two researchers who build evaluations — almost none of whom have read any philosophy, and they arrive split into two camps that talk past each other. The deflationists say it is only next-token prediction and there is nothing to discuss; the inflationists hold up a chain-of-thought transcript and say look, it reasons. My plan is to spend the forty-five minutes, at a whiteboard, not on the model question at all but on the pair of conceptual inversions that I think both camps are assuming without noticing: the Darwinian inversion, in which competence and design turn out to be prior to any comprehender who could have intended them, and the Turing inversion, in which observable behaviour is taken as the criterion for a mental attribute rather than as the report of an inner cause that produces it. If the session works, nobody in the room should be able to go back to the question they walked in with.

Please do not write the talk for me, and do not simply explain the two inversions to me — I can read. What I need is the teaching plan behind the explanation: the order the components have to be introduced in and why, so that each step is doing work the previous one made possible rather than being asserted; the single case or example that should carry the whole session, described in terms of what it must have and what it must conspicuously lack; and the point in the sequence at which the two inversions should be brought into contact, since I take it that half the difficulty is that one is a claim about origins and the other a claim about criteria, and a room that conflates them will leave thinking the pair adds up to behaviourism. I keep reaching for the eye for the Darwinian half and the Turing test for the Turing half, and I have a nagging feeling that both are traps with this particular audience — tell me whether they are, and if so what should replace them.

Then the misconceptions, which I suspect fall into two families: the ones they walk in with, and the ones my own framing will manufacture. I can guess at the first family — that the whole idea means the process is random or informationless, that it licenses the thought that anything goes, that the Darwinian half is a claim about individual organisms rather than about lineages, that the Turing half is a definition of intelligence rather than a redescription of the question being asked — but I want the full map, with each misconception tied to the precise moment in the sequence where I should head it off rather than merely correct it later. For the second family, I want to end by pulling apart two claims that the phrase tends to fuse, namely that the process which produced the competence required no comprehension anywhere in its history, and that the competent system itself comprehends nothing, and I would like to know whether these come apart and whether the room will notice if I let them slide together. Finally, give me one question I can put on the whiteboard at the very end that would separate someone who has actually internalised the inversion from someone who has merely learned to repeat the phrase, and tell me plainly what I should refuse to settle at this level, including whether the model question itself is one of those things.
