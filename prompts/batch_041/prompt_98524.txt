A friend and I spent an evening arguing about the International Astronomical Union's 2006 decision to demote Pluto, and we ended up disagreeing not about the solar system but about what the vote had even done: he insisted the IAU had discovered the truth about what a planet is, while I insisted it had merely rewritten a word. I would like you to analyze the episode through the lens of two-dimensional semantics. Take the pre-2006 use of "planet" as your target and lay out its primary (epistemic) intension and its secondary (subjunctive) intension — what the term would have picked out given how things might have turned out, and what it picks out once actual-world reference is fixed. Then say whether the term has the profile of a natural-kind term with a hidden essence, on the model of "water" or "gold," or whether its content is exhausted by the descriptive criteria we apply, as with a functional or conventional term. Use that to adjudicate the dispute: is the IAU's move — adding the requirement that a body must have cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit — closer to the nineteenth-century discovery that jade is really two distinct minerals, or closer to a committee stipulating a new sense for an old word? Finally, tell me honestly where two-dimensional semantics falls silent here — in particular, whether it can by itself determine which of these two readings of "planet" is correct, or whether it merely presupposes the answer and then formalizes whichever one you hand it.
