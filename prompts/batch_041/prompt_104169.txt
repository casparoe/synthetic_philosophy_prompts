I'm a novelist who each spring teaches a single session in an MFA program's elective on fiction and public life: fourteen writers, one room, two hours. Design an activity that makes them feel the force of the requirement that citizens justify their political positions with reasons their fellow citizens could accept — and feel it as writers, as the predicament of a character who has to argue in public for what she actually believes. The engine should be an admissibility rule enforced live in the room by a participant rather than by me, so that the cost of the rule falls visibly on some people and not others; build the roles so that burden doesn't fall only on the conventionally religious, and give each role a stated position, a real grounding its holder would rather not reveal, and a stake in the outcome. Give me the roles, the rule and its enforcement, a scoring scheme that needs no money, a two-hour timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the distinctions I most want them to feel: between a reason others can understand and a reason they could accept, between sincerity and admissibility in public, and between the matters the requirement is meant to bind and ordinary policy where it isn't. Neither the phrase public reason nor Rawls's name should come up until the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words. Open with the activity's central conceit in one sentence, then give the design, with no preamble and no restating of my request.
