I write a column on philosophy and the sciences, and I am assembling a dialogue for a forthcoming piece on how we decide what is genuinely possible. I need two speakers, and I want them evenly matched.

The first is a modal rationalist: she holds that when a proposition seems genuinely conceivable after careful reflection on the concepts it involves — entertainable without contradiction, not merely picturable — that is real, if defeasible, evidence that the proposition is metaphysically possible. The second is a modal empiricist: he holds that conceivability tells us only about the structure of our concepts and the reach of our imagination, and that a single well-founded claim of impossibility should always outweigh any amount of conceivability.

Give them one concrete dispute, and make it genuinely hard rather than a textbook illustration. The case is this: a world whose objects are arranged so that identity is not transitive — a world in which some object a is the very same object as b, b is the very same object as c, and yet a is not the very same object as c. You may stipulate whatever else the case needs in order to be made precise. The rationalist takes the case to be conceivable and therefore a genuine possibility, which would make identity — and with it a piece of logic — contingent on how the world happens to be arranged. The empiricist takes the apparent conceivability to be an artifact: she is not conceiving non-transitive identity at all, but a chain of similarity or composition relations misdescribed in the vocabulary of identity, and once the description is made honest the apparent possibility evaporates.

Have them argue it through. What distinguishes a genuine act of conceiving from a description that merely wields the words without a coherent content — and can the empiricist state a test for the difference that does not also impugn the conceivability claims he wants to keep? What would it take for the rationalist to abandon her claim, short of an outright contradiction — and if nothing would, is she doing metaphysics or only reporting the contours of her own mind? I also want at least one exchange on a case that loops back on both of them: whether either can conceive of a world in which conceivability is systematically misleading about what is possible, and what each takes that to show — the rationalist that it shows nothing, since a world that misleads conceivers is itself something we could only reach by conceiving, or the empiricist that it is a live option we have no principled way to rule out.

Let each land real hits, and let the exchange actually move: each speaker should answer what the other just said rather than restating a position. Keep both in character as competent philosophers. Skip the literature — I don't need names or citations, I need the shape of the argument.
