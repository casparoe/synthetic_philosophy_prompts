The topic is the split game theory makes between iterated elimination of *strictly* dominated strategies and iterated elimination of *weakly* dominated strategies, specifically once you let mixed strategies do the dominating. The story you normally get is: strict is clean (order of deletion doesn't matter, and you never delete a strategy that's part of a Nash equilibrium), weak is a mess (order matters, equilibria can vanish), so weak is a rough-and-ready heuristic and strict is the real thing.

Here's a tiny game that makes the mixed-strategy half of the strict story look less innocent than it sounds. Row player's payoffs only, three columns X, Y, Z:

- A: 3, 0, 2
- B: 0, 3, 2
- C: 1, 1, 1

C isn't dominated by A or by B — A wins against X but loses against Y, and B is the other way round. But the fifty-fifty mix of A and B pays (1.5, 1.5, 2), which beats C in every single column, so C is strictly dominated the moment you let a mixed strategy be the dominator. So "strict dominance" with mixing is a genuinely stronger requirement than strict dominance with pure strategies, and the standard defense of it is epistemic: a strictly dominated strategy is never a best response to any belief you could hold about the other players.

Fine. But now look at the weak version, the disreputable cousin. If a strategy is weakly dominated, then it's also never a best response to any belief that puts positive probability on every one of the opponent's strategies. So the epistemic argument doesn't obviously draw the line where the textbooks draw it.

And here's the other little game, two rows, two columns, payoffs written as (row, column):

- T: (1,1) against L, (2,0) against R
- M: (1,1) against L, (0,0) against R

T weakly dominates M, and L strictly dominates R. Now run the deletion procedure. Delete R first and you're left with a single column where T and M are identical, so both survive: {T, M} x {L}. Delete M first (it's weakly dominated) and you end up at {T} x {L}. Same game, different answers, depending on the order you work in. Also notice that (M, L) is a Nash equilibrium and M is weakly dominated, so the weak-dominance procedure can delete an equilibrium of the original game — which is exactly the complaint people make about it.

So here's what I want the essay to be about: is the textbook line actually principled, or is "strict good, weak bad" just an artifact of which procedure happens to have nice mathematical properties (order-independence, equilibrium preservation)?
