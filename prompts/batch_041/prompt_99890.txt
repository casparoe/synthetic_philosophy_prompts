I am a teaching assistant on a second-year undergraduate philosophy of language module, and I am marking a formative essay. The question I set was: Explain the Frege–Geach problem for expressivism. In your answer, distinguish the general embedding problem—how moral sentences can appear in conditionals, negations, and other logical contexts—from the more specific negation problem, by which I mean the difficulty of explaining what an expressivist should say about a negated moral judgement such as the judgement that tormenting the cat is not wrong. Then assess whether a minimalist account of truth, on which calling a sentence true is a matter of endorsing or asserting it rather than describing a truth-making fact, is sufficient to solve either problem. You may illustrate with the inference: if tormenting the cat is wrong, then getting your sister to torment the cat is wrong; tormenting the cat is wrong; so getting your sister to torment the cat is wrong.

The student’s answer reads as follows. Expressivism is the view that moral sentences do not describe facts; they express the speaker’s attitudes. The Frege–Geach problem is that this view cannot explain how moral sentences behave in logically complex contexts. Take the inference: if tormenting the cat is wrong, then getting your sister to torment the cat is wrong; tormenting the cat is wrong; so getting your sister to torment the cat is wrong. The argument is valid. But in the conditional the antecedent is not asserted; it is only entertained. If the meaning of tormenting the cat is wrong were just an expression of disapproval, then its meaning would change when it appears embedded, and the argument would equivocate. This is also the negation problem, because negation is just another kind of embedding. The expressivist can reply by using Frege’s distinction between sense and force. The content of the moral sentence remains the same in both contexts; what changes is the force with which it is put forward. Expressivism is therefore a theory of force, not of content. A minimalist about truth can then add that calling a moral sentence true is just endorsing it, so moral sentences can be truth-apt without being descriptive. Together, these points show that expressivism can account for embedding and validity. The only remaining task is to provide a compositional semantics, which is a technical problem rather than a philosophical objection. I conclude that the Frege–Geach problem does not refute expressivism.
