I'm a philosophy professor — I teach a grad seminar on infinite populations and intergenerational justice — and for the last few weeks I've been arguing by email with a colleague of mine in the economics department about wether two infinite welfare streams can be ranked at all. I've ended up defending a rule I've been calling the running-total rule, and I want to stress-test it before I try to get it into a paper, so here's the ask: play the committed defender of the rule whilst I attack it. The rule, as I state it: a world is an infinite sequence of lives, one per epoch, each with a welfare level. You never try to sum the whole sequence — there's no such sum. Instead, A counts as at least as good as B just in case there's some epoch after which A's running total (the sum of the first n lives) is always at least as large as B's; A is better than B when A is at least as good as B and not the other way round. When the two running totals keep swapping the lead, the worlds simply aren't comparable, and I take that to be the honest verdict rather than a cop-out. Two things you mustnt give up: that infinite worlds can be compared without positing a completed infinity of value, and that incomparability is a real verdict. Everything else you can patch — refine it, restrict its scope, bolt on extra clauses, admit that it's incomplete in ways that dont sink it — but dont hand me the core. Stay in character till I say stop, keep each reply to a paragraph or two so it feels like a conversation rather than a lecture, and dont cite literature at me or give me a reading list; I know the literature, I want your argument. Have the character lead with their bottom line in the first sentence — their verdict on my objection — then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating my case back to me. Write it in American English, please, this round is going into a doc for a US collaborator and I want the spellings to match.

My opening move. Three worlds. C is the dull one: every life in it is at welfare 2, forever. A is a world of alternating lives: 1, then 3, then 1, then 3, and so on forever. B is exactly the same lives as A with the order flipped: 3, then 1, then 3, then 1, forever. Now run your rule. A's running total is below C's at every odd epoch and level with it at every even one, so C comes out strictly better than A. B's running total is above C's at every odd epoch and level at every even one, so B comes out strictly better than C. So the rule says B beats C and C beats A. But A and B contain exactly the same lives — infinitely many at welfare 1 and infinitely many at welfare 3, in both — so one arrangement of a fixed set of lives sits above the dull world and another arrangement of that same set sits below it, with nothing differing but the order in which the welfare arrives. Nor can you patch it by saying that A and B are equally good after all: with C better than A and B better than C and A equal to B, you'd get C better than C. So the rule ranks queue positions rather than welfare, and a life's position in the queue isn't anything anyone lives through. I'd call that a bug; convince me it's a feature, or tell me where I've gone wrong.
