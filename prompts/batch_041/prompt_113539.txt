Ok, I need help untangling my own view about animals, and I suspect it's going to embarrass me.

Kant's line, as I've always heard it, is that we have no duties to animals at all, only duties to humanity that happen to get triggered by how we treat animals — cruelty to a dog is wrong because it makes me callous toward people, not because it's bad for the dog. I used to think that was obviously evasive. But when I go case by case my verdicts don't fit a clean direct-duties view either, and now I can't tell which end is wrong.

Here's what I actually think, going by gut:

Torturing a cat for fun, alone, nobody ever finds out: wrong. And I don't think the wrong is that it coarsens me — if I dropped dead right afterwards it would still have been wrong.

Putting down a healthy dog because I'm moving abroad and it's inconvenient, done painlessly at the vet: wrong. No suffering anywhere, so what's the wrong?

Shooting a deer for sport, clean shot, no wounding: wrong, and I have a genuinely hard time saying why. The deer had a decent life and a fast death.

A vet euthanizing a suffering animal at the very end: fine, obviously.

A farmer who raises pigs well and kills them quickly: ok, or at least not cruelty.

Someone who eats factory-farmed chicken without a thought: what she's doing is bad — that hen had a horrible life — but I don't think she's a bad person.

Someone who tells me he only refrains from cruelty because it would coarsen him, and genuinely doesn't care about animals: I think less of him than of the animal lover who does the identical things, even though the animals get identical treatment. Kant seems to say he's doing the right thing for exactly the right reason.

Keeping a smart, social animal alone in a tiny cage for years — no pain, no beatings: wrong.

A researcher doing painful mouse experiments that might lead to a real cure: can be justified, but I want her to be bothered by it, and I can't explain why her being bothered matters if the only duty in play is to her own character.

So the no-suffering cases (the dog, the deer, the caged bird) look like they're about contempt or disrespect — treating the animal's good as something that doesn't count — which sounds like the animal has some standing of its own, i.e. direct duties after all. But the researcher and the callous refuser suggest I care about the shape of the agent's will in a way that has nothing to do with the animal. And the chicken case suggests my verdicts on acts and my verdicts on people come apart: the hen-eater does worse than the sport hunter, but the hunter is the worse person. If a single principle has to rank both, something I believe has to go.

My half-formed principle: the wrong of cruelty is contempt — not the pain, but the taking-it-that-the-pain-doesn't-count. On that view a clean kill for fun is contemptuous while a painful necessary operation isn't. Wierdly it also makes the callous refuser contemptuous even though his behavior is fine, which is maybe what I actually think. Test it against the cases and repair it.

What I want: one principle, or two candidates, stated precisely enough that I can see where each clause bites; the case that motivates each clause; what the principle says about a case I didn't list — say, someone who breeds dogs that will struggle to breathe their whole lives because the puppies are cute and sell, and who is genuinely fond of each puppy; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts I have to give up, and which one I'm probly clinging to for bad reasons.

If the Kantian framing is doing no work here and I'm just asking you to launder my intuitions into a principle, say that plainly instead of complying. And don't hand me the tidiest formulation and pretend it saves everything — I'd rather know which verdict is the odd one out. No citations needed, I'm not going to check your page numbers.
