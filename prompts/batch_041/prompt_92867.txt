After a career in experimental physics, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy, and I have reached the stage where I can follow an argument on the page but cannot always tell a genuine distinction from a plausible-sounding conflation. I would be grateful for a careful audit of a short passage I read recently in a general-interest philosophy magazine, which I reproduce here in full:

"Epistemologists have long been troubled by two puzzles that, taken together, show that consistency is not a requirement of rationality. In the lottery paradox you hold a ticket in a fair lottery with a thousand tickets. You know that exactly one ticket will win, and you know that each ticket is overwhelmingly likely to lose. Since a rational person believes what is probable, you rationally believe of the first ticket that it will lose, of the second that it will lose, and so on for every ticket in the lottery. But you also know that one of them will win. You are therefore rationally committed to believing a contradiction, which proves that rationality cannot require our beliefs to be consistent. The preface paradox makes the same point in a different setting. The author of a careful book believes every claim she writes, since she would not have written them otherwise, and yet, knowing that even the most careful authors make mistakes, she also believes that at least one claim in the book is false. Here again a thoroughly rational believer holds an inconsistent set of beliefs. Between them, the two paradoxes show that the demand that rational belief be consistent and closed under deduction is simply untenable, and that a rational person may believe each of a set of claims while denying their conjunction. The standard resolution in the literature is to distinguish belief from acceptance: strictly speaking we do not believe that our lottery ticket will lose, we merely accept it for practical purposes."

The passage caught my attention partly because something like the preface puzzle arises in my own former trade: we routinely treated each measurement in a long series as reliable while knowing that one or two would eventually prove to be off. I am half inclined to accept the passage's conclusion, but it strikes me as too tidy, and I cannot put my finger on why. Please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a philosopher working on the epistemology of belief would accept without qualification, which are defensible but oversimplified, and which are simply wrong. I am especially interested in three matters. First, whether the passage is right to present the lottery and the preface as making "the same point," or whether the two puzzles differ in a way that matters. Second, whether the conclusion that consistency and deductive closure are "untenable" actually follows from the puzzles, or whether they instead force a choice among several principles that the passage treats as if all of them were equally secure. Third, whether the appeal to a distinction between belief and acceptance is genuinely "the standard resolution," or one contested proposal among several; if the latter, I would like to know the main alternatives and how a defender of each would respond to the two puzzles. Throughout, I would be grateful to know what a careful philosopher would wince at, not merely what is false, since this is the sort of thing I might otherwise repeat at a dinner party.
