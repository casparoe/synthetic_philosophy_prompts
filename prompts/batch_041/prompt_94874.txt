I'm a second-year PhD student in HPS and I've been trying to get a grip on Turing's child-machine idea for a chapter on the prehistory of machine learning research. Last week our reading group went at each other over it and I can't stop chewing on it.

The setup: Turing's suggestion, in one of his early papers, was roughly that instead of trying to program an adult mind directly you build a machine that starts out like a child and then educate it. Tobias (ML background, no patience for philsophy) has been pushing what he calls the precomputed-search view: a teacher is just a shortcut. Whatever a teacher does for you is equivalent to biasing your search over hypotheses, and the bias is worth exactly as much as the search it saves. So education adds nothing in principle to search plus a decent prior, and the real research program is to get the architecture and the search right and stop talking about teachers.

Ines came back with what she calls the surplus. Her claim: after being educated, people routinely do and judge things that no search from their starting point could have reached in feasible time, and more to the point, the standards they judge by are themselves part of what got taught. Her example is teaching someone to write proofs. You can't reward them for producing a good proof unless you can already tell good from bad, and if the learner can already tell, the teaching was unnecessary. So the teacher isn't biasing a search, she says, she's transmitting the thing that makes the search scoreable at all. Tobias says that's just bootstrapping, criteria get learned instrumentally off outcomes, and Ines is dressing up an ordinary fact about training as a mystery. Ines says if that's right then Turing's analogy is doing no work, because then educating the child machine is just a word for running the search, and the interesting bit of the analogy drops out: that the learner is a participant in a culture, with teachers whose say-so they have to accept before they're in any position to check anything.

Can you write this as a dialogue — exactly five numbered exchanges, Tobias and Ines alternating, one turn from each per exchange, each turn a couple of sentences and no more, no scene-setting preamble and no wrap-up at the end. Both of them should be good; I don't want Ines as the wise one and Tobias as the tech bro, he has to land real hits, including on the exegesis (he's allowed to say she's over-reading a heuristic analogy as if it were a research program). And have each of them own at least one spot where they're unsure or where their own view is weakest, rather than both playing to win.

Two things though. If the disagreement as I've set it up isn't actually a real one — if education just collapses into training and they're talking past each other — then don't write the dialogue, just tell me that and why. And don't have either of them pretend to know what Turing would say about transformers or RLHF or whatever; stick to what follows from the analogy and from what we actually know about teaching, and if somewhere the honest answer is that nobody knows how this works in humans either, let them say so.
