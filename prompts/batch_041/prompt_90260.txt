I'm an applied statistician — my day job is combining forecasts, building hierarchical models, and thinking about model uncertainty — and I've been sitting in on a philosophy seminar on the epistemology of disagreement, where I keep having the feeling that this is a debate I already own the machinery for. The two poles, as I understand them, are conciliationism, roughly the view that learning that a competent and equally well-informed judge disagrees with you is itself evidence and should move you toward their view (at the limit, halfway), and steadfastness, the view that you may hold your ground, because you have access to your own reasons in a way you don't have to theirs, or because a bare disagreement is not automatically a reason to distrust your own competence. I'd like a careful translation, side by side, into the vocabulary I actually think in: priors over a parameter or over models, likelihoods, exchangeability, hierarchical models in which each agent is a noisy measuring device with its own bias parameter, pooling rules and forecast combination, comparisons of the informativeness of experiments, and the result that rational agents who share a prior and communicate their posteriors cannot knowingly remain in disagreement. For each side, tell me what survives and in what form. Does conciliationism come out as the claim that a disagreement is higher-order evidence — evidence about the reliability of your own measuring process — so that responding to it is just ordinary conditioning in a suitably expanded model? Does steadfastness come out as the claim that your likelihood and theirs are not exchangeable, either because you hold private information or because you are treating your own reasoning as an additional, better-calibrated instrument? Be explicit about which steps are reconstructions rather than translations, and don't quietly help yourself to a common prior or a shared model space in order to make the reconciliation come out cleanly, since that is close to what is at issue.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which claims in this debate have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary? Candidates I can see, though I may be wrong about which are real: the notion of an epistemic peer as someone whose reliability is genuinely on a par with yours, rather than an exchangeable expert in an ensemble; the idea that there is a question of what you are permitted or required to believe, as opposed to what a rational agent's posterior would be; and the thought that a disagreement can be reasonable on both sides in a way that no pair of probability assignments can be. For each item of residue, give me your view on whether it marks a real limitation of the statistical vocabulary — something the philosophers need that my framework cannot express — or whether it shows that the philosophical claim was doing less work than it appeared, so that "conciliate" names nothing over and above conditioning and the apparent disagreement was really about which model to condition in. I have no formal training in philosophy, so don't assume I know the standard labels or the received ways of framing the options; you can assume I'm fluent in the statistical material and that a model-theoretic reconstruction will land. I'd be perfectly happy with an answer that says the translation is nearly total and the debate is shallower than it looks — I would just rather know which of the two it is, and exactly where the seam runs.
