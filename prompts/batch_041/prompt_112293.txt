For most of this year my partner and I have been trying to decide whether to have a child, and I've been struck by how often our argument turns on something that isn't really about children at all: I cannot know what it is like to be a parent, and, worse, I cannot know what I will want once I am one. A friend in the decision-theory reading group I sit in on told me that this is a well-worked corner of philosophy — the literature on transformative experience — and since then I've noticed that the six of us in that group are all talking past one another: we use the same phrases (that you can't know until you've done it, that it isn't irrational to choose blind) and mean quite different things by them. I would like to settle the question with an instrument rather than another round of argument, so that each of us can see which view we actually hold rather than which label we happen to like.

Please build me a decision tree that sorts someone from a starting commitment to a named position on transformative choice. The tree should open with the one commitment I take everyone in the group to share — that some experiences cannot be understood from the outside, so that no amount of testimony, imagination or evidence available beforehand conveys what they are like — and then branch on the questions where the views genuinely come apart. I care especially about these splits: whether the obstacle is to knowing facts about the experience or to knowing one's own future preferences, that is, whether the problem is epistemic or evaluative; whether the obstacle is permanent or merely expensive, so that proxies, partial simulation and testimony can be graded by how much of the gap they close; whether the right response is to repair decision theory (by admitting second-order evaluations of the process, say), to abandon the demand for rational choice in these cases, or to redescribe the choice as being about which person to become rather than which outcome to obtain; whether the phenomenon is confined to a special class of decisions or is ordinary uncertainty in fancy dress; and whether irreversibility does any independent work, or whether reversible transformations are just as hard and only seem less frightening.

To keep this from collapsing into a recitation of the standard positions, I want one distinction of my own built into the tree, and I want you to say whether it survives: distinguish transformations that change what you believe about the outcomes, transformations that change what you want, and transformations that change what you are able to care about at all — then tell me whether the third category is real or only the second in a dramatic register. Every branch node must be answerable by someone who has never read any of this literature: phrase each one as a concrete case or a plain claim, never as a question about which philosophical label one accepts, and give each leaf a short name for the position it lands on, or mark it unnamed if nobody has defended it. The tree must be able to reach at least these leaves: a view on which such choices have no rational basis and the demand for one should simply be dropped; a view on which you should choose the person you want to become rather than the outcome you want; a deflationary view on which nothing here exceeds ordinary decision under uncertainty; a view on which the remedy is deference, to testimony, to those who have undergone it, or to someone who knows you well; and a view on which the problem is preference change and the test is whether you endorse the process that changes you. Cases that must appear somewhere on the tree: an offer to become a creature that will crave something you now find repellent; a first child; a conversion or vocation that would restructure a life; a transformation decided on your behalf, as when a court or a parent chooses for someone who cannot choose; and a plainly minor case, such as acquiring a taste, that the deflationary view will insist is no different in kind. Aim for ten to fourteen branch nodes and six to nine leaves. Begin the response with the tree itself, with no preface and no restatement of my question, and put after it a short second section covering the branches with no occupant (and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored), any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and the cases you wanted to use but could not place.
