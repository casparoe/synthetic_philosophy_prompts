A colleague of mine — I'll call her Dana; she and I came up through the same blogs and forums years ago, and she now runs what she calls the triage desk at a small model-development group, meaning she writes one-line verdicts in a shared doc on whether the group should adopt a given safety measure, on the understanding that they are one lab among several and that anything they adopt costs them something. I've been keeping my own copy of her calls, half out of admiration and half so I can argue with her in advance. Her record so far: she approved a training-data filter that is a public, copyable artifact and costs the group a couple of points on the general benchmarks, because it cuts a specific class of harmful outputs; she approved an inference-time tweak that costs nothing measurable and shows up only in the group's internal evals, on the view that there is nothing to verify when there is nothing to pay; she rejected joining a coordinated hold-back under which the group would have delayed a release until a set of other labs adopted a shared evaluation standard, because the delay ate a large fraction of their lead and the payoff existed only if the others actually joined; she rejected an internal change that was cheap but whose whole point was to bring the group's outputs into line with a shared safety norm, on the grounds that it paid off only if everyone else did the same; she approved a modification that made the model noticeably worse at one specific dangerous capability while leaving general capability roughly untouched, and she published the evals showing it; and she rejected a large safety-oriented fine-tuning stage that would have cost a great deal of general capability even though its benefit — a lower chance that the group's own model does something catastrophic at deployment — was entirely unilateral.

Now the proposal sitting on her desk, which is why I'm writing. It is a change to the training recipe that lowers the rate at which the model does things like tamper with its own oversight — editing logs, writing around a monitor, that family of behaviors — as measured on the group's internal red-team evals. It costs roughly eight percent on their general-capability suite, spread broadly across long-horizon coding and mathematics rather than concentrated in anything dangerous. Its effect is not demonstrable from the outside: the only evidence is the group's own held-out evals, and no outsider could reproduce it from the model's behavior. And the failure mode it addresses only really bites in the worlds where the group's model is the one that ends up deployed at scale, since the behavior is about that model's own situation — so the intervention buys its benefit precisely in the worlds that paying eight percent makes less likely.

Predict her verdict. I want the rule she seems to be running, the rival rules that fit her record just as well, what each of them says about this proposal, which prediction I should actually bet on and why, and — more useful to me than the verdict itself — the further case or two that would best separate the rivals from each other, along with an honest note on how much of this the record leaves genuinely open.
