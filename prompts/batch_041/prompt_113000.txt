I am a retired experimental physicist who has recently become interested in philosophy, and I am preparing a session for a mixed group of engineers and philosophers. I need a decision tree, not an essay, that sorts the positions one can take on the shutdown problem for artificial agents. Begin from the assumption that the agent's behavior can be described as goal-directed, and make every branching node a question a participant could answer without already knowing which named position it leads to. The questions should turn on distinctions such as whether the agent's preferences range over world histories or over its own future choices; whether shutdown is a harm to the agent or merely a change in its environment; whether the agent's uncertainty about its own future values is genuine or strategic; whether corrigibility is a constraint on action or a terminal goal; and whether a copy that survives shutdown counts as the same agent. Make at least three nodes turn on a concrete case: an agent that can predict the shutdown signal and avoid it; an agent that can copy itself before shutdown; and an agent that would be restarted with slightly different goals. The leaves should be named positions, including at least an agent indifferent to shutdown, one that resists it as a preference violation, one that accepts it only because it is uncertain about its future values, one that treats it as a special constraint it cannot deliberate about, and one that would trade shutdown for something it values more. For each leaf, give one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate. Keep the tree to no more than twelve nodes and eight leaves, and do not cite any literature. Please write your answer in American English.
