I'm putting together a session for a grad reading group on Adam Smith's moral philosophy — we've done Parts I and II of the Theory of Moral Sentiments and we're now deep in the impartial spectator material — and I want to use Smith as a stress test for a pile of stuff he never had to think about. The parts of him I care about here: sympathy as imagined fellow-feeling rather than contagion, the impartial spectator as an idealised judge rather than the actual bystander, the business about us needing other people as a mirror (he says "Bring him into society, and he is immediately provided with the mirror which he wanted before", and the mirror is the countenance and behaviour of the people you actually live with), praiseworthiness vs. praise, and his obsession with self-deceit.

So: what would Smith have made of the following? For each one I want his likely line, plus which of his doctrines that line is actually resting on (in your own words please — dont bother with chapter-and-verse, you'll get it wrong and I don't need it), the strongest reason he might have said the opposite, and what in the case his framework just has no grip on.

1. "Spectator" (yes, that's really what it's called) — a consumer app. You type in what you're about to do or what you just did; it returns a number out of 100 and one sentence. Under the hood it's a model fine-tuned on a few million "was I in the wrong here?" forum posts where strangers rated the poster. The pitch is that its disinterested, never flatters you, never gets tired of you, and works at 2am. There's a paid feature where you re-enter the same event from the other person's point of view and watch the number move. HR departments use it in investigations, parents use it on their kids, 14-year-olds use it before they post. Is this the impartial spectator, or a very good counterfeit of one? And what turns on the answer — is the difference, if there is one, in the idealisation, in the information available, in the fact that it's an aggregate rather than an ideal, or in the fact that it can't be moved by you?

2. Public standing scores: reputation numbers computed from strangers' votes and displayed next to your name everywhere. Smith separates wanting to be praiseworthy from wanting to be praised. In a world where praise is continuously quantified and publicly visible, does that push people toward the first or the second? And does he have anything that predicts what happens to someone with a high score who knows they're not praiseworthy, or the reverse?

3. Pile-ons. Smith's impartial spectator is a singular man within the breast, and as I read him the real, present spectator is what keeps that inner judge honest — you can't trust the ideal one on its own. Now the real spectator is a million strangers with no faces, no co-presence, no stake, and only whatever half of the story got posted. What does that do to the man within the breast? Would Smith say a crowd like that is a spectator at all, or is it something his theory has no slot for?

4. The man of system — the one who treats a society as a chessboard and rearranges the pieces by rule. Now the rules are learned from data and enforced at scale by systems nobody can inspect. Is that more worrying to him than his original man of system, or less? My guess is he's less bothered by the systems being inhuman than by the people who love them, but tell me if I've got that backwards.

Then, honestly: where does this stop being Smith and start being me? Which attributions are doing real work in his text and which are you extrapolating? And what would he have simply gotten wrong — what's in these cases that he lacked the conceptual resources for, such that a "Smithian" answer would be an artefact of our translation rather than his view?

Finish with a confidence note: how sure you are of the overall reconstruction, and the single point in the whole thing you're least sure about.
