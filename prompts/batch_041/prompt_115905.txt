For a graduate seminar on long-term ethics that I've been asked to lead, I want to open with a specification rather than a position. The subject is how we ought to weigh the interests and goods of people who will exist later against those of people alive now — the whole apparatus of social discount rates, rates of pure time preference, and intergenerational weightings. Before anyone in the room defends a number (zero, some small positive rate, a rate that declines over time), I want the criteria that any adequate theory in this area has to meet. Give me the desiderata: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results or structural constraints it cannot contradict, each paired with the consideration that makes it a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp. I am particularly interested in which of the things people routinely demand of such a theory are real desiderata and which are artifacts of assuming a particular formal framework.

I'll seed a few so you can see the register, but do not treat my list as authoritative: sort the genuine requirements from the parochial ones and add whatever I have missed. The one-way direction of influence in time; the moral symmetry between a person alive now and an otherwise identical person who will exist later; the difference between discounting because the future is uncertain and discounting because the future simply counts for less; goods that are not substitutable or commensurable, so that a livable climate or a surviving language cannot be traded off against a larger pile of consumables; the non-identity problem, where the very people a policy affects may owe their existence to it; the likelihood that future people's values will diverge from ours, so that their good cannot simply be read off our preferences; and the causal opacity of long-run effects, where we cannot trace which distant outcomes our present choices bring about. For each, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of a particular camp.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the standard candidates — a positive rate of pure time preference, a strictly zero rate, and a rate that applies to goods but not to welfare — and, above all, flag any pair of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. If the list turns out to be internally inconsistent, that is the conclusion I want to leave the seminar with, so do not paper over the tension in order to produce a tidy scorecard.
