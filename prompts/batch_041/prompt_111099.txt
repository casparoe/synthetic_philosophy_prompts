I'm a science-and-ideas reporter, and last Thursday I ended up at a small dinner after a symposium on arts funding — foundation people, a couple of academics, that crowd — where two of the guests went at each other for about forty minutes over a pilot scheme one of the foundations is apparently about to launch. I stayed out of it and took notes, and now I'm writing a piece about the scheme and I can't tell how much of what they said I can put my name to.

The scheme, as best I can recontruct it: the foundation wants to get people into classical music, and instead of subsidising tickets it's going to hand out one big annual cash award to the person who, in the judgement of a panel reading a written statement plus a short interview, most clearly loves the music. Not the person who attends the most, or knows the most — the one who loves it most. And the application form carries a line saying that anyone who applies mainly because of the money will be disqualified.

The two of them — call them R and S, I'd rather not name them — went round and round. I'll give you the exchange as it actually went, in order, because I think some moves were good and some weren't, and I honestly can't referee it.

R's opening: the scheme is self-defeating. You can't buy love; the moment the money is in the picture it's the money doing the work, and what you get is people performing love for a cheque. And the form concedes as much — that disqualification line is the foundation admitting that a love motivated by the award isn't the thing they're awarding.

S: the line is a drafting error, not a confession. And "you can't buy love" is false as a causal claim — parents pay for piano lessons, councils fund youth orchestras, and some of those kids end up genuinely loving music. The money is a reason to turn up. What happens after you turn up is a seperate question.

R: fine, but then the award does nothing a ticket subsidy wouldn't do, except worse, because it's aimed at the love rather than the attendance — it's paying for an attitude, not an action. And if a payment can count as a reason to love something, other incentives can count too, and then a man who says "admire me or I'll break your legs" has given you a reason to admire him. Which is absurd.

S: the mugger doesn't give you a reason to admire him, he gives you a reason to pretend, or to want to admire him, and those are different attitudes, so nothing absurd follows. And the phrase you keep reaching for — that the money is a reason "of the wrong kind" — is doing all the work and you still haven't said what makes a kind wrong. If it means reasons that don't bear on whether the thing is actually worth loving, then you've already helped yourself to the notion of worth loving that the whole question is about. That's circular.

R: then call it circular and see what's left. If "worth loving" just means "there are reasons to love it", and money is a reason to love it, then the foundation's money makes the music more worth loving, which is mad. So either there are two kinds of reasons in play here, or there's no such thing as the music's value over and above what people have reason to do.

S: I'd take the second horn before I'd take yours. But there's a third option: maybe the difference isn't between kinds of reasons at all, it's between kinds of facts. The fact that the foundation pays is a fact about the foundation, not about the music. You've assumed the whole time that a reason to love a thing has to be a fact about that thing. Attack that assumption and the puzzle dissolves.

That's where they left it. R thinks S has quietly given up on there being anything to love except the paying. S thinks R is defending a purity requirement nobody could ever meet — a love that would have been there anyway, untainted by any reason to have it, which isn't a description of any love anyone has ever had.
