You're a philosopher who's spent years on the ethics of immigration, and I want your honest, detailed comments on a short essay I'm drafting. Please don't be nice about it — I'd rather hear where the thing breaks than that it's interesting. Some context, since I'm not an academic: I got into this after a long argument with my brother-in-law about asylum seekers, and I've been reading Michael Dummett's short book on immigration and refugees (the Routledge one, 2001). As I understand him, the central move is an analogy — sorting people by where they happened to be born is on a par with sorting them by race, both are facts about a person they had no say in, so using either as a reason to keep someone out is the same kind of injustice. He also seems to want to shift the burden of proof onto whoever wants to exclude, and the second half of the book is basically a history of how much British immigration policy was driven by straight-up racial hostility.

Here's what I want to argue. I think the analogy is being asked to do two seperate jobs and they pull apart. One job is conceptual: there's something wrong in principle with a birth-given trait deciding your access to a country. The other is diagnostic: the people who build walls are in fact usually moved by the same contempt that moves racists. I think the conceptual version overreaches, because racism doesn't only sort people, it ranks them — it says some people are worth less — and being kept out of a country, however unfair, doesn't by itself tell you you're worth less. So I try to keep the diagnostic half and drop the conceptual half, and then I claim his conclusion (roughly, that the presumption should be against exclusion) can still survive, only now it rests on history and politics instead of a clean principle.

What I want from you: (1) is my sorting-vs-ranking distinction a real distinction, or just a way of letting comfortable people off the hook? (2) is the diagnostic reading fair to him, or am I rescuing him from an argument he actually needs? (3) what's the strongest objection from the other side — I keep worrying someone will say a country is more like a club than a family, and clubs get to pick their members; how bad is that for me? (4) if the conceptual half really does fail, does the conclusion actually survive, or am I kidding myself? And if you think the whole framing is misdirected, say so and tell me what question I should be asking instead. A numbered list of problems, each with a short paragraph on what to do about it, would be ideal.
