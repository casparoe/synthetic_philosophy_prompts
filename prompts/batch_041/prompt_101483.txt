I recently joined a research group that studies cooperation between agents who may never interact — civilizations separated by galaxies, or possible agents in scenarios too far apart to affect one another — and our discussions have stalled on whether the evidential cooperation argument gives any real reason to cooperate. The argument, as I understand it, runs roughly like this: if other agents reason the way I do, then my decision to cooperate is evidence about what they will decide, so choosing to cooperate can influence outcomes elsewhere even without any causal contact. Half the group treats this as a decisive reason to cooperate across large worlds; the other half thinks it proves too much, since the same reasoning would license cooperating with anyone whose behavior merely correlates with mine — including agents who reward or punish me only in hypothetical cases — and neither side has budged in months. My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis rather than a claim, is that the deadlock is at least partly verbal: one side means by cooperating acting so as to be correlated with beneficial action elsewhere, the other means acting jointly under some shared intention or agreement, and they may be answering different questions under a single name. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is — a genuine dispute within decision theory about causal versus evidential reasoning, a dispute about the setup itself (whether such correlations exist, and what counts as the same agent across worlds), or two separate questions sharing one label — and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress: a formal result, a distinction, a change of question, or a reason to expect none.
