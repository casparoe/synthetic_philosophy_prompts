I retired a few years ago, after a long career in experimental physics, and I have since been trying to teach myself political theory — largely by reading on my own, and largely by arguing about what I have read with a friend who is a historian. Our current quarrel is about Hannah Arendt and political lying. I have worked through her essay occasioned by the Pentagon Papers, together with the earlier essay on truth and politics, and I find her treatment at once illuminating and, I suspect, rather dated. Her paradigm is the lie told by a government: centrally authored, deliberate, and usually in the service of an image or a policy; and her deepest anxiety is that such lying corrodes the shared factual world that a plurality of people must have in common before they can argue with one another at all. I should like you to imagine Arendt confronting the conditions of the present, and to reconstruct, as carefully and as faithfully to her own framework as you can, the position she would have taken.

Suppose she were alive today and had to reckon with three developments that post-date her. The first is the migration of political lying from a single sovereign author to something distributed and emergent — falsehoods that no one person need have authored, in which the various participants may each believe a different fragment of the story, and which are often sustained not by conviction but by the sheer friction of retransmission. The second is synthetic audio and video, which make it possible to fabricate not merely a claim about the world but the apparent evidence for it. The third — and this is the part I find most interesting — is the secondary harm that follows: the mere existence of convincing forgeries lets the genuinely guilty dismiss authentic recordings as fakes, so that the new technology ends up shielding the very liars it was supposed to expose. I should like to know whether Arendt's distinctions — between the deliberate lie and the honest error, and between the political lie and mere concealment — still do the work she needed of them under these conditions, and where you judge her categories to begin to strain.

Please be explicit about the epistemic footing of your answer. Separate what is genuinely settled about Arendt's considered views from what is a contestable extrapolation from them; do not present a reading that specialists dispute as though it were the consensus, and where you are simply guessing how she would have jumped, say so plainly. Kindly write in British English.
