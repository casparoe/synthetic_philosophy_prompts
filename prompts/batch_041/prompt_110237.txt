My credence that the frame problem — understood broadly, as the problem of how a system determines what is relevant to its present task without being told, rather than narrowly, as the problem of representing what does not change inside a logical formalism — is a genuine, still-unsolved problem for contemporary AI is about 0.6; a colleague puts it at about 0.25. Give me your own number, but mainly decompose the question rather than defend a bare figure. Roughly how much rides on each of the following: (i) whether the narrow, classical version has in fact been solved by nonmonotonic, default, or probabilistic machinery; (ii) whether the broad relevance problem is a distinct problem at all, or merely the narrow one restated; (iii) whether a system that leaves most of its knowledge implicit — say an agent driven by a learned language model deciding which stored facts bear on a novel task — sidesteps the frame problem or only relocates it; and (iv) whether 'solved' should mean 'given a formalism' or 'dissolved as an artifact of classical representational assumptions.' Assign each sub-question a rough probability with one line of justification, show how they combine into your final figure, and tell me which term the number is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most. Finally, say whether the gap between 0.6 and 0.25 most likely lives in a disagreement over (i)–(iv) or over what would count as solving the problem in the first place.
