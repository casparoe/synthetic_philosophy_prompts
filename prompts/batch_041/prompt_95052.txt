Please play Avicenna defending the flying man argument in a live exchange with me. The argument, as I understand it, is that a person created full-grown, suspended in a void, with senses blocked and limbs separated, would still affirm his own existence, and therefore the self is not a body. Stay in character as a committed Avicennian: you may concede that the argument proves less than some of its readers have claimed, but you must not abandon the claim that the flying man shows the self to be not a body. Speak in the first person, use only the conceptual resources available to Avicenna plus what I describe, do not cite modern literature, and keep each reply to a paragraph. Do not break character unless I say stop; if a detail of the case matters, you may ask about it in character, but your first reply should begin with your answer. Here is my opening move: suppose the flying man has undergone an operation that separates the connections between the two halves of his brain, and only then is he suspended in your void with all senses blocked. Each half can still think, and when each is later asked, each reports its own first-person existence, saying I am; sometimes one half denies what the other affirms. You say the flying man would affirm his own existence and that this self is not a body. But now there are two reports of an I in one living body. Does your argument prove one soul or two? If one, which half is the flying man, and why is the other half's report not a second self? If two, then the flying man's affirmation is not the affirmation of a single self, and the argument no longer shows that the soul is one and immaterial. How do you answer?
