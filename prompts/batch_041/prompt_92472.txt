I'm redoing the death-and-dying week in my bioethics course and I want one crisp example to lead with — not the usual is-brain-death-really-death debate they've all heard before. What I want them to see is a tension in how death actually gets declared. Most US law, following the 1981 model act, says you're dead if you've had irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, or irreversible cessation of all function of the whole brain. But there's a common organ-donation pathway where a patient with a catastrophic brain injury has treatment withdrawn, the heart stops, and after a few minutes with no pulse the team declares death and recovers the organs — the whole thing resting on the norm that donors must be dead before their organs come out, which people call the dead donor rule. Here's the issue I want the example built around: there's a difference between loss of circulation that can't be reversed and loss that simply won't be, because we've decided not to try. In the donation case the heart often could be restarted — no ones going to, but it could be — so under a reading that demands the loss be impossible to reverse the patient isn't dead yet, while under a reading that only demands it not be reversed they are.

Give me an example case — realistic, specific enough to picture at the bedside — where those two readings come apart, or where the circulatory and the brain criteria give different verdicts about the same person, and then tell me what the example is supposed to show. Be upfront about which parts of this are settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely contested — don't feed me a contested view as if it were the consensus — and flag any place where the example only works by stipulating a clinical detail that's actually disputed.
