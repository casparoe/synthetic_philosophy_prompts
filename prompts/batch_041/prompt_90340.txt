Retired physicist here — thirty-odd years in a standards lab, the kind of place where you spend a week measuring a lump of metal to establish that it hasn't changed. Since I stopped working I've been reading philosophy, badly, and I keep tripping over something about my old job that I never thought twice about at the time.

Here it is. We never measure anything against the world. We measure it against a standard, and the standard is itself a physical object that drifts and has to be measured, and the instrument we measure the standard with was itself calibrated against another standard, on up the line. We call it traceability and we talk about it as a chain, but it definately isn't one — every rung holds up the rung below it and leans on the rung above it. And it has to bottom out somewhere: somebody signs a piece of paper that says this number is the definition, and that's the end of the argument. Then a few years back we turned the whole ladder upside down — instead of the kilogram being a cylinder in a vault outside Paris, we fixed Planck's constant to a number and made the kilogram the thing you go and measure. Which I like, but it means that number is now simultaneously something we found out about the world and something we wrote down by decree.

A friend of mine who actually knows some philosophy says this is what people mean by a tangled hierarchy, and that Escher's drawing of the two hands needs a level that's doing the drawing and isn't itself in the picture. Fine. What I want from you is what the practice commits me to. Does calibration as we actually run it require that inviolate level — a stipulation that isn't answerable to any measurement — or could you run the whole business as a closed loop of mutually supporting comparisons with no privileged floor, the way I gather some people run justification in general? Does the practice presuppose that we're discovering the ratios between things (that two rods really are or aren't the same length) while merely choosing units, and is that split stable, or does the conventionalism leak upward until nothing is discovered at all? And which views does my old lab's procedure quietly rule out, without anyone ever arguing for it?

Last bit, the one that keeps me up. Suppose you replace the picture with the opposite one — no inviolate level anywhere, just a web that adjusts itself. What in a standards lab would actually have to change? My hunch is nothing, and if nothing changes then I want to know whether the practice was presupposing anything real in the first place, or whether I've just been doing the philosophy version of mistaking the map for the territory. So sort it out for me: which of these is a genuine commitment of the practice, which is merely something it fits comfortably with, and where is it just underdetermined and I'm inventing a metaphysics for what is really a filing convention.
