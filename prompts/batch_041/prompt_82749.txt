I'm a novelist — third book, out next spring, about two rival AI labs that have to agree on a joint safety protocol before a launch deadline, and the whole plot turns on the fact that neither side can predict how the other side's negotiating software will actually behave. So I've been reading my way into the technical literature on 'safe Pareto improvements', and next month I'm doing a long on-stage conversation with a researcher who works on them (I'd rather not put her name in this, since she'll be speaking for herself and I don't want to put words in her mouth). I'm supposed to come with questions and I've got alot of half-formed ones, so I need help. Could you draft eight to ten real questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — not "so what is a safe Pareto improvement?", but the ones you'd ask after you've already read the papers.

Here's roughly where I've got to, so you can aim. As I understand it, the setup is a game where each player hands the decision to a delegate or a program, and you can't form probabilities about what the other delegate will do; a safe Pareto improvement is a transformation of the game such that everybody is necessarily better off than under the default way of playing. The part I keep chewing on is that these results seem to get proved using assumptions about how games relate to each other — isomorphic games get played isomorphically, strictly dominated strategies get stripped out — which sound to me like assumptions about how agents play, dressed up as logic. So I want at least one question pressing on whether "no probabilistic beliefs about the other delegate" really means "no assumptions", and a follow-up ready for the reply that those are just symmetry principles everyone already accepts. I also want one on what "safe" is doing in the name: safe for the players, sure, but the two delegating parties can make themselves better off at the expense of everyone standing outside the game, and my whole novel is basically that, so I want her to either own it or tell me why it isn't the framework's business. And one on why a Pareto improvement needs a whole theory to get off the ground — if both sides would genuinely be better off, what stops them? — with the follow-up being, well, isn't that just the old problem of which equilibrium people land on, and what does the new machinery add that game theory didn't have? If any question would just make her restate a definition, cut it.

Two more things I'd like in there. She's also worked on versions where players commit in advance to behave as a function of what the other side's default play would have been, and that seems to presuppose you can credibly reveal your own default — which in real life is exactly the thing people lie about, so I want a question about whether that's a modelling convenience or a hidden assumption. And if there's a result in this literature that reads like a ceiling rather than a recipe — some bound past which these improvements can't be guaranteed for anybody — build a question around that, plus one on what happens when there's no well-defined "default" at all, which is every negotiation I've ever written and, I suspect, most of them that happen. Keep the whole set in a register I can actually say out loud on a stage in front of an audience that mostly hasn't read the papers.
