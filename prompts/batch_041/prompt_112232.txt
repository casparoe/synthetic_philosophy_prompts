I'm writing a long-form essay for a general readership on the strange career of prediction as an instrument of government, and I want to hang it on a single film. The film is Steven Spielberg's Minority Report, the 2002 one, with Tom Cruise as John Anderton, the head of a Washington, D.C., Precrime unit that arrests would-be murderers on the strength of visions supplied by three clairvoyants known as precogs. The idea I want to read it against is one my readers will almost certainly not have encountered: futarchy, the proposal, associated with the economist Robin Hanson, that a polity should settle its ends by vote and its factual disputes by market — fixing its values democratically and then letting speculative markets on outcomes decide which policy actually delivers them, on the wager that a price aggregates dispersed knowledge better than any committee of experts can. The film, notably, dispenses with the market altogether and installs an oracle in its place. I want you to treat it as a thought experiment in governance by prediction — futarchy with the price mechanism swapped out for a band of perfect seers — and to tell me what that thought experiment shows, and where it breaks down.

Where does the futarchy frame genuinely illuminate the film? Precrime appears to have precisely the structure Hanson describes: a value held fixed (prevent murder) pursued through a predictive belief (who will commit it), with the precogs doing the work he assigns to the market. But the precogs are oracles, not prices — centralized, opaque, and claiming infallibility — and I want to know what the film's substitution of one for the other exposes about the proposal's assumptions. What should we make of the minority report, the dissenting vision that gives the film its title and that the authorities quietly purge to preserve the system's reputation for certainty? Is that the oracle's noise, the market's tail risk, or a phenomenon the futarchy account of prediction has no room for? And consider Anderton's own predicament: the program's chief enforcer is himself predicted to murder, and the prediction — once he learns of it, and once his pursuers act on it — appears to help bring about the very act it foretold. That looks to me like the reflexivity problem any regime of prediction must face, and I would like the essay to press it: once the governed can read the forecast, the forecast becomes a cause of the world it was meant only to describe.

But I don't want the film reduced to an illustration of a policy proposal, or the proposal caricatured to fit the film, so be honest in the other direction too. Spielberg is plainly after something other than the aggregation of information — determinism and free will, the ethics of punishing a deed before it is done, the hubris of a state that would know the future — and where the film resists the futarchy reading I want that said plainly, with the specific scenes that resist it. Close on what the film contributes that the futarchy literature, with its market designs and its models, tends to leave out.
