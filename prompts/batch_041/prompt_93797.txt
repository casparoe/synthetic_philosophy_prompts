Two colleagues of mine sat on the postmortem panel for a data-center outage and have been arguing ever since about how the report should describe it. The outage was triggered by a bug, but it ran for nine hours because nobody applied a patch that had been available for months, and there was no automated failover either. Ravi's position: the report was wrong to list the team lead as a "cause" of the outage for not applying the patch. Causes make things happen, he says; a non-event makes nothing happen, so the bug and the missing failover are the only real causes here. Dana replied that this proves far too much — if omissions never cause anything, then a nurse who skips a patient's insulin hasn't caused the resulting coma, which is absurd; the team lead caused the outage in just the way the nurse causes the coma, by failing to do what would have prevented it. Ravi conceded the point about omissions in general, but then pressed a second objection: the counterfactual test by itself cannot pick out *the* cause, since there are countless things nobody did that would also have prevented the outage — nobody flew in an outside repair crew, nobody rewrote the software from scratch. So something over and above "if not for X, then not Y" is doing the work of selection, and he wants to know what. Dana said the something extra is norms: we count a failure to act as a cause only when there was an expectation that the person would act, which is exactly why we blame the team lead and not the strangers. Ravi answered that this makes causation depend on what people happen to expect, so that a change in office culture would change what caused the outage — and that Dana is quietly sliding from what caused the outage to who is to blame for it.

I was on the panel too and honestly couldn't referee this. Adjudicate it move by move: say which of the four moves is right, which is partly right, and which is mistaken, and why. Then say who has the better overall position, and identify what both of them are missing. I'd rather have a definite verdict than a survey of the options.
