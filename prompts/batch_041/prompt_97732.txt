so i'm in a moral psych reading group and we keep hitting the same move, and i want a proper audit of it rather than another round of everyone nodding at each other.

the move is Haidt's taste-buds picture from that book of his. the moral mind is like a tongue with a handful of receptors -- care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, roughly -- and moral disagreement is like two people disagreeing about food. your chili is my poison, nobody is objectively right about the taste, and the best you can do is figure out whose receptors are firing harder. in the book this is doing real work: it's supposed to explain why left and right talk past each other. but in my group it gets used to license a bigger conclusion, namely that asking "who is actually right, left or right?" is a category mistake -- it's taste all the way down, so the grown-up response is tolerance and conflict management rather than adjudication.

what i want: lay out the mapping explicitly -- what corresponds to the tongue, the receptors, the stimuli/ligands, the taste experience, the chef, whatever else -- and then tell me where it breaks. i'm suspicious of three spots and want you to sort the real disanalogies from the cheap shots. (1) taste receptors are basically the same in everyone and have no propositional content, whereas the foundations are supposedly about harm, fairness, etc. so does calling it "taste" quietly presuppose non-cognitivism -- i.e. does the analogy pre-load the conclusion that moral judgments aren't truth-apt, when that was the thing we were supposed to be arguing for? (2) we do sometimes argue people out of tastes (acquired tastes, umami, allergies) and we don't hold anyone responsible for their tastes, but we blame and praise people for moral views, so the "no fault, no argument" bit looks imported. (3) the analogy treats the receptors as fixed hardware, yet the foundations literature itself says they're shaped by culture and get tripped by wildly different inputs across societies -- so is "receptor" even the right role to assign them.

then the payoff: does the tolerant/relativist conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? and if it doesn't survive, sketch a better analogy -- something that keeps the part i actually buy, that intuitions come first and the reasoning is mostly post-hoc rationalization, without pre-deciding whether those intuitions are tracking anything real.
