I'm mid-essay on the Hart/Devlin debate and I've walked myself into an argument that I can no longer tell is clever or garbage, so I'm outsourcing the diagnosis. Here it is, numbered:

(1) A society isn't just a lot of people in the same place; it's a community of ideas, and what holds it together is a shared morality. Strip that out and you don't get a looser society, you get no society.
(2) Whatever a society needs in order to go on existing, it's entitled to secure by law -- even where nobody can point to a single individual who's been harmed.
(3) If a shared morality as such is necessary to a society's survival, then every strand of it is necessary, because there's no way to know in advance which strand is load-bearing: the moral fabric is a seamless web, and one thread pulled can unravel the lot.
(4) So any strand of the shared morality may be enforced by law.
(5) Hence a society whose shared morality says eating meat is a grave wrong may criminalise butchers and meat sales; one whose shared morality treats apostasy as the worst of crimes may criminalise leaving the faith; one that condemns usury may punish lenders.
(6) That's not acceptable. Those laws are unjust, and not because the societies in question were especially nasty -- it's that "our morality disapproves of this" isn't on its own a reason to make something illegal.
(7) So something in (1)-(3) is false, or (4) doesn't follow from them.

My tutor glanced at it and said I'd either got a duff premise or a duff inference and would I please work out which, so that was no help at all. I'd rather not loose (1) or (2) if I can help it. A friend reckons the fix is to keep (3) and just bolt on a filter: only the strands that are *load-bearing* get enforced, and she says load-bearing is definately testable -- decriminalise one conviction and see whether compliance with the others measurably drops. I can't tell whether that repairs the argument or just renames the hole in it, and I suspect it's Devlin's old "draw the line where the ordinary man's outrage runs high enough" with a spreadsheet stapled to it, but I'd like to be told.

So: which step do I cut, why did it look fine right up until someone pushed on it, and what do I pay for cutting it? If the honest answer is that (2) is the weak one rather than (3), make that case, because (2) is the one I'd least like to lose.
