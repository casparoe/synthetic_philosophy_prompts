Some months ago I read a blog post about an idea the author called evidential cooperation in large worlds, and it has been rattling around in my head ever since, mostly because I have nobody to argue with about it. My friends are patient but not interested, and the two people I did bring it up with dismissed the whole thing as science fiction. So I would like a careful account of how much of what the blogger said actually survives scrutiny.

The blogger's version went roughly like this. The universe is very large, and in it there are very many other agents, some of whom are working through their situation in much the same way I work through mine, thinking in the same terms, weighing the same kinds of reasons, drawing on the same evidence. Nothing I do can ever reach them and nothing they do can ever reach me; there is no signal, no trade route, no causal chain in either direction. Even so, the blogger said, because their deliberation runs along the same lines as mine, what I decide is evidence about what they will decide, and so when I choose to cooperate I raise the probability that they cooperate as well, while defecting lowers it. Since the gains from cooperation are collected across all those distant agents at once, cooperating can be worth it even though nobody I am cooperating with will ever be affected by me. On the blogger's telling this also means that those far-off agents must in the end care about the same things I care about, since they reason as I do, which makes the whole thing the golden rule with a proof attached. The blogger then added that in a universe this large everyone's choices are correlated with everyone else's, that the mathematics of this is settled, and that refusing to cooperate with strangers you will never meet is therefore simply irrational; the only people who resist the conclusion, the blogger said, are sentimental about common sense. Two things made me uneasy as I read. The phrase gains from trade suggests two parties who want different things and can nonetheless make each other better off, which does not sit well with the claim that they must care about what I care about. And the talk of raising and lowering probabilities sounded, at least to me, as though I were reaching across the universe and pushing on somebody's decision, which I take it cannot be the point.

What I want is an audit, claim by claim, rather than a summary of the idea. Tell me which parts a careful proponent of it would accept as fair, which are the sort of harmless shorthand a popularizer might use where a specialist would want to be more careful, and which are outright misrepresentations of what is being claimed. If any of the blogger's steps hold only under conditions the blogger left out, say what those conditions are. In particular I would like to know whether the sheer size of the universe is doing the work in the argument or whether something else is, and what exactly the large in the name is contributing. Then tell me whether the blogger's closing conclusion follows from the parts of the account that survive, and if it does not, what the corrected conclusion would look like. I am inclined to think the underlying mechanism, whatever it is, is a real one, and that the ending is far too strong; I would be glad to be told I am wrong about either. Please explain the ideas in your own words rather than pointing me at further reading, since I would rather understand the thing than collect sources.
