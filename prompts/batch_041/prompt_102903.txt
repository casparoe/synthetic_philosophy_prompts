I am putting together a paper for a graduate seminar on Marx and the philosophy of technology, and I should be very grateful for a critical reading of the draft that follows. The paper sets out to disentangle two claims that I believe are habitually run together in discussions of Marx on automation, and to argue that only one of them can bear the weight he places on it. My principal sources are the discussion of machinery in the first volume of *Capital* — including the well-known passage in which Marx argues that once the "general intellect" has become the dominant productive force, labour time ceases to be the measure of value and the value relation is thereby undermined — together with the so-called "Fragment on Machines". I take Marx's central insight to be that the machine is never a neutral instrument: under capital it is a form of capital, and its productivity is inseparable from its function in extracting surplus value. I also lean on the distinction between the formal and the real subsumption of labour, treating real subsumption as the stage at which capital takes over a labour process that it has not itself created, and formal subsumption as the later stage at which capital remakes the labour process in its own image. On that basis I then introduce a distinction of my own, between what I call the *displacement* and the *dissolution* of labour. By displacement I mean that workers are thrown out of employment while labour nevertheless remains the source of value; by dissolution I mean that labour ceases to be the measure of value at all. My contention is that Marx's polemic against the so-called compensation theory — the doctrine that machinery, though it displaces workers, re-employs an equal number of them elsewhere — succeeds only if it rests on dissolution, whereas Marx in fact argues for it as though displacement alone were sufficient. I conclude that this conflation is what leaves the polemic inconclusive, and that a cleaner separation of the two claims restores the radicalism of his position.

I should be glad of comments of two kinds in particular. First, on accuracy: I am anxious not to misrepresent Marx, so please flag any point at which my exegesis is loose, anachronistic, or attributes to him a view he did not hold, and say plainly what the correct attribution would be. Second, on interest: is the displacement/dissolution distinction a genuine contribution, or merely a restatement of something Marx already says more economically — and if the latter, which of his own formulations am I unwittingly reproducing? Please end with a short paragraph indicating the one or two revisions you would regard as most urgent, ranked in order of importance.
