Question 3 (20 points).

On Sunday, Beauty is told the following. A fair coin will be tossed. If it lands heads, she will be woken on Monday, interviewed, and the experiment will then end. If it lands tails, she will be woken and interviewed on Monday, put back to sleep with a drug that erases her memory of that waking, and woken and interviewed again on Tuesday. On every waking she finds herself in an identical room, with no way of telling which day it is or whether she has been woken before. On each waking she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads.

(a) State the two standard answers to this question and the central argument for each. (6 points)

(b) Suppose the protocol is amended: on each waking, before any question is put to her, the experimenter truthfully tells her what day it is. What should her credence that the coin landed heads be when she is told that it is Monday? Answer separately for each of the two positions you set out in (a), saying for each whether the credence moves, and why. What should her credence be if she is told that it is Tuesday? (8 points)

(c) Does the choice between the two answers make any difference to what Beauty ought to do — for example, to whether she ought to accept a bet on heads at even money that is offered to her on every waking, with the stake won or lost at each such waking? (6 points)

Student's answer:

(a) When Beauty is awake, she may be in any of three situations: the coin landed heads and this is her Monday waking; the coin landed tails and this is her Monday waking; or the coin landed tails and this is her Tuesday waking. The thirder answer, defended by David Lewis, holds that these three are subjectively indistinguishable and so should be given equal credence, which puts the credence in heads at 1/3. The halfer answer, associated with Adam Elga, holds that the credence should remain 1/2: on Sunday Beauty was already certain that she would be woken, so the waking itself tells her nothing about the coin, and since the coin's chance of landing heads is 1/2 and her evidence about the coin has not changed, the Principal Principle requires her credence to stay at 1/2. Both sides agree that the coin is fair and that the wakings are indistinguishable from the inside; what is at issue is what credence she should have while she is awake.

(b) If she is told that it is Monday, the thirder's credence rises from 1/3 to 1/2, since only two of the three situations remain and they are symmetric with respect to the coin; this is the same value the halfer assigned before the announcement. The halfer's credence does not move. For the halfer, the fact of waking carries no information about the coin, and being told that it is Monday does not change this: Monday is compatible with either outcome of the toss, so the announcement is not evidence about the coin and her credence stays at 1/2. If she is told that it is Tuesday, both answers give 0, since a Tuesday waking occurs only when the coin lands tails. The halfer's slogan that waking tells her nothing about the coin therefore does not mean that she can never learn anything about the coin from what happens during the experiment; it means only that the waking itself is uninformative.

(c) I take the two answers to be two descriptions of one and the same epistemic situation rather than competing claims about what Beauty should think, and in particular I do not think the choice between them has any consequences for action. The coin is fair, so a bet on heads at even money is a fair bet, and Beauty should be willing to accept it on either answer; what the thirder's 1/3 records is how the situation looks from the inside, where the chance of heads has not changed but the number of occasions on which she may be asked has. A halfer and a thirder may therefore describe her credence differently without disagreeing about how she should bet, and the real philosophical interest of the case lies in how we should talk about credence when the subject's evidence includes facts about her own location in the world.
