I write about science and ideas for a living, and I recently found myself at a workshop dinner seated between two people who had spent the afternoon in different sessions on the same theme — paradox and the concept of truth — and who resumed their argument over the pudding. I took notes on the back of a menu, and I would like a proper adjudication rather than a summary, because I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it.

The subject was an infinite list of sentences. The first says that every sentence after it in the list is untrue. The second says that every sentence after it is untrue. And so on, without end. There is no last sentence, and — this is the point — no sentence in the list refers to itself. Call them S1, S2, S3, and so on.

The first of my companions, whom I shall call Mira, argued as follows. (1) The list generates a paradox: no assignment of true and false to its sentences is consistent — if every sentence is false, then S1, which says that all the later ones are untrue, is true; and if at least one sentence is true, take the earliest true one, Sn; then every later sentence is untrue, so Sn+1 is untrue, yet since all the sentences after Sn+1 are likewise untrue, Sn+1 is true after all, which contradicts the previous step. So the list behaves exactly as the ordinary Liar does. (2) But no sentence in the list refers to itself. (3) Therefore self-reference is not what generates the Liar family of paradoxes; the real culprit lies elsewhere, and any theory that treats self-reference as the engine of the paradoxes has misidentified the mechanism and will tailor its remedies to the wrong fault.

My other companion, whom I shall call Ravi, would not have it. (1) He said the list is self-referential in the only sense that matters, namely circularity. (2) Each sentence's truth depends on the truth of all the sentences after it, so taken together the list's truth value is fixed only by reference to the list's own truth values; the dependency is a fixed point in disguise. (3) He granted that a merely non-well-founded reference chain is not yet circularity, but insisted the list is not merely non-well-founded: the content of each sentence is specified in terms of the totality of which it is itself a member. (4) So the example, he concluded, does not show that self-reference is dispensable; it shows only that people had too narrow a picture of what self-reference is.

Mira replied that this is a redefinition which saves the thesis by emptying it: if any ungrounded dependency is to count as self-reference, then 'self-reference' has simply been swapped for 'ungroundedness', and Ravi has conceded her substantive point while keeping his word. Ravi answered that this cuts both ways — that she is the one trading on a word, since the bare syntactic absence of a reflexive reference was never the interesting claim; the interesting claim concerns the structure of the dependency, and on that score the list is no different from the Liar. They then went round once more, and I lost the thread entirely.

I would like you to adjudicate move by move — rate each numbered move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say why — and then answer the larger question: does the infinite list actually establish that self-reference is inessential to the Liar family, or does it leave that thesis standing? Tell me what each of them is getting right, where each overreaches, and what both are missing about the relationship between self-reference, circularity and ungroundedness. A verdict on the whole dispute, and not merely on the individual moves, would be welcome.
