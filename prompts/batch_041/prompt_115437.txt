I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on technology and society, and what I need is a classification instrument rather than an essay. My students reliably collapse the question of whether artifacts have politics into a single opposition — either technology drives social change on its own, or everything about a technology is a social construction — and I want an instrument that exposes the structure they are flattening. Please construct a decision tree over the positions in this debate.

Build it from the logical space first. Begin with the questions that genuinely divide the positions, ordered so that each node can be answered by someone who has no idea which position it leads to; only then place the named positions at the leaves. Each branch should be labeled with the claim or question that separates its two subtrees, and each leaf should carry the name of a position (or a descriptive label, tagged as unnamed, if no one defends it) together with one sentence naming that position's most awkward commitment.

The distinctions I care most about, each of which should do real work in the tree:

- where the political effect is located — in the material form of the artifact itself, in the wider sociotechnical system that surrounds it (standards, infrastructure, ownership, maintenance, who is trained to operate it), or only in the uses to which it is put;
- whether the constraint an artifact imposes is independent of anyone's intentions, so that a technology can have political consequences no designer foresaw or wanted;
- whether the politics is intrinsic to the artifact or merely relative to contested social goals — whether the artifact settles a live political question or merely makes one option cheaper;
- whether the same function could always be achieved by a differently designed artifact with different political effects, or whether some functions can be realized only through one social arrangement;
- the direction of causation, including positions that reject a single direction altogether and treat the social and the technical as co-produced.

The following positions must appear as leaves: instrumentalism, on which artifacts are neutral tools and all politics resides in use; a weak and a strong version of technological determinism; the view that certain technologies are inherently political because they require, or are strongly compatible with, a particular social order; social constructivism, on which an artifact's political character is settled by the negotiations of interested groups before it stabilizes; the view that politics is delegated to things, so that artifacts act as quasi-agents enforcing rules their designers encoded; an affordance- or design-based view on which artifacts shape but do not determine behavior, biasing outcomes without fixing them; and a view on which the artifact as such has no politics and only whole assemblages do. Add any further position the logical space requires, and mark at least one combination of answers that no one occupies.

Use the following cases as the concrete matter of the tree, describing rather than assuming them where that helps: overpasses deliberately built too low to admit buses, so that a class of people cannot pass; the mechanized tomato harvester, developed to cut costs, whose adoption coincided with the displacement of farm workers; a voting or credit-scoring system whose built-in categories encode a prior political decision; and the seat belt, which saves lives only when people use it. For at least one case, say whether the tree's answer depends on contested historical facts.

Where the tree cannot separate two positions — because they answer every branch node identically — say so explicitly rather than papering over it, and explain why the two are nonetheless distinct. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Aim for between eight and twelve leaves, and do not let the tree exceed four levels of branching. Write for readers who know the debate but not its full topology. Finally, tell me which single consideration, if I accepted it, would force the largest revision of the tree's shape, and identify the one or two places where your construction is most likely to be wrong — a mislabeled position, a distinction that collapses under pressure, or a leaf that no one would actually occupy.
