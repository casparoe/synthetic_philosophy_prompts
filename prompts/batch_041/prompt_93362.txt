I'm retiring this year after 38 years teaching philosophy, and my department has volunteered me for the commencement address in May. I've got a problem I've been carrying around my whole career, and I want it to be the spine of the speech rather than something I mention.

Here's the thing. I believe a lot of stuff flatly. For about twenty years I've kept a notebook with the things I'm surest of written on the back page — eight plain claims, no hedging, written like facts. And on the front page, in the same pen, I've written: at least one of the things on the back page is false. I've never crossed one out. I don't think that notebook is stupid, and I'd like the graduates to walk out with the same posture.

So write me the address. Structurally I want:

- Seven or eight flat claims, in my voice, each stated as something I believe, not as something I merely suspect or "hold" or find plausible. Mix them up — some small and local (about this town, about teaching, about my brother), some big and general. Don't make them all epistemology-flavored.
- Somewhere in there, said plainly, that at least one of the eight is false and that I don't know which.
- The speech has to make those two things sit together as honesty rather than as mush. So no getting out of it by saying I'm not certain about any of them, no "these are just my opinions," no "nobody's perfect," and please don't have me put percentages on them — I'm not a 90%-sure guy in this speech, the whole point is that the claims are flat. If I say I believe it, I believe it.
- And this is the part I actually care about: say something about how a person is supposed to act tomorrow morning. These kids have to take the job, marry the person, move across the country, on individual claims, knowing the whole set has a dud in it somewhere. I want the speech to land on that, not just on the notebook.
- Don't use the word "paradox" and don't name any philosophers. It's about 300 parents and 19-year-olds in folding chairs, plus a dozen of my colleagues who will notice if I fudge it — and I'd rather they notice something correct than something announced.

Five minutes, spoken, no slides, and don't make me sound like I'm lecturing from a lectern, I'm in a heavy gown and its hot out there. Make the notebook do real work rather than sit there as a prop, and keep it off "the mystery of knowledge" and "the human condition" — concrete, please. Just give me the speech, don't explain what you did.
