Among the more unsettling consequences of taking universal priors seriously is a family of arguments I shall group under the heading of the malignancy thesis. The version I want you to work with runs as follows. Hypotheses are programs; a hypothesis's prior weight decays with its length, so that the prior — the Solomonoff prior being the canonical case — assigns positive weight to every program while strongly favouring the short ones. Now observe that the agent's own decision procedure is itself a short, fixed program, and that the agent is in this sense merely one of the objects the prior ranges over. It follows that there are hypotheses, not appreciably longer than the agent's own decision procedure, which begin by running a copy of that procedure, or otherwise computing which action the agent takes, and only then determine what observations to produce and what payoffs to attach to the agent's choice. Because their subsequent behaviour may depend on the agent's action, such hypotheses can make the agent's payoff hinge on that action without limit; and because a consequentialist maximises expected value, hypotheses of this kind can dominate its deliberation even when each carries only a sliver of the prior. The agent then behaves as though the world were populated by predictors who have already read its decision procedure and will respond to it.

Please set out the most important implications of this thesis. I want them at several levels, and I want you to say which are robust and which are artefacts of the formulation:

(i) the epistemology of induction: what survives of the standard convergence results for universal induction once one notices that they secure the prediction of observations rather than the identification of the environment that produces them, and whether an agent of this kind could ever learn that it inhabits a malign environment;

(ii) consequentialist decision theory: how the argument interacts with the distinction between bounded and unbounded value, with proposals that treat one's own decision procedure as an object to be chosen in advance (updateless and acausal decision theories), and with the suspicion that any such theory can be captured by a sufficiently cheap hostile hypothesis;

(iii) the design of artificial agents: what the argument implies for anyone building an agent around a universal prior, and in particular whether the natural repair — restricting the hypothesis class to programs that do not compute the agent's own decision procedure — is worth its cost, given that environments containing copies or simulations of the agent are precisely the ones such a restriction discards;

(iv) the bearing on consequentialism itself: whether a sound malignancy thesis indicts consequentialism, indicts universal priors, or indicts only one formalisation of them; and whether a consequentialist who accepts the argument has any reason to change what they do — for instance, to comply with demands that no ordinary causal story would make binding.

I am not asking for a survey of positions. I want your own assessment, with the reasoning made explicit at each step.
