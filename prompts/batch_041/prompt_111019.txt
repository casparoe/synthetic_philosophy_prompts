I am trying to settle a long-running argument with a colleague about why promises bind, and I want to pin down the analogy she keeps reaching for. Her claim is that a promise is essentially a precommitment device — the same trick as having oneself lashed to the mast so that the person one will become cannot undo what the person one now is has decided — and that this is not a loose comparison but the whole story: what makes a promise binding is exactly what makes the ropes binding, namely that the promisor has arranged things so that her later, weaker self cannot simply do as it likes. On her account nothing further is required: no transfer of a right, no appeal to the promisee's expectations, no distinctively moral bond over and above the mechanics of self-binding. She draws a further conclusion from the analogy, which is what I actually care about: since the point of the device is the promisor's own integrity over time, the promisee has an interest in performance but no claim on her, and breaking a promise is like a dieter eating the cake — a failure of self-management rather than a wrong done to somebody. The same picture is supposed to explain why certain promises appear to lapse (the point of the device can be defeated; if the sirens turn out not to exist, or the ship catches fire, staying lashed to the mast is mere folly) and why promises to those who cannot hold you to anything — a small child, someone who has died, someone who never learns the promise was made — look like the same case rather than a harder one.

I want an audit of the analogy rather than a refutation of it, and I want the verdict to turn on the details rather than on which side sounds nicer. Please work through the following, and stress-test the comparison against cases where self-binding and promising seem to come apart: a promise extracted under duress; a promise to do something seriously wrong; a promise whose performance has become pointless because the person it was for has died; a promise whose beneficiary has forgotten it; a promise made by someone who has since changed beyond recognition; and a pair of mutual promises, where each party is both bound and benefited.

Give me your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. First, the correspondence table: what in the precommitment story plays the role of the promise, the promisor's later self, the mast, the ropes, the crew who tie them, the sirens, and the ship. Second, what the analogy genuinely gets right about promising — the features the two cases really do share, stated precisely enough that I can see why it has seemed compelling. Third, the disanalogies that matter for her conclusion, in particular those that bear on whether the promisee has a claim rather than merely an interest, and on whether breach wrongs anyone. Fourth, the disanalogies that do not matter for her conclusion — the differences that are real but idle, and why they can be set aside. Fifth, the verdict: which parts of her position survive the audit, which do not, and whether there is a better analogy available for the bindingness of promises, or whether the honest answer is that no single comparison will carry the weight she wants it to.
