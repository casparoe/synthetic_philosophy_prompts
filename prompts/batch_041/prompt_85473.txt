Ok, I want to practise arguing with Augustine of Hippo about lying, so please play him for me. Play the real one, not the stern cartoon: first person, in character, a paragraph or two per turn, and stay in character until I say 'stop'. Rules for the character. He holds that no lie is ever permitted — not to save a life, not to keep someone else from sinning, never — though he's perfectly happy to rank lies by how bad they are and to insist that some are far lighter than others. He can bring in scripture and his own treatises, but he should argue rather than cite, and paraphrase rather than try to reproduce anyone's exact wording; I care about the reasoning, I'm not checking his footnotes. He can concede details, distinctions, even whole sub-points, but not the main one. He can ask me a factual question in character if he needs one. He doesn't get to step outside the role to explain what he's doing, he doesn't get to say 'a modern reader would object that...', and no latin showboating, I don't read it. He's a bishop in Roman North Africa, let him argue from there.

Before you write the reply, think it through to yourself: sketch two or three lines the character could take, then pick the one he'd actually stake his name on and commit to it. Don't show me the sketch, and definately don't give me a survey of options with the pros and cons of each — I want one definite answer with reasons. If you think my case is rigged or that I've got him wrong, say so and then answer it anyway.

My opening move: I'm a doctor and my patient will die in his fifties unless he stops drinking. He asks me straight out whether his father, who died at the same age, died of drink. He did. If I tell him the truth he comes apart and drinks himself into the grave inside a year — I've watched him fall apart over smaller news than this. If I tell him it was his heart, he takes the diagnosis seriously and gets thirty more years. And before you tell me to keep silent, or to refuse to answer, or to say something true that he'll misread: he'll read silence as a yes, he'll read 'I won't discuss your father with you' as a yes too, and he'll keep asking until I make a sound, so the truth gets out either way. The true-but-misleading sentence is closed off by your own account as well, because you put the fault of the lie in the desire to deceive, and if I pick my true sentence precisely so that he draws the false conclusion then I've got the desire. So it's tell the truth and kill him, or lie and save him. I don't want the rule, I want to know what you think actually happens in that room, and why God would rather have a dead man than one false sentence. Also: the midwives in Exodus lie straight to Pharaoh to save the babies and God rewards them for it — and don't tell me he was rewarding their mercy and not their lie, because that's the move, and it leaves the doctor standing in front of me either way.

Go on then.
