I'm a sixth-year philosophy PhD student and a longtime reader of the rationalist blogosphere — LessWrong, the old Overcoming Bias archives, the whole Slate Star Codex-era ecosystem — and I've absorbed enough of the standard moves (steelman the other side before you touch it, taboo your terms, ask what would change your mind, argue from the least convenient possible world) that I now find most conference Q&A underpowered in a way that's almost insulting. Next month I present my dissertation chapter at our department's work-in-progress seminar, and I want to walk in having already fought the battle. The chapter defends a reading of Nāgārjuna's catuṣkoṭi — the tetralemma, the four corners: that a thing is P, that it is not-P, that it is both, that it is neither — that I take to be simultaneously deflationary and defusing. My claim is that the four corners are not four truth-values, and that Madhyamaka is not quietly running a non-classical logic (not a truth-value gap, not a glut). I argue instead that the fourth corner is a presupposition-cancelling move: once you notice that all four answers share a presupposition — that there is a thing with a determinate nature (svabhāva) for the predicate to attach to — the question collapses rather than resolves, the way "is the number seven green or not green?" collapses. On this reading the self-referential worry that dogs the tradition (if everything is empty, isn't emptiness itself empty? doesn't the injunction to abandon all views refute itself?) dissolves, because "empty" is being used predicatively only under a presupposition the argument has already removed; the master claim is not a thesis in the sense that would need to be either empty or not.

The audience is the real problem, and it splits badly. There will be maybe nine of us. Two are formal logicians: one works on non-classical logics and is a committed dialetheist who will hear my "neither" and immediately demand to know why I'm not just doing paraconsistency with extra steps, and why I'm so confident the fourth corner isn't a plain truth-value gap — which, he'll note, is exactly what a category mistake looks like on a supervaluationist treatment of presupposition failure. The other is a proof theorist who will want to know precisely what my object language and metalanguage are and will not accept hand-waving about "the question collapsing." Then there's a Kantian-leaning analytic metaphysician who thinks this is all the antinomies with worse prose and will ask what work "presupposition" is doing that "category mistake" wasn't already doing. And finally two people from Asian studies who read Sanskrit and Tibetan and are allergic to what they'll call analytic appropriation: they'll want to know which Nāgārjuna and which text, whether I'm quietly collapsing the distinction between the Prāsaṅgika (consequence-only) and Svātantrika (autonomous-reasoning) approaches, what I make of the commentarial tradition's insistence that this whole apparatus is soteriological rather than a contribution to the philosophy of language, and whether my framing is simply a modern imposition on a tradition that never asked these questions.

Please write me my Q&A prep sheet. I don't want a summary of the scholarly debate and I certainly don't want a defense of my position — I want to know what this specific room is going to throw at me. Give me the objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that particular person would actually phrase it (the dialetheist's version of a worry is not the Buddhologist's version, and the proof theorist's is not the Kantian's), each with a reply I could plausibly deliver in two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objections to concede up front because contesting them costs more than they're worth, which to hold the line on, and — the one I'm dreading — the question I'm hoping nobody asks but that at least one of the two Sanskritists is going to ask anyway. No citations or reading lists; I just want the fight choreography.
