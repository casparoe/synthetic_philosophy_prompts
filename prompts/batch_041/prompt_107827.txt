I teach a seminar on philosophy of mind and AI, and the session on whether machines can have beliefs keeps collapsing into vocabulary: one student says the model genuinely believes it is raining, another says that is only a useful way of talking, and after twenty minutes it turns out that neither has asserted anything the other denies. I want to run the next session off a single handout: a decision tree that takes a participant from a starting commitment to a named position on whether an artificial system can literally have beliefs, and that sorts people by their answers rather than by the labels they like. Build the tree from the logical space outward — the questions first, then the positions that occupy the resulting combinations — instead of listing the famous views in a row, and make every branch node a question about a case rather than about a doctrine, so that someone can walk the tree without having read any of the literature. The starting commitment should be the weakest one everyone in the room will grant, the one the intentional stance elevates into a whole theory: that treating a system as if it had beliefs is sometimes the best way to predict and explain what it does.

From there the tree should branch on the distinctions that actually do the work: whether the question is about a state of the system or about the practice of attributing; whether an attributed belief must be an internal state with a correctness condition, or whether the attribution is what makes it a belief at all; whether, when two incompatible attributions predict equally well, there is a fact of the matter about which is correct; whether a system's causal history, engineered rather than learned, bears on what it believes; whether a state that no process ever reads, and that therefore makes no difference to anything the system does, can be a belief; and whether a system must be able to represent the attributions made to it before any of them can be true. Use at least these five cases as diagnostic nodes, since they are what pull the positions apart: (1) a lookup table storing an enormous list of input-output pairs that behaves exactly as a reasoning model does, so that the two cannot be told apart from the outside; (2) a reinforcement-learning agent whose learned world model encodes the layout of a maze and is consulted when it plans, but which never says anything about the maze and could not report what it encodes; (3) a system that says it believes the meeting is on Tuesday only because the user's prompt cast it as a character and that line was the likely continuation, with nothing else in its behaviour depending on any such state; (4) a representation written into an internal store that no process ever reads, so that it is inert; (5) a system internally and behaviourally identical to (2) but hand-assembled by an engineer rather than learned.

For each leaf, give the position a name if it has one and mark it unnamed if nobody defends it, add one sentence on what the position commits you to and one on its most awkward commitment, and where two different views land on the same answers, keep them distinct only if they genuinely come apart on one of the cases — otherwise flag the pair as indistinguishable by this tree. Note any combination of answers that no position occupies, and say whether you think the reason is that the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Aim for six to nine leaves. Please keep it free of citations and reading lists: I want the positions stated by what they claim rather than by who held them, and I want a student who has read nothing to be able to walk the tree by answering the case questions.
