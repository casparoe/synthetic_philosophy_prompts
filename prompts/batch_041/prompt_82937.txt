So my friend Mark and I got into it for like two hours last night about whether you can even make a moral judgement without having a rule in your back pocket. He's a generalist through and through — thinks that if you say "that was wrong," you're implicitly applying some principle even if you can't state it. I lean particularist (Dancy is the name I always half-remember), because reasons seem to shift with context, and not in a way you can just write into the rule without the rule turning into a monster. We've argued about this alot.

Anyways, I want to run an activity for our department's philosophy society next month: 16 people, mostly second and third years, one 75-minute session. I want them to feel the thing rather than hear me lecture about it. Here's the rough shape I have, but I want you to design the actual thing — rules, materials, roles, timeline, debrief, the whole deal — and fix what's broken in my version:

Everyone gets a card with a consideration on it — "it was a lie," "someone got hurt," "it was done to protect a kid," "it was what everyone around them was doing," that sort of thing. I read out a short case, and each person has to physically move to one of three zones: counts for, counts against, or doesn't count at all. Same card, different case, different zone. Then — and this is the part I really care about — a few people get modifier cards that don't add any weight on their own but switch another card on or off. Some philosophers call these "enablers," I think. Like "the promise was made under duress" doesn't itself count against anything; it just means the fact that it was a promise stops being a reason at all. And some cards should turn the volume up or down rather than on or off. Maybe give one person the job of playing the generalist and having to defend the rulebook as it gets built.

Then at the end the group has to write the rulebook: principles that would predict where every card lands in every case. They should be the ones who discover what that rulebook turns into.

What I want them to walk out seeing: that the same feature genuinely changes valence across cases, that some features do real work without carrying any weight, and that the rulebook either becomes unmanageably hedged or starts predicting the wrong zones on a fresh case they haven't seen.

Please include a debrief that maps what actually happened in the room onto the philosophical machinery, a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time (I've never ran anything like this), and be honest about which part of the particularist picture the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself. And since I'm the one standing at the front: tell me where you think this design is most likely to fail, what I'd see in the room that would make you think the game isn't actually capturing the point, and what would change your mind about the design.
