I work as a researcher for a legislative standards committee, where I prepare the factual findings on complaints that a minister misled the house or the public. The committee treats lying and merely misleading statements differently — the first can end a career, the second usually draws a reprimand — so my finding is not academic. I work from the transcript, the minister's written answer, and the public record; I never get to ask what the minister intended, and the minister has every reason to describe his own intention favourably.

Write me a decision procedure I can run on a single statement in about half an hour. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a finding, and where it must stop and hand the matter to the committee because what remains is judgment rather than fact. Ground it in the philosophical work on the lying/misleading distinction — the roles of falsity, of assertion, of the intention to deceive, and of what is conveyed without being said — but write it so that a colleague who has never read that work can apply it. Our committee currently uses a rule of thumb I distrust: a statement is misleading if, substituting the relevant true fact for what was said, a reasonable listener's confidence in the false proposition would fall sharply. Tell me whether that survives contact with the philosophy, and what should replace it.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a minister who said that no decision had been made, when a decision in principle had been taken but not announced; a minister who said he had never intervened in "the process," meaning a narrower process than the questioner meant; and a minister who answered a question about one policy by discussing another. At least one should come out in a way the committee will not like. If the procedure cannot reliably tell a lie from a misleading statement under the conditions I actually work in, say so plainly rather than handing me a checklist that pretends to settle what it cannot, and where the procedure quietly takes a side on whether the moral difference between lying and misleading is real, flag it.
