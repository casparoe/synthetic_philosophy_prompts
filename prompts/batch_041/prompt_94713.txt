I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I have spent the better part of a year trying to finish a piece about the Anthropocene — not about whether human beings have become a planetary force, which seems beyond dispute, but about the peculiar fact that a term in universal circulation failed to clear the formal bar that would have made it an official division of the geological time scale. As I understand the proposal, it would have made the Anthropocene a series and epoch with its base at a level in the annually layered sediments of a lake in Ontario corresponding to 1952, the primary marker being the sharp upturn in plutonium from atmospheric nuclear testing, supported by a few auxiliary reference sites elsewhere. The bodies that govern the time scale declined to ratify it, and the official position is that we continue to live in the Meghalayan Age of the Holocene. The reasons offered for the refusal were various, and I would rather set them aside than relitigate them here. What interests me is the machinery itself.

Consider how a geological period gets made. A working group is convened, sites are drilled and compared, and eventually a single physical point in a single reference sequence is nominated — what stratigraphers call the golden spike — chosen because some marker at that level can in principle be recognized by anyone, anywhere on earth, and because that marker is as close to globally simultaneous as the record allows. The proposal then travels up a chain of committees, needing supermajorities at each stage, before a final ratification by the international union that oversees the scale. A unit is thus known by its base rather than by its contents: you establish that you are in a given epoch by locating a point in rock, not by deducing it from a theory of what caused what. Now, my puzzlement. If periods are real features of the world, the sort of thing a stratigrapher discovers rather than legislates, what is a vote doing in the middle of it, and what would it mean for the vote to come out wrong? If instead these units are instruments — administrative partitions of a continuous record, adopted for their usefulness — why does the procedure insist on a globally synchronous marker when much of the existing time scale is anchored to the first appearance of a particular fossil species in a particular outcrop, a boundary that is by its nature diachronous, contingent on preservation, and liable to move if someone finds an older specimen somewhere else? I cannot tell whether the Anthropocene was held to a stricter standard than its neighbours or the same standard applied with more nerve, and I would like to know whether that asymmetry is a genuine inconsistency in the practice or merely the appearance of one.

So what I want from you is the implicit theory of the practice, reconstructed and then audited. What conception of a period does this procedure presuppose — that periods are natural kinds with real boundaries, that they are conventions fixed by communal decision, or something in between that the procedure cannot distinguish? Does it presuppose that a boundary must be a material point rather than a process, and that the question of when the Anthropocene began therefore has exactly one correct answer, or does the procedure leave that open? Which rival views does it quietly rule out — say, that the Anthropocene is perfectly real but gradual and diachronous, or that a period might legitimately be defined by the social and economic changes that produced the strata rather than by the strata themselves — and which rivals does it in fact tolerate, so that the practice turns out to be underdetermined between several theories? Is the insistence on a single reference point consistent with the rest of the time scale, and if not, which of the two is the anomaly? Finally, suppose one abandoned the framework and treated the Anthropocene as an ongoing geological event rather than a bounded interval, or as an interpretive tool that no committee could ratify or revoke: what in the practice would have to change, and what would be lost? I would like you to say, claim by claim, how confident you are, and to single out the one point in your reconstruction you are least sure about.
