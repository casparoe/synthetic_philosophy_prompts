I write about medicine, law, and ideas for a general readership, and I keep running into a cluster of arrangements that look coherent until you set them side by side.

Start with the instructions a competent adult leaves about her own future medical care. In many jurisdictions, if I sign a document refusing a specific treatment — dialysis, a blood transfusion, ventilation — while I have capacity, that refusal stays binding after I lose capacity, and clinicians are expected to let me die even if, in the fog of dementia or delirium, I now seem to want the treatment. The directive is the last word. But the converse is not true. I generally cannot, in advance, oblige anyone to keep me on a ventilator, or to provide a treatment a clinician regards as futile, or to help me die; advance requests for treatment bind no one, and the jurisdictions that permit assisted dying have generally insisted on a fresh, contemporaneous request at the end, with the rare written-advance-request exceptions fiercely contested.

Now set beside that the devices we use for self-control. Gambling regulators and operators run self-exclusion schemes that bar a person from casinos and betting sites; someone can put herself on the list and have it enforced against her later self, with consequences for her if she tries to play anyway. Many such schemes, however, build in a delay: you cannot lift the ban the day you ask; you must wait days, weeks, or months. And then consider the contracts the law will not enforce at all — you cannot sell yourself into slavery, cannot consent now to being killed later, and in most places cannot irrevocably sell a kidney, no matter how informed and competent the earlier you was.

So the law lets my earlier self forbid but not command; it binds my later self against her apparent will, but not completely and not everywhere; and it refuses to enforce the very contracts that would most resemble a sale of my future autonomy. I suspect the whole apparatus presupposes a position about what makes a person's will authoritative over time — about whether the signer of the document and the patient in the bed count, for these purposes, as the same agent, and if they do, why the earlier one wins.

I want that position reconstructed, in plain prose, for a reader who is comfortable with ideas but not with the literature. What theory of the authority of the earlier self does this cluster of practices presuppose? Is it a claim about personal identity — that the two are one enduring person, so the earlier choice simply is her choice? A claim about autonomy as a kind of sovereign plan that later selves are bound to honor? Something about protecting the self against its own episodic weakness? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out? Does it presuppose one consistent theory, or does it need several at once? And where is it underdetermined — where would incompatible theories license exactly what we already do? I want the load-bearing commitments separated from the decoration: what the practice strictly requires, and what it merely fits comfortably with.

Then take the alternatives seriously. If we replaced the presupposed view with the claim that only the present, currently competent self has authority over her own body and treatment, what would have to change — which practices would survive, which would collapse, and what would take their place? If instead we made the earlier self fully sovereign, what would follow? Reason it out rather than pointing me to authorities; I am after the structure of the commitments, not a reading list.
