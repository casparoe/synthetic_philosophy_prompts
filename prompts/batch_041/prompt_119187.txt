I am not a philosopher by training, but I have been trying to think through what panpsychism would actually imply for ethics if it turned out to be true. The version I first encountered says that experience or something like experience is a basic feature of fundamental physical entities: each particle, or each field excitation, has its own tiny inner aspect. A second version seems to locate the fundamental subject at the level of the universe as a whole, so that individual minds are not separate little experiencers but more like local differentiations of one cosmic experience. I would like you to compare these two versions specifically with respect to suffering. If the first is right, suffering may be scattered across an enormous number of micro-subjects; if the second is right, suffering may belong to a single vast subject, with our pains being analogous to localized disturbances in it. Which version gives a more coherent account of why suffering matters ethically? Does one make the problem of evil more or less tractable, or make it easier to say that the total amount of suffering is either infinite or zero? And what would each version imply for how we should think about our treatment of other humans, animals, and even fundamental physical entities?

I am also curious whether the comparison changes when the underlying physics changes. If the fundamental entities are not little particles but fields, or if the basic ontology is relational rather than object-like, does the first version of panpsychism still make sense, or does it push us toward the second? In your answer, please treat both versions as serious metaphysical options rather than as obviously absurd, and be explicit about where the ethical reasoning is doing work that the physics cannot settle. I would prefer a comparison that stays close to the details of the two positions and draws out their different implications for suffering, rather than a general summary of panpsychism.
