I am assembling a short set of jokes to open a graduate seminar session on social learning, and the audience will know the literature on information cascades and rational herding well enough to be irritated by a joke that gets the mechanism wrong. Write me one joke — setup and punchline, under 150 words, in American English — whose humor turns on the actual structure of a cascade rather than on the familiar observation that people are sheep.

The setup must be a genuine sequential-choice situation: each person holds a private signal of limited precision, observes the actions but not the signals of those who went before, and decides in turn. The humor has to come from something that follows from that structure — for instance, that the crowd's unanimity carries no more information than the first couple of actions, that the private information of everyone after that point never enters the public record, that the copying is reputational rather than informational, done by people who privately disagree, or that the whole arrangement can reverse on a trivial public event. Set the joke among people who already know how cascades work; it should not depend on their being naive, and it should not be funny merely because the characters are foolish.

Do not build the joke around the names of researchers or papers; I want it to work for someone who knows the ideas but not the citations, and a reader who knows the theory should laugh rather than wince.

After the joke, add two or three sentences, clearly separated from it, naming the exact feature of the theory the punchline depends on and stating briefly why the setup actually licenses it.
