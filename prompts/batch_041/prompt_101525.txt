I am in my final year of secondary school and writing an extended project on fakes and forgeries in art, and my supervisor says my chapter on why originals matter reads as a summary of positions I have found rather than a map of the debate. Please write me a taxonomy, in the form of a tree, of the positions on the question of what, if anything, makes an original work of art more valuable than a copy that is, to any possible inspection, indistinguishable from it.

Build the tree out of the logical space first: each branch should be labelled with the question that separates it from its sibling, phrased so that someone could answer it without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf should carry the name of the position together with one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

I would like the diagnostic questions to turn on concrete cases rather than abstract slogans. Use, among others: a celebrated painting that turns out to have been made by a skilled forger; an openly advertised replica that is a better likeness of the artist's intention than the damaged original it copies; a work whose original has been destroyed and of which only a copy survives; a canvas so heavily restored that almost none of the original paint remains; a work executed largely by assistants but signed and sold as the master's own; and a piece generated by an algorithm that has no single original at all.

Three things I need you to be honest about: which combinations of answers the tree leaves empty, and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive or merely unexplored; which positions the tree cannot separate from one another, and what case would be needed to prise them apart; and which of my cases are doing no diagnostic work, because every position answers them in the same way.

Please write it in British English, and keep it to something I could actually put in a chapter — around eight to ten leaves.
