I'm in a small reading group that's been working through the modern Stoicism stuff — one week it's the Epictetus handbook, the next week it's a self-help book telling us to keep a gratitude journal and only stress about the things we can control. The move I keep tripping over is the core one, where you sort everything in life into what's up to you and what isn't, and then you're supposidly only meant to care about the first pile. Everyone nods along but I don't think anyone in the group could actually tell me what makes something land in that first pile. Your own judgments, fine — but then why not your actions, your habits, your charater, since those seem to come out of your judgments? And the self-help version adds a middle pile (stuff you only partly control, like whether you win the tennis match), which honestly feels like it just gives up on the whole idea.

So before I start arguing with anyone about it, write me the desiderata for a theory of this split. What does any adequate account of the up-to-me vs not-up-to-me distinction have to deliver? I want the cases it has to get right (the tennis match, a stab of grief you can't switch off, a habit you've been building for years, someone else's bad behaviour, whether you'll be alive next year), the distinctions it can't collapse (being up to me vs being mine vs being my responsibility vs being something I can be praised for; and the difference between the theory being true and the theory being useful for living), and the background commitments it has to stay consistent with — especially the awkward fact that the Stoics thought everything was fated and woven into a single causal chain, so up to us plainly can't mean uncaused or not caused by me. There's also the lazy-argument thing where if it's fated that I recover then bothering to call the doctor is pointless; I'd like the theory to say precisely where that inference goes wrong. And it has to say something about the involuntary stuff — the flush or the flinch that even the wise person has — without either pretending those are choices or letting them count as full-blown emotions.

Then flag the part I most want to see: which of these requirements pull against each other, i.e. which pair can't be satisfied together, because I suspect the demand that the up-to-me pile be totally immune to luck and the demand that it still be rich enough to build a life out of are not both had at once. And at the end, a few lines on how the plain two-pile version, the three-pile version, and the reading where it was never about control at all but about what counts as your own doing each fare against the list. Skip anything about whether modern Stoicism is good for your mental health, I don't care about that part.
