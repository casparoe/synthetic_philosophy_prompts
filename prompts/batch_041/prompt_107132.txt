I am convening a graduate seminar on responsibility and punishment, run jointly with our law faculty, and I want your help preparing a session on a move that recurs in the literature and, more insistently, in the arguments of a colleague of mine in the law school.

His opening gambit is an analogy he plainly takes to be decisive. Wrongdoing, he says, is like infectious disease. The offender is like a carrier; the harmful conduct is like the symptoms; and the confinement we actually practice is like quarantine. The point of quarantine, he continues, is not that the carrier deserves it — we quarantine the exposed but still healthy, who have done nothing at all, and we no more blame a carrier for the pathogen than we blame a patient for a fever. What justifies confinement is danger and nothing else. Extend the picture, he says, and the rest falls into place: upbringing and moral education are like vaccination; a settled law-abiding character is like immunity; a law-abiding society is like herd immunity; and criminology is simply the epidemiology of the whole business. His conclusion he states with some relish: once the criminal law is seen on this model, the vocabulary of desert has no work left to do, and the long quarrel between those who, like Dennett, want to retain some notion of just deserts and those who, like Caruso, want to jettison it simply dissolves — the retributivist, on this view, turns out to be practising quarantine while nursing a grudge.

I am less confident than he is, but I do not want to answer him with a gesture. Please audit the analogy rather than the conclusion. Specifically:

1. Lay out the correspondence scheme explicitly — a table of correspondences is welcome — stating what in the criminal-justice target is supposed to map onto the pathogen, the carrier, the symptoms, quarantine, vaccination, immunity, herd immunity, and epidemiology, and flagging any item where the mapping is forced, equivocal, or silently does two jobs.

2. Identify where the analogy breaks. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the use he is making of it — showing that desert is dispensable and that the Dennett–Caruso dispute dissolves — from the ones that are merely picturesque and can be set aside.

3. Say what the analogy smuggles in: which features of the disease-and-quarantine source are quietly imported into the criminal-justice target that the target may lack, or lack in the same degree. Consider, among others, the asymmetry of moral status between carrier and pathogen; the assumption that dangerousness is measurable, stable, and predictable; the temporariness, narrow scope, and routine indemnification of real quarantine; and the difference between a carrier, who is a locus of transmission, and an agent, who can be addressed and can answer.

4. Then the payoff question: does the analogy, correctly drawn, actually favour Caruso's forward-looking side, Dennett's side, or neither? Dennett locates the threshold for desert in the capacity to be moved by reasons, and treats those who lack that capacity — the severely impaired, the very young, the non-human — as patients to be managed rather than interlocutors to be answered. If the offender is a reason-responder, is he a carrier, or is he something the disease model has no room for? Does the analogy genuinely support the conclusion that desert drops out, or does it instead presuppose the very distinction Dennett is pressing?

5. If the disease model is not the best analogy available, propose a better one — one that preserves whatever is sound in my colleague's point about danger-based, non-retributive confinement while leaving room for the offender's standing as a participant in the practice of giving and asking for reasons — and say what the better analogy buys and what it costs.
