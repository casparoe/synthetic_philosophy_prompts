I'm about halfway through a novel and I've hit a snag I can't write my way out of. My narrator is a composer in her late fifties who, a few years before the book opens, signed one of those advance directives refusing certain treatments if she ever loses capacity; by the time we meet her she's lost it, she's cheerful as anything, and she now wants the exact opposite of what she asked for. I've been reading around the bioethics of all this to keep myself honest, and everyone seems to argue from one case — the happy repudiator, the woman who signed while depressed, the patient who wants treatment the family doesn't — and then generalise shamelessly. Before I read any more of it I want the full spec first. So: what would a theory of the authority of the earlier self actually have to deliver? Give me the desiderata — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the things we know about people that it can't contradict — with the consideration behind each one, and tell me which are non-negotiable and which are just the house style of a particular camp. I'm especially interested in the line between treating the directive as testimony about what she would have wanted and treating it as an exercise of authority that survives her change of mind, because I think my whole book turns on it and I dont know which side I'm on.

And since the thing keeps spilling out of the hospital, I'd be grateful if the list stretched to the non-medical versions of the same problem: a directive about a manuscript, a letter to be opened after death, a promise made to a future self who never turned up, instructions about how one's papers or one's body are to be handled. I suspect the medical case is a special case of something much bigger, and that the desiderata shift when the earlier self is dead rather than merely absent — if you think that's wrong, say why, because I'd rather know now than in chapter nine. What I'm really after is the part everyone skips: which single requirement knocks out the most popular answers, and whether there's a pair of requirements on the list that can't both be satisfied, in which case the list itself is the interesting result and I'll take it as the moral of the book. Don't bother scoring the theories against it, I'll do that myself with my sister, who's a nurse and has no patience for any of it.
