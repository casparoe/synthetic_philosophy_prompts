I've somehow ended up running the philosophy-of-maths strand for our undergrads — 18 of them, 80 minutes, every other week, and they've all done some algebra and zero philosophy. What got me here: my nephew asked me at his birthday party whether mathematicians make up numbers or find them, and I heard myself give a total nothing of an answer, then realised later I couldn't have done better without just reciting the standard positions. Thats what I want to avoid for the students. I don't want them able to list the positions, I want them to have felt the pull of both and walked into the wall themselves. So: no lecture up front, nothing named until the debrief.

Here's what I've sketched. Please build it into something I can actually run.

Setup: tables shoved into pairs, one sheet of paper per pair, a big sheet taped to the wall, sealed envelopes, poker chips (no money).

Phase 1, invent (15 min). Each pair gets four symbols — I'm giving different pairs different symbol sets, shapes, letters, whatever — and fills in a 4x4 table for an operation they make up, with exactly one constraint from me: every row and every column must contain each symbol exactly once. They write their table up on the wall.

Phase 2, collide (10 min). Pairs swap tables with a neighbour and hunt for a dictionary: a relabelling of one symbol set onto the other that turns one table into the other. Some pairs will find one, some won't. That asymmetry is the whole point, so don't smooth it over.

Phase 3, find out (20 min). Sealed envelopes, my tables this time, same constraints, again different symbol sets per pair. Inside each: three conjecture cards — bracketing doesn't matter, order of inputs doesn't matter, plus one invented law — and three chips. Before checking anything, they stake their chips on true or false in their own table. Wrong stakes go in my jar, right ones come back doubled. Then they check by hand, exhaustively. At least one card that the whole room stakes on will turn out false, and there'll be a single cell that does it. (Obviously check the truth values of every card against your tables before printing, or the betting is degenerate.)

Phase 4, sort (10 min). The room has to sort all the envelopes into families of tables that are relabellings of one another. Include at least one pair where the dictionary exists but is genuinely annoying to find, so they argue about whether those two belong together. Argument only, no help from me. Then I reveal, and hand dictionaries to the pairs that have them and not the others.

Debrief (25 min). I want the run of questions that gets them from what just happened to the invented-or-discovered fork, plus the mapping from each moment onto each standard position: which moment felt like the objects are real and out there, which felt like we just wrote symbols on paper, which moment is the middle view where the object is the position in the structure rather than any of the tokens, and which moment is where the access problem bites hardest. I want them to notice that they were surest about the theorems they didn't put in.

Two more things. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it — I'm betting the kid who knows what a Latin square is will anounce it in minute two and flatten everything, and I have no idea what to do about laptops. And be honest with me about what the activity can't reproduce. I already suspect it can't touch the epistemology, because in my version the structures always have builders, and I don't see how to fix that without wrecking it.
