I have no academic training in philosophy and have never studied it formally; I read it on my own and have been trying to work out an idea of my own about the self. I would like your comments on the draft below, which is the idea as I currently have it.

The idea I keep returning to is that the self is a center of narrative gravity. On this picture the self is not an extra thing inside the head, over and above a person's body and history; it is a point that a certain kind of story has, namely the story someone tells about their own life, in the way that a physical object has a center of gravity. The center of gravity is not a part of the object, and you will not find it by cutting the object open, yet it is perfectly real in the sense that matters: it is where the object's behavior can be predicted from, and it moves when the object moves. I take the analogy to mean that the self is real but abstract, and that it is the product of narration rather than its source.

Most people who use the analogy seem to stop there. I want to push it, and I think two consequences follow that I have not seen spelled out, though I am unsure whether they are genuine consequences or whether I am stretching the metaphor past breaking.

The first is that a story has a self only if the story loops back on itself. Any biography has a protagonist, so any biography has a center of narrative gravity in a trivial sense; but we would not say that a biography of Napoleon has a self. What is missing is that in a person's own story the protagonist is also the narrator, and the narrating is itself one of the events in the story. The self, then, would be a fixed point of the operation of telling: the thing the story is about is the thing that is doing the telling. I suspect this looping, rather than the mere having of a story, is what does the work, and that it may explain why selves feel at once real and strangely transparent.

The second is that if a self is a center of narrative gravity, then selves should come in degrees and should be able to merge and split, the way centers of gravity in physics can. A couple married for fifty years, or a band that has played together for decades, may tell a story whose protagonist is neither member but the pair; if the self just is the center of the story, there may be one self with two bodies for a stretch of time, and it may divide again later. This suggests that the usual question of personal identity — whether the person now is the same person as the person ten years ago — may be the wrong question. The interesting question may instead be how many narrative centers there are at a given time, and that number may change while the bodies do not.

I worry about two things. One is that I may simply be redescribing something ordinary, since people already say that marriages and bands have a life of their own, only now in fancier terms. The other is that if selves come in degrees, then things I take for granted — that a person is one thing, and that blame and rights attach to individuals — may not survive, and I cannot tell whether that is a discovery or a reductio of the view.

Please give me exactly five comments, numbered, each a sentence or two, and nothing else: no introduction and no summary at the end. I would like them to cover, in whatever order you think best, whether the looping claim is coherent and whether it really distinguishes a self from a mere biography; whether the merging-and-splitting claim follows from the analogy or whether I am equivocating on what a center of gravity is; whether either claim is already a familiar position dressed up, so that I am less original than I think; the strongest objection to the whole picture; and whether my worry about blame and rights is a reductio of the view or a bullet I should bite. Please be blunt about where the reasoning is loose. I do not need references or further reading; I can track those down myself.
