I am a senior at a public high school, and my classmates have voted for me to give the commencement address at our graduation this June. I am asking you to write that speech. I want it built around Maxwell's demon and the thermodynamics of information, and I want the physics to constrain what the speech is permitted to promise rather than serve as a decorative metaphor for perseverance.

Since I have no way to look anything up while I write, let me set out the physics I want the speech to rest on, so that we are working from the same picture. Maxwell imagined a being who guards a small door between two chambers of gas and opens it only to let fast molecules pass one way and slow molecules the other, so that one chamber grows hotter and the other colder with no work being done — apparently a violation of the second law of thermodynamics. The standard resolution is not that the demon is clumsy, or that measuring molecules is merely difficult in practice. It is a matter of bookkeeping: the demon has to record what it has seen, and those records are physical. Once the demon's memory is counted as part of the system, the entropy it appears to have removed is sitting in its notebook. The law is restored when the demon must wipe its memory in order to begin again, and erasing a single bit of stored information carries a minimum thermodynamic cost, known as the Landauer limit. Forgetting is not free, and that is where the debt comes due. Szilard's analysis of a one-molecule engine makes the same point from the other direction: information about a physical system can be converted into work, so information has to be paid for like anything else. The consequence I care about is this. Over a single run the demon can look as though it has won, because the cost is parked out of sight in its memory; the cost reappears only when the process has to repeat. Sorting that cannot be sustained is not free sorting. It is a loan.

That is the structure I want the speech to have. Our principal has asked each speaker to work from the theme that the future is in our hands, and I am not willing to stand in front of my class and pretend that our advantages were manufactured out of nothing. The demon's books say that order created in one place is paid for somewhere else, and that the payment is often made in forgetting. So the speech should be honest about where the cost fell for us — in the people who kept the records, made the lunches, taught the classes, and in the things we dropped in order to keep the schedule we kept — and it should draw the distinction the physics draws: one impressive afternoon proves nothing about the accounting, because the accounting comes due only when the process has to run again. What we can honestly promise is not that we will beat the books, but that we get to decide, more often than the demon did, where the cost lands.

Please write the speech as I would deliver it, in my own voice, first person, with no stage directions, about seven hundred words, which at a normal speaking pace is a little under five minutes. It has to land for three groups in the audience at once. My physics teacher will be in the second row and will notice immediately if I misuse the demon; he will be especially annoyed if I turn it into a lesson about working hard, so the speech has to be physically respectable. My grandmother and several aunts and uncles will be there and will never have heard of Maxwell or Szilard, so the speech cannot become a lecture on thermodynamics and must be intelligible without any of the technical vocabulary I used above. And my classmates, who chose me, should be able to enjoy it. Please avoid the usual graduation moves: entropy as a metaphor for life falling apart, cherishing every moment because disorder wins, and quotations from famous people that I have no way to verify.

After the speech, add a short note, kept separate from the speech itself and no longer than a paragraph, telling me two things: which simplifications I made that a physicist would object to, and which part of the demon story I had to leave out or soften in order to keep the speech honest and still fit the occasion.
