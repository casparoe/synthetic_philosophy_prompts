I'm a philosophy major in a seminar on value theory and normativity, and I'm trying to pin down a claim that came out of our unit on fitting-attitude analyses of value and the so-called wrong kind of reasons problem. I'd like your credence in a specific claim about how that problem should be diagnosed, with the reasoning laid out rather than summarized. I don't need a literature survey or a reading list; I need the argument.

Here is the setup. A machine will donate a large sum to a charity you care about on one of three conditions:

(1) it detects that you sincerely admire a certain admirable person;
(2) it checks, not your psychology but the facts, whether admiring that person would be fitting, and pays out if so;
(3) it pays out only if you admire that person and your admiration is properly based on the very features that make her admirable — a reward for having the attitude for the right reasons, which strikes me as an attitude-theoretic cousin of the toxin puzzle.

The claim I want a number on:

C: The wrong kind of reasons problem is best diagnosed as a problem about the dependence base of the reason. A consideration is a wrong-kind reason for an attitude A exactly when it obtains in virtue of a fact about A — whether A is held, whether A is fitting, whether A is well based — rather than in virtue of a fact about A's object alone. On that criterion, cases (2) and (3) are wrong-kind cases, and the familiar object-given/state-given distinction misclassifies (2).

My own credence in C is about 0.3. A classmate who holds a fittingness-first view — on which reasons for attitudes are just considerations bearing on fittingness, so nothing can be a reason for an attitude except by bearing on whether it is fitting — puts it near 0.75. Give me your number, but mainly decompose it. The terms I can see are these: whether reasons for attitudes form a single kind with a univocal "for" relation; whether fittingness is prior to reasons for attitudes, in which case a payout keyed to fittingness cannot be a reason for the attitude at all; whether case (3)'s self-undermining structure is a distinct phenomenon rather than an instance of the same one; whether belief and the conative attitudes behave alike here; and whether the taxonomy survives once we ask what the wrong-kind reason is a reason to do, as opposed to what attitude it is a reason for. Assign each a rough probability, say how they combine, and tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to and which one my classmate and I most likely disagree about.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or counterexample would lead you to a different verdict on C, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
