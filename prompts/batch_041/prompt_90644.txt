I've had an argument going with two friends for about a month now and I still can't find the word for the thing we're arguing about, so I'm hoping you can either give me the established name or admit there isn't one and give me a decent one. Here's the shape of it. A whole society slides into a way of living that leaves the people in it worse off by almost any measure they'd themselves recognise, and yet nobody chose it, nobody can individually back out, and every generation entrenches it further instead of reversing. The case that started this is farming. My understanding of the current picture is that early farmers worked longer than the foragers they displaced, ate a narrower and worse diet, were shorter and more diseased, and had far more tooth decay — and yet farming spread anyway, not because anyone was marched into it but because an acre of crops feeds more mouths than an acre of wild food, so farming communities outbred and eventually absorbed the foraging ones, and once you're a farmer with cleared fields, stores, and a village to defend you can't just wander off into the woods. What really interests me is that this seems to be where stored surplus, inherited advantage, and permanent hierarchy all begin, and I can't tell whether that's a separate fact or the same trap wearing a different hat. What I want to know is whether there's a name for that particular shape — the unwanted but unavoidable transition, driven by competition and headcount rather than by anybody's preference, and one-way because the world it destroys won't support the old life any more. I've met 'lock-in' and 'path dependence', and I've met the idea of a population pressing up against what the land can produce, and neither feels like the right fit: the first is usually about standards and technologies being mediocre rather than about people being worse off, and the second is about hitting a ceiling rather than walking into a trap on purpose.

It's not only farming, which is why I think it might be a general pattern rather than a quirk of prehistory. Every family in my sister's school catchment ends up paying for tutoring, and every one of them would be better off if none of them did, but the first to stop is the one whose kid drops down the league table, so it continues and gets dearer. Same story with advertising, as far as I can tell — no firm wants to be the only one not doing it, even though the whole industry would be richer if it collectively stopped. And I keep wondering whether the office norm where you answer messages at eleven at night is another one, because it definately ratchets and nobody can be the one to loose first. So: is there an established concept here, and if there is, what exactly does it cover and what does it leave out? What I'm really after isn't the label, it's the carving — what has to be true of a change for it to count as this rather than just a bad deal that people happened to accept, why a transition that leaves nearly everyone worse off can be perfectly stable, whether it's just a prisoners' dilemma or a commons problem under a fancier name (I suspect not, because here the payoffs themselves shift as you go, and there's no fixed set of players), and where the edges are — is there a version of this that ends, and what would have to be different for the trap to spring open? If nothing established fits, please coin something and give me the boundary cases, including one that looks like it but shouldn't count. British English, please, and don't just hand me a term and run.
