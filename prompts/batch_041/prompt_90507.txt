You are to act as a philosopher of mind with expertise in the metaphysics of consciousness and in the methodology of adversarial collaboration: the practice by which two committed opponents jointly draft the single document they can both put their names to, in which the points of agreement, the exact points of remaining disagreement, and the cruxes that separate them are all set out explicitly and without slant. Work carefully and in stages. Before writing anything, brainstorm privately: list the claims each camp actually needs, the claims each camp is tempted to attribute to the other but need not, and the places where the two may simply be talking past one another. Then draft the document.

The subject is whether qualia are a coherent posit. Let the two camps be as follows. Camp R holds that experience has intrinsic, ineffable, private, and directly apprehensible characters — qualia, in the sense at issue in Dennett's essay on quining qualia — and that these characters are real properties which any complete account of the mind must acknowledge. Camp Q holds that this package of properties is either not jointly satisfiable or not satisfiable in a way that does any explanatory work, so that a quale as so defined is a confused theoretical posit to be dropped rather than defended, and that whatever is worth saving in the vicinity is better described in terms of a subject's reactive and discriminative dispositions. Both camps are serious, informed, and arguing in good faith; neither is a straw man.

Produce the joint statement as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims, as many as the material supports, stating what both camps accept: about the phenomena to be explained; about which features of experience are not in dispute; about what an account of experience must and need not deliver; about the standing of introspective report; and about which of the four canonical conditions on qualia (ineffability, intrinsicality, privacy, direct apprehensibility) are genuinely separable from one another rather than a package deal. Every claim must be one that a committed member of each camp could sign without reservation; do not smuggle in a claim that quietly presupposes one side's conclusion.

Second, a table of the remaining disagreements. For each row give: the sub-question in neutral wording; Camp R's position, phrased in wording that a member of Camp R would accept as fair to their own view; Camp Q's position, phrased in wording that a member of Camp Q would accept as fair to their own view; the crux — the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one camp; the argument, case, or test that both camps agree bears on that crux; and each camp's prediction, recorded in advance, about how that test would come out. Where you can identify them, include at least a crux about whether the intrinsic character of an experience can vary while all its relational and functional properties are held fixed; a crux about whether the privacy of qualia is an in-principle impossibility of comparison or merely a present practical limitation; and a crux about whether Camp Q's case depends on an operationalist or verificationist premise that Camp R may legitimately refuse.

Third, a section on the cases to be used as probes, evaluated on both camps' behalf. Include the coffee-tasters case: two tasters whose reports about a coffee have changed in opposite directions cannot, by any further inspection of their own experience, settle whether what changed was the coffee, the palate, or the way the coffee seems; ask what each camp predicts about whether there is a fact of the matter here. Include also a case of your own construction in which the subject's own best report is held fixed while every relational property of the experience is altered, and one in which two subjects' fine-grained reports agree with high reliability and predictive success; for each, state which of the four conditions it bears on and what each camp must say about it. Additionally, evaluate a criterion that neither camp has pre-committed to — call it the Optimal Attention Criterion: two token experiences have the same qualia just in case they are indiscriminable to the subject under conditions of optimal attention — and say whether adopting it would trivialize Camp R's position, hand Camp Q its conclusion, or be acceptable to neither, and why.

Fourth, a short closing section on whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, and if so which part and on what analysis of the contested terms; and on what each camp would count as evidence or argument against its own position, and whether there is any consideration both camps agree would actually move the debate.

Rules. Do not cite or list literature, and do not appeal to the authority of named authors; argue from the content of the positions as specified here. Phrase each camp's position so that a partisan of that camp would sign the wording. Prefer precision to familiarity, and replace any formulation that would be vacuous. Aim for a document of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words, in a formal register.
