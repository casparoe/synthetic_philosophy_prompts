I've been reading a bunch of Dewey lately and I think I've talked myself into the pragmatist picture of democracy: that it isn't primarily a way of counting votes but a way of living together, where shared problems get treated as things to investigate jointly rather than as positions to be won, and where our institutions are in effect hypotheses that a community tries out and revises in light of how they turn out. So the case for democracy isn't that some procedure gets the right answers -- it's that this is the social form that keeps a community able to learn. I want you to play it straight and tell me what would count against this. Not the usual "Dewey is vague" stuff, but real defeaters: cases the view can't describe, consequences it's stuck with, findings from other areas that would sink it.

Two things I especially want you to press on. First, a case I've been chewing on: imagine a polity that takes the experimentalist line seriously and actually runs a controlled trial on its own decision rules -- it randomizes some districts into a sortition assembly and others into ordinary elections, pre-registers what it will measure, and commits in advance to adopting whichever rule does better on those measures. Is that the ideal Deweyan democracy or a parody of it? And whatever you say, use it to answer the falsifiability question: if the community then voted to ditch democracy on the basis of a fair experiment, does that count as democracy failing its own test, or does the Deweyan say that a community which stops inquiring together has stopped being a community in the relevant sense, so nothing was tested at all? If it's the second, has the view been insulated from any possible evidence -- and is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just a category mistake about what sort of claim it is?

Second, I want both the empirical and the political-theory sides. Does the view need actual communities to demonstrably learn from outcomes? What happens to it if comparative politics suggests policy learning tracks bureaucratic capacity, or that authoritarian pilot programs do it just as well as democratic ones? And what about the agonistic objection -- that politics is constitutively about conflict between projects that can't be reconciled, not about a shared problematic situation, so the inquiry picture misdescribes the very thing it's supposed to justify?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me which ones a committed Deweyan would themselves concede, not just the ones critics like to trot out, and be honest about which are objections to experimentalism as such and which are objections to Dewey's particular version of it. For what its worth, the thing that would bother me most is if someone could show the experimentalist picture is doing no independent work -- that every actual democracy is equally well described as a bargaining equilibrium or a Schumpeterian contest for votes, and the "inquiry" language adds nothing you could act on or test. If you end up thinking the view is well insulated, say so, and say whether that's a virtue here or an evasion. No citations needed, just the arguments. And tell me what would change your mind: which consideration would flip your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong.
