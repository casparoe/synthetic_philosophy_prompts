Grade the answer on a 0–20 scale with the corresponding letter grade, then give the point-by-point comments that justify the grade: what is correct and complete, what is loose but acceptable, and what is wrong or does not follow from what precedes it — quoting the specific sentence each comment attaches to and giving the reason. Question. Narrow-sense heritability is defined as h² = V_A/V_P. A twin registry in the (fictional) Republic of Marlow reports, for a composite measure of practical judgment, a monozygotic twin correlation of 0.86 and a dizygotic twin correlation of 0.48. For this exercise, assume the standard twin-model assumptions (additivity, no assortative mating, negligible gene–environment interaction). Note that Marlow is unusually homogeneous in schooling, income, and nutrition.

(a) Estimate h² and decompose the remaining variance into shared and non-shared environmental components.
(b) Assess the three claims in a policy brief written for Marlow's education ministry: (i) about three-quarters of a person's practical judgment is inherited; (ii) because the trait is strongly heritable, training programs are unlikely to raise it; (iii) because the estimate is 0.76 in Marlow, the same figure should be expected in Marlow's more unequal neighbor.
(c) What can and cannot this study tell us about the difference in average practical judgment between Marlow and its neighbor?

Student's answer.

(a) Using Falconer's formula, h² ≈ 2(r_MZ − r_DZ) = 2(0.86 − 0.48) = 0.76. The shared-environment component is c² = 2r_DZ − r_MZ = 0.96 − 0.86 = 0.10, and the non-shared environment takes the remainder, e² = 1 − 0.76 − 0.10 = 0.14. So within Marlow, about 76% of the variance in practical judgment is additive genetic, 10% is shared environment, and 14% is non-shared environment plus measurement error.

(b)(i) This is loose but close to right. The estimate is not a claim about the parts of any one person; it says that 76% of the variance in the population is attributable to additive genetic differences. It also inherits the standard limitations of the twin design, including the equal environments assumption — that monozygotic twins are genetically identical while dizygotic twins share on average half their genes — and if that assumption fails, h² is inflated.

(b)(ii) The inference is invalid. Heritability describes the sources of variation in a population over the range of environments that population contains; it is not a measure of a trait's plasticity, and the same trait can have a very different heritability in a different environment. Whether training programs raise practical judgment is an empirical question the twin design does not settle.

(b)(iii) The brief is wrong here. h² is a ratio of genetic variance to total variance, and total variance depends on how much environmental variation the population contains. Marlow's homogeneity compresses environmental variance and inflates the genetic share; in a more unequal neighbor, with a wider range of environments, h² would be expected to fall even if V_A were identical. The 0.76 figure should not be exported.

(c) The estimate is a within-population statistic: it tells us how much of the variation among individuals inside Marlow tracks genetic differences. It cannot be read as a decomposition of the difference in average practical judgment between Marlow and its neighbor. So the gap between the two countries must reflect environmental differences — schooling, income, nutrition — rather than genetic ones, and the ministry should direct its effort there.
