ok so I'm putting together a seminar on Confucian role ethics and I want a poem to open the first day with — something the students def haven't seen before, not another excerpt from the Analects. You're a poet who actually knows this tradition, Ames and Rosemont and the rest, and specifically the claim that a person is constituted by their roles and relations rather than being some little atom that later picks up roles like hats. Not greeting-card Confucius.

Before you write anything, brainstorm for a bit on the page: what you want the poem to do, what the form has to carry, what you're going to cut. Then write it.

The form is a pantoum, 4 or 5 stanzas. Here's the part I actually care about. A pantoum recycles its own lines — the 2nd and 4th line of each stanza come back as the 1st and 3rd of the next — and I want that recycling to *be* the argument. In role ethics you never get to keep a line to yourself; what you say comes back as somebody else's opening. So the recurring lines should keep their exact wording but change meaning as the relations around them shift, and by the end a line that started out as yours should be arriving from someone else, or from someone who is no longer there.

Voice: someone caring for a parent who's dying slowly. Someone who really does believe the caring isn't a burden dumped on a pre-existing self but is the place where the self is at all. Let the last lines land on that without ever stating it.

Two more things I want in there. First, carry the standard objection too — the one that says if I'm nothing but my roles then I have no standing to judge a bad role, no place to stand outside the family to say this arrangement is wrong. Don't answer it, don't resolve it, just let it sit in the poem as a counter-line that keeps threatening to take the whole thing over. Second, the poem should sound like a real person in a hospital corridor, not like a translated proverb. If you're torn between a plain line and a fancy one, take the plain one.

Constraints: don't use the words self, role, relation, identity, or authentic — show it instead of telling it. No Hallmark ending, nothing like "and I realized I was the lucky one." No quotes from the Analects or any text, no citations, I don't want footnotes. Keep it under about 30 lines. It should be accurate enough that someone who teaches this stuff nods, and good enough that someone who's never heard of Confucius still wants to reread it. Think carefully, then write.
