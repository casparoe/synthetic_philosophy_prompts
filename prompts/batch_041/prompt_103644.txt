I am a doctoral student in cognitive science, working on computational models of letterform perception in the tradition of the Letter Spirit project — the effort to build a system that, shown a few letters drawn in a consistent style, can extrapolate the remaining letters of the alphabet in that same style. The persistent obstacle is that we cannot get the identity of a letter to come apart cleanly from its style. To recognize the extrapolated glyphs as 'a', 'b', 'c', and so on, the system needs a representation of what each letter essentially is; to judge that the resulting set shares a single coherent spirit, it needs a representation of what is merely contingent, the free parameters of the style. Each notion appears to be defined by contrast with the other, and the project stalls there.

The impasse is not only ours. The general question — what makes every 'a' an 'a'? — has been debated, in one form or another, for as long as anyone has theorized about categories, and it has not moved. Three families of answers keep recurring. The first is geometric essentialism: there is an abstract structural schema — a bowl and a stem, a counter, an aperture — and any glyph that instantiates it is an 'a'. The second is family resemblance: no single feature is necessary, membership is graded by a web of overlapping similarities, and there is no fact of the matter about the boundary. The third is prototype- and process-based: 'a' is a category in the visual system, fixed by a prototype or by the statistics of the perceptual input rather than by an abstract form.

The cases that ought to decide between them do not decide. The single-story and double-story 'a' — the open cursive form and the form with the upper bowl and hook — share almost no geometry, yet both are unambiguously 'a'; the same is true of the two forms of 'g'. Meanwhile a glyph can be deformed continuously from 'a' toward 'd', 'q', or '9' without any single step at which it stops being 'a'. And across writing systems, what counts as the same letterform or as a distinct one is not stable: a shape can be an 'a' in one script and a different letter in another, and a glyph can read as a letter or a numeral depending on context.

My hypothesis is that the debate is stalled because it is two questions under one heading — a normative or metaphysical question about what the letter 'a' is, and a psychological question about how the visual system sorts glyphs into categories — with a third, typographic question about what it is for a set of glyphs to belong to one style silently doing the work. If that is right, the participants may not be disagreeing so much as answering different questions while borrowing one another's vocabulary.

Please diagnose this properly. Is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word "letter," a disagreement about which cases count as data, or several questions conflated under a single term? What would count as progress given your diagnosis — a formal result about the space of glyphs, an experiment, a distinction, or an argument that no progress should be expected? And if the identity/style entanglement is real rather than an artifact of our particular representations, say what follows for the modeling project.
