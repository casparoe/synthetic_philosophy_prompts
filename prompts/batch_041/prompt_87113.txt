Write a short story, maybe 1,500 words, set in a country whose courts work like one of those Bongard puzzles — you know the ones, a grid of little pictures with six on the left and six on the right, and you have to work out what makes the left ones left. In this country nobody ever wrote the law down. Every courtroom has a wall of twelve decided cases on it, six in the left column and six in the right, and a new case gets decided by which column it belongs in. You're just supposed to look at the twelve and see it.

Make the story about the moment that breaks. I want a clerk — or a judge's apprentice, or a juror, whichever makes it go — who gets a new case and finds two rules that fit all twelve cases on the wall exactly, no exceptions, and that give opposite verdicts on the case in front of her. One of them should be the natural reading, the one the old judges plainly had in mind. The other should be a monstrosity: it only works if you add a special clause about the colour of the seal on the fourth case on the right, or about whether the case number is prime, or it quietly reverses the meaning of "owner" halfway through. And she has to notice that nothing on that wall rules either one out. Both rules are sitting in the twelve cases like two faces in the same cloud.

Then do something with it, dont just leave it at oh no, ambiguity. Somewhere in the middle, the oldest judge should make the best case he can for the system: the rule isnt in the cases, it's in us — we've been doing this for four hundred years and we all flinch at the same answer, and the flinch is the law. Let the story test that instead of admiring it. Does the flinch actually settle the hard case, or does it just push the problem back one step? What happens when the newcomer doesn't flinch the way they do? And keep a real person's freedom or land or neck on the line, so the ambiguity costs something.

Don't give me a tidy ending where she finds the One True Rule. I'd rather she chooses, and the story is honest about what made it a choice and what would have made it a mistake. Plain prose, no lectures about Goodman, no character explaining rule-following out loud to another character.
