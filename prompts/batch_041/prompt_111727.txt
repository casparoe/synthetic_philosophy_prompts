I'm in a reading group on modality, and one of the regulars keeps insisting that counterpart theory and transworld identity are two notations for the same body of fact — that whatever a counterpart theorist says about an ordinary object like a person or a chair, a transworld-identity theorist can say the same thing in her own vocabulary and get the same verdict. I want to test that claim properly, and I don't trust myself to build the case, because every version I've tried has collapsed into either a difference about which possibilities exist (which she rightly calls cheating) or an abstract puzzle about indiscernible worlds that nobody in the group cares about. So what I want from you is a case built to order: one ordinary scenario, no science-fictional devices, no brains in vats, no gods, in which the two frameworks deliver opposite verdicts on a single de re modal claim about an ordinary subject — something of the form "that person could have been F" or "that chair could have been G" — while agreeing on every qualitative fact, on which possible worlds there are, on all de dicto claims, and on everything non-modal. The divergence has to be produced by the theories and not stipulated by the case; if it can't be produced without importing an extra assumption that neither framework licenses, say so and name the assumption, because a case whose verdicts are written into the setup rather than derived from the theories is exactly the kind of thing my friend will wave away.

Some specifications. The scenario itself should be a single paragraph of ordinary narrative, and that paragraph must not contain the words "counterpart," "transworld," or "haecceity," so that the case doesn't presuppose its own reading. Hold everything else fixed: the same stock of worlds, the same qualitative contents, the same subject, the same modal claim. Because the usual slipperiness here comes from vague similarity judgements, I want you to specify the cross-world mapping by fiat instead of leaving it to intuition — give the relation explicitly (a small table is fine) along with the similarity metric that generates it, and make the divergence turn on one formal property of that relation, such as its being non-transitive, asymmetric, or one-to-many, rather than on a contestable call about how similar two things are. Please don't recycle the familiar illustrations — the swapped indiscernible worlds, the politician who loses his election, the ship rebuilt plank by plank — I want something constructed fresh for this case. Then show the work: for each framework, state the verdict on the claim and the reasoning step that gets you there, and be explicit about which premise about identity or about the logic of cross-world predication each side is relying on. After that, tell me which feature of the case is doing the load-bearing work, what happens if I remove or alter exactly that feature (does the divergence vanish, and if so why), and what the strongest reply is for whichever side comes out worse — the move a competent defender would make to show that the case doesn't really cost her anything.

Finish with a brief structured summary, after the prose, as a single JSON object with these fields: "scenario" (one sentence restating the case), "subject" (the ordinary object and the de re claim at issue), "counterpart_verdict" (the verdict plus the reasoning step that yields it), "transworld_verdict" (the same for the other framework), "point_of_divergence" (the formal property of the counterpart relation that makes the verdicts come apart), "load_bearing_feature" (the single feature the case turns on), "ablation" (the change to that feature and the resulting convergence or persistence of the divergence), "defender_reply" (the strongest resistance available to the framework that fares worse), and "unlicensed_assumptions" (any extra premise the case needs that neither framework supplies, or "none").
