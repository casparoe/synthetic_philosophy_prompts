A journal has commissioned a joint statement from two philosophers of perception who have spent years arguing past each other: one holds that perception is cognitively penetrable, the other that it is encapsulated. The commission is a genuine adversarial collaboration rather than a debate transcript, so the two authors must produce a single text that both would put their names to. Write it.

The two positions, as they are to be understood for this exercise, are these.

Penetration. Some of a subject's beliefs, desires, expectations, moods or category-based knowledge alter what the subject perceives — the content or the phenomenal character of the perceptual episode itself — and not merely what the subject judges, remembers or reports about the scene.

Encapsulation. Perception computes its outputs from a restricted body of information. The effects the other side cites arise downstream, in judgement, memory, decision or response bias, or else from task demands and confounded cues; what look like perceptual effects are post-perceptual processes read back into the perceptual episode.

Three things are given, because both authors have already accepted them and they are not in dispute.

1. The two-index constraint. Every alleged penetration is to be assayed on two independent indices: a report-based measure, which is criterion-sensitive, in that it can be moved by what the subject expects to see, is willing to say, or takes the task to require; and a criterion-free sensitivity measure of the kind psychophysics supplies — discrimination thresholds, adaptation and aftereffect measures, breaking continuous flash suppression, and the like. Neither index is privileged in advance, and the two are permitted to come apart.

2. The three-stage stipulation. For this exercise, perception names a process with three separable stages: registration, the encoding of the distal stimulus; organisation, the grouping and constancy computations that yield a structured scene; and uptake, what the subject is thereby in a position to know about the scene. Both authors accept that the word perceptual may be attached to any of the three, and that a good deal of the literature's disagreement turns on which of them is meant.

3. The online/offline distinction. Both authors accept that a penetration claim may be either online, with the influence operating during the perceptual computation that produces the episode, or offline, with the influence having reshaped the perceptual system beforehand — through learning, training or long-standing expectation — so that the computation then runs unperturbed. They accept that the distinction is worth drawing, though not that it favours either side.

Both authors also accept in advance that the following hypothetical result would bear on their dispute, whatever it turned out to be. Subjects are induced to expect that a stimulus lying at a category boundary — a hue midway between two named colours, say — belongs to one category rather than the other, by a manipulation that leaves the stimulus itself untouched; the report-based index shifts accordingly. Suppose that the criterion-free index does not move. Suppose, further, that a low-level physical change to the stimulus, too small for subjects to report reliably, moves the criterion-free index without moving the report-based one.

The statement is to contain the following.

(a) A numbered list of claims both authors can sign: common ground about the phenomena, including the top-down effects that neither side denies; about what any theory of perception owes an account of; about the logical standing of the encapsulation thesis and of the penetration thesis, including what would falsify each; and about what the standard arguments on each side do and do not establish. No claim in this list may be vacuous — if both would sign it only because it commits them to nothing, it does not belong.

(b) The residual disagreement, with each side's position set out in the words its own adherents would use, not the words of its critics. Where a formulation exists that both would sign, the disagreement has not yet been located; keep pressing until the formulations are ones that each side would recognise as its own.

(c) The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I take it that one is the online/offline distinction; another is whether any criterion-free index can serve as a signature of the perceptual, or whether the perception/cognition boundary can only be drawn by stipulation; and a third is how far the disputed effects survive the strongest available controls for demand characteristics and confounded cues. Add any others you find. For each crux, say which stage of the process it concerns, name the case, experiment or argument that both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance.

(d) An assessment of how much of the dispute is verbal — which part, if any, dissolves once the parties agree on which stage and which mode of influence they are discussing, and what remains once that part has been subtracted. If you conclude that the whole dispute is verbal, say what the parties were in fact disagreeing about.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with one row per crux and columns for the crux, each side's formulation, the shared test, and the two advance predictions, then a short closing section on the verbal dispute.

No citations are required; where a specific study is wanted, describe its design instead of naming it.
