I hold a chair in the philosophy of the social sciences, and I have lately been asked to sit on the academic advisory board of a small foundation that has decided to spend about 1.5 million dollars over four years on what it calls the deep causes of prosperity -- that is, on the long-running dispute over whether the wealth of nations is best explained by institutions, by geography and endowments, or by culture and inherited beliefs. The other board members are economists and economic historians; I am the only philosopher, and the chair has told me that my job is to say where the conceptual rather than the econometric work is. I have a hobby-horse and would like to see whether it survives contact with a careful interlocutor. It is this: much of the dispute looks to me like a disagreement about what an institution is. On one reading -- call it the rules reading -- institutions are the formal and informal constraints that people face, constraints that a coloniser or a reformer could in principle rewrite; on another -- the equilibrium reading -- institutions simply are the shared beliefs and self-sustaining patterns of behaviour that people expect one another to follow, so that a rule on paper whose expectations nobody tracks is not an institution at all. I suspect the empirical camps are often running one reading and the other without noticing, and that this is why the finding that institutions matter and the finding that institutions are sticky sit so uneasily together: the first needs institutions to be the sort of thing that can be installed, the second needs them to be the sort of thing that persists precisely because it cannot be. There is also the awkward matter that the evidence here is quasi-experimental at best -- settler mortality, legal transplants, arbitrary boundaries, resource windfalls -- each a lever that moves many things at once, so that the inference from such variation to a causal claim about institutions specifically, rather than about whatever else moved with them, carries more weight than I think anyone has argued for.

Please give me a prioritised agenda. Assume the funder can support roughly eight researchers for three years, that they are willing to fund philosophy rather than new data collection, and that I have to defend the list to a sceptical economist. Which open questions in this area matter most, and in what order? I want the reasoning far more than the list: for each question, why it is upstream of the others, how much of the rest of the field would shift if it were settled, whether it is the kind of thing a philosopher can make progress on without new data or whether it needs the econometricians, and what argument or evidence over the next three or four years would move it up or down the ranking. Name the criteria you are using and say where they conflict; be willing to tell me which questions are overrated and should get nothing at all, including, if it comes to that, my own hobby-horse. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- the single question you would fund first -- and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating my question back to me.
