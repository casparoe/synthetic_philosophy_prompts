I'm a graduate student in a lab that builds unconventional computing substrates — the details don't matter much, but the short version is that we grow small networks of neurons on electrode arrays and train them to perform simple tasks, and we've recently started collaborating with a group doing something similar in silicon. Last week two of my labmates, Dana (a computational neuroscientist) and Ori (who came in from philosophy of mind), got into an argument during a group meeting about whether the substrate we build on could ever matter for how our systems should be treated, and I've been turning it over ever since, because neither of them seemed to land a clean hit. Dana's position is that moral status attaches to a certain kind of computation rather than to a certain kind of matter, and that the computation can be characterized in advance: roughly, a system matters morally to the extent that it runs a unified self-model that registers its own states as good or bad and integrates information across subsystems into a single point of view. On her view, "which computations matter morally?" is a real research question with an answer that doesn't depend on whether the hardware is biological, so our silicon collaborators are building candidates worth taking seriously. Ori objected that this criterion is empty, because computation isn't intrinsic to a physical system: any system can be described as implementing any computation under some sufficiently clever mapping of its states onto a formal description, so "implements the right computations" can be made true of a bucket of water — the attribution says something about the describer, not the described. Dana answered that the triviality only arises for gerrymandered mappings that do no explanatory work; if we require the computational description to be the one that causally explains the system's behavior and supports counterfactuals, the indeterminacy evaporates, and sentience is precisely the description that does that work. Ori came back that this relocates the criterion from "which computations" to "which computations we find explanatory," which is a fact about our interests, and that in practice the systems we find it natural to describe that way are the ones we designed or the ones that resemble us — so the test will track familiarity rather than moral status. He added that the "unified self-model" condition is doing a lot of quiet work and looks like a projection of our own architecture: a mind organized as a committee, or one with valenced states but no self-model, would fail the test despite plausibly having interests. Dana replied that in that case we should widen the profile rather than abandon it, and that the practical question is what to do under uncertainty — if a system plausibly meets the profile, the cost of wrongly denying it concern is far worse than the cost of wrongly extending it, so we should err toward inclusion. Ori said the precautionary asymmetry isn't free: if we extend the benefit of the doubt to every system that plausibly meets some computational profile, we'll eventually have to extend it to an enormous range of artifacts, and treating non-sentient systems as sentient isn't harmless — it dilutes the concept and diverts concern from animals we already know are suffering.

Please adjudicate this move by move — Dana's initial criterion, Ori's triviality objection, Dana's explanatory-role reply, Ori's familiarity reply and his self-model objection, and then the two moves about precaution. Tell me which of them is right about which specific claim, where each is overreaching, and what both of them are missing. I'm not looking for a winner so much as a map of where the real disagreement lives, and an honest statement of which parts of it are actually decidable and which are being fought over because they can't be.
