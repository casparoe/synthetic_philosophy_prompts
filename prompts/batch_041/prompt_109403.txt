I am putting together a unit for a graduate seminar in applied ethics, and I want the central assignment to be a piece of satire that doubles as a thought experiment. The topic is the alleged moral difference between lying and misleading: whether it matters, morally, that a false belief was produced by an assertion rather than by a true statement, an omission, a presupposition, or a conversational implicature. Please write a mock internal compliance handbook, approximately 900–1100 words, for the invented “Office of Strategic Communication” at a multinational company. The handbook should be written in the deadpan, numbered-clause style of a real corporate policy document, with no editorializing and no winking at the reader. It should codify a distinction between prohibited lies and permitted “truthful misdirection,” and it should be funny precisely because it gets the philosophy right: it should treat the norms of assertion and conversational implicature as enforceable internal regulations, distinguish what is said from what is implicated, and sort approved techniques into categories such as the true-but-unhelpful answer, the presuppositional deflection, the selective citation, the strategic omission, and the technically accurate but materially incomplete disclosure. The stated rationale should be a hybrid that a philosopher will recognize as unstable—a deontological-sounding prohibition on false assertion, paired with a consequentialist-sounding permission for misleading—and the handbook should never notice the tension. Include a grievance procedure for employees who claim they were misled rather than lied to; a training module on deniability; a clause explaining that the policy governs statements but not the inferences a reasonable person might draw; and a brief appendix, perhaps a “Deniability Matrix,” that rates techniques by legal exposure and moral comfort. Include at least one quotation from the company’s fictional Chief Ethics Officer—an invented person, not a real philosopher—whose defense of the policy is internally consistent and recognizably a position one could hold, so that a specialist in deception will find the satire fair rather than clumsy.

Please do not quote any real philosopher, book, or document; invent the company, its officers, and any internal memos. You may use standard philosophical ideas—assertion versus implicature, the difference between lying and merely causing a false belief, the old casuistical trick of mental reservation (saying something true while silently adding a qualification that makes it false in the speaker’s own mind)—without citing sources. The piece should stand alone: no summary of arguments afterward, no footnotes explaining the joke. By the end, a reader who knows the literature should have felt the real question—whether the lying/misleading distinction tracks a moral difference or only a semantic one—without the handbook ever asking it, and a reader who has never read the literature should still be able to follow the story of a company that has turned a philosophical distinction into a compliance loophole. If it helps, you can anchor the handbook with a fictional case: a product safety disclosure, a statement about an ongoing investigation, or a response to a rumor.
