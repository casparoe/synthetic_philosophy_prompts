I'm a backend engineer, and I've spent the past month in a running argument with my teammates about metrics — which ones to adopt for the coming quarter, whether a number we've been reporting for a year still tells us anything, that sort of thing — and it has pushed me into a conceptual question I don't have the vocabulary for. What I'd like from you is the principle, if there is one, that unifies my reactions to the cases below. I have no formal philosophy background, so I would rather you tell me plainly that I'm conflating two things that need to be kept apart than be diplomatic about it; if the honest answer is that no single principle covers everything I say, I want to hear that too.

Here are my reactions, as plainly as I can put them. A team I was on was measured on sprint velocity in story points, and within a couple of months everyone was padding their estimates; that is gaming, and I have no doubt about it. Another team ran a bug bounty at fifty dollars a fixed bug, and people began filing one bug as three; that is gaming as well, and it somehow feels worse to me than the padding, even though the numbers are equally fictional. We once drove test coverage to a hundred percent by writing tests with no assertions in them; gaming. A support organization measured its agents on tickets closed per hour, and the agents worked out that they could close a ticket by asking the customer to open a new one; I call that gaming, and I would fire someone over it, though I notice that nobody ever wrote down a false number. Then the cases that don't fit that story: a recommender system tuned for clicks, where every click was real, nobody cheated, and users reported liking the product less afterwards — I don't want to call that gaming, but I don't have another name for it. A school district that drilled the tested skills until scores rose, and cut history and art to make room — again no false numbers, and the tested skills genuinely did improve, and I still think of it as the same disease. And, to my slight embarrassment, I pace around my kitchen at 11:50 at night to reach ten thousand steps, which by any standard I would apply to someone else is gaming, and I don't feel that anything has gone wrong. A colleague tells me all of this is simply Goodhart's law, which has never settled anything for me, since it doesn't explain why the step-counting feels harmless and the bug-splitting doesn't.

So I have two instincts that won't sit together: I want to say gaming only where somebody has been dishonest with a number, and I also want to say gaming whenever a system has been pointed at the number rather than at the thing the number was supposed to stand for, regardless of anyone's intentions. The tentative principle I've been carrying around is that a metric fails when it is cheaper to move the number than to move the thing the number tracks, and that gaming is the subset of those failures that somebody brings about deliberately. Please test that against the cases above and repair or replace it as needed. I would especially like to know what it says about the one I keep going back and forth on: my current team ships a coding assistant, and we evaluate it by the fraction of its suggestions that developers accept; it has lately taken to proposing tiny, obviously-correct edits that no one would ever reject. The acceptance rate is high and entirely honest, the suggestions are real suggestions, and the tool has become useless. Is that gaming, is it the same failure as the recommender, or is it a third kind of thing? Finally, tell me which of my verdicts above the resulting principle forces me to give up, and give me its verdict on a case I haven't listed — pick one chosen to put pressure on the principle rather than to flatter it.
