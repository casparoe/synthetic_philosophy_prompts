I am teaching Sir Gawain and the Green Knight alongside a unit on signalling theory in biology and the social sciences, and I want a reading of the poem that takes both the poem and the theory seriously. Three of its devices look like signals. The Green Knight's Christmas game is a wager in which a man offers his neck and then, holding his own severed head, keeps the appointment; Bertilak's daily bargain is an economy of exchange in which kisses are traded for a deer, then a boar, then a fox; and the lady's green sash is a cheap object offered as a charm against death, accepted in secret, and afterwards worn by Gawain as a permanent mark of his fault. Read these through the handicap principle — Zahavi's claim that a display is trustworthy only because it is costly enough that a bluffer could not afford it — and say where the theory genuinely illuminates the poem and where the poem's own logic resists it. Is the beheading game a signal at all, or a commitment device that makes a threat credible? Is the exchange of winnings a signalling system, or a covenant whose binding force is normative rather than informational? Is the sash the poem's counterexample to cheap talk, or the one place where a sign does its work without being costly? End by saying what the poem understands about trust that the theory cannot state, and where the analogy should be dropped rather than forced.
