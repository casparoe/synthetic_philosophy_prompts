My friend has a hobbyhorse she calls free-floating rationales — the idea that a bit of behaviour can have a reason behind it, a proper little means-end story (do X and you'll get Y), even though nobody anywhere is doing the reasoning. Not a reason anyone holds; a reason the behaviour answers to. She keeps a running list of cases she's ruled on and I've been trying to reverse-engineer whatever rule she's actualy running.

Her yeses: an E. coli tumbling up a sugar gradient; a Venus flytrap snapping shut on the second touch; a slime mould finding the shortest path through a maze; a beaver's dam; ants laying a pheromone trail to a feeder; a virus's capsid, which is shaped the way it is because that's how you get a genome into a cell. Her nos: a river cutting a canyon on its way to the sea (she says it isn't aiming at the sea, it just can't go up); a thermostat holding a room at 20 degrees; a chess engine searching for mate — she says its reasons are on loan from whoever wrote it; a falling rock; and a neutral mutation that got fixed by drift, which she says has no rationale at all, it's just what happened. She won't commit on what a market's price is doing or on whatever a trained neural net has learned internally — says those are somebody else's argument. And she insists aliveness is a red herring; the virus and the drift case are meant to prove it.

Now the new one, and I want to know what she'll say: a border collie working sheep. The eye, the outrun, the way it slides itself into exactly the spot the sheep need it to be — no shepherd taught it any of that move by move, but shepherds did breed for it, for a long time, on purpose. Yes or no?

More than the verdict, though: give me the rules that fit her list so far, spelled out as rules and not vibes, what each one predicts about the collie, which you'd back and why, and what case I'd have to put to her to actually settle it. And if her list turns out to be two rules that are really one rule in different coats, say so.
