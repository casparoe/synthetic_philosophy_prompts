Writing a short paper on the Allais problem and I want comments on the argument before I commit to it. Mainly: is the central distinction real, and is the thing interesting, or am I just relabeling a known point? Notes below.

Claim: the Allais problem is standardly sold as a counterexample to the independence axiom, and that's a mistake that lets the expected-utility side off too easy. When people take the sure million over the 89/10/1 lottery and then take 10% of five million over 11% of one million, they aren't rejecting a formal axiom about mixtures. They're refusing to treat a lottery as a bundle of branch-wise consequences in the first place.

The usual defense: the 0.89 branch is common to both options within each pair (0.89 of a million in the first pair, 0.89 of nothing in the second), so it cancels, and once it's cancelled both pairs reduce to the same comparison. My objection is that cancelling is exactly the step in dispute. It only looks innocent if you already think the value of a lottery is exhausted by what happens on each branch, which is to say if you've already accepted the sure-thing principle as a constraint on rational preference. That's a substantive claim about what a lottery is, not a theorem. I gather Allais's own view was that the shape of the probability distribution over outcomes is itself part of what's being preferred, which makes the cancel move a bit question-begging. Same complaint about the two-stage rewrite of the problem, where you first draw a ticket and 89% of the time you're handed the million regardless: that version just hands you the decomposition and then treats anyone who flinches as irrational. Ditto the advice to simply revise your preferences until they're consistent (I think that's Savage's move in the Foundations, though dont quote me on it).

What I want from you:

1. Does the independence / sure-thing distinction hold up, or do the two collapse once you're careful? Nagging worry: rejecting branch-wise decomposition is just a fancy name for violating the axiom.
2. Does the certainty do any work? Scale both options in each pair down by the same factor and you get the same reversal with no sure thing anywhere, which seems like it should trouble me more than it does.
3. Am I re-deriving something standard? I have a vague sense that the non-EU literature already seperates independence from consequentialism, and that the old worry about the utility of gambling being part of what's preferred is the same point in a different costume, but I don't know that material well enough to say.
4. Anything that would give the claim teeth, i.e. a case other than Allais where treating a lottery as a whole and treating it branch-wise come apart?

Blunt is good, especially on 1 and 3.
