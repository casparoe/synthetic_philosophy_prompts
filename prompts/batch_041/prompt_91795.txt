I'm a fifth-year PhD student in history and philosophy of science — my dissertation is on how the environmental sciences justify causal claims from messy observational data — and this year I sit as the outside member on my university's Research Safety Committee, which is why I am asking you rather than anyone in my department. We vote Thursday on the written findings for the March incident in the chemistry building, and I have to circulate the reasoning beforehand, because whatever we say about this one case becomes the standard we apply to every future one. What happened is this. A second-year student was running a solvent purification alone at about eleven at night when the vapors ignited; he was hospitalized for eleven days and is expected to recover fully. Two things were wrong with the building, and both were authorized on a single work order: the exhaust fan serving that bay had been shut down for scheduled maintenance and, as part of the same job, the flammable-vapor alarm had been taken offline. The work order sat open for three weeks because the deferred-maintenance account was exhausted. The student had also signed out an after-hours key in violation of the department's lone-working policy, which as far as anyone can determine has not been enforced in a decade. The ignition source itself was unremarkable — an ordinary hot plate, working as designed.

Our standing test, inherited from the general counsel's office, is a but-for test: we name a factor as a cause only if the injury would not have happened without it. Applied honestly, that test names the fan shutdown, the alarm outage, the student's decision to work alone, the hot plate, and the decision to run the experiment at all, and if we were consistent it would also name the solvent purchase and the building itself. It also fails in the other direction. Two years ago we had a discharge-limit exceedance produced by two independent spills in different labs, each of which was by itself enough to put us over the limit, and the but-for test returned no cause at all: the report named nobody, the fine landed on the central budget, and the trustees wanted to know what we thought we were doing. Our chair has proposed replacing but-for with what she calls an intervention test — a factor counts as a cause when there is some way of changing just that factor while holding everything else fixed that would have changed the outcome — and I am supposed to be the person on this committee who knows the philosophical literature here. My honest worry is that her test inherits both problems instead of repairing them, and that in this particular case the fan, the alarm, and the deferred-maintenance budget were all set by one upstream decision, so it may be impossible to change one while holding the others fixed; I am also unsure how to state the outcome we are asking about, since the injury and the injury rather than a near miss seem to give different answers.

What I need from you is the memo I will read out on Thursday: a specific recommendation on what our findings should say caused this injury and what they should say did not, what standard we should adopt going forward and why, and what we should require to be fixed, by whom, and on what schedule. The dissenting view on the committee is that but-for is the only transparent and legally defensible standard and that anything richer will turn the university into a permanent defendant; that deserves a straight answer rather than a dismissal, because I suspect it is half right. You should also know that our risk manager has let it be known that a finding naming the student's after-hours work as a cause would help us in the workers' compensation matter, which is precisely the sort of pressure I do not want shaping a finding about what actually happened. My own half-formed view is that we have been asking one test to do two jobs — to map the causal structure and to decide who pays — and that a two-stage standard is the way out, but I would rather you tell me that this is confused than flatter it. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no numbered lists, no bold or tables, the way one would write a letter to colleagues who have read the file but not thought about it as hard as they should have.
