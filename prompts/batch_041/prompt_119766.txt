I am at work on a novel whose protagonist, a systems analyst who has spent twenty years inside institutions she has stopped trusting, keeps a private ledger of what she calls traps: situations in which capable people, each doing the locally sensible thing, produce an outcome none of them would have chosen. Her editor keeps calling them Moloch traps; she refuses the name. In the chapters I have already drafted she has ruled as follows: two states in an arms race, each of which would prefer mutual restraint but cannot verify the other's compliance — a trap; neighbours who each buy a larger car to keep pace with one another and end up with the same relative standing and less money — a trap; employers competing to trim benefits, so that all of them arrive at the same labour costs and a more resentful workforce — a trap; a lone factory that fouls a river it does not own, with no rivals and no one compelling it — not a trap; two firms failing to adopt a common standard they would both profit from, where the failure traces only to each wrongly assuming the other would refuse — not a trap; firms racing to make a product cheaper and better, where each gains customers and the customers gain too — not a trap.

The scene I have not yet written puts her before a hearing on a market in which two rival platforms have spent five years racing to make their products more compulsive. The escalation has paid: both are larger and more profitable than before. The cost has fallen entirely on their users, whose sleep and attention have measurably degraded. A lobbyist for the platforms contends that this cannot be one of her traps, since no one in the race has lost.

Tell me whether she will call it a trap. State the rule she appears to be running, then name at least two further rules that fit every verdict I have given her just as well, and say what each predicts about the platforms. Give me the prediction you would bet on and your reason for it, and describe the one further case that would most cleanly separate the rules that survive.
