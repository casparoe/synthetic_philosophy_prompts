So I have to write a position statement for my historiography seminar on archives and power, and then defend it in the workshop, and honestly I'd rather find the cracks now than in front of everyone. This is roughly where I've landed:

(1) The archive is an instrument of power before it is a store of facts. What survives is mostly what the state, the parish, the plantation or the firm found it useful or necessary to write down, so the shape of the surviving record is itself a historical artefact, not a neutral sample of the past.

(2) So absence in the record is informative. I've been distinguishing two kinds, though I'm not sure the distinction is as clean as I want it to be: 'documented gaps', where something was written and then lost, or the series just stops, and 'structural absences', where the thing was never going to be written down in the first place because the categories didn't exist or nobody with power had a reason to care. Both should be explained historically rather than just lamented.

(3) Historians should still try to write the lives of the unrecorded — that's the whole point of history from below — and the fact that someone left no documents of their own isn't a reason to say nothing about them.

(4) But what we say has to be answerable to evidence. I don't think it's legitimate to make claims that no conceivable source could have borne on, even when the political motivation is good.

(5) I also think the historian is inside the same machinery: what we choose to study, and the categories we file it under, are themselves selections that shape what future historians will be able to see.

(6) And I want to keep the idea that some reconstructions are simply better than others — that there's a real difference between a well-evidenced account and a wishful one, and that the difference isn't just a matter of whose politics you happen to share.

I suspect at least one of these is quietly freeloading on the others, but I can't tell which, and I definately keep going round in circles. Can you audit the set: which ones actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, and which apparent conflict dissolves once you draw the right distinction? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give, what I'd lose by giving it, and whether there's a cheaper repair. Finally, please separate what's genuinely settled here from what's still contested among people who work on this — I don't want to walk into the workshop defending a live disagreement as if it were the consensus.
