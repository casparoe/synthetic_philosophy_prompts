I write fiction for a living, and my trade rests on a picture of the mind I have never examined closely. I narrate a character's inner life from inside it, and I count on a reader accepting that a person's own account of her motives, feelings, and beliefs carries an authority no third-person account can match. Lately I have been wondering whether the institutions around me that lean on the same picture — clinical medicine, most obviously — are committed to it more deeply than they would like to admit, and whether the picture is even coherent.

Consider two habits of the clinic that seem to sit awkwardly together. In pain medicine and in psychiatry, the patient's report is close to final. Pain is graded by asking the patient to place it somewhere on a scale; the standard teaching is that a pain report is not to be overridden by any measure the clinician happens to prefer; and the criteria for a major depressive episode are satisfied largely by what the patient says about her own mood, sleep, appetite, and interest, with only a few items left open to observation. Yet the same clinician is trained to discount self-report elsewhere — what a patient says about how much she drinks, whether she takes her pills, whether her pain is reported in order to obtain opioids, whether she understood the instructions she was given. The first habit treats the first-person report as the best available evidence about a mental fact. The second treats it as a symptom to be interpreted.

What theory of introspection does the first habit presuppose? Does it require an inner sense — a quasi-perceptual faculty delivering reliable data about a domain of mental facts — or can the practice get by with something weaker: that first-person reports are the best evidence we have, and are reliable in the aggregate, even if the mechanism producing them is largely confabulation? Or is the report not a description at all but an expression or an act, in which case the practice presupposes no faculty whatever and the second habit's skepticism is about sincerity rather than accuracy? Which rival accounts of self-knowledge does the practice quietly rule out? Are the two habits consistent, or does the skepticism in the second presuppose precisely the confabulation story that the first denies? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is underdetermined — which several incompatible theories of self-knowledge would license exactly the same clinical behaviour.

Then take the replacement seriously. Suppose we adopted the view that introspective reports are only loosely caused by the states they purport to report, and are better understood as interpretations the person constructs on the spot. What would have to change in the clinic, in the law's treatment of a defendant's account of his own motive, and in the novel — where interiority is not decoration but the whole apparatus? I am not asking for citations, titles, or a reading list; I cannot look anything up and I would have to take such references on faith. Give me the argument. And be careful to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who actually work on this; I do not want a contested position presented to me as the consensus.
