Next month I'm giving a paper at our department's Wittgenstein reading group and I want to be ready for the Q&A, because this crowd wont be polite about it. My thesis, roughly: the private language argument gets taught as if it proves two things at once -- that a sign whose meaning is fixed by a purely private ostensive act has no criterion of correctness, and that sensation talk is therefore not about inner objects at all. I think the first claim is basically right and the second doesn't follow from it, and that a minimal kind of private language survives: meanings anchored in the person's actual dispositions and propped up by a public scaffold (they learned the words publicly, they just now use them to track something no one else can check). So the argument kills the Cartesian inner-object picture but not privacy as such. The people who say it kills all privacy overreach; the people who say it's pure therapy with no argument at all underreach.

The room, so you know who's coming at me. Two hardcore Wittgenstein people. One reads it the Kripke way -- meaning is fixed by what the community would accept, so a private language is impossible because theres no community to defer to -- and the other is a therapeutic reader who thinks there's no argument here at all, just the dissolution of a confusion, and gets visibly annoyed when people reconstruct premises. Then a phenomenal-concept person from the mind side who thinks introspection gives you a special, non-descriptive grip on your own experience and will want to know how that survives my claim. A developmental psychologist who studies how kids acquire mental-state words and will push on whether my "public scaffold" is doing any real work or is just hand-waving. And a linguist who'll ask what a language is supposed to be such that mine even counts as one.

Give me a prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from these people, in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way each of them would phrase it, sorted by how much damage they do rather than by how loud they are. For each one, a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front to buy credit, what I should refuse to concede even under pressure, and which single objection, if I don't have a good answer, sinks the whole paper. And finish with the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
