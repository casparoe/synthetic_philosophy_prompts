I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'm stuck on an argument I built up while thinking about the uniform priors and sampling procedures I use at work. Each step looks fine to me and the conclusion is one I can't accept. Tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs; don't just recite the standard resolution, since I've read versions of it and can't see where it connects to the argument below.

(1) When nothing in my evidence distinguishes two possibilities, I should treat them as equally likely.
(2) Take a circle with an inscribed equilateral triangle, and consider a chord drawn at random. Nothing in my evidence distinguishes one chord from another, so every chord is equally likely.
(3) There are at least three ways to draw a random chord, each of which is just (2) applied: pick two endpoints uniformly on the circumference; pick a direction and then a perpendicular distance from the center uniformly along a radius; pick the chord's midpoint uniformly over the disk. These yield 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 as the probability that the chord is longer than a side of the triangle.
(4) Each procedure selects the same kind of object, a chord, and each asks about the same event, so the three numbers are three answers to one question.
(5) At most one can be correct, and (1) gives me no way to choose; so the principle of indifference, applied honestly, licenses contradictory answers.
(6) This is not special to chords. Reparameterize any continuum and indifference follows: for a mixture known to contain between one and two parts water per part wine, indifference over the water-to-wine ratio makes the probability that it contains at most one and a half parts water per part wine equal to 1/2, while indifference over the wine-to-water ratio makes the same probability 2/3.
(7) So no probability assignment over a continuum is ever justified by my evidence. Uniform priors, picking a random point, and the sampling in my simulation are conventions I impose, not conclusions I'm entitled to.

I can live without (7) only if I can say exactly where the slide happens. In particular: if the repair is that a random chord must come with a specified sampling procedure, is the paradox then about under-specification rather than about indifference, and does the answer become a fact about the procedure rather than about the chord? And does requiring the answer to be invariant under the symmetries of the setup, rotating the circle and moving it about, actually single out one of the three numbers, or is that a stipulation dressed up as a constraint?
