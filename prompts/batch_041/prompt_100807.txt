For the past month my school's debate club has been arguing over whether the administration may forbid students to wear a particular political slogan on their shirts, and twice the discussion has collapsed when someone said, in a tone that implied the matter was closed, that your freedom ends where my nose begins. I understand the picture well enough — that liberty is a space you are free to occupy until you collide with someone else — but I cannot work out what the sentence actually commits us to, and I have started to suspect that the people who reach for it are each smuggling in a different claim.

Could you lay out the distinct precise theses that this one-liner might be expressing? I would like a menu rather than a single interpretation, with the readings separated along the axes that actually do the work. Among the things I am unsure how to pin down: what counts as the "nose" — bodily integrity alone, or also property, reputation, feelings, mere offense, or a dignitary injury to a group rather than a person; whose freedom and whose nose are at issue, and whether the parties are individuals, associations, or the state; whether harm is offered as a necessary condition on interference, a sufficient one, or merely a defeasible reason that can be outweighed; whether the claim is about what the state may forbid or about what any of us may do to one another; whether "freedom" names the absence of interference or a protected sphere that interference violates; and whether the slogan assumes a baseline of liberty with carve-outs or a balancing test in which nothing is presumptively off-limits.

For each reading, please say whether it is a platitude that no one could deny, a substantive claim that is defensible, or a claim that is actually false — and then say which reading is in play when someone uses the line to shut down an argument about a t-shirt. I do not want a neutral survey that leaves the choice to me. Please commit: tell me which reading you think is correct, or at least which one the slogan is best understood as meaning, and give your reasons; and if you think the line is not worth keeping in circulation at all, say that plainly and explain why.
