I work in human factors and safety science — aviation and hospital incident investigation, mostly — and I have a reasonably firm command of the conceptual toolkit that comes with it: the person-versus-system contrast in explaining error, the image of layered defences with aligned holes, normalization of deviance, drift into failure, the distinction between slips, lapses, mistakes and violations, the claim that violations are often rational adaptations to goal conflict rather than evidence of deviance, the just-culture line between the reckless and the blameless, Safety-I versus Safety-II, and the work-as-imagined versus work-as-done distinction. My grasp of history, by contrast, is thin: two undergraduate survey courses and a couple of trade paperbacks, which is to say I know the outline of Christopher Browning's account of Reserve Police Battalion 101 — middle-aged reservists with no enthusiasm at the outset, an incremental escalation of what was demanded of them, a division of labour in which the shooting was parceled out, the steady availability of the path of least resistance, deference to the officers and comrades who volunteered first — and the outline of Daniel Goldhagen's reply, that these were not ordinary men overtaken by circumstance but willing executioners whose antisemitic convictions were doing the explanatory work. What I want is a systematic translation rather than a summary and rather than an analogy. Take each of the two positions in turn and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually use, marking as you go where the translation is clean, where it requires distortion, and where it has no expression at all. I am especially interested in the point where the two vocabularies collide. Under a system model, the reservists appear to have been conforming to the norms of their unit and their institution, which would seem to make them compliant rather than deviant; I suspect the safety-science apparatus will therefore end up describing the battalion's conduct as a normal outcome of its conditions rather than as a violation of anything. Tell me whether that is right, and what it implies.

Then give me the residue report, and be honest about the direction of the failure. Which parts of Browning's situational explanation map cleanly onto latent conditions, goal conflict, diffusion of responsibility, incremental normalisation and the like, and which parts do you have to smuggle in under those headings? Which parts of Goldhagen's dispositional explanation survive at all in a framework that deliberately brackets intention and motive in order to locate system causes — and if his thesis can only enter as one more background condition, alongside staffing levels and shift patterns, is that a distortion of his claim or a fair verdict on it? Where the translation fails, I want a judgement rather than a list: is the residue a genuine blind spot of safety science, a vocabulary that cannot say culpable, uncoerced, morally motivated conformity; or is it evidence that the original debate was less clear than it looked, that words like willing and ordinary and executioner are each doing several jobs at once and that no framework could carry all of them?

A colleague of mine, who is more confident about this than I am, says the whole dispute dissolves once you run the battalion through a proper investigation: you would find no single-point failure, a long incubation of small accommodations, a reporting culture that punished dissent, and you would file it as a case of drift. I want to know what that move buys and what it costs, and in particular whether it is exculpation by construction — an artefact of a method built to explain accidents without assigning blame — or whether it is simply the correct description and it is the moral vocabulary that is smuggling something in.
