I'm circulating a position paper ahead of a graduate seminar on statecraft and historical reasoning, and a colleague who read the draft has warned me — without saying where — that my views will not survive contact with one another. I would rather find that out now than in the room. What follows is the substance of the paper, and what I want is an audit of it as a set rather than commentary on its parts.

First, historical analogy is not a heuristic that a sufficiently rigorous thinker could discard; it is the medium in which anyone, statesman or model, represents a novel strategic situation at all, and there is no view from nowhere from which a present crisis can be compared with a past one without the present having already been assimilated to something. Second, a past case can be put to three different uses, and I think the distinction does real work: as a template, where it tells you what the present case is and therefore what to do; as a likelihood, where it contributes a base rate to a reference class; and as a landmark, where it fixes a reference point against which one can measure what has changed. Templates are the default, and I take them to be the pathology. Third, the reason analogies mislead is mainly cognitive: the cases that come to mind are the vivid, recent, and emotionally charged ones, so the selection is driven by availability and representativeness rather than by fit. Fourth, and following from that, the failure is one of process rather than content and is therefore mitigable: an analogy reached by deliberately canvassing candidate cases and scoring them for structural fit is worth more than one that arrives by salience, even when both end up citing the same case.

Fifth, the reading of Iraq in 2003 through the post-1945 occupations of Germany and Japan was not a failure of canvassing — the relevant analogies were selected deliberately, argued over, and scored as fitting, and the policy failed anyway — so I doubt that a better process would have caught it. Sixth, the canonical lessons persist for reasons unrelated to their reliability: Munich, Vietnam, and the Thucydides trap are resources for advocates, taken up by whoever needs them and retired when they do not, and the last of these strikes me as a framing with no predictive record rather than a finding. Seventh, history's genuine epistemic value is at the likelihood grade — how often a concession of this kind precedes war, how often a rising and a ruling power come to blows — and I think that is also the only grade at which we escape the business of arguing about whether this case is that case, which is where historical argument usually goes wrong.

That is the position. Please tell me which of the seven are genuinely in tension with which, which pairs only appear to be and dissolve on inspection, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each genuine one, give me the cheapest revision that removes it and say what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set; if a single revision would clean up more than one tension, say so. And if the set turns out to be consistent as it stands, say that plainly and give me the strongest objection to the package as a whole.
