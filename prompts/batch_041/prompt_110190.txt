I'm a novelist, currently about two-thirds of the way through a book set in a type foundry during the years when phototype was displacing metal, and for reasons too tedious to set out I have spent the last month drawing the letter 'a' — hundreds of them, in pencil, at every width, weight, and angle of the pen — partly to settle the display face for the jacket and partly for a chapter in which my protagonist invents a writing system for a language that has never had one. The exercise has turned me into an amateur of a question I cannot answer: what exactly makes a mark an 'a'? A friend of mine, who builds small computational models of design, insists this is the deepest problem in his field and that what he is really chasing is the spirit of a letter, the something that survives when the style is peeled off. I have been keeping a notebook of my own verdicts on cases as they occur to me, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. A geometric sans in the Futura manner, bowl and stem, is an 'a'; the double-storey 'a' of a book serif is an 'a', and I say it is the same letter as the first, not a cousin. A child's scrawl, an open-topped loop with a tail, is an 'a'. An 'a' whose counter is filled solid by a blob of ink is still an 'a'. Whether the bowl is round, triangular, square, or hexagonal is pure style; so is whether the bowl closes at the top or stands open like a 'c' with a stem. A broken piece of type whose stem floats a hair's breadth from the bowl is an 'a' — though if the stem floats half an inch away it is two marks, not a letter, and I freely admit I cannot locate the line between those two. Turn an 'a' upside down or mirror it and it is not an 'a'; I see a letter the wrong way up, not a letter I can read. The Cyrillic letter that is shape-for-shape identical to my Futura 'a' is nevertheless not the same letter as the Latin one, and the Greek alpha is not the same letter either, though I can see it is the ancestor. The capital 'A' is the same letter as the lowercase 'a', despite sharing no shape with it at all.

Two of these verdicts I cannot hold together, and I would rather you tell me which to surrender than smooth them over. First: shape apparently counts for nothing, since a single-storey 'a' and a double-storey 'a' are one letter — yet shape apparently counts for everything, since a Cyrillic 'а' identical in outline to my Latin 'a' is a different letter. Second: if I stretch the stem of my single-storey 'a' a third of the way up the ascender I still call it an 'a', merely a gangly one; but the 'd' in that same font is exactly that shape with a longer stem, and it is emphatically not an 'a'. My own tentative principle, offered for you to test and repair rather than to accept, is this: an 'a' is any mark that a fluent reader of the script, meeting it in the running text of a word, would read as 'a' without hesitation and without noticing anything at all. That handles the blob, the triangle, the scrawl, and the broken type rather well, I think, but it makes letterhood hostage to the habits of readers — would an upside-down 'a' become an 'a' in a community that read everything upside down? — and it says nothing useful about the Cyrillic case, where a reader fluent in the script reads that shape as a perfectly ordinary 'a' that is simply not our 'a'.

What I want is the account itself, not a survey of what others have said about it: the principle or small set of competing principles that gets my verdicts, with the specific case that forces each clause into the formulation, that principle's verdicts on cases I have not listed — say, an 'a' whose bowl and stem are the same weight throughout versus one drawn with a calligraphic thick-thin, or a shape that is an 'a' in every respect except that I have assigned it to the sound /m/ in the invented script of my chapter — and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot keep. If no single principle saves them all, I would like to see the two or three candidates that come closest, what each of them buys and what it costs, and a straight answer about which of my judgments you would give up and why.
