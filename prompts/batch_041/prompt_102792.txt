I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been reading my way into the measurement problem in the many-worlds interpretation for a reading group I run with a few colleagues who are also engineers. I can follow the physics at the level of the wavefunction never collapsing and measurement simply entangling the observer with the apparatus, and I understand the standard story well enough to know that the whole fight is not about branching but about probability: if every outcome is realized in some branch, what is it even supposed to mean that one outcome is more probable than another, and why should whatever plays the role of probability come out equal to the squared amplitude in the Born rule rather than, say, equal for every branch? I've heard enough popular discussion to know that a lot of capable people have answered this in very different ways, but when I try to line those answers up they collapse into a blur of names. What I want from you is a decision tree — an instrument, not an essay — that takes someone who has already accepted the branching picture and leads them, question by question, to a definite position on where probability comes from.

Please do not build the tree out of names or out of who said what. Build it out of the questions first, so that each node is a fork someone could answer without already knowing which label it leads to, and only then attach the names to the leaves. I have no web access when I use this, so no reading lists, no citations, no see-so-and-so — just the positions, described in enough detail that I can tell them apart. Treat the starting point as fixed: the person at the root already believes the wavefunction evolves unitarily and that measurements have many outcomes, and is not asking whether to be an Everettian but which Everettian account of probability to adopt. I'd like roughly six to nine leaves. Certain positions have to appear as leaves, whatever else you include: the one that says the squared amplitude is simply a new postulate bolted onto the theory; the decision-theoretic line on which a rational branching agent's betting behavior is what makes the amplitudes its probabilities; the self-locating line on which the agent's uncertainty about which branch it inhabits does the work; the view that the felt probability is an illusion the theory should explain away rather than reproduce; and the critical view that no consistent notion of probability can be attached to a branching universe at all, so the program either needs an extra ingredient or fails. I also want at least one node that separates accounts on which probability is a feature of the world — a measure sitting on the branches whether or not anyone believes anything — from accounts on which it is a feature of agents.

Please give me the response in these sections, in this order. First, a Setup of two or three sentences, stating the fixed starting commitment and what the instrument is meant to sort. Second, The tree, maybe five hundred to seven hundred words, as numbered nodes: each node a question with its possible answers and where each answer leads, phrased so a reader can answer it without knowing the labels. Third, The leaves, five hundred to six hundred words, one entry per leaf, each with the name of the position (or unnamed if nobody defends it), a one-line characterization, and a single sentence on its most awkward commitment. Fourth, Branches nobody occupies, about one hundred fifty words, listing the combinations of answers that no one actually holds and saying briefly whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Fifth, Positions the tree cannot separate, about one hundred words, naming any leaves that differ only in details my questions do not reach.
