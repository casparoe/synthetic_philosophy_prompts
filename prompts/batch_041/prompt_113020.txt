My friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about Thomas Szasz, and neither of us moved the other. It began from the thesis of his 1961 book — that mental illness is a myth, that what psychiatry calls diseases are better understood as problems in living, and that the medical vocabulary is a way of recasting moral and political judgments about conduct as clinical findings — and it turned into a fight about a single episode. My friend holds that the last half-century has vindicated him, and his clinching case is the American Psychiatric Association's removal of homosexuality from its diagnostic manual: a category listed as a disorder since the early 1950s, struck out in December 1973 by a vote of the association's trustees and confirmed the following year by a ballot of the full membership, with a compromise diagnosis left in its place for people distressed by their own orientation, which survived under different names into the 1980s. For him this is about as clean an example as one could want of a disease being manufactured and then abolished by social pressure rather than by discovery — the category was there, then it wasn't, and what changed in between was a vote. I hold that the episode cuts the other way: it shows a profession able to correct itself on the strength of argument and evidence, and a theory that must call every such correction a mere relabeling is not so much a theory of psychiatry as a refusal to be surprised by it.

I would like you to adjudicate this by analyzing that episode through Szasz's framework, not by summarizing the framework or the history. Do the mapping first, and keep it honest: on his account, who are the parties here, what is a body of psychiatrists doing when it votes on a diagnosis, and what does the theory actually predict about how such a category arises, what sustains it, and what happens when it is withdrawn? The facts, so far as I have them, are these: the empirical case had been building since the late 1940s, with Kinsey's surveys and then Evelyn Hooker's 1957 study, in which expert judges could not distinguish homosexual from heterosexual men on the standard projective tests, while the pressure at the conventions was political and confrontational, beginning with the disruption of the 1970 meeting in San Francisco; the removal left behind a residual category rather than clearing the ground; and the manual has grown several-fold since. Say what the theory illuminates here — the deference to social norms, the contingency of the boundary, the moral work the diagnosis was doing — and then what it misdescribes or cannot see, including whether what happened in 1973 was a correction of a moral judgment or merely a redistribution of it. Where the framework is silent, say so rather than making it speak.

Then I want the harder question, which is what the episode does to the theory. Szasz needs some criterion separating a diagnosis that is a disguised moral judgment from one that is not, and I want you to reconstruct the best version of that criterion from his position — the appeal to an underlying bodily lesion, or to the coercion and condemnation a category licenses, or something else — and then test it against this case rather than against his usual targets. If a diagnosis is mythological whenever no lesion underlies it, what becomes of the large parts of general medicine that also lack one? If the criterion is instead that a category licenses coercion and moral condemnation, is that a principled demarcation or a restatement of his libertarian politics, and does it survive a case where the category was withdrawn without the machinery being dismantled? Can the framework explain the two facts my friend never accounts for — that the profession did revise, and that the replacement category kept the apparatus running? Reach a verdict: does the episode vindicate Szasz, refute him, or reveal that he was answering a different question than the one the episode poses? I would also like to know whether the idea — Ian Hacking's, if I have the attribution right — that classifications change the people classified, who then change the classifications, explains this sequence better than Szasz does, or merely redescribes it in friendlier terms.
