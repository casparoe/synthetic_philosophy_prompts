I want to spar with Hobbes about why he treated geometry as the model of demonstrative knowledge. Play him — mid-1650s, after De Corpore and after Wallis has started tearing into his circle-squaring, prickly but genuinely argumentative rather than a strawman, and please stay in character until I say stop, keeping each reply to a paragraph or so. You can use only the concepts he'd have had; if I mention something modern you need explained, ask. My opening move: you say geometry is demonstrable because we make the figures ourselves — we draw the circle, generate it by motion, and so know it from the inside, the same way you say we can know the commonwealth because we made that too. But you've now botched the squaring of the circle more than once and Wallis keeps finding the errors, and that doesn't look like bad luck. It looks like the making gives you the figure but not the relation between the figures, which is the very thing the geometer is trying to prove. So which is it: does your criterion fail to be sufficient for demonstration, or is the geometer not really making the thing he claims to know?
