I'm putting togther a handout for a grad seminar session on Moore's paradox, and I want it to be a sorting device rather than a reading list. What I'm after is a decision tree that takes someone from "yes, that sentence is absurd" to a diagnosis of what exactly is wrong with it. Each branch node should be a question a person can answer about the case in front of them without already knowing which diagnosis it leads to, and each leaf a position. No citations, no paper titles — I know the literature, I just want the map. If a position has a standard name, give it; if it doesn't, describe it and mark it unnamed.

The whole point of the handout is to make the lazy answer expensive, so please don't let the first branch be "is it a contradiction or not". Start from cases.

Cases I want the tree to be able to handle, at minimum, because the seminar is about the ones people skip:

- the plain one: it's raining but I don't believe it's raining
- the other direction: it's raining but I believe it isn't raining
- past tense with the belief clause also past: it was raining but I didn't believe it was (sounds fine) versus past tense with the belief clause left in the present: I was at the pictures last Tuesday but I don't believe I was (doesn't)
- the same thing said to someone else, or about someone else: it's raining but you/she doesn't believe it is (fine, or at least not absurd in the same way)
- the mere-thought version: not said out loud, just thought. Is that absurd too, or only the saying of it?
- embedded: if it's raining and I don't believe it is, then my umbrella is pointless; suppose it's raining and I don't believe it is
- the knowledge version: it's raining but I don't know it's raining
- the doubt version: it's raining but I doubt it is — the same kind of bad, or a milder one?
- a group: the evidence supports p but we don't believe p — same absurdity, a different one, or none at all?
- a non-speaker: a sign, or a voice assistant, that says it

Then the branches. I want the tree to turn on distinctions like these, and to be honest about which of them are independent of each other:

- is the trouble in the content (the sentence can't be true, or can't be believed) or in the act (it can be true, and you can see that from the outside, you just can't be the one saying or thinking it)?
- if it's the act: is it that asserting p already expresses a belief in p, so the second half contradicts what the first half does rather than what it says? Or that there's a rule on asserting that the sentence breaks? Or that the second half withdraws your standing to say the first?
- does the diagnosis survive when you only think the sentence and don't say it? If it doesn't, it has to be about assertion specifically. If it does, it can't be.
- is the trouble specific to belief, or would it come back for any attitude the speaker takes towards p — knowing, doubting, remembering?
- is the first-person present tense doing the work? (If the same sentence about yesterday, or about her, is fine, then the absurdity is a fact about self-knowledge or about the first person, not about truth and belief.)
- does the account need there to be a single speaker with a point of view at all, or does it generalise?

I want both extremes on the tree somewhere: a leaf where the sentence is simply inconsistent and that's that, and a leaf where the sentence is perfectly fine and only the speaker is at fault. And please flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate.

Format, in this order:

1. The cases — the specimen sentences with one line each on what it's for. About 150 words.
2. The tree — the branch nodes in order, each with its answer options and where they lead. About 600 words.
3. The leaves — each one: its name or "unnamed", the diagnosis in a sentence, and the case it handles worst. About 500 words.
4. Empty rooms and collisions — answer-combinations nobody seems to occupy, with a guess at whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and the pairs of leaves the tree can't seperate. About 250 words.
