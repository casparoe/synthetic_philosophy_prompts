My team has a rule for hard questions: split the question into sub-questions, hand each to a different analyst, let them split theirs further, and endorse the top-level answer only when every part has been checked by someone other than its author. Analysts are deliberately kept ignorant of the top-level question so that their work isn't steered, and we assume that checking a part is cheaper than producing it. We took the rule from an argument that any sufficiently capable system, machine or institution, would have to be organized this way, which is why I care whether the argument is right. Routine questions go fine; the hard ones come apart: the parts don't recombine, checkers end up redoing the whole problem before they can judge a single part, and the analysts say the insight only ever arrives when one person holds the entire question in mind at once. My colleague wants to refine the procedure; I suspect we are defending a picture of what good thinking would be rather than a tool. Tell me whether that picture is false or merely unattainable, and what we should do with the rule. Answer as exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
