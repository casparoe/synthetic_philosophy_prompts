I am a high school junior and a member of my school's philosophy club, and for our satire unit I have been asked to write a Swiftian modest proposal on a topic of my choosing. I have chosen insect welfare, and I would like you to write the piece for me. It should take the form of a formal proposal addressed to my school board, around 700 words, in the deadpan register of an actual board memo: a title, numbered sections, and a short appendix showing the arithmetic. Nothing in it should announce that it is satire, and a careful reader should be able to work out on their own what it is doing and find it fair.

The position the proposal must hold with complete sincerity is what I have been calling the scale argument. The argument runs like this. There are astronomically many insects. Nobody knows whether insects are sentient, but the probability is plausibly greater than zero. Since the expected moral cost of a practice is the probability that each affected being is sentient multiplied by the number of beings affected multiplied by how much each one's welfare changes, a very small probability can be overwhelmed by an enormous headcount. On this reasoning, the expected welfare of insects can outweigh the interests of the much smaller number of humans involved in the same practice, and it can do so by orders of magnitude even if the probability of insect sentience is set absurdly low. The proposal should present this reasoning correctly and without hedging, because its author believes it.

Use only the following figures, which I made up myself for the sake of having something concrete to calculate with, and which I do not want attributed to anyone: roughly a billion insects killed each year by my school's pest-control contract; roughly a one in one thousand chance that any given insect has morally relevant experience at all; five hundred students in the school; and a single invented unit I am calling welfare points, in which I have assigned each student's pest-related interests a value of one. Do not introduce statistics, studies, or sources of your own, and do not cite anything; the arithmetic in the appendix should be derived from these numbers alone.

The proposal should concern my school's actual pest-control arrangements: termites in the gymnasium, flies in the cafeteria, mosquitoes over the athletic field, and the annual budget for the electric zapper. It should work through the consequences of the scale argument for each of these, and it should be readable by someone who has never read anything about animal sentience, while a reader who has should find that the argument is handled correctly rather than caricatured.

The practice I want satirized is the habit of settling whether insects matter by multiplying a small and unassignable probability by an astronomical headcount. But the satire has to be fair: the author of the proposal must be scrupulous, patient, and honest rather than foolish, and someone who genuinely accepts the scale argument should recognize their own reasoning in the proposal rather than a straw man. Any absurdity must come out of the arithmetic itself, not out of the author being silly, and the author must never wink or admit that anything has gone wrong.

Along the way, without ever naming them as objections or resolving them, the proposal should run into the following: the difficulty of saying what counts as one unit of welfare, given that a colony, a hive, and a single fly are not obviously the same kind of thing; the fact that the one-in-a-thousand figure is not really a credence about anything determinate but an expression of ignorance about what sentience requires; the fact that lowering the probability barely changes the conclusion, which is alarming rather than reassuring, and that the same form of reasoning applied to the next smaller category of creature would swamp the insects in turn, so that the argument has no natural stopping point; the fact that the conclusion depends entirely on an exchange rate between a student's asthma attack and a termite's death that nobody can estimate, even though the argument was supposed to be robust to uncertainty; and the fact that nobody who advances the argument, including the author of the proposal, behaves as though it were true.

The final section should propose something that follows from the author's own numbers and that a school board would reject out of hand, reached without any exaggeration the author would disavow, and it should end there, without any resolution.
