I'm a backend engineer at a mid-sized AI lab — my background is distributed systems, and I have no formal training in philosophy or in the safety literature. For two quarters now our platform organization has carried a single roadmap epic called Alignment, and my director has asked me to write the design document that turns it into something we can actually staff. I'd like you to write that document.

Format and length: an internal RFC of roughly 800–1000 words, in a deadpan corporate engineering register, with numbered sections — Context, Problem Statement, Non-Goals, Definition of Done, Proposed Approach, Alternatives Considered, Risks and Mitigations, Rollout Plan, Open Questions. Write it in the first person, as its reluctant author. No external citations, no real researchers or labs named; where the document needs authority, attribute it to invented internal artifacts and invented colleagues.

The humor has to come from the substance, not from the word alignment being vague. Someone who works on this should recognize the real fault lines and find it fair rather than embarrassing. Please build in, without ever announcing them as the joke: the gap between a system doing what its specification says and the specification capturing what we actually wanted; the possibility that what a trained system ends up optimizing diverges from what training rewarded; whether human values are a well-defined target at all, given that people disagree, change their minds, and would revise on reflection; whether this is a technical problem, a coordination problem among labs, or a moral one, and the fact that our org chart quietly answers that question; and the suspicion that the sub-problems don't decompose — that closing one ticket reopens the others. Let the jargon be internally invented but recognizable; the document can distinguish, say, intent alignment from performance alignment, and can invoke Goodhart's law.

Some specifics I want the document to get wrong in revealing ways: a Non-Goals section that disposes of the hardest questions by assigning them to Legal or to a research team that does not exist; acceptance criteria no test could fail; an Alternatives Considered section that dismisses each alternative in one line; a Risks table in which every mitigation is itself an unsolved research problem; and a Rollout Plan with a canary and a rollback that presuppose the failure would be observable, contained, and reversible. Include a review comment thread of four or five comments in which two engineers argue past each other because they mean different things by alignment, and a safety reviewer who keeps asking whose values are in scope and keeps being told that this is out of scope for this document.

End with the author closing the RFC anyway, on schedule, with a date. A reader should finish holding the live question of whether this was ever one problem, without the document ever admitting that it might not be.
