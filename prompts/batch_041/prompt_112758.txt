In my comparative political theory course we keep encountering the claim — made about Singapore and China — that a government can earn its authority through results: sustained growth, public order, rising living standards, rather than through competitive elections. I want to know what that justifying practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory of legitimacy. Does it presuppose that legitimacy is a matter of outputs rather than consent — that some threshold of welfare and order, once met, makes electoral accountability unnecessary — or only that performance is one consideration among several? Which rival accounts does it quietly rule out, and which does it leave compatible, given that a consent-based or rights-based theory might treat performance as evidence that genuine consent is possible rather than as a substitute for it? Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: can a purely procedural account, or a purely prudential one, license the same conclusions, or does the practice strictly require a substantive theory of the people's interests that is knowable independently of what they would choose? Keep the descriptive question separate from the normative one — is the claim that people do in fact accept such a regime as legitimate, or that they should? Finally, tell me what would have to change if someone defended the same practice while refusing any paternalism, treating prosperity and order only as conditions that make genuine consent possible rather than as the ground of authority.
