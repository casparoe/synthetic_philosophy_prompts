I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but I've spent the last few months listening to podcasts and reading forum threads about the so-called hard problem of consciousness, and a colleague recently walked me through an argument that I cannot shake. He grants that the brain does everything we can measure — information processing, report generation, the control of behavior — and that all of this will in time be explained by ordinary science. His claim is only that there is a further fact, what it is like to be the system, and that no amount of the first kind of explanation could ever reach it. What troubles me is not the conclusion but the shape of his reasoning, which he laid out as follows. (1) A century ago there was a hard problem of life: no physical or chemical account of an organism seemed to explain why it was alive rather than a mere mechanism, and so people posited a special life-force. (2) We now treat that as a confusion — once you have metabolism, growth, reproduction, and the rest, there is no leftover fact of being alive to account for, and vitalism was simply a pseudo-problem. (3) The hard problem of consciousness has exactly the same shape: once you have the complete physical and functional account of a system, a further fact appears to be left over, namely what it is like to be it. (4) So either there is no leftover fact in either case, or there was a leftover fact in both. (5) Vitalism was wrong. (6) Therefore there is no leftover fact about experience, and the hard problem is a pseudo-problem.

I cannot decide whether to reject a premise — and if so, which — or to swallow the conclusion. My hunch is that (4) is the weak link, since I feel there is an asymmetry between life and experience that the argument flattens; but I cannot state the asymmetry without begging the question, because the entire dispute is whether experience is exhausted by the physical and the functional. I would far rather keep (1) through (3) and (5), which strike me as plainly true, than accept (6), which seems to deny something I am more certain of than anything else; still, I want to know what I would be paying. Please tell me which premise or step to give up, why it looked so plausible, and what each of the recognizable positions — the one that dissolves the hard problem entirely, the one that keeps the hard problem but denies that the analogy to life holds, and the one that bites the bullet and accepts that experience is something over and above the physical — would have to sacrifice in order to reject it.
