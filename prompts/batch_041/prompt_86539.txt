So here's a thing I keep coming back to. In 1957 a market researcher named James Vicary held a press conference and said he'd been flashing the words "Eat Popcorn" and "Drink Coca-Cola" onto a movie screen in Fort Lee, New Jersey, for a three-thousandth of a second at a time, too fast to read, and that popcorn sales in the lobby went up 57.5% and Coke sales 18.1%. That story is basically the founding case for the idea of subliminal manipulation -- it's why the CIA wrote a report on subliminal perception in 1958 and why the FCC eventually declared subliminal advertising contrary to the public interest. And it was a lie. He never replicated it, a journalist who tracked down the theatre's manager was told no such test ever ran, and in 1962 Vicary admitted the whole thing had been "a gimmick" to drum up buisness for a failing research firm.

I want you to use that episode as a test case for the manipulation/persuasion distinction. There are roughly three families of account of what makes influence manipulative, and I want each run against the case: (a) manipulation is influence that bypasses or short-circuits the target's rational deliberation, so they can't see it coming and can't answer it; (b) manipulation is influence whose manipulative character lies in the hidden intent -- the influencer is trying to move you while concealing that that's what they're doing; (c) manipulation is a matter of mechanism -- seduction, priming, exploiting a cognitive glitch, playing on a vulnerability -- as opposed to giving someone reasons they can weigh.

Now the questions I actually care about. First, which of these has anything to say about the theatre, given that nothing happened there? (a) and (c) are about a mechanism, and the mechanism never ran; (b) survives, since Vicary's intent and concealment were real -- so is he a manipulator who failed to manipulate, or does that account quietly need the influence to work? Second, and this is the part I find genuinely interesting: the story did all the work. Nobody's popcorn habits moved, but the public's beliefs about advertising moved, and so did policy, and the story that moved them was itself an interested piece of salesmanship, told by a man with a business to save, in a shape that flattered journalists and regulators who wanted a villain. Is the panic then a better instance of manipulation than the thing it was a panic about -- and does that mean the concept tracks the target's agency (was the public's deliberation short-circuited?) rather than the mechanism, given that the mechanism here is just a story? Third: suppose the popcorn thing had worked. Does any of these accounts give me a principled line between subliminal influence and an ordinary ad that also bypasses deliberation by pairing a product with a feeling? If the answer is no, say so, and tell me whether the case shows the concept is doing something other than what the accounts say it does -- e.g., that "manipulation" is largely an accusation, a way of discrediting influence you don't like.

Be disciplined about the mapping, and say where the accounts go quiet instead of making them speak. 800 words max.
