I am preparing a proposal for a twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship in political philosophy, normative theory only, with no fieldwork or archival component, and I need it written as a plan for work rather than as an argument. My half-formed idea concerns conscription in states whose declared posture is military neutrality rather than alliance or deterrence-by-punishment. The existing ethical debate over conscription treats it as a claim the state makes on its citizens' bodies and lives for the sake of national defence, and then asks whether the cause is just and whether the burden is fairly shared; both questions presuppose that the conscript is a prospective combatant. I want to ask a narrower question that I believe this framing cannot reach: in a neutral state, is conscription justified in part because it constitutes the neutrality rather than merely serving it? Two thoughts drive the idea. First, neutrality is not a policy a state can adopt unilaterally; it is a classification other states must grant, and it is credible only if the neutral state's forces are visibly unsuited to the offensive operations that would make it a threat, which is why a universal militia, awkward and expensive as it is, may signal non-aggression more convincingly than a professional army usable for anything. Second, if that is right, the justification for conscription rests partly on what the conscripts' service communicates to foreign governments, which raises an uncomfortable possibility: the standard liberal test, which asks whether the same defensive goal could be met by a less restrictive alternative such as a professional or selective force, may fail not because the alternative is unavailable but because adopting it would undermine the very goal. I cannot tell whether this rescues conscription from the usual objections or makes it worse than ordinary defence conscription, since a conscript used as a signal is not even being used to defend himself, and since the risk in the neutral case is latent while the costs in time, career, and autonomy are certain.

Please write the proposal as exactly five numbered sections, each a short paragraph of two to four sentences, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no citations; I will attach the references myself. In order: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer; why the existing positions on conscription leave it open; the method I would actually follow and why it is preferable to the obvious alternatives, namely empirical deterrence research and a general theory of political obligation; what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest account of what fits in twelve months and what would make it too big.
