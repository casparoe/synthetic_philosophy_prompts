I'm a graduate student in decision theory, and a disagreement in our reading group has become stubborn enough that I want the case made in its strongest form. The result at issue is the one usually attributed to I. J. Good: if an agent maximizes expected utility, if the information she would receive is costless, and if she is free to postpone her choice until after she has received and absorbed it, then the expected utility she can look forward to, computed in advance and averaged over the possible observations, cannot be lower than the expected utility of choosing now on her present information. The proof is a single line, since in every possible observation the agent may simply ignore what she has learned and take the act that was optimal on the prior, and an average of maxima cannot fall below a maximum of averages. What I want from you is an argument, as forceful and as careful as you can make it, that this is the whole of the theorem's content: that it is a fact about conditional expectations and the maximum operator rather than a substantive norm of rationality, and that the slogan it is so often recruited to defend, namely that a rational agent can never be made worse off by learning something, is either false or trivially true depending on how one has fixed the outcome space.

To earn that conclusion rather than merely assert it, I would like you to do four things. State the result in enough detail that its assumptions are visible, and say precisely which of them bear the weight. Then exhibit at least one concrete decision problem in which the nonnegativity is an artifact, the clearest candidates being an agent for whom learning is itself an outcome she cares about, so that the theorem's assumption of one fixed utility function over one fixed outcome space is violated by the very question at issue, and an agent whose preferences are not expected-utility, for whom free information can genuinely reduce expected value — the standard illustration being the ambiguity-averse agent whose estimate of an unrelated quantity grows more vague precisely because she learned something about a related one, so that she is worse off for having looked. Then anticipate the strongest reply open to the other side, that the theorem is not empty because it constrains diachronic coherence and rules out, for any expected-utility agent, a genuine preference for remaining ignorant, and that the awkward cases lie outside its scope rather than refuting it; explain why that reply either concedes your point or repairs the theorem only by rendering it unfalsifiable. Finally, and only if you can do it honestly, say what would have to be added to the result for it to constitute a real constraint on what an agent may prefer: whether any strengthening survives once the outcome space is permitted to include the agent's informational state, and what the theorem would then be claiming. I want the argument in your own words, with the assumptions laid bare; I do not want a survey, and I have no use for citations.
