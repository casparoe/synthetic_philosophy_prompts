I'm putting together a reading group session on the killing / letting die distinction and I'd like to open it with a poem rather than a lecture, so can you write me a villanelle? The twist is in the refrains. I want the two repeated lines to be the same line except for one word — something like "I let it happen" set against "I made it happen" — so that the entire moral distance between letting someone die and killing them has to fit inside that single word. You'll probably want to choose your own wording so that you've still got a rhyme to work with, since the refrains have to rhyme with each other, but keep the one-word gap. The form should be doing the arguing: each time the refrains come back they should land a bit differently, and I want the closing couplet, where the two of them finally sit next to each other, to be the moment where the distinction either falls apart or turns out to be real. Don't tell me which one it is — let it sit there.

Setting: a hospice ward at night, a patient in the last few days, a syringe driver, a nurse on nights, family in the corridor who can't agree with each other. I want three moments in it, roughly: the driver that was never started; the driver that was started and then taken down; and the driver turned up when the pain got bad. The middle one should feel like the hinge, because everyone I've ever talked to is comfortable with not starting it and gets the shivers about stopping it once it's going, and nobody has ever managed to explain to me what the actual difference is supposed to be. And the third one, where the nurse means the pain and can see the other thing coming, should have that in it without turning into a sermon either way.

No trolleys, no runaway trains, no fat men on bridges please, I've read enough of those. It should definately be accurate enough that someone who works on action theory wouldn't wince, and it should still be a good poem for someone who's never thought about any of this.

After the poem, give me three or four lines of notes: where it takes liberties with the doing/allowing distinction or leans on a contested reading of it, how confident you are in what you're claiming there, and which single choice or claim in the poem your least sure of.
