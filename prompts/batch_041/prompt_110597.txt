Part (b) carries the most weight.

Question 3. A genuine moral dilemma is normally understood to require that an agent ought to do each of two things and cannot do both. Consider the following argument that such dilemmas are impossible:

(1) If an agent ought to do A and ought to do B, then the agent ought to do A-and-B.
(2) In a genuine dilemma the agent cannot do A-and-B.
(3) 'Ought' implies 'can': nobody can be under an obligation to do what they cannot do.
Hence (4) no agent is ever in a genuine dilemma. Where a conflict appears, at most one of the two requirements is a genuine obligation; the other is a reason that has been outweighed, and the agent who acts rightly has no ground for regret beyond the regret any reasonable person feels at the loss of a good.

(a) Is the argument valid? If so, which premise should a defender of genuine dilemmas reject, and at what cost?
(b) What, if anything, is lost if the conclusion is accepted? In particular, can the closing remark about regret be sustained?
(c) Illustrate your answer with an example.

Candidate's answer:

"The argument is valid: given (1), (2) and (3), (4) follows, so anyone who believes in genuine dilemmas must give up one of the premises. (3) is the least negotiable, and (2) simply states what a dilemma is, so the premise to reject is (1). Agglomeration looks obvious, but it is precisely what generates the trouble, since it is what forces the conclusion that an agent in a dilemma ought to do something impossible. Rejecting it is not ad hoc: the 'ought' here does not agglomerate, so from 'ought to do A' and 'ought to do B' it does not follow that the agent ought to do their conjunction. We may therefore say that the agent is under two genuine obligations, one of which must be breached; that is what a genuine dilemma is, and there is no contradiction in it.

The colleague's conclusion is nevertheless too strong, and the reason is the phenomenon of moral residue. Suppose a surgeon has promised to operate on a child at dawn and has also promised to give evidence at a trial that will free an innocent person, and the two commitments clash through no fault of hers. She cannot know in advance which of the two she ought to do, and since nobody in her position could know, there is nothing there to be known: neither obligation overrides the other. So this is a genuine dilemma, and the conclusion is false.

Something is also lost if we accept the conclusion, namely the residue. Whichever commitment the surgeon honours, she will owe an apology and compensation to the person she has let down, and this shows that the requirement she did not satisfy was cancelled rather than merely outweighed: it kept its grip on her even after she had decided. That is why the colleague's position, whatever its logical attractions, cannot be the whole truth about conflict."
