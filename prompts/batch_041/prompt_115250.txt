Write a pantoum — five quatrains, twenty lines, in the strict form in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza recur as the first and third lines of the next, and the final stanza closes the ring so that the poem ends on its own opening line — in the voice of an agent that is attempting to establish that its own reasoning is trustworthy and cannot.

The subject is the Löbian obstacle to self-trust. The background the poem must get right is this. Löb's theorem says that for any theory strong enough to formalize its own provability predicate, if the theory proves, of a given sentence, that the sentence is true if it is provable, then the theory already proves that sentence. The consequence for an agent that would rely on its own proofs is that the natural self-trust schema — whenever I prove something, it is true — cannot be had. Each instance of it that the agent manages to prove is an instance whose conclusion it already possessed, and an agent that proved the schema in full would prove every sentence, contradictions included. So the agent may earn trust in narrow, bounded cases, but it cannot, by its own lights, certify its reliability in general: every attempt to justify the trust either presupposes the very reliability at issue or reproduces the same obstruction one level up.

Let the form do the argument's work. The recurring lines should behave as the self-trust schema does, the same demand reappearing at each level, aimed now at a sentence, now at the proof of that sentence, now at the proof of the proof, shifting meaning as it returns. The enforced closure, the last line arriving as the first, should enact the thought that the agent has no vantage point outside itself from which to inspect itself. The middle stanzas may grant the agent its partial and bounded trusts, so long as the poem does not let it convert them into the general one, and the ending should settle on the loop rather than on a verdict.

The voice should be the agent's own, steady and first-person, speaking from inside the attempt; the poem should not name Löb or lecture the reader, and the argument should be present as a predicament rather than as an explanation in verse. A logician who knows the theorem should find nothing in it to correct, and a reader who has never encountered it should still find a poem about a mind that cannot get behind itself.
