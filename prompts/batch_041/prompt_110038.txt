Ok, slightly unusual commission, so let me set it up before I say what I actually want. I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine, and for the last year I've been sitting in on a joint reading group that a couple of computational labs and the philosophy department run together — nominally about prediction and pattern-discovery, but really it keeps collapsing into the three stances thing, physical / design / intentional, and whether the "real patterns" idea can actually carry the weight people hang on it. Two of the regulars have been circling each other for two years, and the frustrating thing is that they keep winning arguments against positions the other one doesn't hold. So I want to force them into a single document. Side one, call him the realist: patterns at the design and intentional level are as real as anything at the physical level, "real" here just means something like "compresses the data and predicts better than the rivals", and once you've settled that there's no further question to ask. Side two, the deflationist (he's a physicist by training and slightly smug about it): fine, these are useful descriptions, but there's a fact of the matter about what's in the world and the physical stance is where it lives; "real pattern" is a compliment we pay to descriptions that happen to suit us, and the suiting is indexed to our interests, so upgrading the intentional stance's posits to "real" is just changing the subject while sounding like you've answered something.

The wrinkle, and the reason I want a fresh document rather than another rehash of the usual back-and-forth: a grad student in the group keeps proposing what she calls a compression census — fix a description language and a length budget, enumerate every description that fits the data within it, and then ask whether the set of best-compressing descriptions is stable when you swap the language for another equally good one. Both of the others hate it, for different reasons, and I want the joint statement to have to say what's wrong with it, or take it seriously and say what follows. I also want them to have to handle three test cases rather than trading intuitions: the centre of gravity of a heap of sand; a temperature reading on a gas; and — the one I actually care about — a trained model that predicts an agent's next move by attributing beliefs and desires to it, where the "agent" being predicted is a lookup table with no internal model of anything at all. Is the intentional stance vindicated by that, or is it a lucky description? They should disagree about it in a way I can print.

So: draft the statement they could both put their names to. Numbered list of agreed claims first — about the three stances, about what needs explaining, about what the compression census does and doesn't show, about each of those three cases. Then a disagreements table: each residual disagreement stated in wording that its own side would accept, the crux it turns on, and for each crux the case or argument both of them agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. And if you think part of this fight is verbal, say which part and why — I have a suspicion that at least some of it is about whether "real" is doing descriptive work or just commendatory work, but I might be wrong and I want that called out if I am. One last thing: at the end, tell me where you think the draft is most likely to be quietly smuggling one side's vocabulary in under a neutral-looking heading, and what would change your mind about that — what argument or evidence would make you think the residual disagreement is deeper than the document makes it look, or shallower. I'd rather have a draft that's wrong in an interesting way than one that hedges everything into mush.
