I'm drafting a short essay on Whig history and I want your read on it before I keep going. Rough version, ignore the prose.

The argument: the critique of Whig history has turned into a Whig history of its own. The familiar version of the story is that earlier historians wrote the past as a march toward themselves, flattering the winners and treating the losers as obstacles on the way; then a few critics pointed out that the outcomes weren't fated and the actors had no idea how things would end; and now we, having learned this, don't do it anymore. But look at the shape: a benighted past, a moment of enlightenment, a present that has finally got it right. That's the same shape it claims to diagnose. The critique keeps reproducing the thing it names.

I also think the diagnosis gets over-applied becuase two different sins get filed under the same label. One is explaining an outcome by the fact that it happened — treating the result as if it were the reason. The other is choosing what to study because it bears on questions we care about now. The second is unavoidable and mostly fine; you have to pick a topic somehow, and there's no view from nowhere. Only the first is a real error. Collapse them and you end up suspicious of any history that arrives at the present, which is all history, since the present is where we're standing.

So why is the Whig shape so sticky? My guess: monotone narratives feel like explanations. A story with no wasted motion, where every dead end was secretly a step, has the tidiness of a proof. Contingency just reads as noise. So we keep reaching for the shape even while we're writing about how bad the shape is.

Counterargument I should probably deal with: this is just the cheap observation that critiques mirror what they critique. Fair. But I think the knowing-we-know-better register is doing real rhetorical work in a lot of current writing, and it's load-bearing, not incidental.

I want (a) whether I've got the idea right, i.e. am I misdescribing what the Whig critique is actually aimed at, (b) whether the essay is interesting or just a familiar meta-move in better prose, and (c) what to cut. No reading lists, just your take.
