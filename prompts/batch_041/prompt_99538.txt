I am a graduate instructor in philosophy, and next month I have to run a seventy-five-minute session on Quine's indeterminacy of translation for a group of second-year students who have had some Frege and Russell but have never read Quine. I have been arguing about this with a friend who insists the whole thing is just the obvious point that translation is hard, and I want the session to make the difference vivid rather than to leave the students with a slogan. Do not explain the thesis to me; plan how I should explain it. I need the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one, the one carrying example that should do the work, the misconceptions the students will predictably bring or form and exactly where in the sequence I should head each off, one question or task I can use at the end to see whether they have understood the thesis rather than merely memorized its name, and a short list of things I should openly refuse to cover at this level with a sentence on why each is being deferred. You may bring in stimulus meaning, analytical hypotheses, and proxy functions if each earns its place in the sequence, but do not assume the students already know them. I do not want a reading list, citations, or suggestions for further reading; the plan should stand on its own.

For the carrying example, I do not want to use the usual rabbit and the usual gavagai term, because too many of them will have encountered it in passing and will think they already know the shape of the argument. I want to build the session around a made-up field-linguist case, and I would like you to design it before you plan the sequence. Imagine a linguist working with a small community whose language has a word, tala, used in all and only the situations where an English speaker might say rabbit, rabbit-stage, or undetached rabbit-part, and a particle, shek, used in all and only the situations where an English speaker might say because, and then, or after. The community's origin stories use shek in ways that make causal and merely temporal readings equally compatible with everything the linguist can observe. I want the example to be rich enough that two rival translation manuals can both fit the whole corpus of observable behavior but disagree systematically about reference and about logical form, and simple enough that students can hold both manuals in their heads at once. Tell me what the example must have and what it must lack for that to work, then use it as the spine of the teaching plan. The misconceptions I expect include that indeterminacy is just underdetermination by evidence; that Quine is a behaviorist who denies inner meaning; that the point is about translation difficulty or about cultures being incommensurable; that there is a fact of the matter but we cannot know it; and that the thesis entails that all translations are equally good. Tell me which of these are real misunderstandings and which are defensible readings, where each should be confronted in the sequence, and what the students should be able to say by the end that they cannot say at the start. Finally, say how confident you are in the pedagogical claims you make, and single out the one point in the plan you are least sure about.
