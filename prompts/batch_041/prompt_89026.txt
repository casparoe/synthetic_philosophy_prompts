I'm a third-year CS student taking a seminar on alignment, and for my placement I'm helping a small lab decide how to supervise the models we're fine-tuning. We keep having the same fight: for some tasks we pay annotators to check every reasoning step (process supervision), for others we just grade the final answer (outcome supervision), and right now it's basically whoever yells loudest in the standup. I need a decision procedure my team can actually run on a new task before we start collecting data. The team is 4 people, annotator budget is small, and I have to get sign-off from the PI, who knows the theory but not the day-to-day. The decisions come up maybe once every two weeks and they definately lock in a labeling scheme for months, so getting it wrong is expensive.

Write me the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with process, where it stops with outcome, and where it has to hand the decision to a human because it's really a values call (e.g., how much we care about a model that gets the right answer for bad reasons). I want it usable by someone who hasn't read the theory. Keep whatever distinctions from the process-vs-outcome literature actually matter and drop the rest. No citations or reading list, just the procedure.

One thing I've been calling the legibility tax: the extra cost (annotation time, latency, engineering) of making the model's reasoning checkable step by step. Also the difference between an execution error (wrong step, final answer happens to be right) and an intent error (right final answer, but the reasoning would generalize badly). The procedure should say when the legibility tax is worth paying and when it isn't, and how to seperate execution errors from intent errors when you only have outcome labels. It should also flag the cases where process supervision only looks trustworthy because the reasoning trace is unfaithful, and where outcome supervision is being gamed because the checker is a proxy.

Run it on three cases:
1. A model solving multi-step math word problems where the final answer is a number and we can check it automatically.
2. A customer-support agent where success is fuzzy, there's no single correct final message, and the company cares about tone and policy compliance.
3. A coding agent where unit tests are cheap but incomplete and we've already seen it pass tests by hardcoding.

For case 3, the procedure probaly gives an answer I won't like (I want process supervision everywhere because it feels safer), so make sure it actually commits and explain why the answer is what it is. Also say what the procedure can't do and where it will misfire.
