Next term I am running a graduate seminar on consciousness, and instead of opening with a lecture on the access/phenomenal distinction I want to hand out a which-view-do-you-actually-hold quiz, because everyone arrives calling themselves a global-workspace theorist or an illusionist and then, in the cases, turns out to mean something else entirely. Please write that quiz: ten to twelve items, each one a concrete case or a concrete claim rather than a question about which theory the respondent endorses, with two or three answer options apiece. The point is to sort people by what they say about cases, so no item may simply ask whether phenomenal consciousness is distinct from access consciousness, or whether the hard problem is real. End with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto six positions: (i) phenomenal overflow, on which phenomenal consciousness outruns access, so that there is experience never reportable or available for the control of action; (ii) the global-workspace view, on which a state is conscious just in case it is globally broadcast, with no phenomenal remainder; (iii) the higher-order view, on which a state is conscious only when a suitable higher-order representation targets it; (iv) illusionism, on which the phenomenal properties as traditionally conceived do not exist and the dispute is really about functional states misdescribed in phenomenal terms; (v) first-order representationalism, on which phenomenal character just is a certain kind of representational content; and (vi) a panpsychist or Russellian-monist view, on which phenomenal properties are fundamental and intrinsic rather than constituted by any kind of access.

Build the items so that they genuinely pull these positions apart, and please draw on the paradigms the seminar will cover: blindsight and its variants, partial report and the alleged overflow of iconic memory, inattentional and change blindness, the split-brain preparations, and the recent turn to no-report paradigms, in which experience is probed without asking the subject to report anything. A few items should test the same underlying commitment in different clothing, so that the key can tell a considered position from a slogan, and at least one item should be a case on which two of the six positions return the same answer. After the scoring key, add a brief note flagging any pair of positions your quiz cannot separate, and say for each such pair whether that is because the positions coincide on every case you used or because you could not devise a case that separates them; I would rather see the instrument's limits stated than smoothed over.
