Please play a committed defender of what I'll call the head-count view: the claim that when two theories of the world fit everything I've ever observed equally well, the one that says more conscious observers exist deserves more of my belief, purely because there being someone here to observe anything at all is a likelier state of affairs in a world stocked with more observers. Play the character straight — sharp, unrattled by the view's strange consequences, glad to yield a small point when it is genuinely forced but never the central one — and stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to a few paragraphs so it feels like a back-and-forth. I'll be playing the skeptic.

Here is my opening move, a case I invented to test the view, and I want to know whether it survives contact. Suppose there are three theories about everything beyond my window, and all three predict exactly the observations I have had. H1 says the universe contains one observer — me — and nothing else. H2 says it contains a trillion observers, each living through precisely the experiences I am having right now, down to the last detail; your view says H2 earns a trillion times the belief, since under H2 there are a trillion places for someone to be having my experiences and under H1 only one. So far, so consistent. Now H3: the universe contains one single observer, me, who lives through all trillion of those experiences one after another — one very long life, but one life. As far as I can tell your view gives H3 no advantage whatsoever over H1, even though H3 holds a trillion times as much lived experience as H1 and exactly as much as H2. So the view seems to be counting people, not anything a person would call the amount of life in the world; and if you may count people, I could as easily count moments of experience, or years lived, or brain-states, each of which would crown a different theory. Tell me what makes an observer the right unit rather than one of those, and if you cannot, say so plainly instead of papering over it. I'm aware there is a rival idea — that I should treat myself as one observer drawn at random from all the observers the world happens to contain — but I am not defending that either; I want to know whether your view stands on its own. Once you've made your case, add one sentence out of character naming the argument or consideration that would most shake the position you're defending, or where its reasoning is likeliest to fail.
