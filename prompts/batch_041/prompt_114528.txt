I'm a TA for a second-year seminar on Hume and the Scottish Enlightenment (22 students, 75 minutes, normal seminar room with movable chairs) and I've been handed one whole session on the money and trade essays to do whatever I want with. The reason I care is a bit petty: my flatmate is doing an econ masters and keeps saying Hume's essays on money are just the quantity theory with nicer prose, nothing you wouldn't get from a first-year textbook. I said the interesting part is the interval, he said the interval is a footnote, and we've been going at it for weeks. So I want the students to feel the interval rather than hear me assert it, and then have to say what they think it shows. The line I keep coming back to is from the essay on interest, where he writes that "In the progress towards these changes, the augmentation may have some influence, by exciting industry; but after the prices are settled, suitably to the new abundance of gold and silver, it has no manner of influence." Before the prices settle, money does something; after, it's a name. And in the essay on money itself he says money "is none of the wheels of trade: It is the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy." So money isn't wealth, but adding money isn't nothing either, and the gap between those two claims is a stretch of time plus a distribution — whoever gets the new money first is buying at the old prices. By the end of the hour I want them to be able to say why those two claims aren't in tension and what the resolution costs you.

So: design me a runnable in-class game that puts them inside the interval. My rough thinking, which you should throw out if it's broken — split the room into two islands that trade with each other through a harbour, give each island its own stock of chips, and make players produce goods by doing an actual bit of dull work (copying out ledger lines, whatever), which they then sell for chips and use to buy other goods, so effort is real even though the goods are fixed. Then mid-game a treasure fleet turns up at one island out of nowhere and the chips there double, but I want the doubling staggered: half the island gets it a round before the other half does. I also want production choices to lean on last round's prices, so a price rise pulls extra effort out of them right before it becomes obvious the extra chips buy nothing. And I want the two-island bit to stage the balance-of-trade story: one island ends up hoarding coin and panicking about the other's "surplus." To get them acting like mercantilists without me telling them to, I was thinking of scoring each island on the coin left in its treasury at the end, and then in the debrief pointing out that the treasury score had nothing to do with how much anyone actually consumed. I'd rather over-specify then under-specify, so give me the setup, a materials list, the round structure with timings, the production rule and what it costs, how prices actually get set (I'm worried haggling will eat the whole hour), the injection rules including exactly who gets what and when, the individual and island scoring, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the specific bit of Hume it's meant to be — the oil-not-wheels point, the interval, the exciting-industry line, and the anti-mercantilist point that one country's gain isn't automatically another's loss.

And be straight with me about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time — someone announcing "this is just inflation" in round one, the players who didn't get a windfall checking out, the market clearing too slowly, whatever else you see — and how I handle each of those in the moment. Second, which parts of Hume's view the game definately can't reproduce and I'll just have to tell them, because I don't want the debrief to overclaim. My guess is that it can't show real output rising (the goods are fixed and the "work" is fake) and it can't reproduce the fact that in Hume's story the new money reaches particular people in a particular order — merchants, landlords, employers — and restructures what gets made, rather than landing on a random half of the room. But tell me if I'm wrong about what the game can and can't do.
