Ok so I've been reading the Ant Fugue chapter of Hofstadter's GEB, with Aunt Hillary the ant colony that supposably has a mind, and I don't want another summary of the debate — I want you to actually pick a side and argue for it.

Here's the setup. Take two healthy ant colonies, A and B, and just grant for the sake of the argument that each one is a mind in its own right. Now build a pheromone causeway between them, a narrow bridge the ants can walk back and forth on, and let it run for a few months. The two nests slowly merge: shared foraging routes, a merged brood pile, and eventually behavior that neither A nor B ever did on its own. Call the resulting thing C. Nothing about A or B has been destroyed — every ant is still there doing its ant thing — but the combined system has a stable, distinctive behavioral profile.

I want you to argue that C is one mind: a single subject, one thing it's like to be C, and not two minds sharing a body, and not just a convenient fiction we tell because tracking individual ants is too much work.

Dont get off easy by gesturing at emergence. Deal with these three: the counting problem (why one and not two, when a split-brain patient arguably has two?), the turnover problem (ants die and get replaced constantly, so what exactly is the thing that persists and stays the same mind?), and the reversibility problem (cut the bridge tomorrow and A and B go back to their old selves, which sure makes it sound like the mind was there twice over all along). Take a firm position, define "mind" if you need to, then live with what that definition costs you. Skip the citations, I don't care who said what.
