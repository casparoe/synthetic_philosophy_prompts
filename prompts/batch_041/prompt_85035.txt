I am a retired experimental physicist who spent forty years treating mathematics as a toolbox — differential geometry on a Monday, Hilbert spaces by Friday — and never once worried whether the tools were sound, since they kept predicting what the instruments read. I have lately been reading philosophy of mathematics, and I have discovered, to my considerable discomfort, that the ground I stood on all those years may be less settled than I assumed. So I would like your actual credence, as a number, that ZFC — or whatever system you regard as the working foundation of ordinary mathematics, together with the standard extensions mathematicians actually use, such as large-cardinal axioms — is consistent, in the sense of never yielding a contradiction, and not merely consistent relative to some stronger system that is itself unproved. Please do not tell me that this is unprovable and leave it there; I know that ZFC cannot prove its own consistency, and that is precisely why I am asking for a credence rather than a proof. What I want is the number and the derivation behind it.

Decompose the question into the sub-questions the number actually rests on, and give a rough probability and a one-line justification for each. I would expect the terms to include at least: the weight you assign to the iterative conception of sets as a genuine justification rather than a picture that merely feels compelling; the evidential value of the historical record — several generations of mathematicians failing to find a contradiction — and whether that record is real evidence or largely survivorship; whether the large-cardinal hierarchy and the way it has so far hung together makes inconsistency more or less likely than you would have guessed from ZFC alone; and whether the Gödel and Cohen independence results should move the number at all. Then give me the combination, and finally tell me which of these terms you trust least and what single consideration would move the number most.

One more thing, and it is the part that actually keeps me up at night. If the system were inconsistent, I suspect we might never know it — the shortest proof of a contradiction could be far longer than anything a human or a machine will ever write down — whereas if it is consistent we will of course also never know it for certain. The absence of a contradiction is therefore weak evidence, and I want to know whether that asymmetry belongs inside the credence itself or as a separate term about our own epistemic position. Please treat it as its own consideration and say where it enters the decomposition.
