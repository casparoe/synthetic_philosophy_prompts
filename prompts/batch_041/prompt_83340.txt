I work in the rulemaking division of a state agency, and every few years we rewrite the definitions in our regulations that turn on continuum predicates: 'chronic', 'significant', 'substantial', 'impaired', 'material', 'safe', 'adequate'. There are about a dozen per cycle, each currently drawn by whichever analyst owns the file, with a rationale that amounts to 'this is where the science seems to point' or 'this is where the regulated parties said they could live with it'. We are challenged on both flanks: administrative and judicial review for drawing arbitrary lines, and commenters for refusing to draw them at all. I have roughly a day per definition, a law degree, no training in philosophy, and a politically mixed appointed commission that signs off and will not read more than two pages.

I want a procedure my division can actually run, repeatedly, on any continuum predicate, that keeps faith with what the philosophy of vagueness genuinely establishes rather than with folk wisdom about slippery slopes. Assume I cannot look anything up: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no reliance on named doctrines I would have to go read. Whatever distinctions you use, state them in the procedure itself.

Concretely, I want:

A triage step first, in order, that separates a genuinely vague predicate from one that is merely under-specified, ambiguous between two readings, or multidimensional in a way that hides a value tradeoff. Say what evidence in the file settles each verdict and what to do when the verdict is 'not vagueness'.

Then a tolerance audit: a test that takes a proposed line and asks whether the rationale offered for it survives being applied to cases one imperceptible step away, so that I can see on paper whether the rule's own justification would walk the line anywhere it likes. Specify how to construct the chain, how many steps, and what counts as failing.

Then a sharpening warrant: a written record, for the line I choose, of which clear cases it must not flip and which rival lines it rules out, plus a rule for how much of that record goes in the preamble so the choice reads as reasoned rather than arbitrary.

Then a choice among the available shapes of rule — a single bright line; a bright line with a rebuttable presumption or gray band; a graded scale — with criteria for picking, and an explicit warning if graded semantics tempts us into graded penalties, since our remedies are binary.

Then a penumbra protocol covering the hard cases: a case near but not in the penumbra, a case where it is unclear whether the case is in the penumbra at all, and a case where two of our definitions interact so that each looks sharp only because the other absorbs the vagueness.

Finally, the honesty requirements: mark every step that is a judgment call dressed as procedure; say where the procedure stops with a no; say where it hands the decision to the commission because the question is what we are willing to tolerate rather than where the boundary lies; and address whether it matters for how I write the rule whether the boundary is sharp but unknowable, or genuinely indeterminate, or moves with context. If that distinction changes nothing about the drafting, say so and say why.

Run it on three definitions: 'chronic' exposure, where the continuum runs along two axes, level and duration; 'significant' impairment measured on a single instrument scale; and 'safe' level of a contaminant, where I suspect the real dispute is about risk tolerance. At least one of the three should come out with a verdict I will not like, and you should not soften it. Close with what the procedure cannot do.
