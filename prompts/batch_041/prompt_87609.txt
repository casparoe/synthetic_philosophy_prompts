Ok so I'm a fourth-year philosophy student, my dissertation is on social ontology, and I've talked myself into a view I'm only about 70% sure I beleive. Here it is: social kinds aren't held together by shared belief or collective acceptance at all. What constitutes a social kind is deontic. To be a dollar bill, a widow, a woman, a white person, a citizen, is just for there to be a standing — spread across the relevant people — to hold you to treating someone as such, and to hold one another to the entitlements and obligations that come along with it. Beliefs about the kind are downstream of that. Money isn't money because we all think it is; it's money because you can be refused, reported, shamed or sanctioned for not taking it, and because you can demand things in virtue of holding it.

I can already hear a couple of the obvious objections, but I want the full map, not the easy ones. What would actually count against this? I mean cases where the kind obviously exists but there's no accountability structure at all; cases where there's a robust accountability structure but no kind; cases where the deontic facts and the kind's extension come apart. And arguements rather than cases — does the view have the explanatory order backwards (belief first, accountability second)? Does it over-generate, so that any enforced convention counts as a social kind? What grounds the deontic facts — if the answer is more social facts, am I in a regress or a circle? Does it draw the natural/social line in the wrong place? And how does it do on the three cases I actually care about — race, gender, money — where my suspicion is that it fits suspiciously well, which might itself be a problem.

Two things I want stated honestly: which of these a defender of the view (me, on a good day) would simply concede and which they'd have to fight; and whether the view is so elastic that nothing could count against it — and if so, whether that's fatal or just the price of a view this abstract.

Sort by decisiveness, worst first.
