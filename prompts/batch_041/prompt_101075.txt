I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on decision theory and rational choice — about eighteen students, mostly philosophy and economics majors, comfortable with probability but not with measure theory. Every year I put the Allais choices on a slide, they dutifully report the usual pattern, and nothing lands: it stays a fact about other people rather than a felt pull. I want an activity that gets them to feel the force of the tension between the certainty effect and the independence (sure-thing) axiom from the inside — to make the choices themselves, live with them, and then discover, through the structure of what happens rather than through anything I say, that their own preferences can't be held together. Crucially, I don't want it to be a rehash of the textbook pair I already show them; I'd like the mechanic to carry them somewhere the standard presentation doesn't go. Two things I'd especially like built in: first, a sequential or commitment element, so that the same person can be led to choose differently depending on whether they fix their choice in advance or only after a first stage has resolved, and can feel that the difference is not just noise; and second, a trading or brokering role, so that someone in the room is actively trying to buy or swap the positions the others hold, and the discomfort of the inconsistency is felt as a price rather than as a logical remark.

Please give me the full runnable design: the cover story, the exact lottery pairs or prospects (with numbers, and the small real stakes — tokens, candy, a modest prize pool, no cash), how the choices are elicited so that people commit before they see what others did, the roles and rules for the sequential stage and for the broker, a timeline that fits the 90 minutes, and materials I can photocopy. The idea should surface in the mechanics: by the time I name Allais, the independence axiom, or the sure-thing principle, they should already have run into the problem themselves. Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the formal apparatus — the common-consequence versus common-ratio structure, the role the axiom plays in the expected-utility representation, and what exactly has to give if one keeps the preferences (and what one is giving up by keeping the axiom instead). Tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time — where the stakes will be too small to bite, where a clever student will try to game the broker, where the group will split and the split will muddy the point — and how to handle each. Finally, be candid about which feature of the paradox the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
