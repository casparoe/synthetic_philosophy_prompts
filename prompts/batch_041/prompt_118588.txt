I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or game theory beyond what I've absorbed from popular writing, and I have an analogy my team leans on that I'd like you to audit rather than endorse.

The situation I actually care about is a shared, congestible resource: many independent senders on a common link, each deciding how aggressively to transmit. Politeness costs me throughput; aggression gains me throughput unless enough others are aggressive too, in which case everyone's performance degrades. My team has read the standard treatment of the iterated prisoner's dilemma, and we describe our situation as follows. One round is one exchange over the link. Cooperating is backing off politely; defecting is over-sending. The reason future behavior matters is that we keep encountering the same peers. Punishment is the throughput collapse that follows a period of over-sending. The payoff matrix is whatever a sender gains in bandwidth versus what it loses when the link degrades. We use this to argue that we do not need to design enforcement, quotas, or reputation infrastructure into the system, because repeated interaction will discipline free riders on its own, and that building governance is wasted effort.

I'd like an explicit audit of how far this holds. Give me the correspondence table as you see it, what plays the role of the players, the moves, the payoffs, the memory, the punishment, and the horizon, and then tell me which of those correspondences are actually load-bearing for the conclusion we draw and which are decorative. Where does the mapping break? A few candidates occur to me, though I don't know how much they matter. In the version we're borrowing from, players are matched at random in a large well-mixed population, the payoff matrix is fixed, symmetric, and known to everyone, moves are simultaneous, and nobody makes mistakes; our world violates most of that. Identity is cheap to discard here, so punishment may not stick to anyone. A participant can unilaterally rewrite its own algorithm, which sounds to me less like making a move than like changing the payoff matrix mid-game. The horizon isn't merely long, it's undefined. I'm also not sure our situation is even a two-person two-move game; it looks more like a many-player game with a threshold, where the damage only begins once enough people are aggressive. And I gather that once a repeated game is open-ended, cooperation is not the only thing that can be sustained, which makes me worry the theory licenses our conclusion only in the vacuous sense that almost anything is sustainable. Separately, I've read that people cooperate in one-shot anonymous versions of this game far more than the model predicts, which suggests the model's account of the players may be the weak link rather than the setup.

So: does the conclusion, no enforcement needed, repeated play will do the work, survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? If it fails, is there a better analogy for what we're actually in, or is this a case where no fixed-matrix game is the right picture at all? Be concrete about which discrepancies would matter to a designer deciding whether to spend a quarter building enforcement, and which are differences that make no practical difference.
