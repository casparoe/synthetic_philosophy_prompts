I'm a retired chemical oceanographer, 68, spent my career on carbonate chemistry, and for the last five years I've been reading philosophy the way I used to read journals. I've ended up with a view I'd like you to try to break. I think the question why is there something rather than nothing is not a deep question but a malformed one, because nothing is never a state of affairs that could have obtained: every attempt to describe an empty world smuggles something in — a spacetime, laws, a standpoint, a contrast with the actual world. I have a littel test I use, call it the subtraction test: take any proposed empty world and subtract everything it says exists; whatever residue is left over (a framework, a rule, the fact that there are no facts) shows it wasn't really nothing. So the question dissolves, and the only real job left is to explain why it feels unavoidable. What I want from you is a map of what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Not the usual brute-fact stuff. I mean the arguments, cases, intuitions, or formal results that should actually move me; for each, say what would have to be true for my view to survive it. And give me an honest read on whether my subtraction test begs the question by defining nothing so tightly that no empty world could ever pass, plus what my view is committed to that I might not have noticed — e.g. does it force the universe to exist necessarily, or collapse into saying existence isn't a predicate, or something worse? If the view turns out to be well insulated, say so and say whether that's a bug or a feature. No citations, I dont want a reading list, just the map.
