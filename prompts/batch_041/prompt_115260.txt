I'm a third-year undergraduate taking a philosophy of biology module, and I've been set a long essay that has to do something more constructive than adjudicate. For six weeks our seminar has been divided over the two dominant accounts of biological function: the etiological (selected-effects) account, on which a trait's function is fixed by the history of selection that established it, and the causal-role (systemic) account, on which a trait's function is whatever contribution it makes to the capacities of the system containing it. The two camps have been talking past each other in a way I now find tedious. The etiologists complain that the systemic account cannot distinguish a heart's pumping blood from the noise it makes, and cannot explain why a malformed heart is malfunctioning rather than merely differently constituted; the systemic camp replies that the etiological account makes function ascription impossible for any trait without a selection history and smuggles a normative notion of proper function out of purely descriptive facts. My supervisor's suggestion was that I stop taking sides and instead produce the document the two sides would have to sign if they were locked in a room together. So please draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed etiologist and a committed causal-role theorist — a text both could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made as exact as possible.

I need the following. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what phenomenon both accept needs explaining, which verdicts on particular cases both accept (a healthy heart, a by-product that contributes nothing to fitness, a trait currently aiding survival but never selected for that role, and a trait whose current contribution differs from the one it was selected for); what each account concedes the other explains well; and what any adequate account must deliver. Second, the disagreements, each given in two forms — a neutral third-person formulation, and then the wording each side would use for its own position, such that neither could reasonably object to how their view has been stated. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. Please work these cases in as material for the cruxes, since they are the ones our seminar could not resolve: a human gene expressed in a yeast cell that rescues a mutant unable to synthesise a particular metabolite, where the question is whether the human gene has the function of making that metabolite in the yeast and whether it had that function before anyone put it there; a pig heart transplanted into a human recipient, where the type has a selection history of circulating blood but the token organ has no history in that kind of system; a gene that has arisen de novo and now contributes to fitness; and a trait fixed by drift that presently aids survival. For each crux, state a case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about how it comes out, written down in advance. I would also like you to say whether any part of this dispute is merely verbal — in particular whether the two camps are answering different questions rather than giving rival answers to one — and, if so, which part and why; and what each side would count as evidence or argument against its own account, together with whether there is anything both would agree would actually move the debate, or whether the disagreement is in principle unresolvable given what each takes the explanandum to be. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table with a column for the neutral formulation and a column for each side's own wording, then a crux-by-crux section with the shared case and the two recorded predictions. No bibliography or references are needed — I need the document itself, not a reading list — and please write it in British English.
