my old calculus teacher had a line he'd wheel out whenever Zeno came up: the paradoxes are just infinite series — a half plus a quarter plus an eighth and so on — and once you know the series adds up to one, the whole thing evaporates. the runner does infinitely many things, sure, but the times shrink, so it's fine, no paradox, it was a puzzle about sums and the sums got solved in the 1600s. i've been repeating that for years, mostly to feel smart. i'm now reading around the topic properly and i don't think the analogy does the work he thought it did.

can you audit it? i want the correspondence laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the runner, what plays the role of the terms, what plays the role of the limit, where the finish line sits — and then the places the mapping breaks, with some sense of which breaks matter for the use he was putting it to and which are just nitpicks. things i'm already suspicious of: his partition has no first step, so the series picture seems to presuppose exactly what was supposed to be in question; and convergence tells me something about a total, not about whether infinitely many acts ever get completed, which feels like a different question wearing the same clothes. also — does this analogy even reach the arrow and the moving rows, or is it quietly only a story about the dichotomy and Achilles, dressed up as a story about Zeno?

then the payoff. if the analogy only covers half the paradoxes and only answers a question nobody was forced to ask, does his conclusion survive — solved, move along — or was the analogy carrying that conclusion the whole time? and if there's a better picture to think with, say what it is, what it buys, and what it quietly assumes. no reading list please, i want the audit not the bibliography.
