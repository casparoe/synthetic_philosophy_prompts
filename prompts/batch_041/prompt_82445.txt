I've been going in circles on the transformative-experience thing and I want someone to just tell me what kind of fight it actually is. You know the setup: some choices -- having a kid, going to war, emigrating somewhere you don't speak the language, the whole vampire-offers-you-a-bite thought experiment -- are supposed to be special because you can't know what they'll be like until you're in them, and you can't know what you'll want afterwards either, so you can't just run expected value on them. There's a camp that thinks its a real, deep problem for decision theory, and a camp that thinks it's ordinary decision-making under uncertainty dressed up -- you can still put probabilities on outcomes, you can still value the discovery itself, and so on. They've been trading the same four or five moves for years and nobodys budging. I don't want another round of the argument, I want a diagnosis of why it's stuck.

My hunch, take it as a hunch: I think 'transformative experience' is one label pasted over at least three different things, and each side is quietly working with a different one. First, you can't predict what the experience will be like (quality). Second, you can't predict what you'll care about afterwards, because the thing rewrites your preferences (values). Third, even once it's over you can't compare the before-you and the after-you, because there's no common scale to weigh them on (comparability). My guess is the first is just ignorance and decision theory swallows it whole, the second is the actually interesting one, and the third is really a fight about personal identity or about whether values can be compared at all -- a different subject wearing the same name. So: is the stalemate mostly people answering different questions under one heading, and if so which sub-question is carrying the weight?

And is any of it verbal? If one side defines a rational choice as the one that maximizes expected utility given your current preferences, then obviously the transformative case comes out irrational -- that looks like a definition doing the work rather than a finding, and the fight might just be about whether 'rational' ought to mean that. Or is there a hidden formal question in there -- something about what any decision rule can do when the utilities themselves are unknown, which if someone actually proved it would settle a chunk of this?

Last thing: what would count as progress here. Would a clean taxonomy of the three things be progress or just relabeling? Would a theorem help, or is the whole dispute downstream of a disagreement about what decision theory is even for -- a device for ranking options you already want, versus a device that tells you what to want and do? And if you think nothing is going to move this, just say that and explain why.
