Okay, so I'm running a session in a first-year seminar where we pair Le Guin's 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas' with the repugnant conclusion, and I keep feeling that the pairing is a bit of a cheat, but I can't articulate why. I want you to read the story through population axiology properly -- not just 'utilitarianism says one for many, discuss' -- and tell me what actually holds up.

Here's my half-formed take, argue with it. The standard move is to call Omelas a dramatization of the repugnant conclusion. I think that's wrong. The repugnant conclusion is generated by adding lives -- a huge number of people whose lives are barely worth living beating a smaller number of very good lives -- and the weirdness comes precisely from the fact that nobody is made worse off by the addition. Omelas isn't that. The child is an existing person, not a possible addition, and the child's misery is what buys the city's happiness: it's a causal trade, closer to scapegoating than to mere addition. If I'm right, then the story is a seperateness-of-persons case wearing population-axiology clothes, and my students are learning the wrong lesson.

But I'm not confidant, because other bits of the story do look like real axiological work. The child is kept at arm's length -- we get almost nothing about its inner life, and the narrator keeps hedging and refusing to pin the city down -- so we can't tell whether the child's life is barely above zero or well below it, and on some views the whole verdict turns on which side of that line a life falls. Then there's the fact that everyone knows. The citizens aren't ignorant beneficiaries; they know exactly what the deal is, and I've never seen an axiology with a place for knowing. And the ones who walk away don't change the numbers at all -- they just leave -- so I want to know whether walking away is something the framework can even represent, or whether the story is pointing at a kind of refusal that no amount of aggregation captures.

What I want: a reading that takes both sides seriously, the story and the machinery -- mere addition, the sadistic conclusion, critical levels, lexical priority, the separateness of persons, whatever actually fits. Where does the analogy to the repugnant conclusion hold and where does it break, and does the story know the difference or is it just being suggestive? Does the narrator's vagueness track a real indeterminacy in these cases, or is it just style? And end by saying what the story adds to the debate that the arguments alone don't. Don't bother with citations and don't quote at me, I know the text. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning -- no preamble.
