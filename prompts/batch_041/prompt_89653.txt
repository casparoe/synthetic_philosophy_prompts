Right, so for the seminar on moral conflict I want to nail down the strong claim, and I'd rather have you build me a case than talk around one. The claim is that geniune moral dilemmas are possible — genuine in the strong sense: both options are all-things-considered required of the agent, the agent can do each but not both, and so whichever way they go they do something wrong. No "wrong but excused," no "tragic but they did the right thing," no "it's really just a hard call."

Build the case to these specs:

- Realistic. A person with an ordinary life and ordinary attachments. No trolleys, no ticking bombs, no Sophie's choice, no Agamemnon, no Abraham — I've heard those a hundred times and they drag too much baggage behind them.
- No self-entrapment. The agent must not have got into it through some earlier culpable or avoidable act. If the only way to get the conflict going is "well, they shouldn't have made two promises," that isn't the case I want.
- Full information. The agent knows every non-moral fact that bears on the situation. This isn't about not knowing which way to turn; it's about knowing exactly what's what and still being stuck. So no merely epistemic dilemmas.
- Both demands have to come out of one and the same source — the same promise, the same role, the same realtionship, whichever you pick. That's to block the cheap fix of ranking the sources against each other ("loyalty beats honesty"); if both demands flow from the same source, any weight you give that source lands equally on both horns.
- Neither horn may be merely permissible. If the most you can say is "either is fine, they just have to pick," you've built a tie, not a dilemma.

Give me the scenario in a paragraph or so, then do the work: for each horn, say exactly what makes it required rather than merely advisable; walk back through the specs and show me where the case satisfies each one; and tell me which single feature of the case is doing the heavy lifting, i.e. what happens to the whole thing if you lift that feature out.

Then put on the other hat. Someone who thinks genuine dilemmas are impossible will say those specs can't all be met at once — if both acts are required then so is doing both, and if you can't do both then you can't be required to do both, so something on my list has to give. Say how that person attacks the case, which spec they'd have to deny to do it, and whether the price is worth paying. And if you decide no case can meet all five specs, don't just tell me that — give me the impossibility argument and say which spec you'd relax and what that costs you.
