I'm in a graduate seminar on the neuroscience of consciousness, and we spent the first week on the Libet experiments and the last two on the higher-order family of theories — higher-order thought, inner-sense or higher-order perception accounts, and the more recent state-space or Bayesian versions — and nobody in the room has connected the two, which I take to be a real gap rather than an accident. I want you to make that connection as a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions. Here is the episode as I have it, so you don't have to reconstruct it: subjects in the 1980s experiments made a spontaneous movement at a time of their choosing while watching a fast-moving clock, scalp recording showed a readiness potential building for several hundred milliseconds before the muscle acted, and subjects dated their conscious urge or intention to move to roughly two hundred milliseconds before the act, with a further hundred-odd milliseconds in which some could still cancel. Libet read this as showing that the initiation is unconscious while a conscious veto preserves something like free will; a later reanalysis argued that the averaged readiness potential need not be a decision signal at all, since a noise-driven threshold-crossing process will produce a similar buildup once trials are averaged; and some later work reported that interfering with medial frontal motor areas shifted subjects' reported time of the urge while leaving the movement itself unchanged. Please don't hand me the standard free-will-versus-determinism talking points — I've had those. A classmate insists that the higher-order theories are just a redescription of these data and nothing more, and I want to know whether that charge is fair.

So: in the higher-order framework, what exactly is the first-order state and what is the higher-order representation in this paradigm? Is the felt urge itself the first-order state that a higher-order representation renders conscious, or is the urge already the higher-order representation of an underlying motor preparation — and does the answer differ across the thought, inner-sense, and state-space versions? Which version gives the most natural reading of the timing, and does any of them make a prediction about it that the others don't? Higher-order theories notoriously tolerate misrepresentation, since a higher-order representation can be wrong about the state it represents, so tell me precisely what the reported urge is allowed to get wrong, and whether the shifted-intention results are a vindication of that tolerance or a reductio of it.

Then reverse the direction. Does the Libet case give the higher-order family any support it would not have had otherwise, or does it expose a gap the literature has papered over? Be specific about what these theories are simply silent on — causal efficacy, agency, the metaphysics of the will — and about whether the classic regress, in which the veto is just one more state that would need its own higher-order representation, has a non-question-begging answer inside the framework. Finally, say what happens to your whole analysis if the reanalysis of the readiness potential is right. Keep the mapping disciplined: name who or what occupies each role, resist letting the theory explain what it has no business explaining, and mark the places where the honest verdict is that the theory says nothing.
