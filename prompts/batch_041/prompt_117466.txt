I am presenting at a two-day workshop on population ethics next month, and I would like a Q&A preparation sheet. My paper defends a deflationary thesis: where worlds contain infinitely many lives, there is no fact of the matter about which is better. This is not vagueness, and not a gap that better mathematics would close; the betterness relation itself fails to be determinate in the infinite case. The argument runs through two principles, which I state and defend separately and then show to be in conflict. Replication Invariance: if one world is obtained from another by a bijection of lives onto lives that preserves the value of each life, the two worlds are exactly as good (duplicating every life is the intuitive case). Addition Intuition: adding a life of positive value to a world makes that world better. Because adding a single life to an infinite world yields a world that is also a bijective image of the original, the two principles cannot both hold. I argue that each is independently compelling, that each of the available ways of giving one up is costly, and that there is no non-arbitrary way to rank those costs, so no extension of our betterness ordering to infinite worlds is privileged.

The audience is a specific and awkward mix: three totalist population ethicists who will want to save totalism at almost any price; one person-affecting/contractualist who regards the whole problem as a reductio of impersonal value; one decision theorist who works on infinite lotteries and non-expected-utility choice; and one set theorist who will notice that Replication Invariance leans on treating equinumerosity as value-preserving. Please prepare me: the objections I will actually get, attributed to the kind of audience member who will raise each and phrased the way that person would phrase it, ordered by how much damage they do; for each, a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest; what I should concede at the outset rather than be forced to concede; and the one question I am most hoping nobody asks.
