A friend in my reading group laid out this argument and I cant find the exit. He says it's Leibniz's position cleaned up, and that it proves way too much, so something in it has to be wrong.

(1) God is perfectly wise and perfectly good, so if he creates at all, he creates the best of all possible worlds.
(2) By the principle of sufficient reason, nothing in the world is idle: everything that happens belongs to the whole that made this world the best one.
(3) So any world that differs from ours in any respect is worse than ours.
(4) Take any evil -- say a child's illness. Consider the world just like ours except she never falls ill, with everything downstream adjusting however it must. By (3), that world is worse.
(5) So the illness is earning its keep: it's a necessary part of what makes this world the best, and removing it costs more than it saves.
(6) So we can never have a reason to prevent a particular illness -- doing so makes things worse overall.
(7) Same move for injustice: the whole is best, so nobody is ever really wronged, and wanting redress is a confusion about the structure of reality.
(8) But (6) and (7) are absurd, and Leibniz clearly thought we should relieve suffering.

Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? My friend says the escape is a distinction Leibniz draws between what God wills for a creature considered on its own and what he wills once the whole series is on the table -- so the best world can be a bad deal for you in particular and that's supposed to be fine. Another friend says the problem is further upstream: there's no best world to begin with, since you can always add one more happy creature and get something better. I'd like to keep (1) and (2) if I can.
