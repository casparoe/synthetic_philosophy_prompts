I write historical fiction, and the argument I keep having — with my editor, with readers at events, with myself at the end of a draft — is the one about how we are to judge the dead. The pattern never varies. Someone says that Jefferson, or Churchill, or Columbus, was a man of his time, and that it is anachronistic to hold him to ours. Someone else replies that plenty of his contemporaries saw the wrong plainly enough, so his time cannot excuse him; that the same defence would have excused every atrocity in history; and that its real function is to spare us discomfort. Then both sides restate themselves and nothing moves. I have heard this argument often enough to doubt that more examples will settle it, so I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two sides are not disagreeing about the facts, or even about whether the conduct was wrong, but about what the phrase his time is doing — whether it names an epistemic limit (what he could have known), a moral climate (what almost everyone around him believed), or a standard of fair blame (what it is reasonable to demand of an agent formed as he was). If the parties are trading one sense for another, that would explain why each can concede the other's points and still not budge. A second possibility I cannot rule out: one side is engaged in causal-historical understanding and the other in present accountability, and the phrase is a hinge between two different enterprises. And as a novelist I spend years inside such a person's head, which makes the blame-relation feel like it belongs to a different activity from the one I am engaged in — so it would not surprise me if the whole thing turned on an intuition about when someone is a fit target of blame at all, an intuition neither side can argue for.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of the kind of disagreement this actually is, argued from what the parties say rather than from a survey of the literature; a verdict on whether my hypothesis is right, wrong, or only part of it; and, given your diagnosis, an honest account of what would count as progress — a distinction, a criterion for when the defence applies, a reframing of the question — or an argument that nothing would, and why. I do not need citations; I want the diagnosis itself.
