I work on compiler backends and have a reasonable grounding in computability theory and type theory: I can read a fixed-point construction, I know why a quine is not the same thing as a metacircular evaluator, and I have a tourist's familiarity with procedural reflection and reflective towers. A friend in a reading group has been pressing Hofstadter's account of the self on me — the 'I' as a self-referential symbol, the strange loop, the tangled hierarchy — and I keep having the same two-part reaction. Every time he explains it, it sounds like something I already have a word for; and every time I supply my word, he tells me I have missed the point. I would like you to settle this by actually doing the translation rather than gesturing at its possibility.

Restate, as faithfully as you can, Hofstadter's position on what the self is and how it arises, in the vocabulary I actually use — fixed points, diagonalization, Kleene's second recursion theorem, self-reproducing programs, self-interpreters and reflection, stratification and groundedness, the distinction between a syntactic construction and a semantic one. Please keep these four things separate rather than letting them blur into one another: (a) self-reference as syntactic self-reproduction, as in a quine; (b) self-reference as semantic aboutness, where a program's value depends on its own code; (c) self-representation, where a system contains a model of itself and can evaluate it; and (d) whatever extra thing Hofstadter means by a loop being strange — a level-crossing, a hierarchy that tangles rather than stays stratified. For each substantive claim he makes, say whether it translates cleanly into my vocabulary, translates only under distortion, or has no counterpart at all.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the interesting part. Three places where I suspect the translation will fail, and where I want your honest verdict on whether the failure indicts my vocabulary or his exposition: first, he insists the loop is not epiphenomenal — that the self-symbol really does things. In my world, a high-level description of a computation has no causal power that the transition relation does not already have, so either his claim is the harmless one that high-level descriptions are indispensable, or it is a claim I literally cannot state. Second, he ties the self to how much of the world one has internalized, so that selves come in sizes and we are partly resident in one another's heads; I have no notion of a self-model that is graded in that way, and I want to know whether that is a gap in my vocabulary or a loose metaphor on his side. Third, a system that evaluates its own truth value is ungrounded and has no well-founded model, which is exactly where paradox lives; so does the strange loop require the system to be non-well-founded or even inconsistent, or does reflection let it remain grounded in a way that defuses the paradox? Finally, tell me plainly whether my instinct is right — that a strange loop is just a fixed point, or a quine, dressed up — or whether there is something in his account that survives the translation and that I, with my vocabulary, am genuinely missing.
