I am a third-year undergraduate taking a course on comparative constitutional politics, and the seminar portfolio this term requires one satirical piece on an institution we have studied. We have spent several weeks on the gap between the legal powers of a head of state and the conventions that actually constrain them, and I would like you to draft the piece for me.

For this task, please take on the role of a constitutional lawyer who knows the conventions of the British, Scandinavian, and Japanese monarchies in close detail and who also writes satire professionally — the kind of writer who can be funny about reserve powers without getting a single convention wrong. Think carefully about the constitutional mechanics before you begin: the comedy has to come out of the real logic of the office, not out of invented absurdity, so that a specialist laughs rather than winces.

The setting I want you to work with is an invented realm, the Kingdom of Ravnmark, which has just codified its constitution for the first time. In the name of transparency, the Palace's Office of the Head of State has published a recruitment pack for the position of Monarch. Write that pack. It should take the form of a civil-service human-resources document — a person specification with essential and desirable criteria, a schedule of duties, a conflicts-of-interest section, terms and conditions of appointment, a short benchmarking section, and a closing section on how to apply — with numbered sections, in a deadpan register, at around 900 words. The target is the practice of constitutional monarchy, not any particular monarch or family.

The relevant features of the invented constitution are these, and you should build the pack around them:
- the monarch's public acts require the countersignature of the responsible minister;
- the reserve powers — dismissing a government that has lost the confidence of the legislature, refusing a dissolution, choosing a prime minister after an inconclusive election — may be exercised only where the ordinary machinery of government has failed, a formula the constitution leaves undefined;
- a Royal Acts Tribunal may declare that an act was taken without proper advice, but cannot undo it;
- the monarch keeps a sealed record of the weekly meetings with the prime minister, opened to historians after thirty years;
- the monarch may not vote, hold membership of a party, or be sued in the realm's courts;
- the throne passes by inheritance, the constitution is silent on what happens if the heir declines, and abdication requires both an act of the legislature and the monarch's own assent.

Each of the following must appear in the pack:
1. Essential criteria that quietly describe someone with no opinions, no ambitions, and no qualifications for any other post, but stated as professional virtues — discretion, evenness of temper, stamina for ceremony.
2. Desirable criteria that would disqualify any working politician: a harmless hobby, no known views on anything contested, the capacity to read aloud with conviction a text written by somebody else.
3. A duties schedule that includes the reserve powers, with the observation that they fall due perhaps once in a century and that no training will be provided.
4. A conflicts-of-interest section covering family, private wealth, religion, and charitable patronage.
5. Terms and conditions: appointment for life, no resignation procedure, and the fact that the only exit requires the officeholder's own signature.
6. A benchmarking section comparing the post with an elected ceremonial presidency, in which the pack's own reasoning reveals why the hereditary arrangement is preferred — namely that the office is useful precisely because its holder has no democratic mandate to spend.
7. A closing section on how to apply, making clear that the post is not open to applications and that the successful candidate was identified at birth.

Keep two horns in view throughout: if the monarch has no real power, it is hard to see why the office should be hereditary; if the office retains real power in the form of the reserve powers, it is hard to see why it should be hereditary either. Let the document indict itself with both without ever noticing that it has done so, and let the reader feel the force of both by the end.

Constraints: name and caricature no living person; invent no real royal family; do not cite real statutes, cases, or scholarly sources, and do not attribute doctrines to named writers — references to Ravnmark's own constitutional articles are welcome, but nothing should depend on authority the reader cannot check. Keep the prose deadpan and never wink at the reader. A reader who knows nothing about constitutional monarchy should still be able to follow the document and see what is being mocked. Before drafting, spend a few lines setting out the constitutional functions and the standard objections you intend to bury in the pack, and then write the pack itself.
