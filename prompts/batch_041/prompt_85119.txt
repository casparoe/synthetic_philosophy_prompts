I have been trying to sort out what I actually think about deferring to my future self, and I want a principle that accounts for my verdicts. The rough principle I was taught — the reflection principle, I believe it is called — is this: if you are certain that at some later time you will be x confident that A, then you should be x confident that A now. It gives the answer I want in some cases and strikes me as plainly false in others. The cases below are the ones that decide the matter for me.

1. *The Ledger.* Tomorrow morning I will sit down with the rainfall records and will then be 95% confident that it rained here on the fourteenth. Right now I have not seen the records. I should be 95% confident now, and this seems to me exactly right.

2. *The Amnesia Draft.* Tonight I will take a pill that erases the past month from my memory. That month contained the only evidence I have ever seen bearing on whether a certain treaty was signed in March or in April, and I know that after the pill I will be at 50/50. I am at 80/20 now, on the strength of that evidence. I should stay at 80/20. Deferring to the person I will be tomorrow would mean discarding evidence I still have today, and I take that to be absurd.

3. *The Guru.* I have agreed to spend tomorrow at a session run by a man whose methods I know to be effective and epistemically worthless: by the end of it I will be 85% confident that the moon landings were staged. I should not be 85% confident of that now, and I should not be 85% confident of it tomorrow either, if I could contrive to keep my head. The difference from the Ledger is that the Ledger's future self is responding to the world, and the Guru's future self is responding only to the Guru.

4. *The Confidant.* Tomorrow I will spend two hours arguing with a colleague I regard as roughly my equal. He will put the case for a hypothesis I now put at 80/20, and I know from experience that I come out of such conversations much closer to my interlocutor than I should — call it 55/45. I think I should move partway now, to about 65/35, and not all the way to 55/45. My future self will have heard arguments I have not heard, which is a genuine gain in information; but I also know that the mechanism by which those arguments will move me is not their force but my tendency to fold in the presence of confidence, which is not a gain in anything.

5. *The Complacency Implant.* A hypnotist will put me under tomorrow and implant in me the conviction that I will pass an examination. I know two things about the implant: that it will take, and that the resulting confidence will make me careless, so that I will in fact fail. I should now be *less* confident of passing than I was before I heard about the hypnotist — certainly not 90%, and in fact below whatever I thought beforehand, since I now have evidence that a cause of failure is on its way.

6. *The Confidence Pill.* Same story with the sign reversed. The hypnotist will make me 90% confident I will pass, and the confidence will make me prepare harder, so that I will in fact pass. Here I think I should be confident, and near 90%. This is the case I keep going back and forth on, because the causal story is the same as in 5 and only the direction differs, and because I dislike the idea that a state I know to be manufactured can be a reason to believe anything.

Taken together, my verdicts seem to track whether my future credence is a *sign* of the thing it is about, and not whether it is mine. The tentative principle I would offer is this: defer to your future self to exactly the degree to which you would defer to a stranger who had arrived at that credence by that route, if you knew the route and knew nothing else about him. On that test, the Ledger's future self is a stranger who has read the records, and I defer almost entirely; the Guru's is a stranger who has been worked on, and I defer not at all; the Confidant's is a stranger who has heard an argument and also has a known tendency to over-persuade, and I defer a little; and in 5 and 6 the stranger's credence is a sign of the outcome only because the credence will help cause the outcome, which I take to be a reason to follow it in 6 and to invert it in 5.

What I want from you is a principle — or a small set of candidates, if one will not do — stated precisely enough to be applied to a case I have not described. In particular I want to know what it says about two cases I cannot settle. First, a meter that reads my brain and prints the credence I would arrive at on some question after a week of solitary deliberation, if I never saw the printout: I cannot shake the feeling that I should simply adopt the printed value, and I am bothered that adopting it makes it self-fulfilling. Second, a drug that will tomorrow make me dogmatic about whatever answer I happen to hold, so that I know I will be made irrational without knowing in which direction. I also want to know whether the principle I end up with can be given the kind of coherence argument that is usually offered for the plain version — the argument that a policy of refusing to defer exposes one to a sure loss — and, if it can, whether that argument does not also prove too much, since the Guru's victim seems exposed to a sure loss in the same sense.

Tell me which of my six verdicts you would overturn, and be definite about it. I would rather have a principle that costs me a verdict I like than a taxonomy of the options. I am not asking for a survey of what others have said and I do not need references; I want your own view, argued. Where you are unsure, say so, but say what you would go with anyway.
