I'm putting together a reading-group session on decision theory and I want a dialogue I can hand out, because the discussion always gets too abstract when we just argue about it ourselves. The two characters are Mara and Dev. Mara works on evidential decision theory and has become convinced that it extends to what she calls evidential cooperation in large worlds. Dev is a game theorist who is also an evidentialist about ordinary Newcomb cases -- he one-boxes, he gets why correlation matters -- but he thinks the large-world extension is a mess. They're in a seminar room after a talk, coffee going cold, and neither is a strawman: Dev knows the evidentialist arguments as well as Mara does, he just thinks the move from one-boxing to cooperation across causally isolated galaxies is doing something illicit with correlation and reference classes. Mara thinks Dev is being parochial about what counts as a decision problem. I don't want citations, reading lists, or name-dropping; just the two of them talking.

Write the dialogue, around 1200 to 1800 words, with speakers labeled. Let them start from a concrete case: two civilizations in separate Hubble volumes, no causal contact ever, each running a decision algorithm that, when it hits this exact choice, either pays a cost to set up a cooperative policy or free-rides on the other. Mara says that choosing to pay is evidence that the other pays, because both are running the same algorithm, so the expected payoff of paying is high. Dev says that only works if the two algorithms are identical in the relevant respect, and in a large world there are infinitely many agents with every degree of similarity; the reference class is either gerrymandered or ill-defined, and any measure over it is a prior smuggled in as decision theory. Mara should have a real reply -- maybe that the reference class is fixed by the causal history of your algorithm rather than by arbitrary similarity -- and Dev should press that this just relocates the problem. Let them go back and forth for real, each responding to the other's actual point rather than talking past each other, and let them reach a partial clarification rather than a tidy victory. Include one moment where Dev concedes something that costs him, and one where Mara has to give ground. I'm not looking for a verdict at the end, just a good argument that makes the disagreement sharper.
