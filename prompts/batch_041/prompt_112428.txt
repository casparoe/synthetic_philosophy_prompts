I retired from the lab a few years ago (physical chemist, thirty-odd years of spectroscopy) and I've spent the time since trying to teach myself philosophy, mostly phil of mind and phil of math, plus a lot of half-digested podcasts in between. I'm in a reading group where we take turns writing down our position on something so the others can go at it, and it's my turn, on Gödel. Before I write it up I want to know whether the thing actually hangs together, because I have a nagging feeling it doesn't and I genuinely can't see where.

Here's what I think:

1. I'm a physicalist — the mind is what the brain does, nothing spooky. And I'd bet that whatever the brain does, it's the kind of thing a machine could in principle do; maybe not a Turing machine exactly, but some physical process, and so some computational one.

2. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem: any consistent, recursively axiomatizable (I looked that up once, roughly means a computer could list the axioms) formal system strong enough for arithmetic has sentences it can neither prove nor disprove, and in fact has true sentences it can't prove. The second theorem says such a system can't prove its own consistency, if it is consistent.

3. These are theorems of arithmetic, and provable in fairly weak arithmetic at that. Nothing mystical about them, no special access to a Platonic heaven required. They're about what follows from what.

4. I think Penrose, and Lucas before him, are basically right: we can see that the Gödel sentence of a system we accept is true, and the system can't, so human mathematical understanding is not the operation of any single consistent formal system. We do something no algorithm does.

5. Relatedly, I don't think we can ever prove that our own mathematical reasoning is consistent. We take it on faith, and the second theorem says we can't do better. I also read somewhere that the Gödel sentence for a system is provably equivalent to the statement that the system is consistent, which surprised me but I think I believe it.

6. I also think the incompleteness theorems get applied to absolutely everything and most of it is garbage — to consciousness, to the limits of science, to whether there could be a theory of everything, to politics. Ninety-nine percent of that is people who don't know what the theorems actually say.

7. And I've never understood how Gödel's completeness theorem sits with the incompleteness ones. Completeness says first-order logic is complete, every valid formula is provable; incompleteness says arithmetic isn't. Both are named after him. I'm sure there's no real contradiction but I couldn't tell you why, and it bothers me every time I think about it.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and how bad is each clash? Rank them by severity rather than telling me they're all in tension. For each real one, what's the cheapest thing I could give up and what would that cost me? I'd rather not give up 1 or 2 if I can help it. And if the fix for one of them is something I've heard before — that maybe our mathematical beliefs aren't the output of one consistent system, or that we might just be inconsistent — tell me what that move actually buys, because if 1 is true I don't see how it helps.
