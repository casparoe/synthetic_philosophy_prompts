Next month I have been asked to give a short talk to the meditation and contemplative-science group I have sat with for years: about twenty people, mostly long-time meditators, a few from a Buddhist background, two retired clinical psychologists, and one member who teaches introductory psychology at the community college. They take the present moment seriously, and most of them are quietly hostile to the suggestion that experience is constructed, which they hear as a polite way of saying that the thing they have spent decades learning to attend to is not really there. I am not an academic; this is something I have read about and thought about on my own, and I need to be ready for the questions rather than for the applause.

Here is what I am defending. When two dots are flashed in quick succession, one above the other and in different colors, we see a single dot travel between them and change color halfway, and we see the change as happening before the second dot has appeared, even though no one could have known the second dot's color until it was shown. I take this to show that the brain does not accumulate a stream of experience and then preserve it; it settles on a story and then dates the story's events backward to fit. The two familiar ways of reading this I have ended up calling Orwellian, where the experience itself is rewritten, and Stalinesque, where only the report or the memory is, and I want to argue that both are wrong, because both quietly assume that an experience has a definite location on a single objective timeline. My alternative is that an experience is dated to the moment the brain commits to a coherent story, what I have been calling the commitment clock, and that the apparent order of the events we perceive is set by that clock, which is how evidence arriving later can fix what we seem to have seen earlier. I call this retro-binding, and I think it is also what the present moment really is: not a window onto now, but a commitment.
