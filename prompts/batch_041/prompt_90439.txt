I am a high school student preparing a position paper for our student government, and I would like a careful analysis rather than a general essay. Our district has adopted an AI monitoring system that scans essays submitted through the learning-management system, messages sent through school email accounts, and posts in school-managed discussion boards. The system flags language associated with self-harm, threats, and bullying; flagged items go to a counselor and, for threats, to an assistant principal. The vendor also retains metadata about all scanned messages for eighteen months and provides aggregate dashboards to a research consortium. The district defends the policy on two grounds: that students have no reasonable expectation of privacy in school accounts, and that even if privacy is a value, safety outweighs it. I have been reading about an account of privacy that treats privacy not as secrecy or as a reasonable expectation of being unobserved, but as contextual integrity. On this account, whether an information flow is appropriate depends on the norms of the context in which the information was shared: who the parties are, what kind of information it is, what purpose it is being used for, and what transmission principles govern it. A message to a friend through a school email account and a formal essay submitted to a teacher may both sit on school servers, but they belong to different contexts and may be governed by different norms. Please describe the most important implications of this contextual-integrity view for how our student government should evaluate the district's policy. I am especially interested in implications that bear on (1) whether the district's no-reasonable-expectation argument actually answers the contextual-integrity view, (2) which distinctions the policy should draw among different kinds of school-managed communication and different recipients of flagged information, and (3) what concrete changes the policy would need if the view is correct. Please be specific about the trade-offs and avoid giving me a reading list or citations; I need the implications themselves.
