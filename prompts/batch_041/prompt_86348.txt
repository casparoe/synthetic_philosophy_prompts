I am preparing a one-page handout for an advanced seminar in the philosophy of perception, and I want it to be a decision tree that sorts students by what they actually believe about the timing of experience, before they have met the standard names for the positions. Please write it, on the color phi phenomenon.

Open the handout with a short, neutral preamble stating the phenomenon. Two dots of different colors are flashed in rapid succession at two separate locations. At suitable intervals, subjects report seeing a single dot travel from the first location to the second and change color about halfway along its path, so that the new color is experienced before the second dot has appeared. Make clear in the preamble why this is puzzling: the motion itself is unremarkable, since apparent motion is a familiar effect, but the content of the second flash appears to be present in an experience of an event that the subject places before the second flash.

The tree should carry a student from the starting commitment "I accept that subjects in this experiment really do see the color change before the second dot appears" to a named position at each leaf. The following positions must appear as leaves, and the branch questions should make the differences among them visible:

- the two competing families of explanation for the effect — one holding that the two stimuli are reconciled before their content enters the subject's experience, so that the experience itself contains the early color change (the family often called Stalinesque), the other holding that the experience is veridical in real time and the revision is to memory afterward (often called Orwellian);
- the view that the question of when the content entered experience has no determinate answer, because it presupposes a single place and moment at which content becomes conscious;
- the view that no experience of an early color change need be posited at all, because the effect is adequately explained at a lower level of description — for instance, by the two stimuli producing equivalent internal states;
- the view that the second stimulus retroactively modulates the processing of the first, so that neither a pre-experiential buffer nor a post-experiential memory edit is required (if this collapses into one of the others, say so);
- the view that what the subject reports is a construction or judgment rather than a report of anything experienced.

Constraints on the tree:

- Six to nine leaves. Every branch node must be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to; no node may name a position or borrow a label from the literature.
- Every node must turn on a distinction the positions genuinely dispute. The tree should at least register the difference between filling in the trajectory and filling in the color; ask whether the effect can be explained by the subject's expectation or prior knowledge of the second color rather than by any retrospective revision; ask whether the disputed content was experiential or only doxastic; and ask whether any possible experiment could distinguish the two families of explanation described above.
- For each leaf, give the position a name — or mark it "unnamed" if the literature has none — and add one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.
- Note any combination of answers the tree leaves unoccupied, and say briefly whether the reason is incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere inattention.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot in principle separate, and state explicitly whether the two families of explanation can be distinguished by any experiment, together with what follows for the tree if they cannot.

The students will do no outside reading for this session, so the handout must stand on its own: no citations, no reading list, and no dependence on the reader's having heard of any particular author.
