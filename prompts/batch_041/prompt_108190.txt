I've ended up teaching Sen's liberal paradox next week and I'd like you to plan the session rather than explain the paradox to me — I know what the result is, I don't know how to get a room into it. It's 90 minutes, MA political theory, and the group is split down the middle: maybe half have done a real social choice course and can recite Arrow, the other half have never seen a preference ordering in their life.

Here's how I plan to state the two conditions, so we're on the same page. Weak Pareto: if every individual strictly prefers x to y, society prefers x to y. Minimal liberalism: there are at least two individuals, and each of them is decisive over at least one pair of alternatives — meaning their preference between those two just is the social preference, no aggregation required. That's it. Two people, one pair each, and it's supposed to be embarrassingly weak. And then you can't have both once you let people have any preferences they like.

What I want from you:

The order the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what. Specifically: do I need to build any of the Arrow apparatus at all (social welfare functions, pairwise independence, the general possibility theorem) or does hauling it in actively hurt me here? Half the room is going to try to file this under Arrow no matter what I do, so tell me where in the sequence I head that off, not just that I should.

The carrying example. I don't want the prude-and-lewd book one — partly because it's memorized and they'll get lazy, partly because I think the prude's prudishness drags everyone into a censorship conversation when the actual crux is nosiness about someone else's pair. If you think I'm wrong about that, say so and give me the version of it that does work. Either way give me one example that carries the whole thing, and tell me what it has to have. My own guess is that it has to be a case where what each person is decisive over is genuinely their own business — their own case, not the whole social state, because students always misread the right as a veto over what the other person does — and where the third preference that closes the cycle is something they'd actually have rather than a stipulation. If it needs the full 3x3 laid out, fine, but tell me why.

The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually surface in the room, and the exact point in the sequence where I should head each one off. My guesses, and I want you to correct and reorder them: (a) "this is just Arrow again"; (b) "liberal" heard as the American political label, or as some thick doctrine, rather than the bare minimum it is; (c) that minimal liberalism hands you a right to stop other people doing things; (d) that the paradox shows liberalism is incoherent, or that the only available fix is to drop the liberty condition; (e) that any of this is about voting rules or real democratic institutions. What have I missed, and which of those am I wrong about?

One question to put to the room at the end that shows whether they've got the shape of the result rather than the two labels — something that would catch someone who can chant "Pareto versus liberty" but can't say which condition the theorem actually forces you to give up, or why dropping the liberty condition is the expensive move rather than the cheap one.

And what I should openly refuse to explain at this level. I'd rather say "we're not doing that today" than wave at the escape routes — domain restrictions, rights as constraints, what Sen himself did with it later — and have them half-remember it.

Last thing: is there a moment in the 90 minutes where the Arrow half of the room becomes an asset instead of a liability, and how do I stage that?
