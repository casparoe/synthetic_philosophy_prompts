In a number of democracies, bodies of randomly selected ordinary citizens are given real decision-making or agenda-setting power: the jury is the oldest example, and the more recent "citizens' assembly" -- convened to deliberate on a single well-framed question such as constitutional change or climate policy -- is a newer one. In each case the random body is trusted with a narrow question, and the ordinary business of legislating remains with elected representatives who in practice make a career of it. Many of these same systems also cap the number of terms a president or governor may serve while placing no comparable limit on legislators, who can be returned to office indefinitely.

I would like a philosophical reconstruction of what these arrangements presuppose. Consider the apparent inconsistencies the asker is puzzled by. First: we will let twelve randomly chosen citizens decide whether a stranger spends the rest of his life in prison, but we will not let randomly chosen citizens set a tax rate -- what does that asymmetry assume about the kind of judgment a decision requires? Second: sortition is in one respect the most radically anti-careerist device imaginable, since a randomly selected body cannot be campaigned for or captured, yet citizens' assemblies are almost always convened by, and report back to, the elected officials whose authority they are meant to check -- does that practice presuppose that the elected body retains a legitimacy the random body lacks, and if so, on what view of legitimacy? Third: term limits are imposed on executives but not on legislators; does that presuppose that the harm of long tenure is entrenchment in a single office rather than incumbency as such, or that the executive wields a kind of power -- command, initiative, the ability to entrench a faction -- that a single legislator does not?

For each of the three, reconstruct the implicit theory of political competence, representation, and accountability that the practice only makes sense under, and state which rival theories it quietly rules out. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely happens to fit well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible views would each license the same arrangement. Where the presuppositions conflict with one another across the three practices, say which of them a coherent defender of all three would have to give up, and what the institutional arrangement would look like if it were rebuilt on the presupposition you think is doing the most work.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence -- your overall verdict on what these practices presuppose -- and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
