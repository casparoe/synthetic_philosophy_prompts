I am a novelist, and I use AI assistants in the course of my work — to check the plausibility of a scene, to pressure-test a character's reasoning, occasionally to borrow a voice I can't quite hear on my own. In doing so I have mapped the edges of what these systems will and won't do, and the edges don't line up in any way I can explain. I would like you to reconstruct the moral theory those edges imply, say whether it is coherent, and tell me what would have to change if we replaced it with a rival one.

Here is the map I've drawn. First, if I ask how a poison works, how a particular lock is picked, or what a body looks like after a given interval, the assistant will usually answer — provided I mention that it's for a novel. Second, if I push to the point where the answer has to be true to be useful — the actual lethal dose, the actual sequence of tumblers, the actual rate of decay — it often balks, even though the novel needs the truth to be convincing; and this puzzles me, because a fiction exemption that withholds the facts seems to defeat the very thing it means to protect, accurate fiction being accurate fact in a different key. Third, on another axis the rule reverses: I may invent a person and have the assistant speak in that invented person's voice without apparent limit, but if I ask it to speak as a real person — a relative, a public figure, someone recently dead — it refuses, though the two cases seem to differ only in whether the person exists. Fourth, there is an asymmetry I find stranger still: the assistant will write a character who argues that he deserves to die, that a certain group is contemptible, or that his crime was justified, but it will not write those same sentences in its own voice, as its own view, even when I frame the request as a mere exercise in argument.

What I want from you is not a way around any of this. I want the theory underneath it. What does each of these policies presuppose about the moral status of speech — that what matters is the content of what is said, the speaker's endorsement of it, the effects it is likely to produce, or the deception it involves? Are the first two policies consistent with each other, and with the third and fourth? Does the fiction frame function as a claim about causation (stories do not cause harm), about intention (I do not mean it), or merely about institutional and reputational risk? Does the refusal to speak as a real person presuppose that identity is a kind of property, that grief is an interest worth protecting, or that impersonation is a species of lying? Tell me which of these commitments the practices strictly require and which they merely happen to fit, and note where the practice is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same behavior. Then tell me what the map would look like under a rival theory — a pure content rule, a pure harm rule, or a rule that forbids deception rather than harm — and, since I write fiction for a living, what the practice would have to become if we took seriously the thought that a novelist's accurate depiction of a real harm is itself a real thing.
