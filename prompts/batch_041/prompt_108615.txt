Me and a friend have been going around in circles about Aumann's agreement theorem for like two months now and I genuinely can't tell anymore wether we're having a real argument or just talking past each other. It started when he told me our long-running disagreement about a forecasting call we both care about means one of us has to be irrational, on the grounds that two Bayesians with the same prior can't agree to disagree once they know each other's credences. I've read enough now to know the proof is fine and nobody disputes it. What I can't figure out is what it's supposed to tell us about actual disagreement, and it kinda seems like nobody else can either.

Here's the split as I see it. One camp treats the theorem as a real constraint: if two people are coherent Bayesians with a common prior, and it's common knowledge what each of them thinks, they agree — so persistent disagreement must come from different priors, or one of them being incoherent, or the common-knowledge condition failing somewhere. The other camp thinks it's basically a triviality: the theorem just says two functions of the same underlying state can't be incompatible functions of that state, and the assumptions are so strong and so rarely satisfied that it has no bearing on any disagreement you've ever actually had. Both camps agree on the math and disagree about what the math is doing. The common-prior assumption is the flashpoint — one side reads it as a substantive claim that there's a shared objective chance out there, the other as a coherence constraint that just falls out of being a Bayesian at all.

My hypothesis, and I'd like you to tell me if it's wrong, is that this is two questions wearing one hat: a static formal question about what's possible given a fixed information structure, and a normative question about what I should believe when someone I respect disagrees with me. The theorem only answers the first, because the rationality in it is thin — coherence, not good reasoning. But maybe the real disagreement is downstream of something unresolved about priors, in which case it's substantive and not verbal.

So diagnose it. Which parts are verbal (people using rational or agree to mean different things), which are about a hidden formal question — does the result survive if you drop the partitional picture or allow unawareness, is there a version with no common prior at all, and is it even true that common-prior agents can't agree to disagree about the fact that they disagree — and which parts are bedrock differences about what a prior is or what an agent is? And then given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or is this one of those things where the impasse is the honest resting point and I should just stop arguing with my friend about it?
