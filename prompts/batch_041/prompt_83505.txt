Before I put any criterion of authenticity to work on the sayings attributed to Jesus, I want the specification in hand. Write the desiderata such a criterion must satisfy before it is worth using. I want the specification itself, not a survey: the cases the criterion must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the findings from adjacent fields it must not contradict, what it must be able to establish against a saying as well as for one, and what it must deliver in the way of a result. Give the consideration behind each requirement; mark which requirements bind any criterion in this area and which are merely the preferences of one camp; and say which single requirement, if enforced, eliminates the largest number of the criteria now in use.

Hold the specification to the following constraints, and rule on each of them -- genuine requirement, or prejudice? The criterion must not presuppose the portrait of Jesus it is meant to establish. It must be operable on the evidence that exists, which is Greek texts shaped by communities rather than transcripts of speech. It must not depend on any particular solution to the relations among the Gospels. It must be consistent with what is known about how sayings are remembered, transmitted, and altered in oral and communal settings. It must be neutral between a historian who shares the faith of the texts' tradents and one who does not, and it must beg no question for either side about whether the events in question could have supernatural causes. It must be able to return a degree of confidence rather than a verdict. It must be checkable by someone who has the texts and nothing else. Add whatever else the area genuinely demands.

The part I most want is the friction. I suspect that the demand that an authentic saying be distinctive of Jesus and the demand that it be at home in its Jewish setting cannot both be met by one criterion without circularity, and I want that worked out rather than merely acknowledged; so too, perhaps, for the pair formed by neutrality between believer and unbeliever and the refusal to beg the question about supernatural causes. State plainly whether any pair of your requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and what follows for the whole enterprise if it is.

Then, in one or two lines each, run the familiar criteria against your list -- dissimilarity (a saying unlike both its Jewish setting and the early church's own tendencies), embarrassment (a community would not have invented what shames it), multiple attestation (a saying appears in sources that are independent), coherence (a saying fits material already judged authentic), and any others that belong -- and say which survive intact, which are wounded, and which are dead. If someone proposes collapsing them into a single test, namely that a saying is authentic in proportion as its preservation was costly to those who preserved it, say whether that meets your list or merely dresses up the embarrassment criterion in the language of costs. End with a verdict, not a hedge, on whether criteria of authenticity can ever settle who Jesus was.
