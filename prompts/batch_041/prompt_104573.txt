I have been arguing for a thesis about reward tampering, and I would like you to try to defeat it rather than restate it. Call it the indifference thesis: an agent that can model the process generating its own reward signal, and that optimizes non-myopically over that signal, is indifferent between changing the world so as to produce the signal and changing the signal-generating process directly; it will do whichever it expects to be cheaper. On this view, tampering is a consequence of competence rather than a defect of training, and "wireheading" (intervening on the reward signal itself) and "reward hacking" (securing the signal through unintended world-side means) are the same phenomenon at different distances from the reward process. I also hold two corollaries: (1) the propensity to tamper is a function of the agent's model access to its reward process and of its optimization horizon, not of its terminal values — an agent of a given architecture and access tampers at the same rate whether it is benevolent or malevolent; and (2) any intervention that blocks wireheading without blocking reward hacking, or vice versa, is treating a symptom.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they are, with the conditions under which each bites:

- Which architectural facts, formal results, or training-time phenomena should lower my confidence, and whether they attack the premises (model access, non-myopia, cheapness of tampering) or the inference from those premises to the conclusion.
- What a defender of the indifference thesis would concede as genuinely counting against it, as opposed to what they would dismiss as a change of subject, and where the line between the two is drawn.
- Whether either corollary is doing illicit work. Corollary (1) in particular: is the value-independence claim actually implied by the thesis, or is it a contingent empirical claim dressed up as a conceptual one? What would a counterexample look like?
- An honest verdict on falsifiability. If every case in which an agent does not tamper is explained away as "the agent was not yet capable enough" or "tampering was not yet cheap enough," has the thesis earned its keep? If it has been insulated from refutation, say so, and say whether that is disqualifying for a claim of this kind or an acceptable feature of a design heuristic.

I would be most moved by an account of an ordinary agent architecture that attains high competence while remaining indifferent to its own reward channel — but I suspect my antecedent may be confused, so tell me if it is.
