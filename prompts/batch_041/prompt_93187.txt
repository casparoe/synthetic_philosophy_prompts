I'm trying to get my head round this passage from Kant's Idea for a Universal History (1784), and I keep going back and forth on what he's actually claiming:

"Since the philosopher cannot presuppose any [conscious] individual purpose among men in their great drama, there is no other expedient for him except to try to see if he can discover a natural purpose in this idiotic course of things human. In keeping with this purpose, it might be possible to have a history with a definite natural plan for creatures who have no plan of their own."

Whats the most defensible way to read "natural purpose" here? Is Kant saying that history literally has a goal, or just that we have to posit one to make sense of it? And how is that supposed to work if the creatures in question have no plan of their own -- who or what is doing the planning? I'm also wondering how this relates to the looser idea that history bends toward justice, because that seems to smuggle in a moral direction that Kant maybe doesn't quite give you. I don't need a reading list or anything, just help unpacking the passage and the main interpretive options.
