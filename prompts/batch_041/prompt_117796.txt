I'm a PhD student in philosophy running a 75-minute session for a reading group of roughly eighteen graduate students in normative ethics, and I want them to feel the demandingness objection to consequentialism from the inside rather than hear it asserted. The version I care about is the one that usually gets glossed: not a single large ask, but an escalation with no stable resting point, so that whatever level of giving a person actually settles on must be defended by a principle that would equally have licensed them to stop far earlier. They should run into that themselves and only hear the objection named at the end.

Design the activity around private budgets of discretionary resources and a sequence of allocation decisions across rounds, each round offering cards that convert resources into averted harms at stated rates, with the group's aggregate choices revealed afterwards. Build in at least three cards: one strictly more cost-effective than anything preceding it, so that an earlier stopping point becomes hard to defend; one whose payoff turns out to be worthless, disclosed only after the fact; and one whose payoff requires a threshold of group participation. Have each participant write down, before the last round, the rule that fixes their stopping point, then apply it to a card that just barely beats their previous best.

Give me the rules, materials, and benefit scheme (no real money), a round-by-round timeline, and a debrief that maps each mechanic onto the piece of the objection it is meant to make vivid: the escalation, the personal prerogative, the empirical premise about how much good a unit of resource can do, and the collective-action reply. Say what will predictably go wrong the first time — someone gives everything in round one and turns it into a purity contest, the dud card makes others disengage — and how to handle it. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the real cost of real sacrifice, the difference between what a theory requires and what it may demand of someone's deliberation, and the dependence of the objection's force on contested empirical claims.
