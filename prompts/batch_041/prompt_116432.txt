You are an expert on analogy-making, fluid concepts, and the design of reasoning assessments. I need your help operationalizing a theoretical claim for a very practical setting. The claim, roughly, is that analogy-making is not a matter of matching fixed features but of re-perceiving two situations under a shared conceptual frame, so that what counts as the same change depends on which differences a solver treats as slippable and which as load-bearing. I run a small puzzle lab that produces a reasoning curriculum. Our puzzles use an invented microdomain called the Kestrel tiles. A puzzle shows two panels, A and B, each a 3x3 grid of tiles. Every tile has a shape (circle, triangle, square), a shade (pale, medium, dark), and a position. The solver is shown a new panel C and must produce a panel D such that the change from C to D is the same kind of change as the change from A to B. There is no answer key, because in this microdomain there is usually more than one defensible reading of the source change. I need a reusable decision procedure that my graders can run on a proposed D. They are undergraduates with no background in cognitive science. They have about three minutes per answer and can ask the solver for a one-sentence rationale but cannot interview them. The decisions are low-stakes for the puzzle itself but high-stakes for a research dataset, because I need inter-rater reliability. Any rejection has to be signed off by me.

Please write the procedure in continuous prose only, as a long letter to me. Do not use headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold or italic text, or tables; if you must enumerate, do it in sentences. The procedure should give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of purpose or value rather than fact. It should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on at least three invented cases, at least one of which the procedure resolves in a way I will not like, and say what the procedure cannot do.

The cases should include a straightforward case where the source change moves one tile and the proposed D moves the analogous tile. They should include a case where the source change alters a feature such as count or shade and the proposed D alters a different but related feature. They should include a hard case where the solver's answer is elegant but relies on a slippage that the procedure may or may not license; in that hard case, make the procedure's verdict depend on the solver's one-sentence rationale, and show what happens when the rationale is present and when it is missing. Include, either as one of those cases or as a fourth, a case where the procedure cannot decide and must hand the decision to me, explaining exactly what information is missing and why it is a value judgment rather than a factual one.

Before you write, think carefully about the tension between two bad outcomes: a procedure so permissive that every clever answer passes and the dataset becomes noise, and a procedure so strict that it only accepts the most literal reading and misses the point of the theory. Brainstorm privately how to navigate that tension, then present only your final procedure and applications. Your answer should be useful to someone who has never read the theory and should not mention any books, articles, or authors; no citations or reading lists.
