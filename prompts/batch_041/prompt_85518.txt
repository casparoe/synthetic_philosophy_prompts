I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and my cohort spent most of a long evening arguing about whether the natural-kinds literature earns its keep. The discussion went nowhere because we kept switching examples — one person would insist the elements settle the question, someone else would counter with species, a third would bring up race, and we never compared like with like. I'd like a cleaner verdict. Take the following candidate categories and sort them into tiers by how much inductive weight they can bear: that is, by how confidently a working scientist should use the category as the antecedent of a generalization meant to transfer to a novel population, sample, or setting, as opposed to using it as a convenient label for a locally useful clustering. The list: (a) the chemical elements, individuated by atomic number; (b) water, as the substance picked out by both everyday usage and chemistry; (c) gold, likewise as used by laypeople and by materials scientists; (d) biological species under an interbreeding-based conception; (e) biological species under a lineage- or ancestry-based conception; (f) human racial categories; (g) human sex, understood by gamete type; (h) major depressive disorder as currently operationalized in psychiatric diagnosis; (i) anger, as posited by basic-emotion theories of affect; (j) quark flavour; (k) weed, as the term is used in agronomy and weed science; (l) money, as used in economics.

For each entry, give me a tier — I'd suggest four or five tiers, and you should say where the boundaries fall and why — plus a one- or two-sentence justification, and an explicit answer to two further questions. First, is the category's membership fixed by a shared causal or structural feature, or by a decision, a convention, or a historical accident, and does that distinction line up with your tier placement or cut across it? Second, would you call the category a looping kind — one whose members tend to change in response to being classified, or to the social uptake of the classification — and if so, does that raise or lower the confidence a scientist should place in generalizations that deploy it?

Two constraints on the answer. First, the tiers should not track how respectable or well-entrenched a category is, but only inductive robustness in the sense above: a category can be politically fraught and still land high, or mundane and land low. Second, where you think two entries are really one category in different clothing, or where a single entry should be split, say so rather than forcing a clean ordering — I would rather have a partially ordered answer with the tensions flagged than a tidy list I can't use. Conclude with a sentence or two on which single comparison on the list is the most philosophically instructive, and why.
