Two friends of mine got into an argument about something I'd never heard of before, called pain asymbolia. The way they described it: some patients with certain kinds of brain damage say that things hurt — they can point at the pain, describe where it is, how strong it is — but it doesn't bother them at all. They can laugh while it's happening. They don't seem to learn to avoid whatever's causing it, even though they'll still pull their hand back from something hot. My friend Dan thinks this settles something big: if you can have the pain without the awfulness, then the awfulness was never part of the pain itself, it's a separate reaction tacked onto it, and we've been wrong to treat pain as something that's bad in its own right. Rosa says that's backwards. What the patients lost is exactly the part that makes pain pain, she says, so what they've got left is a sensation that resembles pain but isn't the real thing, and Dan's argument just redefines pain so he can't lose. Dan says that if that's her move she can never be wrong — any case where pain isn't awful just gets ruled out as not real pain — and anyway the patients themselves insist it hurts. Rosa says they insist because they're describing the sensation, but hurting is doing double duty there and they're using it in the thin sense; the thick sense, the one that actually matters, is gone. Dan comes back: then when we say a baby or a dog is in pain and it's bad, which sense are we using, and how would we ever know the thick part is present? Rosa says the thick part is just the motivational bit, the urge to make it stop, and whether that's there is a fact about the animal you can study, not something mystical. They went back and forth like this for an hour and neither budged, and I dont know the literature at all. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right about which point — not just who wins overall — and what both of them are missing?
