You are an expert in Bayesian decision theory and in value-of-information analysis, with enough exposure to regulatory practice to know what survives contact with a deadline. Before you write anything, think on paper: begin by setting out in a few sentences what the result I describe below does and does not establish, and what conditions it needs, and only then build the procedure. I would rather see your working, including the parts you are unsure about, than a polished answer that hides the joints.

My situation. I head the risk-analysis unit at a national product-safety regulator. A few dozen times a year a hazard signal reaches us — a cluster of incident reports, a foreign regulator's alert, a supplier's disclosure, an industry study — and within a fixed statutory window (usually about eight weeks) our board must choose between ordering a recall, requiring a design change or warning label, and taking no action. My unit writes the briefing the board votes on. I have two analysts, no decision authority of my own, and no power to extend the window. Every briefing contains the same argument: someone says we should commission more evidence before the board votes, and someone else says there is no time. I need a procedure that settles that argument rather than restaging it.

The result I want operationalised is the one usually credited to I. J. Good: for a decision-maker who is about to choose an act, the expected value of a free look at the evidence before choosing is never negative, and is exactly zero only when no possible finding would change which act is chosen. The standard justification is that you can always ignore what you learn. I believe that settles nothing in my world, because nothing here is free, we are not one person, and some findings cannot be ignored once they exist.

Write me the procedure, not an essay on the theorem. Requirements:

1. A staged sequence of questions in the order an analyst should ask them, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it must hand the question to a human because the question is about values or governance rather than about facts. Assume an analyst with one week, a spreadsheet, no simulation software, and no outside consultants.
2. Keep the conditions of the result visible rather than buried. Test each condition in turn, and say plainly what is and is not licensed when a condition fails.
3. Give me a screen that needs no computation. Value of information on a study can never exceed the value of perfect information, so if the delay and the study together cost more than perfect information about the hazard is worth, stop there. Tell me how to get a crude bound on that when we cannot compute one.
4. Make the flip test an explicit step: information that cannot change which act we choose is worth exactly zero, however interesting it is, and however much a board member wants it.
5. Say what to do when the answer is not a number and no one can supply a probability.
6. Handle the question of who the we is. The result is about a single agent with one probability function and one utility function. My board has five members, our general counsel, and a public that bears the harm.
7. Handle the ways a finding stops being something we can ignore: a study whose launch is public and prompts the importer to reformulate before we can act on the result; a measurement that is itself an intervention, such as publishing a hazard ranking; a proxy measure whose relation to the actual harm is contested.
8. Handle the distinction between the expectation before the draw and the outcome after it. Collecting evidence and then learning nothing decision-relevant, or learning something that makes us look foolish, is not evidence that collecting it was a mistake, and my analysts need a way to say this that a hostile post-mortem will accept.
9. Two studies are not worth the sum of their separate values; value them in sequence, incorporating the first before pricing the second. Build this in.
10. Do not let the procedure degrade into always gather more.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a chemical in a children's product, forty incident reports of a rash, a plausible mechanism, and a proposed twelve-week sensitisation study in a contract laboratory, with the board voting in eight weeks; (b) a proposed consumer-attitude survey about a hazard that no act in our repertoire is sensitive to; (c) a study whose launch would be publicly visible and would give the importer time to reformulate the product before we could act on the results. At least one of the three should come out against what my unit would like to do, and you should say so directly.

Do not give me citations, a reading list, or references to the literature; I cannot check them and I do not need them. Give me the procedure, and flag any step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure, any step that will misfire, and anything the procedure cannot do at all.

Finally, throughout, separate what is settled among people who know this material from what is genuinely contested. If the extension of the result to institutions, to groups with conflicting priors, or to agents who are not expected-utility maximisers is contested, say that it is contested and do not present your own resolution as the consensus. If the way I have stated the result above is not the way it is actually established, correct me before building on it.
