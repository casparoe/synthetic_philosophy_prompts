I cover philosophy for a general-interest magazine, and I'm writing a feature on the free-will debate that I'd like to keep off the usual track. Here is the claim I want a number on: some agent could be morally responsible for an action she performs even though, holding the actual history fixed up to the moment of acting, no alternative was open to her — with "open" read categorically, not in the merely conditional sense that she would have acted otherwise had she so chosen. Give me your credence that this claim is true, and decompose it rather than asserting it. Roughly one line of justification each, then the combination:

1. that a case can be described that instantiates the claim without either letting the intervener wait for a prior sign — which would itself be a residual alternative — or building in determinism and so begging the question against the incompatibilist;
2. that, such a case being granted, the residual flicker is too thin to ground responsibility;
3. that responsibility's real condition is sourcehood or self-expression rather than alternatives, in which case the claim holds for reasons independent of whether the cases work at all;
4. that the intuition of responsibility in such cases is robust rather than an artifact of narration — of keeping the intervener offstage;
5. that moral responsibility has a single necessary condition of the kind at issue in the first place.

Then tell me which of those terms your number is most sensitive to, and what argument or result would move it most. And a closing judgment for the piece: is the remaining disagreement between the defenders of such cases and their critics substantive, or largely a fight about what could-have-done-otherwise was ever supposed to mean?
