I'm a few months into a seminar paper on internal vs external reasons and I've got a snag that I think a properly engineered case would solve. All the standard cases run the same direction: the amoralist who doesn't care whether anyone gets hurt, or the agent who supposedly has a reason but can't reach it because he's deluded/depressed/not imaginative enough. Internalist comes out permissive (no reason, no irrationality), externalist comes out strict (of course he's irrational, look at what he's doing). I want the reverse. Build me a case where the internal reasons theorist — the one who says your reasons are a function of your existing motivational set plus sound deliberation from it — has to say the agent is rationally at fault, while the objectivist (reasons come from facts about what's worth doing or caring about, not from desires as such) has to let her off: no reason there, so nothing to reproach her with, rationally speaking. Constraints, and they're the annoying part: one paragraph, mundane contemporary setting, no sci-fi, no trolleys, no exotic mental states. She's not ignorant of any fact, and there's no error of means-end reasoning for either theory to point at and fix. She's not weak-willed, addicted, blocked, depressed, self-deceived or deluded — no felt pull that she fails to resist, no compulsion; she just doesn't do the thing, and if you asked her she'd agree without hedging that she cares about it more than anything and that she ought to be getting on with it. Also, the only person affected is the agent herself, so nobody can smuggle a moral reason in to do the work.

And it can't be the amoralist, the egoist, or anyone whose indifference is to other people's interests — I want this to have nothing to do with morality at all. Which means, I think, that the thing she cares about has to be one the objectivist is forced to say isn't worth caring about, so that for the internalist her not being moved is a defect in her, and for the objectivist it's just a fact about her, like a taste. Please don't hand me the usual grass-counter or pointless-hobby example; I've seen it and I want something that doesn't feel like it's been in the literature since 1979.

After the case, do the check. State which reason each theory says she has and why, what each says about her failure (irrationality? criticisability of some weaker kind? nothing at all?), and name the principle doing the work in each verdict. Then tell me which single feature of the case is load-bearing — what happens if I delete it, e.g. make the object genuinely worth pursuing, or make her struggle and fail — and whether the case just collapses back into the standard amoralist one at that point. Then the honest bit: how would a Williams-style internalist attack the case, where's the escape hatch, and does taking it cost him something he wanted to keep? Also flag the soft spot for me, because I think it's the weak joint: plenty of objectivists also hold that rationality is a matter of responding to your apparent reasons, and if you build that in, the acquittal flips. So say whether the case can be rebuilt so the flip doesn't happen, or whether that tells us the two theories can't actually be pulled apart in this direction at all. And if the reverse direction can't be built under these constraints, don't fudge it — tell me exactly which constraint breaks it and build the closest thing that survives the rest. The point of the exercise is for a section where I want to argue that the internalist's he-has-a-reason-and-he's-failing-rationally and the objectivist's there-is-no-reason-here are not the same kind of claim, so I need the verdicts to fall out of the theories rather than be stipulated up front.
