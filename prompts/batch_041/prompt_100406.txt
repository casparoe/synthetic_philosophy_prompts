I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend — she's finishing a dissertation on Wittgenstein and kept insisting that my whole way of talking about knowledge and doubt is incoherent — and I've been trying since to work out, on my own, where I actually stand. What I want from you is not an exposition of anyone's position but a principle, if one exists, that captures the verdicts I find myself giving about which of my convictions can sensibly be doubted and which cannot. Here they are. (1) When I hold up my hand and say I have two hands, I would not say I doubt it; but I also resist saying I know it, because I don't know it the way I know the kettle is boiling — there is no checking to be done, and nothing I could cite that wouldn't already presuppose the thing itself. (2) I know my own name: it's on my passport, I've answered to it for forty years, I've seen it printed on documents. So here is a proposition I treat as beyond doubt and yet also seem to know on perfectly ordinary evidence, which is not at all how the hands case feels. (3) If someone seriously doubted that two plus two is four, I'd think they hadn't understood the question, but I'm unsure whether that's the same phenomenon as (1) or something else entirely, since it isn't about the world at all. (4) In the lab where I used to work, nobody ever questioned that the balance was reading correctly; when it later turned out to be miscalibrated, the doubt was entirely ordinary and got settled by evidence. So whatever exemption from doubt these propositions enjoy seems relative to a practice, and revocable. (5) A cousin of mine was raised in a group where the leader's word was treated as unquestionable; I think she's wrong, but I can't see how I'd argue her out of it, and saying she is simply mistaken seems to miss the fact that this functions for her the way my hands function for me. (6) When a family story about how a relative died turned out to be false, revising it didn't feel like changing my mind about an ordinary fact — it felt like the ground shifting under a lot of other beliefs at once.

Is there a single principle that gets all of these — one that distinguishes a genuine hinge from a merely very certain belief, and accounts for the differences between (1), (2), (4) and (6) rather than lumping them together? I suspect there isn't, and if there isn't, I want to know which of my verdicts is the one I should abandon, and why that one rather than another. Please also say what your preferred principle predicts about two cases I haven't listed: whether my unexamined conviction that the building I work in will still be standing tomorrow is a hinge for me or an ordinary expectation I've simply never had occasion to test, and whether my unexamined assumption that my colleagues are not actors staging an experiment on me is the same kind of thing as the hands case. I would much rather have a precise principle with a clearly stated casualty among my verdicts than a tidy summary that contrives to save them all.
