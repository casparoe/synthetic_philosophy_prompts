ok so ive been reading around disjunctivism and theres a question i cant shake. i get the argument from hallucination fine, or i think i do: two subjects, one seeing a tomato, one having a hallucination, the experiences are subjectively indistinguishable, and the disjunctivist refuses the inference to "so theres a common kind of experience here" -- the hallucinators state gets a purely negative characterisation, "its as if there were a tomato", with nothing positive to say about it. fine. but ive started to suspect the argument from illusion is a seperate argument wearing the same name, and that alot of the textbook presentations just lump them together for tidiness.

heres what bugs me. in a bog-standard illusion theres an object in front of you and youre causally hooked up to it in the ordinary way -- you just get one of its properties wrong. the stick looks bent, the white wall looks yellow under the lamp, the moon looks bigger at the horizon. that looks like a case of seeing the stick plus misrepresenting it, which is exactly what a naive realist wants to say about ordinary perception anyway. so why is illusion supposed to be the thin end of the wedge that leads you to sense-data or to a common kind of experiential state? it seems to me the wedge only bites if you sneak in an extra premise, something like: when two experiences are subjectively indistinguishable, the subjects evidence is the same in both, and evidence supervenes on what its like. without that premise i dont see the sense-datum theorist getting off the ground.

can you do four things:

1. lay the argument from illusion and the argument from hallucination out seperately, and say precisely where they diverge and where the common kind assumption actually does its work in each. i want to see which premise each one needs.

2. explain the difference between denying a common kind of *experience* and denying a common kind of *evidence*, and whether someone could sensibly be a disjunctivist about the first while being a common-kind theorist about the second, or the other way round. does that combination even hold together, or does one collapse into the other?

3. tell me what a disjunctivist ought to say about a case i keep coming back to, which i think sits awkwardly between illusion and hallucination. youre standing at a lake on a dead still day. theres a tree on the far bank, and directly below it in the water theres its reflection, and the reflection is good enough that for a second you genuinely cant tell which one youre looking at. now: the light reaching your eye came off the same tree either way, it just bounced off the water first. so in both cases the tree is causally responsible and you might get its colour and shape exactly right, and the only thing you get wrong is where it is. is that one kind of experience or two? is the reflection case a case of seeing the tree or not? and does it matter that here the two indistinguishable experiences share an object, so the hallucination style argument cant even get started -- does illusion do any independent work, or is the reflection case just a perception with a locational error, in which case illusion turns out to be perfectly compatible with veridical perception and the whole wedge collapses?

4. close by saying whether you think the argument from illusion earns its keep once youve already got the argument from hallucination, or whether its basically rhetorical padding.
