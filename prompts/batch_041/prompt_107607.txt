I teach a graduate seminar on intentionality, and one of my colleagues — a philosopher of mind whose work I admire and who has published on both Dennett and Searle, and who resents being enrolled in either camp — has over several years, in seminar discussion, in referee reports, and in marginal comments on student chapters, committed herself to a fairly stable set of verdicts about which systems have original rather than merely derived intentionality. As I have reconstructed her record: a thermostat that cycles the furnace, no; a chess engine playing at grandmaster strength, no; a honeybee's waggle dance, yes, though she wavers and calls it marginal; a bacterium tumbling up a sugar gradient, no; a dog that begs at the table, yes; a corporation that tracks its competitors' pricing, no; a mouse that learns a maze for a reward, yes; a present-day large language model that answers questions about the world, no; a mobile robot whose obstacle-avoidance controller was hand-coded by an engineer, no; a human being, yes; and, when pressed about the honeybee, she said the dance is about the nectar and not merely a response to it, which she took to settle the matter.

Next week she sits on a dissertation committee for a student whose chapter concerns an artificial-life simulation: digital organisms that have reproduced with mutation and selection for several million generations inside a virtual world, whose internal states have come to carry information about the location of simulated food, and about which no human ever specified what those states are supposed to represent. The student argues the organisms have original intentionality; the other committee member says a simulation can no more have it than a spreadsheet can, because whatever the states represent is still owed to us. She has not told us which way she will come down, and I want to be able to anticipate her, partly to protect the student and partly because I think her record is more interesting than any single principle she would avow. So: predict her verdict on the digital organisms, reconstruct the rule or rules she has been running, give me your best guess at which she is actually running and why, and name the further case that would most cleanly tell the surviving rules apart. Do not tell me what Searle or Dennett would say unless it bears on her.

Please structure the response in five named sections, in this order: "The track record," one short paragraph that restates her verdicts and flags any that pull against the others (roughly 100 words); "Candidate rules," two or three rules stated crisply enough to generate predictions, each with the verdicts it accommodates and the ones it strains (roughly 250 words); "Predictions for the new case," what each rule says about the digital organisms, including any that are silent or indeterminate (roughly 200 words); "My best guess," her likely verdict and the reasons for preferring that reading of her (roughly 150 words); and "The discriminating case," the single further case that would separate the rules that remain standing, with a sentence on what each rule predicts about it (roughly 100 words).
