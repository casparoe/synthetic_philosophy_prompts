I serve on the ethics committee of a mid-sized community mental health system, and we are drafting a decision procedure for our clinicians on what we have been calling Ulysses contracts: instruments in which a patient, while competent, specifies in advance how she is to be treated when she later loses decisional capacity — consenting now to admission or medication she may refuse then, or refusing treatment she may then demand. Our clinicians will run this procedure at the bedside, often in under an hour, usually with a resident or a crisis nurse rather than a psychiatrist trained in ethics. The instrument has to be legible to them. The decision they reach will sometimes be the difference between holding a patient against her expressed wishes and discharging someone into a situation she will not survive. Our medical director must sign off on any override of a patient's current refusal, and our legal counsel must sign off on any template language, so the procedure must tell those two people, separately, what they are being asked to authorize.

Please write the procedure. It should be a set of questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a decision, and — as important as the rest — where it must hand the question to a person because no procedure can settle it. Then run it on three cases: (1) a woman who, while well, executed a directive refusing electroconvulsive therapy, and who now, in a severe episode, is asking for it and is arguably competent to ask; (2) a man who consented in advance to involuntary admission and who now refuses it, with no ambiguity about the trigger conditions; (3) a patient whose directive was signed during a period when, on the record, she was still ambivalent, so that it is unclear whether the earlier preference was ever stable.

Some substance to work with, though correct me if any of it is confused. Our committee keeps rediscovering that binding is not one thing: an instrument can remove an option (no one will write the order), raise its cost (the patient knows that skipping appointments leads to admission), or transfer the decision to a third party (a named agent with authority to admit). These fail in different ways, and discussion of individual precommitment tends to blur them. Second, every device is only as binding as the cost of circumvention, and someone always holds the key — the clinician, the named agent, the court. I would like the procedure to require that, for any instrument, we name the keyholder and state what the later self would have to do to get out. Third, the hardest cases are not those where the earlier and later selves disagree, but those where the later self's disagreement is itself the symptom — where refusing the treatment is evidence of the condition the treatment addresses. I do not believe that can be resolved by a rule, and I want the procedure to say so rather than pretend otherwise. One committee member proposes that every instrument carry a mandatory adversary, a person whose only job at each review is to argue for release; another proposes that irrevocability is the entire point and that any release valve defeats it. The procedure should be usable under either proposal, or should say which one it presupposes.

Finally, I would like a separate section, a page or so, on a question the faculty senate has put to me. It wants to know whether the same procedure could govern the entrenchment clauses in our new governance charter — supermajority requirements, an endowment spending formula, protections for academic freedom. My suspicion is that the analogy breaks, because a body of people across generations is not one person across time, and because the dissenters bound by a constitutional precommitment were never parties to the agreement in the way a patient is. Tell me whether the break is deep enough that the bedside procedure would mislead if transplanted, and if so, precisely which steps have to change. I am not asking for a reading list or a survey of constitutional theory; I want the reasoning and the procedure, and I want the disanalogy stated sharply enough that I can explain it to the senate in ten minutes.
