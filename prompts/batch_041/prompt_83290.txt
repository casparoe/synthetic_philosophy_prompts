I run the negotiation-strategy practice at a small advisory firm, and we are increasingly called in on deals that are built as a fixed, published sequence of rounds. In the deals we care about, the party whose turn it is may either call the deal and take the larger share of whatever is on the table at that moment, or pass, which grows the pot by a fixed factor and hands the turn to the other side, with the final round binding and both sides knowing all of this in advance. A recent example, which we can use as the running case: five rounds, a pot that starts at ten million dollars, growth of forty percent per pass, and a caller who takes sixty percent of the pot with the remainder going to the other side. My analysts can all reproduce the standard backward-induction argument, which says the first mover calls at once, and my clients all ignore it, and the counterparts in these deals mostly ignore it too, passing well past the round where the theory says the game should have ended. The partners are tired of the two available answers, trust the theory or trust your gut, and have asked me for something a junior analyst can actually run. Our volume is roughly fifteen of these a year, each between fifty and five hundred million dollars, and we usually have under a week between rounds and no more than an hour of analyst time to spare; the engagement partner signs the recommendation and the client's CFO sees it, so it has to be defensible in one page to someone who has never heard of a game tree.

Please write that decision procedure. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to a person because the question is one of judgment rather than computation. It has to get from the theoretical solution to a stopping-point estimate for a counterparty whose rationality and depth of reasoning you cannot observe, using only evidence an analyst can actually gather in the week before a round: what happened in earlier rounds of this deal, what the counterparty's own principal-agent situation does to their payoffs, whether their deadline is publicly and credibly committed, and whether the same counterparty will recur across our portfolio, in which case the game on the table is not the game being played. The hard cases the procedure must handle are a deadline that is not common knowledge, because one side can exit without warning and nobody knows when; a counterparty who is known to be instructed not to be the one who calls; and a deal where the pot is large enough that the sure, early, smaller payoff beats the expected value of playing on under any plausible belief. Mark plainly the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as arithmetic, and say what the procedure cannot do. Then run it on three cases and show your work at each step: a public-sector procurement with published rules, a committee as the buyer, and no prospect of repeat business; a labor negotiation with a public strike date and a leadership that faces a member vote; and a two-round acquisition in which the acquirer has publicly committed to a walk-away price. At least one of the three should come out with a recommendation our clients will hate, namely calling early for the smaller share, and I want the memo to say so without hedging. Finish with a short section, in plain language, that an engagement partner can read aloud to a client who is emotionally attached to the big pot.
