So this is going to sound like a stupid family-dinner complaint, but I've been chewing on it for a few weeks and I genuinely can't tell if it's a real thing or just me being pedantic. My uncle said at dinner that "Western values" just means the rule of law, free speech, individual dignity, treating women as people rather than property, that kind of list. I said most of those things got defended by people in Baghdad and Beijing and Kerala long before anyone in Paris or London got around to them, and he said sure, but the West is where they got institutionalized and where they're still defended, so they're Western values in the relevant sense. Fine. Then I said Western states have run colonies and camps and mass surveillance and have jailed people for saying things, and he said that's a betrayal of Western values, not an example of them. And a friend of mine from the other side of the aisle says human rights talk is just Western values in universalist costume, so for her the same phrase is an accusation rather than a boast. Here's the thing I keep tripping over: the word seems to be doing a descriptive job (these are the values of this particular civilization, here's what's on the list) and an honorific job (these are the good ones, and we have them) at the same time, and whenever the descriptive side would embarrass the honorific side, the descriptive side gets quietly adjusted. Failures become lapses, successes elsewhere become borrowings, and the list itself gets built to fit the moral he wants to draw. It's not exactly that he's wrong about any particular claim, it's that I can't construct any evidence that would make him give the term up, and he doesn't seem to think that's a problem.

Is there a name for this? I know the lazy answer is "it's a contested concept," and I half-remember something from mid-century philosophy of language about definitions that are really recommendations dressed up as descriptions (Stevenson? Gallie?), but honestly neither of those feels like it lands on the specific thing, and I don't want a label anyway, I want the carving. So please take this seriously and work it out properly: what is the phenomenon here, precisely? How is it different from ordinary vagueness or from a term that's just fuzzily bounded; from a family-resemblance term; from an essentially contested concept in the strict sense, where the dispute is supposed to be part of what the concept is rather than a defect in it; from a persuasive definition, where someone has visibly swapped in a new descriptive content; and from the idea of a signifier with no fixed content that gets filled in by whoever's doing the politics? Which of those comes closest, and where does each one break down on my uncle's case? Is his move even a position, or is it a way of talking that mimics a position? And if nothing on the shelf fits, coin something and give me the definition plus the boundary cases: is "human rights" the same phenomenon, or "the scientific method," or "feudalism," or "terrorism," or "Asian values" (which I gather people used the same way, mirror-image, in the nineties)? I'd also love a diagnostic I could actually deploy in the moment -- a question I could ask that would separate someone using the phrase as a real, if fuzzy, category from someone using it so that no evidence could ever count against it -- and an honest verdict on whether that test even works, or whether it just punishes people for being sloppy instead of ideological. You're a philosopher of language with one foot in ideology critique; brainstorm the candidate concepts first, then commit to a view.
