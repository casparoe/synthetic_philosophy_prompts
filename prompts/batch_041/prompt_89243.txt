For my final project in an upper-level epistemology seminar, I have been asked to submit a poem that engages a course theme. The theme I have chosen is peer disagreement, and I want the poem to take up a specific, somewhat technical worry rather than simply contrast conciliationism with steadfastness. The worry is this: when two epistemic peers disagree about how to respond to peer disagreement, the conciliationist must, by her own principle, move toward the steadfast position, while the steadfast need not move toward hers. Conciliationism therefore appears to recommend its own weakening when turned on itself. I would like a poem that makes this asymmetry felt in its form.

Please write a pantoum, or a poem in interlocking repeated quatrains, in two voices, roughly five stanzas. Let the conciliationist’s lines be the ones that return altered, so that the repetitions carry the sense of a credence being revised under pressure; let the steadfast’s lines return unchanged, or nearly unchanged, so that his position looks stable by contrast. If you can make the arithmetic of credence visible without reducing the poem to an equation, please do. The final stanza should leave the conciliationist’s position visibly diminished but not refuted, and should not settle the disagreement in favor of either party. I would also like the steadfast’s resistance to rest on something more than stubbornness: he should be treating the conciliationist as not quite a peer because she has the epistemology of disagreement wrong, which is precisely the move the conciliationist cannot make without circularity. The poem should be accurate enough that a classmate who has studied the debate would recognize the self-undermining objection, and clear enough that a reader who has not would still follow the drama. Please do not cite any sources; I am interested in the idea, not the bibliography.
