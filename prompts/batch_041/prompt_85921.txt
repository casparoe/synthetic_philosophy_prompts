I am a high school student, and next week I have to run the student-led session in our philosophy elective — sixteen students, one forty-five-minute period, and nobody prepares outside class except me. I want to build the whole session around one idea from Daniel Dennett that our course has not touched, the one about what he calls free-floating rationales. My understanding is that a system can be correctly described as doing something for a reason, so that the reason does real explanatory and predictive work, even when nothing inside the system represents that reason and no mind anywhere holds it. The shape of a shell can be said to be for resisting predators, and that is a true explanation rather than a metaphor, yet the shell has no view about it and nobody chose it. What I want my classmates to reach is that the rationale is real while the reasoner is absent: whether there is a reason and who, if anyone, has it are two separate questions, and the second one can come back empty. They need to arrive at that themselves rather than hear me announce it, which means the activity has to stay mechanical at the level of the participants.

Please design the session for me, working as someone who knows this literature well and has built teaching games before. Think carefully about the mechanics before you commit to anything, because the obvious designs end up telling the students the answer. My one hard constraint is that the rules must be purely mechanical: each participant follows a fixed rule they can execute without understanding why, and whatever is interesting has to be produced by the rules rather than intended by anyone. I want a moment where the group is asked to state the reason for what just happened and writes down a genuinely good reason that belongs to no one. If you can add a later phase in which a small change in the environment makes the same mechanical rules produce something plainly stupid, so that the rationale which looked like wisdom now looks like a trap, please do: the point is that the rationale was never in the participants, which is exactly why it can come loose from the situation it was fitted to. I would also like one moment where the tempting answer to who had the reason is the group as a whole, so that they can see that answer is the same mistake one level up.
