I am a software engineer at a mid-sized telehealth company, and I have been asked to write the specification for a consent flow that will govern whether patients' session notes can be used to train and evaluate an internal language model. I have no formal background in philosophy, and I do not want a legal checklist or a reading list; I want a reusable decision procedure that my team and I can run whenever we are about to ship a feature that depends on someone's consent. The decisions recur at least monthly: a new data use, a new research partner, a change to an existing policy, a request to re-use data for a purpose nobody contemplated when it was collected. The stakes are high, the users are often distressed or unwell, and the people who sign off are a product manager, a privacy counsel who is not always available, and me. I need the procedure to be honest about which steps are genuine tests and which are judgment calls that cannot be eliminated.

Please ground the procedure in what the philosophy of consent actually says, rather than in compliance folklore, and translate it into something a practitioner without the theory can follow. I would like the procedure to run as a sequence of questions, with a clear account of what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the sequence terminates in a no, and where it must be handed to a human because the question is about values rather than facts. It should treat scope, specificity, time, and revocability as first-class, not as afterthoughts, and it should distinguish deception, coercion, and the subtler case of manipulation or undue influence, including the difference between a threat and an offer that merely makes refusal costly. I recently came across the idea that consent is a normative power, in the sense that a valid consent changes what others are permitted to do rather than merely recording a preference, and I want the procedure to take that seriously: if the act would be wrong without the consent, the procedure should test whether the consent actually changes the permission, not just whether the user clicked. It should also say how to handle competence when the person is an adult patient with a fluctuating condition, and when the person is an employee or a contractor whose livelihood may depend on saying yes.

Then apply the procedure to at least three concrete cases: a modal that treats continued use of the app as consent to train models on de-identified session notes; a research partnership in which patients are approached in a waiting room and told that declining may affect their standing in a care program; and a one-time consent to future research that is deliberately broad. For each, tell me what the procedure says, where it is uncertain, and what evidence would move the decision. I also want one case where the procedure yields an answer I will not like, and a candid account of what the procedure cannot settle. Please keep it operational: the output should be something I can paste into a design doc and defend in a review, not an essay about autonomy.
