Ok, so context: I'm a novelist — literary fiction, my last book was about a hospice nurse and it did okay — and as research for the one I'm writing now (it's about a woman who becomes convinced that some kinds of suffering can never be paid back, no matter how much good you pile on top of them) I've talked a philosopher into doing a recorded conversation with me at a small festival next month. She's a negative utilitarian of a particular stripe; she calls her position a "lexical threshold" view. Roughly what she holds: pleasure and pain ARE comparable up to a point — a bad headache is worth a nice afternoon, a rotten week is worth a good month, you can do the arithmetic down there — but past some level of suffering the scale breaks and no amount of pleasure, not a lifetime of it, not a galaxy of it, can make that suffering worth it. She thinks that's why the whole tired debate over whether pleasure and pain are "commensurable" is badly posed: the answer isn't yes or no, it's yes in one region and no in another.

I don't want softballs, I want questions that actually dig into the guts of this. So: you're a philosopher who works on value theory and population ethics, and you're also a good interviewer. Think carefully before you write anything, and start by brainstorming like fifteen candidate questions in a scratch list, then throw out the weak ones and give me your best eight to ten, in the order you'd ask them.

I want each of the following covered, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give:

1. The threshold itself. Where is it, exactly? Sharp line or fuzzy one — and if it's fuzzy, doesn't she just recreate the sorites problem she's supposedly solving? Also, is the threshold a threshold of intensity, of duration, or of kind (only certain flavours of suffering — torture, grief, a certain kind of depression — count as the "lexical" ones)?
2. The word "lexical." I have a half-memory that lexical just means non-Archimedean, i.e. that no finite amount of the lower-ranked stuff ever equals one unit of the higher-ranked stuff, and that it does NOT require anyone to be infinitely valuable. Make her say which she means, and press her on whether that distinction is doing the work she needs or is just hiding the real problem.
3. Is this actually just very steep prioritarianism wearing a costume? What does the lexical claim buy her that a steep-but-continuous curve can't? Give her the obvious reply and then have the next question ready.
4. Within a single life versus across lives. Is the threshold a fact about how one person's good and bad add up, or about how we compare different people's lives? Does summing across lives even make sense here, and does her view imply anything ugly about whether it's good or bad to bring new people into existence?
5. Mixed cases. A life containing terrible suffering but also enormous joy and meaning — does she really want to say it can't have been worth living? Put the person in front of her who says "I'd do it all again," and don't let her wriggle out with "that person is deluded."
6. The practical teeth. If a speck of above-threshold suffering is lexically bad, does that make almost anything that risks it impermissible — does she end up paralysed, or committed to overriding people's autonomy to stamp it out? And how does she stop her view from being unable to condemn a world of mild grinding misery just because it sits below the line?

One more thing: she's a bit of a deflector. Whenever anyone presses her on where the threshold actually is, she says something like "that's a question for psychology, not philosophy" or "the view doesn't need a number." I want the sequence to make that deflection harder to use each time, and I want to end on the single question where she can't use it at all.

No "what drew you to philosophy" filler, no "tell us about your book." It's for a literary audience but don't dumb it down. And don't bother with citations or reading lists, I don't want them and I won't use them.
