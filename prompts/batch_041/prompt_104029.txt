You are a philosopher of art and of the emotions, with a specialist's grasp of the paradox of tragedy — the puzzle of why we seek out, return to, and value artworks that make us sad, frightened, or otherwise pained. I want your help working out what principle, if any, lies behind my own reactions to painful art. Begin by brainstorming the main families of solution that have been proposed for the paradox — the idea that the emotions involved are make-believe rather than real, catharsis and discharge, second-order or meta-responses to one's own emotion, the thought that painful parts are compensated by other values in the whole, and the role of control, safety, and the knowledge that one can stop — but do not merely report them. I care about which of them, or which repair of them, actually fits the verdicts below. Be precise, take the cases one at a time, and be willing to tell me that a verdict of mine has to go.

Here are my verdicts.

A film in which a young protagonist dies of an illness: I sob, and I count the evening among the best of my year; I press it on friends. A documentary about a real famine: I feel ill, switch it off, and feel I have been made to witness something I had no right to look at; I would not call it good. A tragedy in which the hero destroys himself through his own flaw: I find it exhilarating, almost pleasurable. A wordless sad song: I love it, and I cannot say what it is about. A tear-jerker built to wring out tears — a manipulative film, or a charity advertisement with swelling strings: I feel used, and I dislike it. A tragedy in which a child dies: I cannot enjoy it, though I think the work is great. A sad ending that is true to the character: I prefer it to a happy ending, even though I want the character to be happy. A sad ending that arrives from nowhere, punishing a character for no reason: I feel cheated. Watching a friend actually grieve: no enjoyment at all, and no sense in which it is good. Re-watching the film from my first case, knowing how it ends: I am moved as much or more.

My tentative principle is this. I enjoy a sadness in art when the sadness is earned — when it is the fitting response to what the work presents, and the work is not fishing for it — and I do not enjoy it when the emotion is engineered, misplaced, or aimed at a real object that demands action rather than contemplation. Test this against the cases, repair it where it fails, and if it cannot be repaired without turning ad hoc, give me the simpler principle that fits best and say which verdict it overturns.

Deliverables. First, one candidate principle, or at most three, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to new cases myself. Second, for each clause, the case that forces it. Third, the principle's verdicts on these cases I have not yet considered: a tragedy in which innocents suffer horribly but the work is very beautiful; a sad film about a real historical atrocity; a sad song I love until I learn it is about the singer's own dead child; a video game in which I deliberately choose the sad ending; grief for a fictional villain; and a sad film I admire but never want to see again. Fourth, an explicit list of my verdicts that the principle cannot accommodate. Fifth, a short, honest statement of what the principle cannot do — in particular, where the distinction between enjoying a work, valuing it, and merely being moved by it collapses, and where you are making a judgment call rather than deriving an answer. Think carefully, and flag any point at which the paradox itself looks like an artifact of a confusion in my verdicts rather than a genuine puzzle.
