I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or law, and next month I have been asked to give a short talk at a law school's one-day workshop on rules and self-governance. I was invited because of a rule-change system I designed for an online community I moderate, which is a descendant of Nomic: a game in which the rules, including the rules for changing the rules, are themselves in play and can be amended during play. I need you to write my Q&A prep sheet.

My thesis is that the so-called paradoxes of self-amendment — a rule that repeals the rule permitting repeal, a rule that entrenches itself against amendment, a rule that forbids all amendment and is then amended — are not paradoxes at all, and that they arise only from a particular design choice. In my variant, every amendment must name the rule currently in force that authorizes it; an amendment takes effect only at the next turn boundary, never during the turn in which it is enacted; and the rule governing when amendments take effect may itself be changed only through that same staged process. Under these three constraints, I claim, no rule is ever applied to its own enactment, and everything that looks like self-referential paradox is a violation of staging. I also distinguish a rule that changes another rule at the same level from a rule that changes which level is authoritative, and I claim that only the second kind generates the paradoxes, so my design simply prohibits it. What is left over, I argue, is not a problem of logic but a problem of authority: any such system needs some procedure that settles whether an enactment was authorized, and that procedure is not itself derived from the rules.

The audience is mostly constitutional-law academics, with two legal philosophers and a couple of people who study how online communities govern themselves. They treat amendment as an exercise of authority rather than as a logical operation, they are suspicious of importing computational metaphors into law, several of them work on entrenched clauses and on whether an amendment can lawfully destroy the amendment procedure itself, and they will care far more about who decides than about what follows from what.

Give me the five objections this room will actually raise — not the ones I get from my gaming group — ordered by how much damage they would do to the talk, each phrased the way one of these people would phrase it, each with my reply and a note on where the reply is soft. The fifth and last item should be the objection I am hoping nobody asks, wherever it falls in that ranking. Commit to a position: tell me which of these objections have real force and which reply I should hold rather than hedge, instead of laying out my options for me to choose from. Write it as a numbered list of exactly five items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion.
