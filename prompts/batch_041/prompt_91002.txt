I'm taking a philosophy of mind seminar and we spent two weeks on the Turing test — the imitation game, the standard objections, all the usual stuff — and for my final essay I want to write about a modified version that a friend in the class came up with, because the normal version feels way too easy to me now. Could you basically write that essay for me? American English, please. Here's her test, she calls it the continuity test: you interrogate the system over text for as long as you want, no time limit, no fixed script, ask it anything. But at some point during the session, without telling you, the people running it are allowed to do exactly one of three things behind the curtain — swap in a completely different system, restart the same system so it keeps nothing from the conversation so far, or just leave it alone. Your job isn't to guess machine vs. human. Your job is to say, at the end, whether the thing you were talking to stayed the same entity the whole time, and if it changed, roughly when. She thinks this is a better behavioral test than the classic one, because a mind isn't just a stream of plausible replies, it's a single someone having them, and that's supposed to show up as your ability to notice when the someone is gone. She also lets the experimenters swap in a different human as one of the options, which I think makes the whole thing messier, because two different people can be similar enough that you'd never clock the change.

Honestly I've gone back and forth on this alot. It seems like you could pass or fail this for reasons that have nothing to do with having a mind, and "stayed the same entity" sounds like exactly the kind of thing you're inferring from behavior, which is the move the classic test already got criticized for. But maybe that criticism cuts the other way, and the continuity test is what actually exposes it.

So: write the essay, weigh the arguments, and land somewhere — I don't want a wishy-washy "both sides have a point" ending, I want a verdict. Tell me whether the continuity test is a genuine improvement on the behavioral approach, just a disguised version of it, or something that shows the behavioral approach can't reach what actually matters, and defend whichever you pick. Somewhere in there deal with the obvious dodge that a good enough system could fake continuity by keeping a running summary of the conversation and a consistent persona, and say whether that faking counts as passing the test or as the test falling apart. And be concrete: name at least one kind of system that would pass the classic test but flunk the continuity test (or the reverse), so I can see what the disagreement actually cashes out to.
