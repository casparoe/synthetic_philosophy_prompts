I'm writing a paper on karma as a theory of moral desert and I need the thought experiment for it — the case itself, built to spec, not an argument about karma.

The version of the view I'm working with: a wrong act causes suffering later, sometimes in a later life, and the suffering is deserved because its the ripening of that act. Wrongdoer and sufferer are linked by a causal chain that runs through death, but needn't share a body, memories, or character. Grant all that as the setup, I'm not asking you to defend it.

The case has to isolate one variable: how much of the person has to survive for the desert to hold. Everything else stays fixed — the wrong is stipulated to be serious, the suffering stipulated to be roughly proportionate, and the causal link stipulated to be real. Only the degree of psychological continuity between the one who did it and the one who suffers it changes.

So: one mundane scenario (present day, kitchen-table scale — a chronic illness, a business going under, whatever; no afterlife scenes, no cosmic courtroom), in three versions. V1: the sufferer remembers doing it and is recognisably the same character. V2: no memory of it, but the same traits and dispositions that produced the act are still there. V3: no memory, and in character the opposite — someone whose whole life has been spent resisting exactly that kind of act.

Then the check, version by version: what the karma view says and why; what a retributivist says, run twice, once on a psychological-continuity account of personal identity and once on an account where some bare subject persists regardless of memory or character; and what a straight consequentialist says, including whether the desert question is even live for them.

Then three closing things. First, which feature of the case is actually doing the work — if I made the wrong trivial, or the suffering wildly out of proportion, would the interesting divergence survive? Second, what happens when I turn the dial all the way down: the ripening lands on someone with no continuity at all, a stranger, or a whole community. Third, the obvious defence — someone says the desert attaches to the life-stream rather than the person, or goes further and says there's no enduring person there at all, just a causal process. If they take either line, does the case still bite, and does karma still count as a theory of desert rather than a theory of causation with moral decoration?

One constraint I care about: the verdicts have to come out of the theories, not out of me stipulating them. If I've rigged it so V3 just is the case where retributivism says no, tell me, and tell me how to un-rig it. And keep each version short enough to read out loud in a seminar.
