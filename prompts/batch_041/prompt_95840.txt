I have an argument I assembled for a reading group on induction, and I have reached the point where I no longer trust my own handling of it. The setting is deliberately small, so that nothing hidden is doing the work.

The game: nature produces an infinite binary sequence, one bit at a time. A strategy is any function from finite binary strings to {0, 1} — told the bits so far, it names the next bit. There are no computational limits, no costs, and a strategy may be arbitrarily complicated. Let U be the distribution on sequences under which each bit is an independent fair coin toss. Then:

(1) For every strategy s and every n, the expected number of correct predictions s makes among the first n bits, under U, is exactly n/2. This is a two-line calculation rather than a deep theorem: for any history h, exactly half of the ways the sequence can continue have 0 next and half have 1, so whatever s says at h, its chance of being right there is 1/2. I have also checked that randomizing does not help, so please do not tell me the repair lies there.

(2) Hence under U no strategy outperforms any other — not even the constant strategy that always says 0.

(3) Hence no strategy is more accurate than any other, and the apparent superiority of methods that extrapolate regularities — projecting a trend, fitting a curve, trusting a physical law — is not a fact about those methods.

(4) What separates a method that succeeds from one that fails is therefore the cooperation of the world and nothing else. A method's track record records the world's cooperation, not the method's merit.

(5) And the only thing that could justify an inductive method is its track record — its having worked so far.

(6) Therefore no inductive method is justified. And by (2), none is preferable to any other: the strategy that always says 0 is exactly as well supported as the method behind any working science, and when the stakes are high there is no reason to prefer one to the other.

(7) But (6) is not something anyone can act on. I would stake my life on the science-backed prediction and not on the always-0 rule, and I take that preference to be rational rather than a mere habit.

Someone told me this is a miniature version of the no-free-lunch theorems, which is why I cannot dismiss it as a trick. A friend says the trouble is that (1) is a statement about an average over all possible sequences, and that the actual world is not the average of all possible worlds. To me that sounds like a slogan rather than a diagnosis.

Where does the argument actually break — which numbered step do I reject — and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather keep (1), which I can prove on the spot, and I would rather keep (7), which I take to be non-negotiable. Name the step, explain why it looked compelling, and price at least two candidate repairs against each other: if I give up (5) and concede that induction has no justification of the kind I was asking for, what is left of my confidence in science, and is that concession merely Hume's conclusion restated? If instead I keep (5) and deny that (1) bears on it, precisely which inference from (1) to (3) or to (4) is invalid, and by what principle — bearing in mind that (2), read as it is written, looks true? I would also like to know what the residual problem of induction looks like once the argument is repaired, whether anything of the original worry survives, and whether the repair can be stated without appealing to the world being regular, since that is the very thing in dispute. Use only what is on the table: do not hand me further theorems I have not stated.
