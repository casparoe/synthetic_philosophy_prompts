The question is whether it makes sense to say a large language model believes anything — that it believes Paris is the capital of France, say, or that some step in a proof is valid, when its outputs and internal goings-on reliably track the fact. I've met the dispute from two directions. One side is Daniel Dennett's idea of the intentional stance: to attribute a belief is to adopt a predictive strategy that earns its keep when it lets you anticipate what a thing will do, and the move is just as legitimate for a thermostat, a frog, or a chatbot, because what matters is whether the pattern is real and useful, not whether there's a private inner something behind it. The other side says a belief isn't a stance but a state with a specific job — it has to be formed, revised, and deployed in the right way, wired into other states and into action — and that a system whose "beliefs" are produced by the very process that produces the sentences reporting them has nothing doing that job. Both sides seem to grant the same facts about behaviour and architecture, and yet nobody has moved in decades; the exchange once had about thermostats and chess programs is now being had about chatbots, word for word.

My hypothesis is that the camps aren't really disagreeing about minds but about what an explanation is supposed to deliver: one side wants a compact predictive instrument, the other wants a story about what job a state does inside the system, and no fact about the model can arbitrate between demands like those. Is that right, or is there a real empirical or conceptual question underneath — something about the difference between a state that causes a report and one that merely accompanies it, or about what makes an inner state the particular state it is?
