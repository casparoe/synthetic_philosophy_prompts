A bit of background: I did thirty-odd years in geology, retired, and then got talked into sitting on the board of a small coastal field station -- they wanted a scientist on it and apparently a retired one counts. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy, mostly ethics, and now I see these problems everywhere, which my wife says is an occupational hazard of the hobby.

The board is deadlocked and I have to circulate a written opinion before the comittee meets on the 14th, so I'm asking for help drafting it.

The case: we manage a seabird island. Invasive rats got ashore in the nineties and the colony is down to about a fifth of its historic size. Our ecologists want a single aerial bait drop in the autumn window -- standard approach, and it genuinely works. Then it gets complicated. Two of our younger staff, both good field biologists and not cranks, have come out against it on grounds that have nothing to do with whether it works. Their position is that the rats are sentient individuals and that killing tens of thousands of them to protect a bird colony isn't obviously a good trade; they'd rather we spent the money on trapping across several seasons, which kills fewer animals and kills them more humanely, even though it's slower and dearer. And the fishing families who've used that bay for generations don't care about either argument. Their worry is bait getting into the intertidal and into what they harvest, and their real complaint is that we have never once asked them anything.

The vote is 4-4 with one recusal (her husband works for the agency that issues the permit), so a tie means we do nothing, which means the birds keep going down. A mediator has floated what she calls the two-track plan: no aerial bait anywhere in the bay's catchment, ever; trapping and hand-dispatch on the headland for three seasons; and the fishing families get a seat in the co-management group plus the monitoring contract. The ecologists get their threshold (colony stabilised within five years), the welfare people get a method they can live with, the locals get the bay and the respect.

My problem is philosophical and I don't have the vocabulary for it. Everyone keeps saying this is a win-win, that each side is giving up what it cares less about to get what it cares more about, and that's fine as far as it goes, but I keep wondering whether that's a real argument or just a trade analogy borrowed from economics and dressed up. Can you trade away something that isn't a preference at all -- that's just what you think is true about what we owe a rat -- for something on the other side of the ledger? And I'm worried about a version of the worst-of-both-worlds objection: the trapping plan kills fewer animals per year, but it may drag on for years and could end up killing more overall, and a failed partial attempt leaves a bait-shy population that is much harder to clear later. So the compromise might be worse for the animals than the very thing the animal people were objecting to.

What I need: a short written opinion I can circulate and that the board can act on. One reccomendation, with the reasoning, plus a time frame -- if we adopt the two-track plan, when do we pull the plug and go back to the aerial drop, and who decides. Don't just lay out the options, pick one. And please take the strongest objection seriously rather than swatting it away; I think it's the one that says some things simply aren't the kind of thing you can put into a trade, and if that's right then the whole win-win framing is a way of not facing the disagreement. I'd also like your view on something procedural: the chair wants a memo everyone can vote for, and I can't decide whether that means I should write it as if the deal is already agreed, or as a proposal with the dissent spelled out. If I set out the dissenters' reasons in full, does that strengthen the compromise or blow it up? Two pages max -- the board reads nothing longer.
