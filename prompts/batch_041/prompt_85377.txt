I'm a poet, and I've been asked to open a small interdisciplinary symposium on the Anthropocene with a short poem I'll read aloud. The invitation grew out of an argument I keep having with a friend on the stratigraphy side of things: I said the 2024 rejection of the proposed epoch settles nothing about whether we're in one, and he said it settles exactly what a geological period is, and we've been circling each other since.

Write that poem. Twenty to thirty lines, loosely metered, no fixed rhyme, but with one hard constraint: it must carry a recurring refrain line that stays verbatim except for the date inside it, and that date must change each time the line returns — drawing on at least three of the start dates that have genuinely been proposed, from the spread of farming, through the early modern dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide that some connect to reforestation after the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas, through industrialization, to the mid-twentieth-century radionuclide signal. The refrain's stability against its shifting date should be the poem's engine: the form holds still while the content is exactly what's being fought over. One stanza should earn its place by naming what a candidate site has to be — a lake bottom that never mixes, a section continuous and undisturbed and open to anyone who wants to sample it — and the poem should stage the act of deciding: a vote, a marker driven into rock, a body of people authorized to point at a place and call it the beginning. End with the boundary officially rejected and the epoch, in some other sense, not. Don't resolve whether it's real.

After the poem, add a short prose note: where you took liberties with the stratigraphic debate, which contested reading of it you committed to, and what would have moved you to a different formal choice — if, say, you came to think the dispute is largely verbal, or that a single global boundary was the wrong instrument from the start.
