Last night I spent the better part of two hours arguing with a friend about whether a trait's function is fixed by its history — by what it was selected for — or by what it currently does for the system it sits in, and we kept reaching for the same tired examples without either of us managing to make the disagreement come apart cleanly. I would like you to build the case that would have settled it, or at least exposed precisely where the two views divide.

Please construct a single, realistic biological case in which the etiological (selected-effects) account of function and the causal-role (Cummins-style) account return opposite verdicts on one and the same question: whether a given trait has a function at all. I want the disagreement to run in the direction I find harder to stage — the causal-role account should affirm a function and the etiological account should deny one — and I want the case to be hard for the etiological account in a particular way: not the easy version, in which the trait has simply never been shaped by any selection process, but a version in which a selection process has unmistakably shaped the trait, so that the only live question is whether that process is of the right kind. The trait must make a robust, non-accidental contribution to a capacity of the system that contains it; the one thing it lacks is the right sort of selection history.

Constraints:

- It must be a real, recognised kind of biological situation, not a fable: no swampman, no Twin Earth, no invented organisms, and no appeals to what a designer intended.
- Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph, and do not lean on the stock examples (the heart, the appendix, the eye, the peacock's tail).
- Present the case so that the two versions — the one where the accounts agree and the one where they pull apart — differ in exactly one feature, and identify that feature explicitly.

Then do the following. For each account, state the verdict it returns and the precise clause of the account that generates it. Say which feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to both verdicts if you remove it — for instance, by supplying the missing selection story, or by taking it away. Finally, put the case to the etiological theorist's most credible defender: how would she resist it — by denying the function, or by widening what counts as selection — and what would that move cost her elsewhere, that is, what else would then have to count as a function that she would rather it did not?

Please write your answer in British English.
