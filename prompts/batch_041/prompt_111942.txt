For the past several months I have been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group, and we keep circling back to the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, which, as you likely recall, runs roughly as follows: because simultaneity is relative to a frame of reference, and because for any event one cares to name there is some observer for whom that event is happening now, every event lies in some observer's present; since no frame is privileged over any other, all events are equally real, there is no objective present, and we should accept the block universe. I find the argument both seductive and slippery, and a presentist friend of mine has begun developing a reply she calls "temporal perspectivalism," which I would like you to help me assess by setting it directly beside the original argument.

Her claim is that the Rietdijk–Putnam argument at most establishes an indexical fact and never a metaphysical one. "Now," she says, is like "here": which place counts as "here" depends on where the speaker stands, and no one concludes from the relativity of "here" that all places are equally real or that spatial location is an illusion; "here" is not the name of a privileged property of a location but a token-reflexive device that picks out different locations on different occasions. By parity, she argues, the Rietdijk–Putnam argument shows only that "now" is token-reflexive and frame-relative, which a presentist can happily grant, while denying that any of this makes past and future events exist in the way present ones do. What I want from you is a careful comparison of the two positions rather than a verdict on who wins the wider dispute.

Specifically: is temporal perspectivalism a genuine alternative to the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, or merely a re-description of it dressed in linguistic clothing? Does the analogy between "now" and "here" hold all the way down, or does it break at some identifiable joint — for instance over whether simultaneity relations are objective or a matter of convention, or over what work the word "real" is doing when the eternalist deploys it? If the analogy does break, exactly where does it break, and which of the two positions does that break favor? Please also say how each position would characterize the other's central move (would the eternalist call hers a verbal dodge, and would she call his a conflation of indexicality with ontology?), and close by indicating which of the two, if either, you think is genuinely entitled to its conclusion.
