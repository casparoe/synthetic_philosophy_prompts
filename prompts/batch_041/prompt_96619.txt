Having spent my career in the natural sciences and only recently turned to philosophy of mind, I am trying to calibrate how much of what I encounter in popular venues I should trust. A former colleague in cognitive science recently sent me the following précis of a methodological proposal he found intriguing. I have not read the original paper, and I would like your help in assessing the précis rather than the proposal as such.

The précis reads:

Heterophenomenology is the method of studying consciousness by treating everything a subject says as fiction. The investigator collects the subject’s reports, brackets the question of whether they are true, and constructs a heterophenomenological world — the world as the subject believes it to be. Because the method never assumes that reports correspond to real inner experiences, it is a form of behaviorism: consciousness is identified with the disposition to make certain reports. This is why heterophenomenology is often called the eliminativist approach to consciousness. A recent extension applies the method to large language models: since their outputs are just text, we can treat them as heterophenomenological subjects without worrying about whether they are conscious. The extension’s proponents claim this shows that the hard problem of consciousness is a pseudo-problem, because once we have the heterophenomenological world of a system, there is nothing left to explain.

How much of this is right? Please go sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher working on consciousness would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. I am particularly interested in whether the précis’s conclusion — that the extension to language models shows the hard problem to be a pseudo-problem — follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it is an overreach. I would also like to know what, if anything, the heterophenomenological method actually commits one to regarding the existence or nature of consciousness, and whether treating a language model as a subject in this sense is as straightforward as the précis suggests.
