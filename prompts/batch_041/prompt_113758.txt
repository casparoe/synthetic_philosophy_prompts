I'm a science and ideas journalist and I'm trying to write a long feature about how different countries handle sex work, but I keep hitting a wall that I'm pretty sure is a philosophy problem, and I'd definately rather not fudge it. Three regimes.

Sweden — and now France, Ireland, Norway, Iceland: selling is legal, buying is a crime. She's a competent adult the state refuses to punish, but nobody may legally help her: employ her, share a premises with her, arrange a booking, and that's procurement. The state also funds exit programmes.

Germany and the Netherlands: legal and licensed. German sex workers have to register with the authorities, and the law bans sex without a condom; Dutch municipalities license the brothels and the windows, and the whole justification was that bringing the trade above ground would make exploitation easier to police.

New Zealand: decriminalised outright and folded into ordinary law, so the job comes with health and safety duties and access to social security like any other. Yet the same Act keeps the advertising ban (classifieds only), refuses visas to anyone who plans to work in the industry, and its own statement of purpose says that decriminalising is not the same as morally endorsing it.

What I want is the implicit philosophy of each — what theory of consent, harm, exploitation, commodification and the proper reach of the criminal law each one only makes sense under. What theory of consent does Sweden need? If the seller can consent, who exactly is the victim of the crime the buyer commits, and how can there be an offence that the supposed victim can't waive — or does punishing the buyer presuppose something else instead, legal moralism, or a harm that belongs to women in general rather than to her? Does the German registration-and-condoms regime presuppose that this is work, or that it's a public health hazard wearing the costume of work? Is New Zealand's not-endorsing clause a coherent position about law and morality, or just a way of decriminalising while keeping the stigma? And is the immigration bar in that same Act evidence of an unstated limit to the labour model — citizens only?

Then the bit I actually need for the piece: for each regime, seperate the commitments the practice strictly requires from the ones it's merely compatible with. My suspicion is that alot of people defend these laws with theories the laws don't need — you could run the Nordic law on several different moral stories, and the same goes for legalisation. So where is each practice underdetermined? And finally, what would have to change on the ground if you swapped the theory out? If you really took New Zealand's labour model seriously, what would Sweden have to give up? And what would a genuine work-based model have to say about the fact that we don't register and license the customers of every other exploited worker — nobody has to sign a form with the state to buy a cheap t-shirt made in a firetrap factory.

No reading list, I just need the argument. And be straight with me about where these laws might not be consistent with themselves at all.
