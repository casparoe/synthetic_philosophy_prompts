My friend Rosalind's first book comes out next month — it's a popular account of the many-worlds interpertation of quantum mechanics, and the whole thing is really one long defence of the claim that people who worry about where the probabilities come from in that picture are worrying about nothing. I'm giving the launch speech at her local bookshop, and the room will be split right down the middle: half of them are folk from our old physics department who know exactly which arguments she's leaning on, and the other half are her family and the choir she sings with, who will glaze over the second anyone says the word amplitude. I want to celebrate her properly — the book is genuinely good, and funnier than it has any right to be — but I've never managed to swallow the central move. Her line is that the probabilities were never in the physics in the first place, that they're just facts about how a rational person ought to bet when she doesn't know which branch she's about to find herself in, so the derivation is fine provided you stop asking the wavefunction to hand you a number. My objection, which I can never state cleanly after a glass of wine, is that this seems to conjure the numbers out of nowhere: if the argument works, it ought to work for any old story about splitting, and then we'd never need the amplitudes at all — but we obviously do, we always get the same squared numbers out, so something physical is doing real work and I can't see where she's paid for it. There's also a bit near the end where she says no experiment could ever count against the theory, and she means that as a selling point, which I find quietly alarming. Anyway, I'm not going to stand up and accuse her of circularity in front of her mum.

Could you write the speech for me? Keep it short — the shop is tiny and everyone will be standing — and warm, and funny where it can be, with no equations and no jargon in the speech itself, because the choir half will switch off otherwise. It has to land for both halves of the room, but the disagreement needs to actually be in there and actually be fair, and it needs to be obvious that I'm teasing someone I love rather than scoring points off her. Give it to me as exactly five numbered beats, each one or two sentences, with no throat-clearing at the start and no summing-up at the end.
