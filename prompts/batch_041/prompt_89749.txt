I teach a graduate seminar in contemporary distributive justice and I am drafting the 75-minute essay section of the final. My students can all recite the standard taxonomy — utilitarianism, the priority view, telic egalitarianism, sufficientarianism — and almost every one of them will reach for Parfit's leveling-down objection as a knockdown against egalitarianism and a vindication of prioritarianism. I want a question that makes the recitation useless and forces the structural work to be done in front of me.

I will put two things on the exam sheet. First, a pair of stipulations in my own words: call a distributive view loss-proof just in case it never ranks one outcome above another when the second is at least as good for every individual and strictly better for at least one; and call a view gap-sensitive just in case it treats the size of the distance between better- and worse-off people as something with intrinsic, not merely instrumental, value. Second, a case with two persons and three outcomes: (100, 50), (50, 50), (80, 60).

The question should make candidates do four things. (a) Argue that a view is loss-proof exactly when its ranking of outcomes is monotone in individual well-being, so that the leveling-down objection is not a debating point between camps but a constraint on which views can treat gaps as intrinsically valuable. (b) Sort the standard positions by that criterion, and say what the priority view's immunity costs it — specifically, whether a value function that is a sum of individual-level terms can explain why it matters that some people are worse off than others, as opposed to merely being at a lower absolute level. (c) Say what a gap-sensitive view must concede about the weight of its egalitarian component in order to remain loss-proof, and whether that concession leaves the component doing any work in the cases the objection is about. (d) Take a side on the dilemma I take to be the real crux: if egalitarians repair the damage by making equality's value conditional on its making someone better off, they either retain a gap-sensitive component and forfeit loss-proofness, or they do not, and it is then unclear what distinguishes them from prioritarians who care only about absolute levels. I want a candidate to commit to an answer to (d) and defend it, not to lay the options out side by side.

To keep the answers checkable, require the model answer to run at least one gap-sensitive value function of the form total well-being minus a weighted gap through the three outcomes, and to show that the verdict on leveling down flips as the weight rises — so that a grader can check the arithmetic and see that the objection cannot even be stated without a number.

Please write the question itself — self-contained, since the stipulations and the case appear on the sheet — together with a model answer of about 700 to 800 words and a marking scheme out of 25 marks. The scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make, assign each a mark value, and add a one-line note for each on how candidates typically get it wrong; it should also state explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer, which I expect to be whether the candidate identifies the monotonicity claim and commits on the dilemma, rather than whether the labels are handled correctly. No citations, reading lists, or attributions anywhere: the students have no library access during the exam, and the model answer should not lean on who said what.
