I constructed the following argument for a graduate seminar in medical ethics and now distrust it, because it appears to show that a competent adult can be permanently bound by a decision she has since repudiated — a conclusion I cannot accept.

(1) What ought to be done for a patient is determined by that patient's autonomous will.
(2) An advance directive executed while competent is an exercise of that will.
(3) A patient in advanced dementia who is now content, and who asks only for ice cream, is no longer competent, so her present preferences are not autonomous.
(4) Hence what ought to be done is what the earlier, competent self directed, even against the present self's wishes.
(5) Nothing in (1) through (4) depends on the later self being incompetent.
(6) So a competent adult could be bound by an earlier directive she now repudiates — say, a contract by which she asked to be prevented from drinking.
(7) But we are sure that a competent adult may always change her mind, so (6) is false.
(8) Therefore one of (1) through (5) is false, though each seems independently obvious.

I would sooner surrender (7) than (1) or (2), but I want to know what that surrender would cost. Identify the step or steps that should be rejected, explain why each looked compelling, and price the available repairs: which premise each repair sacrifices, what it does to the distinction between the earlier and the later self, and whether the resulting view can still honor ordinary advance directives at all. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
