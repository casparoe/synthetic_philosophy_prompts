For three years I have sat as one of two public members on the state board that reviews civil commitments at our four psychiatric hospitals, a seat I took in part because I cover psychiatry and the mind sciences for a metropolitan daily. The board is nominally advisory, but the hospitals treat our findings as binding, which is why the document I owe my colleagues this week matters more than most of what I publish.

On the fourteenth we vote on an amendment to our review standards, drafted by our counsel at the request of the clinical directors. It concerns the weight the board may give to a patient's refusal to accept his diagnosis. Under the amendment, when a patient declines medication or discharge planning, the board may treat his failure to recognize that he has a mental disorder — the draft calls this impaired insight — as affirmative evidence both that the disorder is present and that it substantially impairs his judgment. The directors' rationale is not cynical. A condition that distorts a person's self-assessment, they argue, cannot be confirmed or denied by that same self-assessment; and in discounting the denial we are not overriding autonomy, because what we are overriding is a symptom. Our counsel's memo observes the awkward consequence that a patient's agreement and his disagreement both end up confirming the diagnosis, and asks the board to say in writing why this is not circular. One member, who has been circulating Thomas Szasz and the argument that mental illness is a myth and psychiatry a branch of social control, wants us to strike the grave-disability prong entirely, on the ground that it licenses commitment for poverty and eccentricity.

The case forcing the issue is a thirty-four-year-old man, diagnosed with schizophrenia, picked up after a landlord found him living in his car. He has no history of violence. He can state the side effects of his medication more accurately than I can, and says he stopped taking it because of weight gain and sedation that left him unemployable. He says the diagnosis is a story told about him by people whose interest lies in controlling him. His treating psychiatrist testified that he lacks insight. Our advisory neuropsychologist sent a note on anosognosia — the neurological syndrome in which patients with right-hemisphere damage deny that a paralyzed limb is their own — arguing that unawareness of illness is a documented, brain-based fact that no commitment standard can wave away.

Write the board's opinion, to circulate on the tenth, recommending a concrete course of action: adopt the amendment, reject it, or adopt a narrowed version with a definition of impaired insight we could actually apply at the bedside. Give the reasoning we can defend to the clinical directors, to the disability-rights member, and to the patient's lawyer; take the strongest consideration on the side you rule against seriously enough that its proponents would recognize it; and be explicit about what the criterion would have to look like in order not to certify itself. I do not need a literature review or a reading list. I need the argument, at the length a busy board member will actually read.
