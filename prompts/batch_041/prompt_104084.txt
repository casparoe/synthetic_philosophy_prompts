I need a constructed case for a graduate handout on the problem of logical omniscience. The intended reader already accepts the standard idealizing closure principles — that a knower's knowledge is closed under known entailment, and that logical truths are known — and the case is meant to force the concession that something in that picture has to give. Because the point is to persuade rather than to illustrate, the verdicts must be generated by the case rather than built into it; a case that stipulates the outcome is worthless.

The case must involve two agents who are identical in everything the standard responses treat as epistemically relevant. They hold the same premises and the same evidence, with the same credences. They grasp every concept involved and are aware of every proposition in the relevant closure, the target proposition included. Each can execute any single inferential step correctly, and each accepts every step if it is laid out. Neither is confused, careless, or irrational. They differ in exactly one respect: agent A has actually run a short chain of reasoning that combines several of her premises, and agent B has not. Choose an ordinary situation — everyday practical reasoning or simple arithmetic, not a self-referential or specially engineered formula — and build the chain so that its length and difficulty are unremarkable. Agent B must have had ample time and no obstacle; the gap between the two must not be explicable by lack of time, lack of ability, lack of evidence, or any defect of rationality, but only by the fact that the inference has not been performed.

Two structural constraints. First, the gap must arise only at the level of multi-premise reasoning: single-premise closure must hold for both agents, and whatever principle your case puts pressure on must be one that combines two or more premises. Second, the scenario itself must be short, no more than a paragraph, with the analysis kept separate. Do not appeal to agents with unlimited computational power, infinitely many premises, or infinitely long formulas.

Then audit the case against the following four responses to the problem, giving each one's verdict on both agents and the reasoning behind it:

1. The restriction response: the closure principles are sound, but they govern only the fragment of the agent's vocabulary and commitments that she currently has in play; outside that fragment no closure is claimed.
2. The non-standard-worlds response: entailment does not imply accessibility, because the worlds an agent's beliefs admit include worlds at which logic itself fails.
3. The process response: knowledge and belief attach to procedures actually executed rather than to sets of propositions held, so what an agent knows depends on the reasoning she has run.
4. The deflationary response: there is no substantive difference in epistemic state between the two agents, and knowledge-talk is scorekeeping over what they would assent to and how they can be criticized.

For each response, say whether it can separate the two agents and why. For each that cannot, identify precisely which feature of your case blocks it, and whether the response could be amended to separate them without collapsing into one of the others. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the work, and say what happens to each of the four responses if that feature is removed — in particular, whether removing it collapses the case back into plain logical omniscience or into a case the response handles comfortably. State which closure principle your case refutes for agent B, and why agent A's knowledge is untouched by it. Finally, say how a defender of the response that fares worst would attack the case and whether the attack can be met, and close with what the case shows about whether logical omniscience is a problem about knowledge or about the idealization built into the semantics.
