I'm designing a seminar session that pairs Kurt Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five with the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for the block universe, and I want a reading that takes both the novel and the physics seriously rather than using one as wallpaper for the other. Billy Pilgrim becomes unstuck in time; the Tralfamadorians tell him that all moments are permanently laid out, that his death is merely one bad moment among many, and that the future is fixed and cannot be altered; and the two-word refrain that follows every death in the book has always struck me as the emotional register of a certain metaphysics of time. What I want is not a warm-up illustration of eternalism and not a celebration of the book's fatalism, but a genuine two-way reading: where the physics illuminates the novel, where the novel outruns or distorts the physics, and what the novel contributes to the debate that the argument alone cannot.

Start by reconstructing the Rietdijk–Putnam argument in the form the novel needs: what exactly is supposed to follow from the relativity of simultaneity, and what extra premise about the reality of distant events — or about "real for" behaving like "simultaneous with" — the inference quietly requires. Then ask how much of that the Tralfamadorian cosmology actually is. My own suspicion, which I'd like tested rather than confirmed, is that the book serves up a block universe with a fatalist moral bolted onto it, and that the two come apart: eternalism is an ontological thesis about what exists, whereas the claim that nothing can be changed is a thesis about agency, and the standard reply is that a block universe can contain deliberation, resistance, and grief as fully as anything else — they are simply more located events. Does the novel recognize the distinction, or does it trade on the conflation? I also want the disanalogies on the table: being "unstuck in time" is a narrative device that eternalism does not license, since a block universe gives no travel between its parts; and the Tralfamadorian fiction-within-the-fiction, in which all the clumps of text are meant to be taken in at once, spatializes time in a way that even a B-theoretic block does not — a block still has earlier-than and later-than relations, so it is an ordered structure and not a heap of simultaneous slabs.

Finally, tell me where the novel resists the reading and where it earns something the philosophy can't. The non-linear form, the Dresden material, and the moments when grief and guilt break through the metaphysics all seem to push against the consolation the Tralfamadorians offer, and I want a verdict on whether that consolation is a genuine consequence of the block universe or an evasion dressed as one — especially for a survivor of atrocity, for whom "everything is permanent, so nothing is lost" cuts both ways. Which scenes should I put in front of the group to make the stakes concrete, and does Vonnegut, in the end, vindicate the block universe, undermine it, or dramatize the cost of believing it?
