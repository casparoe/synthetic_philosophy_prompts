I run the weekly anomaly-triage rotation at a lab that does mechanistic interpretability on open-weight language models. Between ten and thirty odd behaviors get reported to us each week: a prompt that yields a confidently wrong date, an attention head that lights up on inputs it has no business caring about, a sparse-autoencoder feature that fires on things that look unrelated to each other. The reports come to us in pairs, and the first question on every pair is whether the two anomalies come from the same mechanism. Right now we settle it by argument, and the argument is usually about whose intuition is better. I want a procedure instead.

Give me the procedure, and make it decide the question rather than restate it. Specifically:

First, a criterion of same-mechanism precise enough to fail on some pairs. Build on the working vocabulary we already use rather than replacing it. We say two anomalies share a mechanism when the same difference-making variable, at a level of description fixed in advance, is responsible for both; we distinguish the numerically same circuit from the same kind of computation; and we hold that the level of description — weights, features, computation — is a choice we make and must justify by the claim we want to make downstream, not a fact we discover.

Second, an ordered procedure a rotating engineer can run in about thirty minutes per pair: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, where it stops with split, where it stops with merge, and where it hands the pair to a person because the question is about what we want our explanations to do rather than about the network. Build in that merging carries the burden of proof, since a wrong merge contaminates a claim we will later generalize and a wrong split costs a week of work. Restrict it to what we have on hand — the two prompts, the two outputs, activation patching (running one input with a hidden state borrowed from another), feature attributions, the logit lens — and no training runs. End with a verdict line we can paste into the ticket.

Third, run it on three pairs. (a) Two unrelated prompts that both elicit a wrong date, where patching shows the same mid-layer block is necessary for both. (b) A refusal flip and a sentiment flip under the same prefix, both loading on a single feature. (c) A pair we have already written up as one circuit, which I expect your procedure to split, and which I will not enjoy. Say what each verdict costs us.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle: which residual disagreements are about the network and which are about what counts as a satisfying explanation, and which step is the team lead's call rather than the engineer's. Keep the whole thing usable by people who have never read any philosophy of science and have no time to start.
