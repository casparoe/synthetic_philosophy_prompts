I spent my working life in a laboratory, and since retiring I have been reading moral and legal philosophy. I keep returning to a feature of American capital punishment that strikes me as a curious piece of engineering, and I would like to know what it presupposes.

A capital trial in the United States is bifurcated. The same jury first decides guilt under the ordinary standard of proof, beyond a reasonable doubt, and then, if it convicts, holds a second hearing at which it must choose between death and life imprisonment by weighing aggravating circumstances against mitigating ones — that the killing was especially cruel, say, or that the defendant would remain dangerous. The system then adds protections that ordinary convictions do not receive: in some states the appellate courts must review every death sentence and compare it with sentences imposed in comparable cases, and the executive may commute the sentence for any reason or none. In some jurisdictions a sentencing jury may also treat a lingering doubt about guilt as a reason for a life sentence, even though it has already found guilt beyond a reasonable doubt; and the federal courts have held that a convicted defendant who later produces new evidence of actual innocence has no claim to relief so long as his trial was otherwise free of constitutional error.

Here is what puzzles me. If the standard of proof is set by the balance of harms, and the harm of executing an innocent person is the gravest the system can inflict, then one would expect the threshold for conviction to rise whenever death is a possible sentence. It does not rise; the procedures multiply instead. The practice therefore seems to presuppose that the asymmetry of harms is not — perhaps cannot be — expressed in the standard of proof, and that accuracy is to be bought with process rather than with a stricter rule. I would like to know whether that is the right reading, and whether it is coherent. The same apparatus also seems to say two things at once about what is being punished: a system that withholds relief from a demonstrably innocent convict, yet lets a jury weigh lingering doubt, appears to hold both that legal guilt is all that matters and that it is not.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory of punishment and of proof that this practice requires. Separate what it strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates, and identify any parts that presuppose mutually inconsistent commitments — in particular, whether a retributive justification of the death penalty can treat the risk of error as anything more than a practical inconvenience, and whether the elaborate machinery of error reduction presupposes a consequentialist outlook that the retributive justification cannot supply. Tell me where the practice is underdetermined, so that several theories of punishment would license it equally well. Finally, say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced — for instance, by a decision-theoretic account that let the threshold for conviction vary with the severity of the sentence, or by a retributivism that measured punishment by desert alone and treated the risk of error as irrelevant to its justification.
