I am a philosophy professor, and this spring I have been asked to give the closing plenary at my university's teaching-and-learning symposium: roughly eighty faculty, most of them from the natural sciences and engineering, fifty minutes, one image at most, and an audience that has been promised a philosopher's view of education and is, I expect, braced to hear platitudes. The occasion has a live hook — the faculty senate is currently debating whether to replace our distribution requirements with a short list of measurable competencies — and I am expected to connect the talk to that. I intend to build the session around Whitehead: his warning about ideas that are received into the mind but never used, tested, or thrown into fresh combinations; his account of learning as a rhythm that moves through romance, precision, and generalization; and his insistence that the aim of education is not the accumulation of information but the formation of an active and connected intelligence. I do not want you to write the lecture. I want you to design how I should teach it.

First, the order of ideas: what has to be in place before what, and why — given that this audience has no philosophical background and a professional stake in the assumption that their own teaching is already sound. Second, the one case or example that should carry the entire session, together with what it must have and what it must lack in order to do that work for these particular listeners (a laboratory protocol? a problem set? the graduate student who passes every examination and understands nothing?). Third, the misconceptions they will bring in or generate as I go — I am anticipating at least these four: that the three stages are a restatement of ages-and-stages developmentalism, that romance means making the material entertaining, that precision means drilling, and that the whole thing amounts to an argument against teaching facts — and, for each, the exact point in the sequence at which I must head it off and the move that does it; tell me also if any one of those four is not in fact a misconception. Fourth, the question or task I should leave them with that would reveal whether the idea has genuinely landed rather than merely been applauded, and what a wrong answer to it would look like. Fifth, what I should deliberately refuse to attempt in fifty minutes, and how to refuse it without appearing to withhold something.

I know Whitehead's texts well enough; what I need from you is the architecture of the explanation, not a summary of his views.
