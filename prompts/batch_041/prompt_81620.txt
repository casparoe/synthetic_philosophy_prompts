I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of religion, and I'm asking for something kind of odd, so bear with me. Two of my friends in the department — both of them sharper than me, which is the annoying part — got into it at a bar last week over the stone paradox, and I've been chewing on the argument ever since. What I want from you is a dialogue between them, but the real version of the fight, not the version where one of them says "God can do anything" and the other one wins in two moves. The first friend is what I'd call an essentialist: omnipotence, on her view, is nature-relative — a being is omnipotent when nothing that a being of that sort could do is left undone, and "make a stone too heavy for an omnipotent being to lift" doesn't describe any action at all, it's a phrase with a false presupposition, like "the sound of the color blue." She doesn't think that's a dodge; she thinks it's the whole point, and she'll say we do this everywhere, that nobody calls a grandmaster weak because he can't castle his rook sideways. The second friend is a voluntarist: he thinks that's a shell game, that on her view "omnipotent" just means "can do whatever's doable," which is true of a rock, so the word has been quietly emptied out. He wants omnipotence to mean maximal power over everything that exists, and he's fine saying that some things we can state aren't things anyone can do — he just doesn't think you get to settle which by definition. They both know all the standard moves, so don't have them walk through the stone case like it's a revelation; have them fight about what work the solution is doing, and have each one actually answer the last thing the other said.

The reason I'm asking is that we came up with a case around 1am that neither of them had a canned answer for, and I definately want the dialogue to get there without resolving it too fast: can God make Himself not omnipotent? Not lose a fight, but lay the attribute down and be a finite being from then on? The voluntarist says yes — nothing about "a being who was omnipotent and then isn't" looks contradictory, we can describe it perfectly well, so by his own lights it's on the table, and if God can't do it then there's something He can't do, full stop. The essentialist says it was never on the table, because omnipotence is what God is rather than something God has, and "God stops being God" is exactly the kind of pseudo-task she's been talking about all night — she'll also point out that everyone agrees God can't sin or err and nobody counts that as a defect of power, so why is this one different? He'll come back that "can't stop being God" is a strange sort of freedom, that an inability you can never shed is the closest thing to a limit there is, and she'll say he's confusing power with independence. Let them also try out the older distinction between what God could do considered in Himself and what God has bound Himself to do — I suspect he'll say it just relocates the problem and she'll say it's the same distinction she's been making all along, but let them work that out. They keep circling back to whether God could make a second omnipotent being, which I think is the same puzzle in a hat, so they can touch on that too if it comes up naturally.

Two equally competent people, neither one a strawman, real back-and-forth rather than parallel monologues, somewhere around 1200 to 1500 words, and end it at a genuine standoff or with someone conceding a small point but not the whole thing. For what it's worth they're both compatibilists about free will and neither is personally religious, so please don't have either of them pull the "it's a mystery, let's pray on it" move.
