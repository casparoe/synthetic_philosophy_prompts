I have come to hold a view that most people treat as either unserious or sentimental, and I want to know what would count against it. The view: the transition to farming — taken in aggregate, across the regions where it occurred and over the millennia it took — was a net loss for human welfare. Not that any particular community chose badly, and not that farmers were miserable, but that the average person was worse off afterwards along the dimensions that matter, and that the transition persisted because it was a trap with no exit rather than because it was worth it.

Handle the defeaters in three registers, and keep them distinct. (1) Empirical: the case is usually built on skeletons, and skeletons are treacherous — a population that is sicker but survives long enough to develop chronic, visible lesions can leave a record that looks worse than one that dies quickly of acute causes and never leaves a mark. Anything you cite in either direction has to survive that asymmetry. (2) Conceptual: "worse off" needs a currency, and I am not assuming the candidates — calories, longevity, leisure, morbidity, autonomy, absence of hierarchy — agree with one another. If they do not, does the thesis dissolve into a comparison we have no way to make, or can it be stated without a single currency? Relatedly, the claim is about averages; if farming raised carrying capacity, the sum of welfare may have risen even as each life went worse, and whether that tells against me depends on a contested position in population ethics rather than on evidence. (3) Dialectical: is the thesis falsifiable as stated, or has it been quietly protected by never fixing which dimensions count?

So: what would count against the thesis — which findings, arguments, and conceptual or formal results should move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? Indicate which of them a committed defender would concede rather than dismiss. If the thesis turns out to be well insulated, say whether that is a fatal defect or a price worth paying.
