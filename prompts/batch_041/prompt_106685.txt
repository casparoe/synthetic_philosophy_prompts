I'm convening a reading group of eight non-economists — two biologists, a lawyer, three literature people, and a pair of lapsed economists — for a single ninety-minute session on Hume's essays on money and trade. What I need from you is a teaching plan, not an exposition of the material. The group will arrive with two firm convictions: that Hume is essentially an early quantity theorist, and that his case for free trade is the part worth their time. My aim is narrower than that. I want them to leave with a grip on his claim that an increase in the money supply can quicken industry during the interval before prices adjust, and on his argument that low interest rates owe more to frugality, industry, and the confidence of lenders than to any abundance of money. Plan the arc for me. Which idea has to be in place before either of those claims can be heard as an argument rather than as a slogan; the order in which the pieces must go, with the reason each depends on the one before it; the single carrying example, which I would prefer to be one I invent rather than the familiar historical ones, and what that example must contain and must leave out if it is to do the work; the misconceptions the group will predictably form at each stage, where I expect a durable one is that Hume is simply anticipating the long-run neutrality of money, and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off; and one question I can put to them at the end that would show whether they have understood the interval argument rather than merely remembered its conclusion. Tell me as well what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and whether the free-trade material should be cut altogether. Write in American English, and give me continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
