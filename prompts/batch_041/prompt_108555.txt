I spent my working life as a quantitative geneticist, much of it in a plant-breeding programme, and in the years since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy of biology. Next month it falls to me to lead a session of our reading group — eight or nine people, mainly graduate students in philosophy, fluent with argument and innocent of statistics — on the concept of heritability. I have ninety minutes.

I do not need the concept explained to me; I need a plan for making it land. Give me the order in which the ideas must come, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be in place before heritability can even be defined — I assume some grasp of variation and of what a variance component is, but tell me how much of that machinery is genuinely necessary and what can be carried by a picture — and then what must follow, and why each later piece cannot come earlier. I should like the session organised around one question, which I take to be the one that matters: what is heritability a property of? If you think a different question should be the spine, say so and defend it.

Choose the single example that should carry the session. I had intended to open with adult height — a trait whose heritability in twin studies is about as high as any in human genetics — set against the fact that mean adult height rose steeply over the twentieth century, in some countries within two generations. Tell me whether that example should carry the session or should be held back as a late illustration; if the latter, what should carry it instead, and what the carrying example must have and must lack if it is not to do the audience's thinking for them.

Then the misconceptions, in the order they will surface, separating the ones they arrive with from the ones my own exposition will plant, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Four worry me in particular, and I want to know whether the worry is misplaced in any case: that a heritability of 0.7 assigns seventy per cent of an individual's trait to genes; that a highly heritable trait is one the environment cannot alter; that a difference between two groups is evidence of a genetic difference whenever the trait is heritable within each group; and that heritability is a fixed property of a trait, so that the same trait must have the same heritability wherever it is measured — for that last one I want the norm of reaction to do real work, not to be mentioned and dropped.

Two further matters I want handled carefully, because they are where I have seen philosophers come unstuck. First, the gap between the additive genetic variance and the total genetic variance — between the heritability that predicts the response to selection and the heritability that does not — and whether that gap can be made vivid in ninety minutes without the algebra. Second, the fact that part of what is counted as genetic variance is variance in environmentally mediated effects, the genotype–environment correlation that the textbooks introduce and then quietly forget, so that 'genetic' in the technical sense is not what the audience will hear by the word.

Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to explain at this level; tell me where the concept is genuinely contested rather than merely misunderstood; and give me one question to put to the room in the last five minutes that would show whether they have understood what heritability is a property of — something they cannot answer by reciting a caveat.
