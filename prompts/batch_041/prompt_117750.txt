I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have lately been reading history in the evenings. In the course of that reading I have assembled the following argument, which appears to show that the standard historical periods — the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, the Scientific Revolution — are not features of the past at all but artefacts of how we describe it. The conclusion strikes me as too strong, and the argument seems to prove a good deal more than I bargained for, so I would like you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up would cost.

The argument:

1. A period name such as the Renaissance purports to pick out a stretch of time possessing a shared character and bounded by real changes rather than convenient cuts.
2. Consider any proposed boundary, such as the one conventionally drawn near 1500 between the medieval and the early modern. For any single feature — a style of painting, a farming technique, a form of government, a vocabulary — the change is gradual, and no two features change at the same moment.
3. Hence there is no moment at which any given feature flips from medieval to modern.
4. There is no privileged list of features to consult; any list we draw up reflects what we already care about.
5. Hence the boundary is drawn by us rather than found in the record.
6. If the boundary is drawn rather than found, then there is no fact of the matter about where the period begins or ends.
7. If there is no fact of the matter about where a period begins or ends, then there is no fact of the matter about what the period is.
8. Therefore the Middle Ages, the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution are not in the past; they are in the describing.

The pattern is familiar to me from my own work. If I run a clustering algorithm such as k-means over data containing no clusters at all, it still returns clusters: tidy, well separated, reproducible, and wholly an artefact of the algorithm. So the fact that historians always manage to find periods is no evidence that periods are there. If the argument goes through, moreover, it appears to go through equally against biological species, against medical diagnoses, and against the Industrial Revolution — indeed against almost any classification whose instances shade into one another.

Two people have offered me ways out, and I cannot tell whether either is a genuine position or merely a restatement of step 8. One tells me the answer is simply that these are vague concepts, like baldness; but I can see that they are vague, and I do not see how that helps, since the argument never claimed the boundaries were sharp — it claimed that where we put them is a choice, and that the choice answers to nothing in the record. The other tells me that periods are instruments rather than discoveries: tools for organising a narrative that may be better or worse, but not the sort of thing that can be true or false.

Of the steps, I would most like to keep 2, 3 and the clustering analogy; 8 is the one I cannot bring myself to accept. Please diagnose the argument. Say which step or steps you would reject, explain why they looked compelling in the first place, and set out what it costs to reject each of the alternatives I might prefer to keep — in particular, whether someone who accepts 2 and 3 can still hold that the periods are real in any interesting sense, and whether the clustering analogy tells against the periods or in their favour. If the argument is sound as it stands, say so, and tell me which premise is worth paying to give up. Please write your answer in British English.
