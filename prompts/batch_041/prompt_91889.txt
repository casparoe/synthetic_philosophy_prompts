I have spent enough time in rationalist forums to read the Athenian amnesty of 403 BCE as a precommitment device—a city binding itself not to relitigate the civil war—and that reading runs into trouble in a passage from the Kenyon translation of Aristotle’s Athenian Constitution, chapter 40: “And yet a third such action was when one of the returned exiles began to violate the amnesty, whereupon Archinus haled him to the Council and persuaded them to execute him without trial, telling them that now they would have to show whether they wished to preserve the democracy and abide by the oaths they had taken; for if they let this man escape they would encourage others to imitate him, while if they executed him they would make an example for all to learn by. And this was exactly what happened; for after this man had been put to death no one ever again broke the amnesty.” Help me understand this passage philosophically. Is Archinus’s summary execution a betrayal of the amnesty or its necessary enforcement? If the amnesty is supposed to be a collective act of forgetting, what does it mean that the democracy must remember—and kill—to make the forgetting stick? And how should this complicate the usual story about Athenian democracy’s failures, where the Mytilenean debate, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates are read as episodes of the demos acting on passion rather than law? I am especially interested in whether the amnesty is best understood as a precommitment device, a performative contradiction, or something else, and how that bears on the trial of Socrates a few years later, which some read as a violation of the amnesty’s spirit. Give me a close reading of the passage and the conceptual stakes, not a survey of scholarship.
