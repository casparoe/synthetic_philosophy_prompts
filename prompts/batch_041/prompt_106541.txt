I sit on the board of a small community playhouse — a converted grain store in a mill town, a hundred and twenty seats, a company of amateurs plus two paid staff — and I have been asked to give the opening-night remarks for the first production of our season, Molière's The Misanthrope. The awkwardness is entirely of my own making: I am the board member who spent the last two meetings arguing from Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert about the theater, and the board has now asked me to open the season on the assumption that I can say something warm about the whole enterprise. What I actually believe, following that letter, is that a theater is not a community but a crowd of isolated spectators; that it represents feeling rather than producing it; that a playwright who depends on an audience will flatter its taste rather than correct it; that the actor's art is a trade in passions he does not feel; and that what Rousseau set against all of this was not better plays but festivals, where the people assembled are participants rather than an audience. I have made my own position harder by choosing the play, since Rousseau treats The Misanthrope as the exhibit: the comedy in which the sincere man is made ridiculous and the smooth man of the world is handed the last word, which is the lesson a stage teaches whether it means to or not.

The room will be mixed, and each part of it constrains what I can say. The board chair recruited me and expects a celebration, and has already hinted that the speech should end with thanks. The actors are sitting in the third row, including the woman playing Célimène and the man playing Alceste; they have given six weeks to this and must not be told that their craft is a fraud. The donors want to hear that their money bought something the town needs. And there is one retired schoolteacher in the back who has read the letter and will know immediately if I sand the edges off. So what I need is remarks of roughly eight hundred words that stay honest to Rousseau — I would rather the speech be strange than false — and that do not simply announce that he must have been wrong because we happen to enjoy theater. His position has to remain live inside the speech, and the speech has to find the ground that genuinely remains: whatever can honestly be said to a room of people about to watch a play, by someone who suspects that a play is the wrong shape for what they came for. Please do not put any quotations from Rousseau or from anyone else into it; I don't want it resting on words I cannot verify, so paraphrase or allude instead. You may name him. And the ending should ask something of the room rather than thank it.

Give me the speech first, under the heading "The remarks," at about eight hundred words, in a register that would sound right read aloud from a stage to neighbors. Then a second section headed "Notes on the choices made," of three hundred to four hundred words, telling me which of Rousseau's claims did the structural work and where, what the speech deliberately refuses to say and why, how the passage for the actors and the passage for the donors are meant to hold together, and — since this is the part I am least sure of — whether the ending I asked for is a genuine way out of Rousseau's objection or only a way of changing the subject.
