For thirty-five years I did experimental work on the thermodynamics of aqueous solutions; since retiring I have been reading philosophy, and I have arrived at a question I cannot settle unaided. Frege's distinction between sense and reference, and his insistence that a sense is something objective rather than a private mental image, seem to me to bear directly on the Twin Earth thought experiment, which was devised long after his death. My own reading is that he would have dismissed the physical identity of the two Oscars as irrelevant to meaning, since for him it is not the contents of a mind that make an expression meaningful; but because he also held that the sense of a term determines its reference, a difference in reference between the two planets would have to be matched by a difference in sense — which would make him an externalist about sense, not merely about reference. I would like you to predict his reactions, and to do so in light of a variant of the case that the standard version obscures. On my variant neither planet has one watery substance: Twin Earth's oceans hold a mixture of H2O and XYZ in proportions that vary from ocean to ocean, and the inhabitants, who introduced their word by pointing at the local liquid, know nothing of this. Our own water is no tidier: it is never the pure compound, being always a solution and always a mixture of isotopic variants of the same compound. Given his insistence that a concept must have sharp boundaries to be serviceable in logic, what would Frege have said about the reference of 'water' in such a world, and how would his verdict differ from what he would have said of the tidier case? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, noting where my own reading goes astray if you think it does, with no introduction and no conclusion.
