I host a fortnightly interview podcast on economic history and moral philosophy, and next month's guest is a scholar of famine who has just published a book defending a deliberately immodest thesis: that the familiar division of famines into "natural disasters" and "political crimes" is a category error, because every major famine is at once a production shock and a distributive choice, and the moral weight belongs entirely to the second term. Three claims carry the book, and I want the interview built around them rather than around the history. First, she argues that what makes a famine a crime is not any intent to starve people but a pair of conditions she calls "known shortfall, unaddressed": the authorities knew, or could readily have known, that people were starving, and nonetheless declined the remedy that was available to them. Second, she holds that the famine-prevention mechanism in democracies is informational rather than electoral — it is a free press that renders starvation politically visible, not the ballot, that has kept major famines out of countries such as post-independence India. Third, she treats the Irish famine, the Bengal famine of 1943, and the Great Leap Forward famine as a single series, on the ground that each exhibits a state or imperial authority overriding a functioning food market in pursuit of some other priority. Draft eight to ten questions that press on the content of these three claims rather than inviting her to narrate the catastrophes.

I want at least one question on each of the following, and each of those should be followed by a second question keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. On Ireland: how the "known shortfall, unaddressed" test handles a case in which the governing power's own ideology — laissez-faire economics together with a providential reading of the blight — made the remedy appear illegitimate rather than merely inconvenient, and whether the phrase "could have acted" survives that complication. On Bengal: whether the entitlement collapse Amartya Sen describes among rural wage-earners is really an instance of a state overriding a market, or instead a market clearing in the ordinary way while a war and a cyclone did the rest; if she concedes the latter, ask what remains of the "single series" claim. On the Great Leap Forward: whether the category of "crime" requires intent, and if it does not, whether the term is doing any work beyond "very bad outcome"; if she retreats to "foreseeability," ask what standard of foreseeability she would be willing to accept from her opponents. On the informational thesis: what she makes of famines that have occurred under a comparatively free press, and of authoritarian states that avoided famine altogether; if she replies that the press is necessary but not sufficient, ask her to name the other necessary conditions. Close with one question that forces her to specify what evidence would count against the thesis as a whole, and one that asks her to rank the three cases by culpability and defend the ranking.
