A colleague in our decision theory reading group circulated a handout summarizing two problems that have been much discussed in the literature on rational choice. The handout reads as follows:

"Counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail are two versions of the same problem, and both show that causal decision theory is untenable. In counterfactual mugging, Omega flips a coin. If the coin comes up tails, Omega asks you for $100. If it comes up heads, Omega gives you $10,000, but only if it predicted that you would have paid had the coin come up tails. When the coin comes up tails and Omega asks for $100, causal decision theory says refuse, because your payment cannot causally affect the already-settled coin flip. Evidential decision theory says pay, because paying is evidence that you are the sort of agent who would have been rewarded in the heads branch. In XOR blackmail, Omega sends you a message that says: 'I have sent you this message if and only if your house has termites or you pay me $1,000, but not both.' A termite infestation would cost $1,000,000. Causal decision theory again says refuse, since your payment cannot affect whether your house has termites. Evidential decision theory says pay, because paying is evidence that the message was sent because you paid rather than because of termites. In both cases, the evidentialist is willing to pay a small sum to avoid a large loss, and the causalist is not. Since the evidentialist does better in both cases, these problems show that evidential decision theory is the correct theory of rational choice, and that anyone who refuses to pay is being irrational. The only remaining question is whether you should pre-commit to paying; everyone agrees you should, which just confirms that paying is the right choice."

I suspect this is unfair to causal decision theory and also misdescribes the relationship between the two problems. I would like a careful audit. Please go claim by claim and tell me: which parts would a decision theorist accept, which are common simplifications, which are subtle misrepresentations, and which are simply wrong? In particular, I want to know whether the two problems really have the same structure, whether evidential decision theory really prescribes paying in both, whether it really does better in both, and whether the conclusion that CDT is refuted and EDT vindicated follows from the accurate parts. Also, what would a defender of causal decision theory say in response, and what would a defender of evidential decision theory say about the summary's use of their view? I am trying to get clear on this before we discuss it in the group, and I do not want to rely on the handout's framing if it is misleading.
