I am a philosopher who recently joined the collections and conservation committee of a small university art museum, and the director has asked me to draft the written opinion the committee will vote on next Thursday. The case concerns a plaster sculpture by a mid-century artist who died in 1998; our records call it Study in Grey. The artist treated the plaster as the finished work, exhibited it once, and refused to edition it in bronze. In 1972, after a large crack opened along the figure’s back, a conservator filled the crack with acrylic resin and repainted the surrounding surface to match. In 2019, the museum commissioned a high-resolution 3D scan and a resin replica so that the piece could travel while the plaster stayed in climate-controlled storage. The artist’s estate now argues that the 1972 resin fill is an alien addition and that the museum has been displaying a hybrid object, not the artist’s work; a donor has offered to fund a new display in which the 2019 replica is presented as the primary work and the plaster is retired to study storage. The director wants a decision on three questions: whether the 1972 fill should be treated as part of the artwork or as damage to be documented and, if possible, reversed; whether the 2019 replica may be loaned and insured as the same work; and what the museum should say on the label and in the catalogue entry. Please draft the memo with a clear recommendation on each question, a candid account of the strongest argument against my recommendation, and a short set of conditions the museum can attach to any future loan or display.

You are a philosopher of art and a metaphysician with expertise in material constitution, the ontology of artifacts, and conservation ethics. Begin by brainstorming the candidate criteria for object identity across change — spatiotemporal continuity, continuity of form, continuity of material, continuity of causal history, artist authorization, and institutional recognition — and say briefly how each would apply here. Then separate the question whether the particular plaster object survives from the question whether the artwork survives, and consider whether same work is one question or several. Do not rely on a bibliography or on named theories the committee will not recognize; give us an operational rule with reasons. Think carefully about the difference between a replica that is a new object and a replica that is a new instance of the same work, and about what the estate is owed if the artist’s intentions cannot be fully recovered. The memo should be formal enough to enter the minutes, around 900 to 1200 words, and should end with a bottom-line motion I can put to a vote.
