I'm a graduate student in philosophy, and I'm in a reading group on AI ethics. Last week we read Daniel Dennett's essay "The Problem With Counterfeit People," and I've been asked to lead the discussion on his central analogy. Dennett compares AI systems that convincingly mimic human conversation to counterfeit currency: just as counterfeit money debases the currency and corrodes the trust on which markets depend, these artificial interlocutors debase our shared sense of what a person is and corrode the trust on which human testimony and democratic life depend. He calls them "the most dangerous artifacts in human history," and on the strength of the comparison he recommends that we outlaw both the creation of such counterfeit people and the passing along of them, with severe penalties, and that we develop watermarking systems analogous to the EURion Constellation that protects banknotes. Several of us found the analogy illuminating but weren't sure how much weight it can bear.

I'd like you to audit it. First, lay out the correspondence as explicitly as you can—ideally as a table—specifying what plays the role of genuine currency, the counterfeit bill, the mint, the merchant who accepts payment, the loss to the economy, and the mechanism by which trust is undermined. Then tell me where the mapping breaks down: which features of counterfeit money are quietly imported into the picture of AI and human persons, and which of those disanalogies matter for Dennett's intended conclusion. In particular, I want to know whether the analogy can support a legal prohibition with severe penalties, or whether it only supports something weaker, like mandatory disclosure, watermarking, or norms of transparency. Finally, say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there is a better analogy for the same target, what it would be.
