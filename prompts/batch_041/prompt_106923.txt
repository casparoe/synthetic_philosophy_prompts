I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I need a specification for an adequate theory of work and leisure rights. Please write the desiderata that any such theory must meet. I want the requirements list, not a survey or a history. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it: the phenomena the theory must explain, the cases it must get right, the puzzles it must dissolve, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the empirical or institutional constraints it must respect. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular political or cultural camp, and identify any pair or subset of requirements that I should expect cannot be jointly satisfied. The conflict is the part I most want: if a theory cannot simultaneously honor a strong right to work, a strong right to leisure, and a strong right to choose one's own hours, say so and explain why.

Use the following as test cases, and add any others you think the theory must handle: a salaried knowledge worker who is formally free to stop at 5 p.m. but whose promotion depends on answering messages at midnight; a hospital resident on an eighty-hour week; a gig driver who chooses her own hours but cannot refuse surge periods without losing income; a French employee under a thirty-five-hour week who is asked to work additional hours; a Japanese employee whose death from overwork is recognized as karoshi; a retired person who wants part-time work but would lose pension eligibility; a parent doing unpaid care work; and a person who finds meaning in work and does not want a shorter week.

The theory should also respect distinctions such as: a right in the strict sense versus a mere liberty or entitlement; negative liberty versus positive provision; individual rights versus collective bargaining claims; paid work versus unpaid work; time off versus income security; voluntary overtime versus coerced overtime; and work as a commodity versus work as a calling or civic duty. It should be consistent with basic facts about labor markets, firm scheduling, and public budgets, but it should not simply assume that the Protestant work ethic is correct. It should be able to explain what is wrong, if anything, with karoshi and with a culture that treats overwork as a virtue. It should be able to say whether a thirty-five-hour week is a right, a policy, or a remedy for a different problem. It should be able to handle the possibility that leisure is valuable in itself and the possibility that it is valuable only as recovery for work.

Finally, briefly score the following three approaches against your list, without citations or reading lists: the Protestant work ethic, a statutory maximum-hours regime like the thirty-five-hour week, and a right-to-disconnect regime that limits after-hours contact. Tell me which requirements each approach fails, and which single requirement does the most to eliminate the weakest approach. Keep the scorecard short; the desiderata are the deliverable.
