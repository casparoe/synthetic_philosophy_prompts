I'm a second-year PPE student and I've drifted into a seminar on famine and development that has quietly rearranged how I read the news. The through-line of the term has been Sen's entitlement approach: the claim that famines are usually failures of people's ability to command food — through wages, prices, access to markets, and transfers — rather than failures of the total supply of food, and that calling such an event a natural disaster is itself a consequential act of classification rather than a neutral description. We spent one week on 1943 Bengal, where the standard food-availability story sits awkwardly beside the wartime inflation and speculative hoarding that seem to have done much of the killing; another on the Irish potato blight, where the pathogen was natural but who starved depended on what the authorities chose to do about exports and relief; and a third on the Great Leap Forward, where an official framing of bad weather has been contested ever since. I keep noticing how much work the phrase natural disaster does, and how rarely it is defended as a description rather than deployed as a verdict.

For a creative assignment I'd like to write a satire of exactly that classificatory habit. The form I have in mind is an internal claims-handling manual, or a short batch of claim-denial letters, from a fictional mutual insurer that has filed every historical famine under its Act of God exclusion and is now explaining to policyholders why nothing is payable. About 650 words, deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader, no editorializing narrator — the voice is a mid-level adjuster who is competent, slightly bored, and entirely sincere. The humour has to come from getting the entitlement analysis right rather than from naming it: each denial should, in the flat vocabulary of an insurance file, set out the facts that a development economist would read as a collapse of exchange entitlements — a procurement policy that moved grain out of the affected districts, an export decision defended on fiscal grounds, a wage that quietly stopped buying what it used to buy, a price spike driven by hoarding and rumour — while the ruling line stays, every time, that the proximate cause was an act of God and the loss is therefore excluded. The adjuster should never diagnose the situation as political; the reader should be able to. I'd like one short internal memo at the end, from the actuarial side, noting drily that the payout turns entirely on which committee has the authority to name the cause, and that the exclusion has, in the company's history, never once been applied against a famine that a government was keen to have reclassified. A specialist in Sen's work or in famine history should find it fair and funny; a general reader should be able to follow it without the jargon. You can keep the cases recognizable but needn't supply exact figures or dates, and please don't include references or reading lists — I only want the piece itself.
