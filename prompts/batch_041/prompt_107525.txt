Every piece of digital text I have ever written rests on a division of labor that nobody ever voted on. The text itself stores characters; the shapes come from the font. The same file can be set in a blackletter face, a geometric sans, or a coarse pixel face for a tiny screen, and we say the text is unchanged throughout. Unicode, which fixes what the characters are, treats the double-story a of ordinary prose and the single-story ɑ of phonetic transcription as two different characters, and treats the Latin a and the Cyrillic a — which many text faces draw identically — as two different characters as well, while the bold and italic letters used in mathematical typesetting were given code points of their own. Yet a geometric face may draw the very same character as a single-story a, so the very shape difference that marks two characters in one context is a free stylistic choice in another. Downstream, everything that consumes text — a screen reader speaking a document aloud, a search engine matching a query, a spell-checker, a line-breaking algorithm — treats the letter as the character and pays no attention to the shape at all.

What conception of the letterform does this practice presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit theory and then interrogate it.
