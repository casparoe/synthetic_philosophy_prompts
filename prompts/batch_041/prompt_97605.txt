I'm in a third-year seminar on aesthetics and machine learning, and I've been keeping a private list of verdicts about AI-generated work so that I can work out what I actually believe before I have to commit to a paper topic. I'd like you to look at the list and tell me what principle, if any, unifies it. I don't want a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want the principle stated precisely, applied to cases I haven't listed, and then an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. My verdicts are gut-level, and I don't defend all of them.

Here is the list. A model trained to amplify whatever textures it finds in photographs produces a lurid, symmetrical image: I don't think that is a work of art by itself, but I do think it becomes one when a human selects it, titles it, and hangs it in a gallery. A model given a three-hundred-word specification (composition, palette, the exact position of a figure, the mood of the light) produces a competent painting: I think the human is the artist and the model is a brush. But now a model given the single word 'loneliness' produces an image that startles the person who typed the word and that they could not have specified in advance, and I find myself wanting to say the machine did something creative and the human is more like a discoverer. A system that mutates a population of images over many generations while a human picks the most interesting offspring each round also feels creative to me, but jointly, and I would resist saying that either party alone is the creator. Against all of these: a model trained on the complete surviving corpus of a single painter, which then produces a canvas that experts cannot distinguish from the painter's own work, strikes me as not creative at all, closer to forgery or plagiarism, even though the canvas is new and no one has seen it before. Two more cases I cannot get to sit still: a model that returns a hundred near-identical variations on a request seems to me not to have been creative at all, even if one of the hundred happens to be good; and a model that outputs the text 'this sentence is my artwork' strikes me as making no art, whereas a human who exhibited exactly that sentence would be making art, and I can't articulate why the difference matters.

My tentative principle is that creativity requires the producer to be able to surprise itself -- to hold some representation of what it is doing that its own output can violate -- but I suspect that either excludes too much (the interactive evolution system, which seems creative to me) or too little (the mimicry model, which on some readings does surprise its trainers). Is there a single principle that delivers my verdicts, or a small set of candidates each of which gets most of them? Please state each precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, tell me what it predicts about at least two cases I have not listed, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why I should give that verdict up.
