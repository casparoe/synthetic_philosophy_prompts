Im putting together a handout on free-floating rationales — Dennett's thing, or whoever's, I always half-remember who coined it: the idea that a trait can have a reason for being the way it is even when nothing and nobody has that reason. Spider's web is the stock example: shaped as if to catch flies, no mind anywhere holding 'catch flies'. Dennett wants that to count as a real rationale rather than just our projection onto the web. What I want from you is a taxonomy of the positions you could take on what it takes for one of these rationales to be real, built out of questions rather than out of names.

These are the four questions I think are actualy doing the splitting:
- Does the rationale have to be represented somewhere in the system — some inner state that functions as a reason — or can it be wholly unrepresented?
- If it's unrepresented, what anchors it: a selection history that installed the design, a feedback/learning process running right now, or nothing over and above the interpreter's own normative gloss on the behaviour?
- Does it have to be doing causal work in the system as it stands, or is it enough that it was selected for and is now sitting idle?
- Does it need a beneficiary, some entity whose good the rationale serves, or can it float free of interests too?

Four questions gives sixteen cells and I don't want sixteen items. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two (three if you really need it), no preamble and no closing paragraph. Pick the five cells that matter: the occupied ones a philosopher would actually defend, plus the most interesting empty one. Per item: the combination of answers that lands you there, the name of the position (or 'nobody' if it's empty), and one sentence on the most awkward thing that position has to swallow. No item should just be 'the Dennett view' — if a cell happens to be occupied by something that already has a name, fine, but the answers come first. If two of your five can't realy be told apart by these four questions, say so inside the item rather than pretending they're distinct. No citations, no reading list.

Four cases I'd like the branches to survive, since they're the ones that keep tripping me up: a spider's web; a species whose warning colouration is still there long after the predators it warned off went extinct, so the rationale looks idle; a reinforcement-learning agent whose policy is optimal for its environment but whose weights encode nothing you'd call a reason; and prices, which track scarcity that nobody represents.

If one of the cells I'd expect to be occupied comes out empty, spend one of the five slots on it and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered to go there. I'd rather have a gap diagnosed than five tidy positions.
