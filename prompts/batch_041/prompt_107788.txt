I'm a novelist — second book just out, which is how I end up at these kinds of dinners — and last week two friends of mine got into it at my kitchen table over something a third friend did, and I've been chewing on it ever since. The friend who did the thing, Nadia, quit a job she was good at and turned down a promotion that was not coming around again, to spend two years looking after her father while the dementia took him. She's back now, the job's gone sideways, someone else has her old office. She says she'd do it again, doesn't regret it, but she also says it isn't true that staying with him was *better* than the job — those two things weren't the kind you can line up. That's what set them off.

(1) Marcus: if they can't be lined up then "I did the right thing" is just a noise she's making. Either the time with her father was worth more, or the career was, or they're dead even, and they're obviously not even.

(2) He had a follow-up move: ask her whether she'd rather have had the same two years with her father *and* kept the pension and the health insurance. She'd say yes. So one option can be improved on — which means they're comparable after all. And she'd also say no salary on earth buys that time back, so it isn't that she can't tell them apart. "Incomparable," he said, is just a word for "I can't rank them," which is about her, not about the options.

(3) Deb said he's running together two seperate things: (a) there's no single scale you can score both options on, and (b) there's no fact about which was better. She says (a) can be true while (b) is false. Her big move: it's not either/or, because some pairs are comparable without one being better and without them being exactly equal — nudge one of them up a little and it still doesn't beat the other, but it isn't the same as the other either. She said that's why the deliberating felt the way it did and why a bit of grief afterward isn't a math error.

(4) Marcus: then how is her choice different from a coin flip, and why do we think better of her for it? Deb: because once she commits, the commitment is part of what makes it right — those two years are worth what they're worth partly because she chose them; a friendship isn't a friendship if you'd sell it, and that's not because friendship scores highest on some scale. Marcus: that's renaming "I decided" as "I was right," and it means she can never be wrong — every choice self-certifies. Deb: she can be wrong, if her reasons were self-serving, or she was secretly running from something, or she'd have done it to be seen doing it. She just can't be wrong by the ledger not adding up. Marcus: and "her reasons were good" is a scale. Then he tried one more thing: fine for her own life, but put her on a committee handing one ICU bed to two patients and see how far "it isn't better either way, I just committed" gets you. Deb said that's the point, it's all context-dependent; he said that's just retreating.
