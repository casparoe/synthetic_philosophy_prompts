I spent 35 years in a soils lab and retired into philosophy books, which is how I ended up on a hike last weekend refereeing an argument I was not equipped for. My friend Deb and her old roommate Marcus went at it for a solid hour over a puzzle Marcus says he read about somewhere -- a scout gets dropped into an unknown territory and gets a garbled radio message. He thinks it's called the Judy Benjamin problem, I have no idea.

The case they used, with Marcus's made-up numbers: before the message you figure you're in the East territory with probability 3/4 and the West with 1/4, and if you're in the East it's a coin flip whether you're in the north strip or the south strip of it. Then a scout you take to be maybe 80% reliable tells you: "if you're in the East, you're in the north strip."

Deb: the message is about a conditional, not about East versus West, so your credence in East should sit exactly where it was, and all the news is inside the East -- the north strip goes up, the south strip goes to zero. Marcus: thats a rule you made up. The message is evidence, full stop. "If East then north" is false exactly when you're in the south-east strip, so hearing it is evidence against that strip, and your credence in East should fall along with it. And he said it gets worse: if the scout is only 80% reliable you have to use that, and Deb's rule never even looks at reliability -- it would give the same answer if the scout were a coin flipper.

Deb: you can't use the reliability without knowing what the scout would have said if things had been different. What's the chance he'd have said that sentence if you were in the south strip? You'd have to invent it, so you'd be inventing your answer. Marcus: so invent it out loud, at least then it's visible and revisable. Deb: and "if East then north" is not the same as "not south-east," that's the material conditional, and nobody means that when they say "if." Marcus: then stop using a rule that behaves exactly like it.

They asked me to settle it and I said I'd write it down and think. Can you go through it move by move -- who's right about what, and what are they both missing? If there's a defensible answer for Marcus's numbers, I'd like that too. I don't need a reading list, I can't get to a library, and please write it in American English.
