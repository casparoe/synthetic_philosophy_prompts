I've spent a fair amount of time in the rationalist blogosphere, so I'm used to the idea that you should be able to attach a credence to anything you believe and update it in light of evidence. Reading On Certainty recently, I keep hitting a wall. Wittgenstein seems to say that a whole class of propositions — that I have two hands, that the world has existed for a long time, that the people around me aren't automata — stand fast for us, aren't subject to doubt, and can't be justified by anything more basic. He also gives the image of a river-bed that can shift over time, and suggests that these propositions are less like things we believe and more like the frame within which believing and doubting happen at all. Fine. But here's the thing I can't get past.

If these hinge propositions are groundless — if there's nothing more basic that supports them — then in what sense is holding them rational? It seems to me the honest Bayesian move is just to treat them as ordinary beliefs held with very high but sub-1 credence, updated like anything else, and Wittgenstein's insistence that they're exempt from doubt starts to look like a refusal to do the epistemically responsible thing: a demand that we stop asking for reasons right where it gets uncomfortable. And the consequence seems worse than that. Once you concede that the hinges are groundless, don't you lose the standing to say that someone whose river-bed is wildly different from mine — the flat-earther, the astrologer, the person who thinks the world began in 1900 — is doing anything wrong? If we each simply have the ungrounded frame we happen to have, and if our doubts only make sense against some such frame, what makes my frame better than theirs, and how am I supposed to go about deciding which hinge propositions to adopt in the first place?

What I want is a straight answer about how to handle hinges as a rational agent: should I be trying to assign them credences, revise them, choose among them — or is the whole framing already off? And please tell me what would change your mind about your answer: which argument or consideration, if it turned out to be true, would lead you to a different verdict, and where in your reasoning you think you're most likely to be getting it wrong.
