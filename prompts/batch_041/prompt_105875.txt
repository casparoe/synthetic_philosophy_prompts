I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I run a fortnightly reading group at my company. Two of us have now spent the better part of a year arguing about whether intelligence is one thing, and I would like to stop arguing past each other by producing the document we should have written at the outset: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between the two positions we actually hold.

The first position — the single-capacity view — holds that intelligence is one general capacity. Its core claim is that when you measure how well people, animals or machines perform across a wide and varied range of tasks, one dominant dimension of variation keeps reappearing, and that this dimension is not merely a convenient summary of a particular test but something real: a general ability that helps to explain why the same individuals tend to do well or badly on otherwise unrelated problems.

The second — the pluralist view — holds that 'intelligence' names no single natural kind. On this view, what we call intelligence is a loose bundle of partly independent competences, and the dominant dimension is an artefact of how tests are built and scored: overlapping task demands, shared response formats, the way items are selected, weighted and normed. The general dimension is then real enough as a description of one battery, but it does not pick out a cause, and a battery constructed on different principles would cut the mind at different joints.

Please write the joint statement that these two camps could both sign. It should contain the following.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — what both sides accept about the phenomena, stated so that neither would balk. Separate what can be agreed about observable performance and measurement from what cannot. Include any claim about correlation structure that both accept, and be explicit about the further claim each side attaches to it.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one genuine point of difference, with the two positions given in wording that each side would accept as a fair statement of its own view rather than a caricature. Mark each row as substantive or merely verbal, and for the verbal ones say what the parties are really doing when they disagree — for instance, using 'real', 'capacity' or 'intelligence' in different senses.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux, give a case, argument or test that both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. For at least one crux, say what would count as evidence against each side's own position, not merely against the other's.

Please build the cruxes around the following material rather than around textbook examples, and make the predictions concrete enough that we could actually run the test.

- A battery deliberately constructed to be maximally heterogeneous — for instance, spatial rotation, verbal analogy, social inference, a foraging or resource-allocation puzzle, and a procedural-learning task — administered to human participants, to at least one non-human species, and to two language models, with a single scoring rule fixed in advance. What does each side predict about the correlation structure among these tasks, and about whether one dominant dimension survives? Write each prediction as a sentence the relevant side would sign before seeing any data.

- A case in which a system performs excellently across every task in the battery for one shared reason — it has, say, a very large memory, or is an unusually good next-step predictor — so that the tasks may all be measuring the same underlying thing rather than a general ability. Does that support the single-capacity view, undercut it, or neither? Give each side's answer, and say what it would take to distinguish a genuine general capacity from a shared confound.

- A transfer case: something trained on one task and then tested on a task with no obvious shared content. What does each side predict, and how would each interpret a null result?

Finally, add a short closing section on what both sides agree would actually move the debate, and what both agree would not, even if it were established.

Do not include reading lists, citations or appeals to who said what; reason from the positions themselves. Where you find that a disagreement dissolves once the terms are fixed, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a crux. Please write in British English.
