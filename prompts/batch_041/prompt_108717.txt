I am a sixth-form student, and in our philosophy club we have lately been discussing Pascal's wager. One member has taken to ending arguments with the challenge 'What have you got to lose?' He used it twice last week. First, when I raised the many-gods objection, namely that if the wager works for the Christian God it seems to work equally well for any god who promises an infinite reward, he replied with the slogan. Second, when I said that belief is not something one can simply decide to have, he replied with the same line. In both cases he treated it as a conversation-stopper.

I should like a precise map of what that one sentence could be claiming in this context. Please set out the distinct readings as a menu, from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, say what it commits the speaker to: what 'lose' is supposed to mean; whether the claim is one of dominance, expected utility, or something else; what it assumes about probabilities; how many possible gods it allows; and whether it presupposes that belief is under voluntary control. Then say which readings are mere platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false or question-begging, and, most importantly, which reading, if any, would actually support my friend's two dismissals and which he most likely has in mind. I am not after citations or a reading list; I want the logical geography of the slogan itself, so that I can take it back to the club and say exactly where his reply succeeds and where it does not.
