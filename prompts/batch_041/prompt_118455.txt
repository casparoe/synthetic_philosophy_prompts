You are a philosopher of action and an experimental philosopher who has taught the Knobe effect and the surrounding methodological debates for years. I am preparing a handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar, and I need a decision tree that sorts students by which position they actually hold about what the Knobe effect shows — not by which label they find attractive.

Before writing the instrument, brainstorm privately: lay out the space of possible explanations for the asymmetry, note which combinations of commitments are coherent, and only then build the tree. Do not show me the brainstorm; show me the instrument.

The finding to start from: in Knobe's original studies, subjects read about a chairman who adopts a program that will increase profits and also harm the environment. The chairman says he does not care about the environmental harm and wants only the profits. Most subjects judged that he harmed the environment intentionally. When the case is reversed so that the program helps the environment, only a minority judged that he helped the environment intentionally. Paraphrase this finding in your own words; do not quote the vignette or any source.

Design constraints:

- The first branch node must ask whether the asymmetry is a feature of the folk concept of intentional action or an artifact of how the judgment is made. Every later node must be a concrete, answerable question — a case, a contrast, or a prediction about what subjects would say — not a request to choose a camp.
- Every leaf must carry the name of a position (or be marked unnamed if no one defends it) plus one sentence stating that position's most awkward commitment. The tree should include at least: the view that the concept of intentional action is itself normative; the view that the concept is non-normative but judgments are distorted by blame or moral evaluation; the view that the asymmetry reflects a skill or awareness inference about the agent; the view that it is a pragmatic feature of the question rather than of the concept; the view that it is an artifact of the vignette's wording or of subjects' interpretation of the task; and the deflationary view that the effect is real but not about intentional action at all. Add any further positions the logical space requires.
- Build the tree from the distinctions, then locate the positions in it. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, mark it and diagnose whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Use at least three diagnostic cases beyond the original chairman case. Suggested candidates: a case where the side effect is morally neutral but the agent is indifferent to it; a case where the side effect is good but the agent is independently blameworthy for something else; a case where the agent is a machine or a non-human animal; a case where the agent says she does care about the side effect but proceeds anyway; a case where the side effect is foreseen with certainty versus mere probability; and a case where the question is asked with a verb other than intentionally. Invent at least one further case of your own that you think does genuine diagnostic work, and say what it separates.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and explain why.
- End with a short scoring key: for each leaf, the pattern of answers that leads there, and a note on which answers are diagnostic versus which are compatible with several leaves.
- Aim for eight to twelve branch nodes and six to nine leaves. Write in a formal, polished register. Do not include citations, reading lists, or bibliographies. Do not attribute positions to named authors unless you are certain of the attribution; describe the position instead.
