I'm recording a long-form interview with a philosopher who defends tranquilism: roughly, the view that what makes a life go well is the absence of craving and suffering, with pleasure and the other apparent goods counting only insofar as they quiet craving. Her version is strict — she denies that positive affect is ever intrinsically good, holds that tranquility is a purely negative condition (the absence of agitated craving rather than a feeling of calm), and presents the view as a rival to hedonism rather than a species of it. Draft eight to ten interview questions on the content of the position rather than her career. I want at least one question each pressing (a) whether a mere absence can carry the weight she puts on it — what makes a craving-free life better than one belonging to a being that never had the capacity to crave — with a follow-up for the reply that only creatures capable of craving can be well-off at all; (b) how the view ranks a serene, uneventful life against a restless one full of achievement and attachment, with a follow-up for the answer that the restless life simply contains more craving; (c) whether she can distinguish the tranquility she prizes from the flattened affect produced by drugs, injury, or mere adaptation, and what she says if she cannot; and (d) the quietist worry that the view licenses indifference to injustice, with a follow-up for the reply that tranquility is compatible with engagement. Close with a question that forces her to name something she would count as evidence against tranquilism. Give each question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and keep the questions out of the territory of "what first drew you to this view."
