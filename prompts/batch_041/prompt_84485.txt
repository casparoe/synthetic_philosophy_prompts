I teach a graduate seminar on idealization and unrealistic assumptions in the social sciences, and for one session I would like something better than another exegesis of the F-twist. Please write a piece of sustained institutional satire: the editorial policy and submission guidelines of a fictional journal — give it a plausible name, something on the order of the Journal of Instrumental Social Science — whose declared house methodology is the F-twist, the view associated with Milton Friedman's methodological writings that the realism of a theory's assumptions is irrelevant to its assessment, a theory being judged instead by the accuracy of its predictions within the domain to which it is applied.

Length and form: roughly 800 to 900 words, organized as numbered sections, followed by a short appendix. The appendix should be a certification form filled in for one specimen submission — a two-line abstract plus the author's signed declarations about the assumptions. Keep the numbering and cross-references internally consistent, as they would be in a real policy document.

Voice: deadpan. The document is written by a competent, slightly weary editor who is entirely convinced of the policy's good sense. It never winks, never explains its own joke, and does not end with a punchline or a moral.

The comedy has to come from following the F-twist literally, in the flat administrative prose of an actual journal policy, so that a specialist recognizes the position as one that is really held rather than a caricature of it. With that in mind, the policy must resolve, in the way a real policy would, the difficulties it creates: how an author certifies that an assumption is false, and what the journal will accept as evidence of falsity; what becomes of a submission whose assumptions turn out to be true, with an explicit and entirely unembarrassed account of why such a submission is disadvantaged; how the journal treats as-if formulations and the familiar figure of the expert practitioner who behaves as though solving equations he could not state; and how the policy applies to work that is counterfactual or policy-conditional rather than predictive, where the assumptions are load-bearing rather than decorative. Include an appeals procedure whose standard for an assumption being materially unrealistic is left conspicuously vague, and construct the document so that a reader who reads it closely sees that the entire policy rests on that vagueness — though the editor never concedes as much and evidently does not notice.

Then include one dissenting paragraph: an excerpt from a memo by a member of the editorial board who accepts the policy's premises but objects to its consequences. State the objection at its strongest rather than as a straw man, and leave it to the reader to decide who has the better of it.

Constraints: no named living persons; no citations, dates, or quotations from any actual work, and nothing that depends on the reader's recalling the details of a particular essay, since the piece may be read by students who have never opened Friedman and by colleagues who know the essay well; the specimen paper in the appendix should be plainly fictitious. The target is the methodological position and the editorial practice it licenses, not any person or school.
