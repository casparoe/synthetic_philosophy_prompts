I'm in a small decision-theory reading group that's been meeting for a few months, and next session it's my turn to lay out my own view on betting and rationality so the others can take it apart. Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) When I get to choose my stake and I'll face opportunities of the same kind again and again, I size the bet by the Kelly fraction — the fraction that maximizes expected log wealth — because that maximizes the rate at which my wealth compounds. (2) For a one-shot decision under uncertainty I'm an expected-utility maximizer, and over the range of money that is ever actually at stake for me my utility is close to linear, so I should take any bet with positive expected value. (3) What makes Kelly right isn't that log happens to be a nice concave function; it's that a long sequence of bets compounds. The average of the growth over time and the log of the average across many parallel copies of me come apart, and what governs what actually happens to me is the first of those. I'm fairly confident the same gap is the real content behind the St. Petersburg puzzle and behind the puzzle about paying to insure against a small chance of a large loss — both look paradoxical only if you average across copies instead of across time. (4) Relatedly, what I want out of a rule is the outcome I'll typically live through, not the average across a population of copies of me: if a strategy makes me almost certainly rich while leaving a few copies destitute, that isn't a cost I'm paying. (5) I'm suspicious of arguments whose force comes from taking the number of trials to infinity. A fact about a limit is a fact about a limit, and I want to be told why it is a reason for me to do something now. (6) In practice I bet about half Kelly, since I gather that costs only a quarter of the growth rate, which seems worth it. (7) If someone offered me my entire liquid net worth on one favorable coin flip — three to one, so a clearly positive expectation — I'd take it, on the grounds that I'm young enough to earn it back. (8) And I don't see why any of this should be special to money: for a research program or a long-term project meant to do good, the thing to maximize is the geometric mean of the outcome rather than the arithmetic mean — the arithmetic mean is for people who get to try again.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and how badly in each case? Order the tensions by severity rather than flagging them all as equally bad, and for each one give me the cheapest way to restore consistency together with what that fix costs me — including, where it comes to that, which commitment I'd have to give up rather than merely reword. If a single revision would clean up the most, say which one and why. I genuinely don't know which item is the weak one and I'd rather find out now than in front of the group, so don't go easy on the package. Please don't just tell me what the standard positions in this area are called; I want the specific joints where these commitments bind, including any pair where my stated motive for one commitment can't support the verdict I give on a particular case.

Begin with the bottom line in your first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my list.
