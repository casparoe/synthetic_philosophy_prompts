I am a graduate student sitting in on a seminar on the philosophy of computation, and I have been trying to adjudicate a disagreement between two of the regulars that I suspect is more interesting than either of them realizes. Both accept that computational complexity theory is mathematically respectable and that P versus NP remains open; the dispute is over what any of it could tell us about the limits of rational agency.

The first position, which I will call the structural reading, holds that hardness results are facts about problems rather than about our algorithms or our hardware. On this view, NP-hardness is preserved under every reduction anyone has ever cared about, does not depend on our choice of programming language or machine model, and would survive any physically realizable computer; therefore an agent required to solve such problems optimally in general is not merely impractical but impossible, and any normative theory that models agents as expected-utility maximizers or as ideal conditionalizers is not describing an unattainable ideal but describing nothing at all. The second position, which I will call the bookkeeping reading, holds that complexity classes are defined relative to a representation, a machine model, and a decision format; that any single finite instance can be dispatched in constant time by a sufficiently large lookup table, so that hardness claims are always claims about infinite families rather than about the situation an agent actually faces; that changing the encoding (to unary, say) or the machine (to an analog or otherwise non-Turing device) changes the classification; and hence that a hardness claim is never a fact about the world but only about our accounting conventions, so that any philosophy erected on it inherits the arbitrariness.

What I would like from you is a careful comparison of the two. Are they genuinely inconsistent, or do they answer different questions — the structural reading a normative one about ideals, the bookkeeping reading a descriptive one about classifications — and if the latter, where exactly does the disagreement become substantive? I am particularly interested in whether the structural reading can survive the observation that NP-complete problems arising in practice are frequently solved quickly, and in whether the bookkeeping reading can survive the observation that a problem's hardness seems robust across encodings no one would call bookkeeping choices. Please also say which reading does better justice to the distinction between worst-case, average-case, and typical-case difficulty; identify the bridge principle each side needs in order to get from a statement about algorithms to a statement about agents, since I suspect that is where the real work is being hidden; and end by defending one of the two positions, stating the strongest version of the position you reject before you reject it.
