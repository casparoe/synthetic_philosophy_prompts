I convene a final-year undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of quantum mechanics, and the paper will close with one essay question on the probability problem for the many-worlds interpretation, answerable in about forty-five minutes. I want that question to turn on a line of argument my students find tempting: that because the Schrödinger dynamics are deterministic and every branch is equally real, every outcome occurs with probability one; that the appearance of randomness is therefore nothing but self-locating ignorance; and that the Born rule is at bottom a betting convention which no experiment could ever overturn. Candidates should have to say exactly what is right in that line of argument, exactly where it fails, and what an Everettian must add — or concede — in order to answer it, rather than reproducing the standard decision-theoretic derivation (Deutsch, Wallace and others) as a set piece. Please write the essay question itself, stating the argument briefly so that candidates have something concrete to attack; a model answer of about 500 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 that distinguishes an A from a B by naming the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — for instance, conflating 'every branch is real' with 'every outcome has probability one', or treating the derivation as showing that the Born rule is merely a convention. The whole response must be under 900 words and in British English.
