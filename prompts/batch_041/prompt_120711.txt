I'm running a session on Molinism for our outreach reading group next month. Fifteen-ish people, grad students from other departments plus a couple of very sharp retirees, no modal logic, no systematic theology, and they've all read one popular essay, so they can already say that God knows what free creatures would do in circumstances that never obtain. Which is exactly the problem -- they can say it and they have no idea what's load-bearing in it. Sixty minutes, whiteboard, and I've taught it twice and it died on the board both times. Don't explain Molinism to me; give me the teaching plan.

What I want: the order the pieces have to go in, with the dependancies spelled out -- what has to land before what, and why nothing else will do at that slot. The one example that carries the whole hour, and what it has to have and what it must not have. The misconceptions they'll form, in the order they'll actually form them, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off. One question I can put to them at the end that tells me whether they got the structure or just memorised the slogan. And what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud.

Two things to react to, not just work around. First, my candidate for the carrying example is the ordinary accusation: you'd have taken the money if he'd offered it. I like it because it has the shape of the thing -- a free choice, in a circumstance that never obtained, asserted as true -- but I'm worried it hands them the natural-knowledge reading on a plate, ie that we know the counterfactual by reading the person's character, which is precisely the mistake I'm trying to head off. Tell me whether it survives, and if it doesn't, what the failure teaches me and what I should use instead.

Second, I want to try doing the first twenty minutes with the words possible world nowhere on the board. Tell me if that's possible, and if it isn't, what breaks and at what minute.

My guesses at the misconceptions: middle knowledge is just foreknowledge or prediction; God makes the free act happen; the counterfactuals are true because God knows them rather than the other way round; it's Calvinism with extra steps; would and will are doing the same job. Add the ones I've missed. And skip the biblical examples as the carrier -- they'll have heard those and it muddies the water.
