For the past several months my high school philosophy club has been stuck on one question, and I have come to think we are not really arguing about the question we think we are. I would like you to diagnose our impasse rather than settle the underlying issue for us.

The question is whether a person whose preferences go in a circle is irrational. Suppose someone prefers a bicycle to a skateboard, a skateboard to a scooter, and a scooter to the bicycle, each by a small margin. The first side in our club says this person is irrational because they can be turned into a money pump: knowing the preferences, we can offer to trade the scooter for the skateboard for a small fee, then the skateboard for the bicycle for a small fee, then the bicycle back to the scooter for a small fee, and the person accepts each trade because each one improves things by their own lights, yet ends exactly where they started with less money. A rational agent, on this view, is not someone who can be made a guaranteed loser, so the cycle of preferences must be a defect. The second side says the argument proves far less than that. The person can simply refuse to trade, or resolve in advance not to, and it is not obvious that exploitability is the same thing as irrationality; a person might be exploitable because of what they value without valuing wrongly. They also point out that if the person anticipates the whole sequence, the argument has to explain why the later trades would still be accepted, and it is unclear whether the loss comes from the preferences or from a bad policy for managing them.

I can see that both sides accept the same description of the trades and still do not move, which is why I suspect the disagreement is somewhere else. My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the two sides are answering different questions under one name: one is asking whether a set of preferences at a single moment can be internally coherent, and the other is asking whether a person can be a single rational agent across time, with one standpoint that governs a whole sequence of choices. If that is right, then neither side's arguments bear on the other's question, and the sense of stalemate is an artifact of the shared word rational. A competing possibility is that a hidden question is doing the work: whether a person who sees the pump coming can rationally commit to a policy of refusing trades, and whether that commitment is itself a preference or a constraint on acting on preferences. A third possibility is that the disagreement is bedrock, resting on whether a person is one chooser or a series of momentary choosers, and that no argument can settle it.

Please diagnose this. Say what kind of disagreement it actually is, using the positions as I have described them, and tell me whether my hypothesis is right, partly right, or wrong, with reasons. If the impasse is verbal, say exactly which word or distinction is doing the damage. If a factual or formal question is hidden underneath, identify it and say what would answer it. If the sides are answering different questions, name the questions. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis, whether that is a distinction, a formal result about what a forward-looking agent can commit to, a change of question, or nothing at all because the issue is genuinely unresolvable, and explain why.

I am asking for a committed answer. Take a definite position on the diagnosis and defend it rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me. If you think the whole dispute is a pseudo-problem, say so and argue for it. If you think one side is simply confused, say which and why. Where you are uncertain, say what your uncertainty rests on and what would reduce it.
