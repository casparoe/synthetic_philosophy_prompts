I've been reading in the mechanist literature on explanation, and I'm trying to get my own position into presentable shape for a workshop on anomaly detection in learned systems, where I'm the resident philosopher. Before I circulate a statement I'd like an audit: I suspect my commitments don't hang together, and I can't see where the fault line runs. Here is where I've landed.

1. A mechanism is individuated by its causal profile — the organized activities and the difference-making relations among them — and not by the material that realizes it. Two systems built out of different stuff can run one and the same mechanism.

2. There is no privileged decomposition of a system into parts and operations. Decomposition is relative to an explanatory question: the parts you get are the parts that question calls for, and a different question will call for different ones.

3. Anomaly detection should be sensitive to internal organization, not merely to input-output behavior. If a system brings a different organization to bear on an input than the one it brings to bear on the inputs I have reason to trust, the detector should fire even when the output is indistinguishable. The entire point of detecting anomalous mechanisms rather than anomalous outputs is the case where behavior agrees.

4. "Runs the same mechanism as" is an equivalence relation: reflexive, symmetric, and transitive. I need transitivity for a practical reason. When the detector tells me that two inputs are handled by the trusted mechanism, I want to add both to the trusted set and chain onward from there; without transitivity the trusted set is just a list.

5. Once a question is fixed, there is a fact of the matter about whether two runs are runs of the same mechanism. I don't want the relativity in (2) to collapse into "it's all interpretation."

6. I distinguish what a system implements from what it exercises on an occasion — I've been calling these the standing reading and the occasion reading. The standing reading is the mechanism the system is built to run; the occasion reading is the mechanism it actually exercises on a given input. This may be nothing but terminology, but it's the frame I keep using, and it's how I state the verdict below.

The verdict I keep returning to: the system I work on answers some inputs by retrieving a stored answer and others by computing one. When the gate that decides which route to take is doing the same thing in either case, I want to say the two inputs are handled by the same mechanism — retrieval and computation are branches of one procedure, and the outputs agree. I hold this fairly firmly.

So: which of these can be held together and which cannot? Order the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad; say of each whether it is a genuine clash or only looks like one, and why the appearance dissolves; for the genuine ones, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me; and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most.
