I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy (philosophy of biology, mostly) and I keep getting stuck on something that I think is a real philosophical puzzle but I can't tell if it's just me being fussy about language.

Here's the thing I keep noticing. In clinical medicine we treat fever, cough, and inflammation as things to knock down — antipyretics, cough suppressants, NSAIDs, basically at the first sign of them. But the exact same clinicians, in the exact same visit, explain them to the patient as the body *defending* itself, fighting the infection, doing what it's supposed to do. And then in other contexts the same system trusts those responses: watchful waiting for ear infections instead of antibiotics, letting a fever "run its course," telling people not to overuse antibiotics because the immune response needs to do its work. So one day a nurse will suppress a 38.5 fever and say "your body is fighting this off" in the same breath, and the next day the guideline says wait 48 hours and let the body fight it off. I don't think that's straightforwardly a contradiction, but I want to know what has to be true for it not to be one.

Somebody's phrase for the background idea here is "free-floating rationales" — reasons without a reasoner. The thought being: natural selection can build a system whose parts behave as if they were designed for a purpose, and the "reason for" the behavior (why the response exists) need not be anyone's "reason why" they did it. No one represents the goal; the goal is nonetheless tracked. I'm confident I can state that idea. What I can't do is say what the clinical practice above actually commits to.

So: what does that practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Does it require that fever and inflammation have genuine functions in the non-metaphorical sense, or can the whole teleological idiom be translated into purely causal-mechanistic talk (set points, selection histories, net effects) with nothing lost? Which rival views does the practice quietly rule out — eliminativism about function, say, or the idea that "adaptive" implies "good for you"? Is the practice internally consistent, or do the antipyretic norm and the watchful-waiting norm presuppose different things? And be careful to separate what the practice *strictly requires* from what it merely fits comfortably with, because I suspect a lot of it is underdetermined and several theories would license the same behavior.

Then tell me what would actually change if you replaced the presupposed view with the rival one. Would the antipyretic norm collapse, or just become a bet about net effects? Would "let the fever run" survive at all?

And please push back if I've set this up badly. If the practice doesn't presuppose anything interesting — if "the body is fighting the infection" is just a pedagogical figure of speech with no commitments — then say that plainly rather than constructing a theory to satisfy me. Same if you think the tension I'm pointing at is manufactured by my own phrasing. I'd rather be told the question is confused than get a tidy reconstruction that flatters the premise. Where you're genuinely unsure, say so and tell me which part of the answer you'd bet least on.

Write it in American English.
