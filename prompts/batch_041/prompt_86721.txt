ok so I just spent two hours arguing with my friend about free-floating rationales (the reasons-without-reasoners thing — nature having a rationale for something even though nobody's running the rationale) and I've come out with a position I genuinely can't tell is coherent. here's roughly where I landed, and I want you to poke holes in it rather than tell me it's all fine:

(a) When we say the bird flies south when the days shorten, that's a real explanation and not a cute metaphor — evolution can explain behaviour in reason-giving terms even though no bird does the reasoning and nothing designed the bird.
(b) There's nothing spooky about that: all "because" means there is that the trait would have been selected for, plus the actual history of how it got there.
(c) I'm a realist about biological functions. A heart really has the function of pumping, that's not just us finding it handy to talk that way.
(d) Reasons have to be assessable as correct or incorrect. If something can't be right or wrong it isn't a reason, it's just a cause.
(e) And I don't believe in norms hanging around the universe on their own — a norm is real only when something is actually being held to it, i.e. when there's a reasoner doing the holding.
(f) A thermostat doesn't have reasons. "It turns the furnace on because the room is cold" is shorthand for our rationale, not its.

Then my friend hit me with the enzyme case and now I'm stuck. Take a kinase: it binds its substrate because the shape fits, and it binds it for the sake of the reaction downstream. The rationale there is completely free-floating — no representation anywhere — and it's the exact same shape of explanation as the bird. If I say fine, the enzyme has a free-floating rationale, then (d) and (e) are in trouble and (f) starts looking like I'm just privileging biology because I like biology. If I say no, I need a line that lets the bird in and keeps the enzyme out, and "it's a whole organism" sounds like a promissory note I'd be embarrassed to write down.

So: which of these actually clash, which ones only look like they clash, and what's the cheapest revision in each case? I care more about getting (b), (d) and (e) straight than about (f) — I suspect (f) is downstream of whatever I end up saying about the enzyme, though that might just be me dodging. Rank the tensions by how bad they are rather than saying they're all in play. Also tell me how confident you are in your read and which single step of it you're least sure about.
