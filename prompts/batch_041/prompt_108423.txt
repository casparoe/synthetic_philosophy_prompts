I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, writing a dissertation chapter on the structure of hedonic value, specifically on whether suffering can be lexically prior to pleasure. I've been developing a view I'd like your credence in, and I want the number decomposed rather than asserted.

Call it stratified lexicalism. Hedonic value comes in two strata. In the shallow stratum, pleasure and suffering trade off on a single cardinal scale, so every suffering has some finite quantity of pleasure that compensates for it. But there is a floor: an intensity of suffering at which the exchange rate diverges, so that above the floor no finite quantity of pleasure compensates, and every life above the floor is lexically worse than every life below it. The part I think is genuinely underexplored is the structure of the floor. I don't treat it as a fixed intensity. I assign each suffering episode a lexical weight that is a convex, increasing function of its intensity, rising without bound as intensity approaches the floor from below, and I integrate that weight over the episode's duration. The consequence I care about is that concentration matters: two lives containing the same total suffering can land on opposite sides of the lexical divide depending on whether the suffering is packed into a few intense episodes or spread thin, and a long enough stretch of moderate suffering can accumulate enough weight to cross.

What I want is your credence that stratified lexicalism is coherent — that there exists a well-defined value function meeting these constraints which is neither a mere relabeling of a single-scale view nor committed to a sorites, where two lives differing by an imperceptible amount fall on opposite sides of the divide. For what it's worth, I put myself near 0.65, but I suspect I'm double-counting the fact that I built the thing.
