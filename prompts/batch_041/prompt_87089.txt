You are acting as a philosopher of practical rationality with expertise in decision theory and the philosophy of action, and with a particular feel for how institutions quietly encode theories of the people they govern. Your task is not to survey the literature or to name schools of thought for their own sake; it is to reconstruct, as precisely as the material allows, the implicit philosophical commitments of a concrete practice, and to say how much of the practice those commitments actually carry. Begin by brainstorming the candidate reconstructions before you commit to one; lay out at least two rivals; then argue for the one you find best and be explicit about what would dislodge it.

Here is the practice, in three connected parts.

First, a charitable foundation runs a bidding game at its annual benefit. A twenty-dollar bill is auctioned. Bids begin at one dollar and rise in one-dollar increments. The highest bidder pays what they bid and receives the twenty. The second-highest bidder must also pay what they bid and receives nothing. The auctioneer is instructed never to halt the bidding and to keep it going as long as anyone will raise. In practice the bidding often climbs past twenty dollars and occasionally past forty, and the foundation advertises the game, jokes about it, and budgets the proceeds.

Second, the same foundation's investment committee works by strict expected-value analysis: it declines any project with negative expected net present value, discounts future returns, and treats money already spent as irrelevant to what should be done next. Its written policy says that past costs are not reasons.

Third, the same foundation asks its program officers to sign commitment contracts that bind their future selves -- locking funds, pre-registering decisions, and removing their own later discretion -- on the ground that their later selves cannot be trusted to follow through.

Now reconstruct what this institution must believe about the agents it deals with. Does running the bidding game presuppose that bidders are simply irrational, or only that they are rational with non-standard preferences -- and if the latter, which ones? Does it presuppose a divided self, a present bias, or a single agent whose earlier choices legitimately control his later ones? Does it presuppose that the escalating bidder's choice is a mistake by his own lights, or could escalation be defensible given reputation, threat, or commitment? Is the auction best read as a money pump of the kind the coherence arguments describe, and does that framing claim more than the practice needs?

Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined -- that is, where several incompatible theories would license the same behavior. Then address the tension: the same body that treats sunk costs as no reason at all in its investment decisions depends on sunk-cost reasoning to raise money. Is that inconsistency genuine, or does it dissolve once we see which theory of agency each part presupposes? Finally, run the replacement test: if we substituted a rival theory of the agent -- say, one on which preferences may be time-inconsistent and ought to be respected rather than bound, or one on which there is no fact of the matter about the bidder's true preference -- what in each of the three practices would have to change, and what would survive untouched? Where your reconstruction is speculative, say so, and say what further feature of the practice would settle the question.
