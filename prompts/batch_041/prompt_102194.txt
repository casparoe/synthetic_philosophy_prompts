Two colleagues of mine have been arguing for years about gambling and rational choice, and I want to force the argument into a form where each can see exactly what the other is committed to. One is a thoroughgoing expected-utility maximizer: for him a utility function is an exogenous modeling choice, the logarithm is one concave utility among indefinitely many, and the prescription to stake a fraction of one's wealth proportional to one's edge has no special claim on a rational agent beyond the claim any other consistent utility function has. The other is an advocate of geometric rationality: she holds that for any gamble whose outcome compounds, the rational policy is the one that maximizes expected logarithmic wealth, and that this is not merely the stipulation of one utility function but something forced by reflection on how a single agent's wealth actually evolves as the bets accumulate. I want the two of them to produce the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, the document they could both put their names to, and I want you to write it.

To keep this from floating free of the mathematics, fix it to concrete cases and make the cases do the work. Consider a sequence of independent even-money bets, each of which the agent wins with probability sixty percent and loses with forty percent, offered repeatedly on a bankroll the agent may stake in any fraction each round; then the same bet offered exactly once; then a variant in which indefinitely many independently financed copies of the agent each face that same sequence; then a variant in which the stake is fixed rather than proportional to wealth. Hold both parties to the same arithmetic on all of these, and require that any claim either of them makes be tied to one of these cases rather than to a slogan.

The statement should do several things. First, set out the claims both can sign, including the mathematical ones: what the optimal policy is in each case, where the two camps' recommendations coincide and where they diverge, and what the dispute is emphatically not about. Second, state the remaining disagreement in the strongest form each side would accept as a fair rendering of its own position, in wording its own proponent would sign off on, so that neither is left answering a caricature. Third, identify the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, as opposed to questions on which the two merely talk past each other. For each crux, name the case, argument, or calculation that both parties agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about how it comes out. If you judge that any part of their disagreement is merely verbal, a difference in what they mean by rationality or in the objective a policy is being judged against rather than a disagreement about any fact, say which part and why. And wherever one side's position cannot be stated without a premise the other rejects, make that premise explicit rather than smoothing it over.

Write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter that both of them are to sign: no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables, just paragraphs that carry the argument from one point to the next. I would far rather have the formulations exact and the prose plain than have it sound impressive.
