I'm a retired analytical chemist -- thirty-five years of building and calibrating instruments before I got bored and started reading philosophy, which is now basically all I do. Next month I've been asked to give the opening remarks, seven or eight minutes, at the launch of our city's little futarchy pilot, and I want help writing them.

Quick background in case you need it: futarchy is the idea the economist Robin Hanson came up with -- you vote on what you want, and then you let betting markets figure out how to get it. Our council voted that for this pilot the thing we care about is cutting traffic deaths. There are two proposals on the table: a street-redesign plan that narrows lanes and drops the speed limit, and a tech-and-enforcement plan with cameras and speed governors on city vehicles. Before anything is decided, two conditional markets open -- roughly "traffic deaths in three years if we do the redesign" versus "traffic deaths in three years if we do the tech plan." Whichever one the market says is lower, that's the one the city does.

The room is going to be split down the middle and that's the hard part. Half of them are forecasting people and mechanism-design folks who will know immediately if I'm fudging the conditional-market mechanics. The other half are residents, a cycling group, a few drivers, two council members who voted against this, and local reporters. If I say something that sounds like I'm selling it, I lose the second half. If I hedge too much, the first half decides I'm a tourist.

What I want the speech to actually do, and where I want the philosophy to be doing real work rather than being decoration: the thing that nags me, as someone who spent a career measuring things, is that you can never score these forecasts. With a thermometer I can check it against a fixed point. With a conditional market, the losing branch never happens -- nobody ever finds out how many people would have died under the plan we didn't pick. So the instrument never gets calibrated and never gets falsified, and yet we're going to let it choose. I want that said out loud and honestly, not as a gotcha. Second thing: the metric is fixed in advance, so the plan that games the metric may be the one that wins. And under both of those, the deepest one -- the whole scheme assumes you can cleanly separate what we want from what will work. But picking traffic deaths as the number already decides a lot of the argument; some of the redesign people think the real good here is something else entirely and it just doesn't show up in the count. There's a known objection in this neighborhood that values and beliefs can't be pried apart like that, and I'd rather face it head on than pretend it away.

Take the role of a philosopher of the social sciences who also actually understands decision markets and the measurement problems in science -- someone who can talk about calibration and construct validity without sounding like a textbook. Start by brainstorming for yourself: what are the strongest objections a smart skeptic in that room is holding, and which ones can be honestly answered and which can't? Then write the remarks.

Two constraints: don't cite anything or give me a reading list, I don't need it and I won't read it. And don't let it collapse into "well, nobody knows anything" -- the speech should leave the market people feeling I took their idea seriously and the skeptics feeling their worry got said out loud in the room, and end on the question I actually want people arguing about at the reception, not on a conclusion. Casual register, please, I'll be reading it off paper and it should sound like me. Oh and please spell check it for me, I'm terrible at that.
