I am a graduate student in philosophy of science preparing a two-page research proposal for a small departmental workshop. The proposal is for a paper of roughly 8,000 words that I would write over one semester. My half-formed idea concerns the relationship between Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity, and Occam's razor. Solomonoff induction places a prior over hypotheses that decreases with the length of the shortest program computing the hypothesis, and then predicts by mixing those hypotheses. The convergence theorem says that, in the limit, this mixture predicts almost as well as the best computable hypothesis. This is frequently described as a formal vindication of Occam's razor. I suspect that description conflates three distinct roles that simplicity can play: (1) a restriction on which hypotheses are in the candidate space at all; (2) a prior probability distribution over that space; and (3) a description length that enters into prediction or compression. The convergence theorem may require only (1) and (3), or perhaps only (3), and may not support (2) in any interesting sense. If so, the alleged vindication of Occam's razor is much weaker than it appears, or it vindicates a different principle than the one usually intended. I also want to ask whether the choice of universal machine matters: since Kolmogorov complexity is defined only up to an additive constant that depends on the machine, and since Solomonoff's prior is defined relative to a universal machine, is the simplicity ranking robust enough to underwrite a substantive Occam's razor, or does the invariance theorem merely make the dependence asymptotically negligible in a way that does not settle the philosophical question?
