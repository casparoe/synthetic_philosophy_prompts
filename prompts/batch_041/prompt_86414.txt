I teach a grad seminar on modality and reference, and at the end of term we have a party where everyone has to bring a philosophy joke — last years winner was the one about the philosopher who dies and goes to heaven, and honestly it was a low bar, so I've been deputized to write this year's. I want you to be my ghostwriter: a professional philosopher who is also genuinely funny, the kind of person who could write for a philosophy podcast where the jokes land without a laugh track. Think hard about the material before you commit to a punchline, and please don't be stiff about it.

The joke needs to turn on rigid designation and the necessary a posteriori. Not on philosophers being pedantic, not on possible worlds being like parallel universes, not on someone just saying well actually — the punchline should be a situation where somebody gets the modal profile of a name wrong, in a way that only lands if you actually know the machinery. To keep it concrete, here are a couple of scenarios I'd like you to consider, though you can invent your own if you find something better, as long as its this specific: (a) a christening where the person doing the naming points at the wrong baby, so that on the Kripkean picture — reference fixed by the initial baptism and then passed down a causal chain of transmission, not fixed by what anyone meant and not synonymous with any description — the parents spend the next twenty years sincerely uttering sentences about someone else, and necessarily so, since there is no possible world in which that name refers to the kid they meant; or (b) a town that names a new bridge after the first person to cross it, and the first thing to cross it turns out to be a goat. Two things worth keeping in your back pocket: the mirror-image case of the contingent a priori, where a stipulative definition hands you knowledge that's a priori but could have been otherwise, and the fact that identity statements between rigid designators are necessary if true at all — but everyone is tired of the water/H2O example, so don't build the whole joke out of that.

Before you write anything, brainstorm three or four distinct comic angles on this material (reference-fixing, causal chains, transworld identity, the necessary/a posteriori split, whatever else you see), one line each on what the funny thing would be, then draft your two best as actual jokes. Then pick one and tell me in a sentence or two exactly which feature of the theory the punchline turns on, so I can tell whether you're actually using the material or just doing vibes. Aim for something I could tell at a table of grad students plus two faculty who have read far too much Kripke — funny to them, and not embarrassing if a logician happens to be standing nearby.
