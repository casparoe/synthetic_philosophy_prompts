Bit of an odd one and I'm not sure it even has a clean answer, but I'm hoping you can help.

I'm a backend dev at a small games studio, nine of us, no philosophy background at all (I did a CS degree and that's it). We make a puzzle game, and one of the things players love is that every level's loading screen shows a little three-line verse. The first letter of each line spells out a five-letter word, and that word is what you type into a hidden terminal at the end of the world to unlock the secret level. It's not decoration, it IS the puzzle for that level. Our art director writes them. They're good, and they rhyme, and the rhyme matters to her.

We're now shipping in German, Japanese and Brazilian Portuguese, and the localisation studio has sent back two versions of each verse and basically said "pick one, we're not doing a third round for free".

Version A keeps the three lines, keeps the rhyme, and keeps the acrostic spelling exactly the same five-letter word -- but the verse now says something completely different from the English. In one case it's about a cat instead of a storm. Version B keeps the meaning pretty much word for word and it honestly reads better as a poem than the English does, but the first letters come out as something like XQZFB, so the answer to the puzzle is just gone.

My producer says B, because the puzzle word is "just a word" and nobody will notice, and to be fair the German B is lovely. The localisation lead says A, because the acrostic IS the string, and if the letters don't spell the word then we've shipped a broken level. Neither of them will move and it's on me to write the spec for the next batch of languages, so I have to decide something.

Here's the thing that's nagging me though. I don't think either of them is even describing what "the text" is. Is the acrostic part of the verse, or just a thing that happens to be true of the verse? If a translation keeps the acrostic but changes what the verse says, have we translated the verse at all? And if it keeps the meaning but drops the acrostic, have we translated the *puzzle*, or only the poem sitting on top of it? A mate of mine keeps going on about some book by Douglas Hofstadter where he translates one short Renaissance French poem about a hundred different ways and declares basically all of them wrong in some way, and says the whole game is choosing which wrongness you can live with. Fine, but I still have to ship.

So what I want from you: (1) tell me if there's a principled way to decide this, rather than whoever argues loudest, and (2) give me the actual rule to write into the spec, in plain terms, for the languages we haven't done yet, and (3) the bit I really care about -- when the constraint genuinely can't survive, what do we change: the verse, the puzzle, or the fiction that the two are the same thing?

Please write it in British English (I'm in the UK and "localization" makes me twitch). No reading list please, I'm not going to read a book about a French poem, I just need the rule.
