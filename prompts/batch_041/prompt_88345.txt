I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and for the better part of a year a reading group I'm in has been circling one dispute without either side giving ground. What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than another round of advocacy. The dispute is between the contextualist treatment of knowledge ascriptions and the pragmatic-encroachment (subject-sensitive) treatment. Both were motivated, roughly, by the same small class of cases — the ones in which the subject's evidence and reliability are held fixed while the practical cost of being wrong varies, and the ascription of knowledge seems to flip — and both present themselves as the view that takes the practical element seriously. But they locate that element in different places. Contextualism puts the variation in the word: the truth conditions of 'S knows that p', as uttered by a particular ascriber in a particular conversational setting, shift with that setting's standards, while whether S knows remains a matter of S's epistemic position alone. Encroachment puts the variation in the fact: whether S knows is itself partly determined by S's practical situation, and the expression 'knows' can keep a stable semantic value throughout. They look like rivals because each claims to be the best explanation of the same intuitive data and each charges the other with misdescribing the phenomenon — one with intellectualising the knowledge relation, the other with psychologising the semantics. In the group the split has hardened: one of us insists the whole thing is a pseudo-dispute, since the two theses concern different subject matters and could be true together, and another insists they make incompatible predictions about a single utterance in a single context, so that what we have is a real first-order disagreement about what makes an ascription true. Neither will say what would settle it, and that, more than anything, is what I want from you.

My hypothesis, held loosely, is that the impasse is a disagreement about the explanandum rather than the explanans — that the two camps largely agree on the data and disagree about which level of description the data are data for, and that the debate persists because the linguistic question (how does the expression 'knows' behave?) has never been cleanly separated from the metaphysical one (what is it to know?), against a background assumption that meaning and metaphysics must march in step, so that a semantic thesis and a metaphysical thesis about the same vocabulary have to be either allies or rivals. But I can imagine other diagnoses and I don't want to be flattered into mine. It may be that the practical enters at several distinct joints — the truth conditions of the ascription, the content that the ascriber's purposes fix, the standard of justification internal to the relation, the reference of the concept, or merely the pragmatics of asserting an ascription whose truth value is fixed independently of the stakes — and that 'contextualism' and 'encroachment' are umbrella labels for several different theses that have been run together, some of which are compatible and some not. It may also be that a hidden empirical or linguistic question is doing the work: whether the stakes effects are robust, whether they attach to truth or merely to assertability, and what ordinary use of the term actually looks like. Or the disagreement may bottom out in intuitions about the cases that neither side can argue for, in which case the honest answer is that no amount of philosophy will move it.

So diagnose the impasse. Say what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, two questions under one name, a disagreement about the data or about what counts as evidence, or a clash of unarguable starting points — and defend the diagnosis by reference to what each side actually claims and to the cases that divide them, rather than by reference to which position is currently in favour. Where my hypothesis is right, say so and sharpen it; where it is too neat or simply wrong, say that instead. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the rivalry, a demonstration that the two views are notational variants of one another or that they are genuinely incompatible, a formal constraint on what a semantic theory for 'knows' must deliver, an empirical or corpus result about the behaviour of the term, or an argument that we should stop expecting progress at all, and why. I would rather have a diagnosis I can act on than a survey.
