I'm building a handout for a reading group on the definition of life, and I dont want another list of the usual criteria (organisation, metabolism, reproduction, response to stimuli). What I want first is the spec: what does any candidate definition actually have to deliver to count as adequate? My angle is a bit unusual, because I also sit on a small group that advises on whether a sample counts as a biosignature, so my definition has to be usable by people who may only ever get one scoop of ice, on a world whose chemistry might be nothing like ours, with no chance to watch anything reproduce. So the purpose is partly practical, not just conceptual. Keep that in mind when you decide what's non-negotiable.

Give me the requirements list. For each one: the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and whether you think it's non-negotiable or just the prejudice of one camp. Cover at least these, but add whatever I've missed:

- the standard hard cases: viruses, prions, viroids, plasmids, the pared-down synthetic cell with a minimal genome, mitochondria, a mule, a seed in a drawer, a frozen tardigrade. Say what each one actually pressures, and if a case is doing no work, drop it rather than padding the list.
- the awkward ones where the same lump of matter is alive at 9am and dead at noon with nothing added or removed, and where something can only be alive in company (a lichen, a Portuguese man o' war, a slime mold, mitochondria again). Does your list force a definition to pick a unit -- organism, lineage, gene, consortium? And is that a bug or a genuine requirement?
- nested life: parts that were once independent lives and now aren't. Does a definition have to allow an entity to be both a life and a part of a life, or is that just a mess we can legislate away?
- mortality: some organisms don't seem to age at all. If a definition builds death, or reproduction-into-death, into being alive, is that an empirical error or a legitimate simplification?
- two cases I made up, and I'd rather you treat them as real tests than as riddles: (a) a lab reactor that maintains itself far from equilibrium, grows and splits, but whose parts vary at random with no inheritance whatsoever -- self-maintenance with no evolution; (b) something on another world that behaves functionally like life but has no individuals and no cells -- a slowly-mixing medium that keeps re-making its own pattern as a whole, with no boundary and no lineage you could point to. Does a definition that requires Darwinian evolution, or one that requires an individual, have anything to say here, or does it just go quiet?

Then the part I most want: which of these requirements can't be had together. I suspect at least one pair is jointly unsatisfiable -- something like, precise enough to settle the Earth cases, and general enough to be usable on a world where nothing is a cell, and decidable by a lab that gets one sample and no second look. If that's right, say so and say what it costs and who should give ground. If the tension dissolves, show me how.

And which single requirement knocks out the most otherwise popular answers?

Keep the scoring of actual contenders light -- a couple of sentences each on the main families (metabolism-first, evolution-first, self-maintenance or autonomy views, and the deflationist line that it's a handy label with no real category behind it). I'll argue about those with the group. Dont send me reading lists or citations, I can't look anything up from here and neither can you.
