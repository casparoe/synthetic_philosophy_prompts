Hey — I cover science and ideas for a living and I've got a peice I keep failing to write about state apologies, so let me think out loud with you for a minute.

Here's the case I want to use. Kevin Rudd, Australian PM, Feburary 2008, stands up in parliament and says sorry to Indigenous Australians for the Stolen Generations — the kids taken from their families under policies that ran for decades. John Howard had refused to make that apology for the eleven years before him. And the Rudd apology was worded so it acknowledged the wrong without committing the country to compensation. That's the episode; those are the facts I'm sure of, and I don't want the analysis hanging on anything fussier than that.

Here's the lens, and I think it's the right one but tell me if it isn't: the performative. Austin's point that some utterances don't describe the world so much as change it, plus the conditions he and Searle laid down for a performative to actually come off — there has to be a recognized procedure with a recognized effect; it has to be executed correctly and completely; the person doing it has to actually have the thoughts and intentions the act requires; and they have to behave in line with it afterwards. A promise is the model case: it either works or it misfires, and if you don't mean it then it isn't a promise, it's a lie wearing a promise's clothes.

So run the Rudd apology through that. Who is the speaker — a man, a party, a nation, a chain of governments stretching back before anyone in the room was born? What is the recognized procedure and what is its recognized effect — does saying sorry in parliament discharge anything, does it move anyone's legal or moral standing, and if there's no conventional effect then has the performative even got a convention to lean on? Is it sincere, can a corporate speaker be sincere at all, and does sincerity even matter here the way it does for a promise? Then the other side of it — the uptake. Whether the addressee accepts it, whether a we that includes people who did nothing and people not yet born can apologize on behalf of people who did, and whether a refused uptake voids the act or just wounds it.

Then flip it. Don't just tell me speech-act theory explains the Rudd apology; tell me where it jams. My suspicion is that state apologies are a case the theory never really anticipated — an act with no single author, no clean addressee, no conventional effect, and a hearer who can refuse the uptake and thereby, on the theory's own terms, make the thing misfire. So does the theory break here, or does it turn out that state apologies aren't apologies at all but something else wearing the clothes — an acknowledgment, an expression of regret, a bit of political ritual? And if they're something else, what does that say about Austin's picture: is it too tied to the one-speaker, one-hearer, here-and-now case to handle collective and historical action, or does it actually handle this fine and I'm just being precious?

Give me the mapping first — players, procedure, which conditions get met and which don't — and then an honest verdict on the theory, including the parts it's just silent about. And skip the reading list, I only want the argument.
