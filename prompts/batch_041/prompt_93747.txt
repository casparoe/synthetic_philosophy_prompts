At the closing meeting of my existentialism seminar I want to read a poem rather than deliver a summary, so I am asking you for a villanelle on bad faith, under two constraints. First, the poem must never use the term or explain the concept: it should be spoken throughout by someone enacting it, so that a reader who has taught the account recognises it without being told its name. Second, give the two refrains to the two flights Sartre treats as one and the same evasion — the retreat into facticity (I am simply what I have always been: a waiter, a coward, a nature) and the retreat into pure transcendence (I am nothing at all, so nothing I do is finally mine) — and let the villanelle's returns do the work that the metastable structure of the thing does in Sartre: a belief with no deceiver and no dupe, which therefore cannot hold itself and must keep reasserting itself, each refrain quietly undoing the other. Use the waiter whose movements are a little too precise and the woman who leaves her hand in her companion's as material, but do not present them as his examples. Do not resolve the poem into a synthesis or a moral: end with the refrains side by side and still unreconciled, so that someone who knows the account sees that the two evasions are one gesture and that neither is an exit. After the poem, in a short prose note, say where it takes liberties or leans on a contested reading, how confident you are in that reading, and which single point you are least sure is faithful to Sartre. Write in British English, and end with a final line of the form \answer{...} holding a one-sentence summary of the poem's central claim.
