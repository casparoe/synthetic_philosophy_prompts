I'm assembling a casebook on when a system's behavior is genuinely its own rather than merely produced in it, and I need one case built to order. First, the apparatus the case has to exercise. Call behavior sphexish to the extent that it runs to completion on its cue and cannot be exited by the system itself. Three rival diagnostics of sphexishness are in play, and I need a case on which they come apart.

1. Routine-revisability. A system is sphexish to the degree that its behavior is generated by a procedure it cannot alter once that procedure has failed. On this diagnostic, a system that rewrites its methods as soon as one of them misfires is flexible, whatever it is aiming at.
2. Aim-revisability. A system is sphexish to the degree that its terminal aim is immune to anything it learns. Here a system that rewrites its procedures at will but never lets any observation count against its ultimate end is the paradigm of rigidity, while a system with a frozen procedure and a corrigible end is flexible.
3. Cue-detachment. A system is sphexish to the degree that it cannot treat the trigger of its own behavior as an object — cannot register that the situation is a decoy, a test, or the cue without its usual backing. Here flexibility is the capacity to exit a routine because the cue has been recognized as merely a cue.

Build me a single case on which these split as follows: routine-revisability says flexible, aim-revisability says rigid, cue-detachment says flexible.

Constraints. Realistic and contemporary; one paragraph. No science fiction, no brains in vats, no indeterminacy at the physical level, and no mechanism of the form "an implant, drug, or lesion made it so." The subject must be the kind of thing we pre-theoretically credit with agency — a person, a non-human animal, or an institution acting through its officials — not a machine and not a laboratory construct. The aim-revisability verdict must not be earned by making the aim thin: survive, maximize reward, and keep the institution running are disqualified, since they make the diagnostic vacuous; the aim must be specific enough that we can see what it rules out. The routine-revisability verdict must not be earned by giving the system only one procedure; the system must demonstrably repair, replace, and recombine its methods. And the case must survive the obvious rebuttal from the aim-revisability side, that what looks like an unmoved aim is really an aim being re-specified or evidence being missed: include a fact that the system registers as evidence, that it treats as decisive whenever it bears on a method, and that by its own standards bears against the aim — and let the aim not budge. Do not secure this by stipulating that the aim never changes; a reader who accepts the second diagnostic must be able to see from the described behavior why the aim is fixed.

Then give me a variant of the same case differing in exactly one respect, in which the first two diagnostics swap verdicts. Name the one respect.

Then the working-through. State the case; for each of the three diagnostics give the verdict and the reason, citing only features of the scenario. Say which feature is load-bearing, what becomes of the split if that feature is removed, and what becomes of it if the feature is strengthened. Write the strongest attack open to a defender of the diagnostic that fares worst, and say whether the case can be repaired against that attack or whether the attack succeeds. For each diagnostic, name an intervention a bystander could actually perform on the system that would count as evidence for or against that diagnostic's verdict, and say what the diagnostic predicts about the outcome. Close with the free-will upshot: on which diagnostic, if any, does the behavior stop being the system's own, and is the remaining disagreement substantive or verbal once the diagnostic is fixed. A case that stipulates its verdicts rather than generating them from the described behavior is a failure; the casebook exists to show that the compatibility question turns on which diagnostic one adopts.
