so the hospital system I work in has a rule for deciding which treatments to fund: work out the quality-adjusted life years a treatment buys, divide the cost by that, fund anything under the threshold, reject anything over. standard cost-effectiveness stuff. but there are two carve-outs I can't square. one, for a handful of ultra-rare conditions they fund drugs that blow way past the threshold, on the grounds you can't just say no when theres no other option on the table. two, for end-of-life and palliative care there's a separate process where the QALY arithmetic isn't allowed to be decisive -- quality of life at the end, they say, isn't the kind of thing you price. meanwhile the same policy doc says the whole point of the QALY is that it puts every treatment on one scale so you can compare any two of them.

what view about the values at stake does this system actually presuppose? does the cost-per-QALY machinery presuppose that health states are commensurable -- that they share one common scale -- or does it only need them to be rankable against each other? and what do the exceptions presuppose: that rescue and end-of-life dignity are lexically prior to the arithmetic, or that they're incommensurable with aggregate health, or just that the people writing the rule have other commitments the formal rule doesn't capture? reconstruct the implicit theory, say which rival views the practice actually rules out vs which it just happens to fit, flag anywhere the two halves are pulling against each other, and tell me what would have to change if you swapped in a straight pluralist view where some values don't trade off at all. and is the carve-out a bug -- an inconsistency -- or is it the honest part? keep it concrete, don't give me a tour of the literature.
