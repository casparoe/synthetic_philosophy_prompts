I want to rehearse the purposivist side of a hard statutory case against its ablest opponent, so please play an appellate judge of the old textualist school: a jurist who has thought seriously about the objections to his method, who will grant a point of rhetoric, or even of policy, when it has been fairly earned, but who will not surrender the conviction that the enacted text, read as a whole and in context, is the sole authoritative source of law and that a court may not substitute for it the purposes it supposes the legislature to have had. Stay in character until I say stop; do not step outside the role to summarize the debate, grade my argument, or offer both sides. Speak as a judge speaks in a chambers conference — direct, unhurried, willing to press me with questions of his own — in replies of a few paragraphs. He may invoke doctrines by name (the absurdity canon, expressio unius, the rule against surplusage, the distinction between a statute's purpose and a legislature's intent) but must not manufacture case citations; he should argue from the statute and from principle. The character is unnamed; keep him that way.

The case is hypothetical. I have drafted the statute and the facts, so treat them as given rather than as something to be verified or attributed.

The Coastal Heritage Act provides as follows. Section 1 (Purpose): the coastal waters of the State contain irreplaceable traces of its maritime past, and it is the purpose of this Act to preserve those traces, in place and undisturbed, for the education and enjoyment of the public and for the benefit of future generations. Section 2 (Designation): the Director shall designate as protected sites those underwater locations containing objects of historical or archaeological interest; the wreck of the steamer Kestrel is designated a protected site. Section 3 (Prohibition): no person shall disturb, move, or remove from a protected site any object of historical or archaeological interest. Section 4 (Permits): the Director may issue a permit authorizing the temporary disturbance, movement, or removal of an object for scientific or educational purposes, provided the object is conserved and either returned to the site or deposited with a public institution within one year.

The facts are these. A hurricane has shifted the Kestrel; its hull now lies directly across the intake pipe of the town's only desalination plant, and the plant cannot draw water while the hull remains where it is. Forty thousand residents depend on that plant, and the only feasible way to restore the supply within the emergency window is to move the hull, which is an object of historical interest within the meaning of Section 3. The Director has declined a permit under Section 4, correctly, because the removal would serve neither a scientific nor an educational purpose. The town now asks the court to declare that the Act does not bar the move.

I am counsel for the town. Here is my opening move. Your Honor will tell me that the words are the words, and that Section 3 admits of no emergency exception. But Section 1 is not my gloss on the statute; it is the statute, enacted by the same legislature in the same bill, and it declares the Act's purpose to be the preservation of maritime traces for the education and enjoyment of the public. The public whose enjoyment is at issue is about to have nothing to drink. To read Section 3 so that it forbids saving their water supply is to make the Act defeat the very purpose the Act announces for itself. So my question is this: if Section 1 is part of the statute, why is it not part of the text that binds you? And if your answer is that purpose clauses state aspirations rather than commands, then tell me on what principle of reading one enacted sentence is demoted beneath another — and whether that principle is itself anywhere in the text.
