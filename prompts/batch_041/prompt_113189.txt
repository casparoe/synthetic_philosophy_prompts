I teach a small grad seminar on the measurement problem, and I'm putting together the take-home final this week. I want the one long essay to be about probability in the Everettian picture -- the Born rule business -- but every way I try to phrase it comes out as an invitation to recite the standard three-step story: Everettians have no probabilities, the decision-theoretic derivation is supposed to hand them over, critics cry circularity, everybody goes home. I get a page of that and I learn nothing about whether the student actually sees where the problem lives. One of my students asked after class whether all the branches happening just means the probability is 1, and I realized that question was better than most of the seminar, so I want something in that spirit. What I want is a question that forces them to state the problem precisely before they're allowed to evaluate anybody's solution to it: they should have to say why a Hilbert space, a Hamiltonian and a branching structure that realizes every outcome still doesn't by itself give you the claim that the probability of spin-up is one half, i.e. why that has to be earned by argument rather than read off the formalism, and why the obvious answer (just count the branches and divide) is a non-starter.

Then I want them to have to take a stand on something live. In lecture I've been drawing a distinction between measure-first and credence-first ways of posing the problem -- measure-first asks what feature of the branching structure could possibly play the role of a probability measure, credence-first asks what a rational agent who finds herself in one branch ought to believe about the others -- and I'd like the essay to make them place the main proposed fixes on that map and defend the placement: the decision-theoretic route that runs from Deutsch through Wallace's book, the symmetry route that tries to squeeze the weights out of entanglement alone (the envariance thing), and the more recent self-locating-uncertainty line, which says that after decoherence the agent is simply ignorant about which branch she's on and that this is ordinary ignorance of a familiar kind. The crux I care about is wether the self-locating story is a genuinely different strategy or just the decision-theoretic move in new clothes, and, behind that, what a non-question-begging derivation would even have to look like -- what is a derivation allowed to lean on without having already assumed something probability-shaped.

So: please write the question, a model answer of roughly 600-700 words, and a marking scheme. Make the scheme five must-hit points worth 20 marks in total, and for each point add a one-line note on the way students typically miss it (I dont need citations anywhere -- it's closed book, 45 minutes, and I'd rather they spend the time thinking than name-dropping). Also, the question should be answerable by someone who's done the reading but not by someone who's only watched the popular videos, so please make the seam between those two students show up in the marking scheme rather than in the wording of the question itself.
