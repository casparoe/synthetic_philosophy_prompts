I'm a third-year undergraduate finishing a metaphysics seminar on vagueness — we've spent the last month on the sorites, on three-valued and supervaluational treatments, and on epistemicism — and I've chosen the Florida recount of 2000 for my term paper, partly because I'm tired of heaps of sand and bald men, and partly because I suspect the textbook framing of the debate quietly assumes that what is at issue is always a single predicate with a single borderline region. The facts, as I have them (I'm not asking you to look anything up): after the election of November 7, 2000, the certified margin in Florida was 537 votes; the disputed ballots were punch cards, some with chads only partly detached, which the tabulating machines had recorded as no vote; Florida law directed the county canvassing boards not to reject a ballot where the voter's intent was clear, and the state supreme court glossed a "legal vote" as one bearing a clear indication of the voter's intent; the counties applied different tests for which chads counted; the state supreme court ordered a statewide manual recount of the undervotes under that intent standard; and on December 12, 2000, the U.S. Supreme Court stopped the recount, holding that the absence of uniform standards for judging voter intent violated equal protection and that no compliant recount could be completed before the federal safe-harbor deadline. The ballot-by-ballot studies the news organizations did afterward found that which candidate would have come out ahead depended on which chad standard was applied. My seminar partner thinks the whole comparison is a category mistake — that election law is a matter of what the rules authorize, not of what is true — and if she is right the exercise fails before it starts, so I'd like you to meet that objection rather than step around it.

What I want is an analysis that runs in both directions. Tell me first what each theory actually says about this episode, and be careful to say which of the several candidate loci of vagueness each view is even addressed to: the predicate "is a legal vote for Gore," the physical predicate "is a detached chad," the statutory standard itself (which is vague in part because it contains "clear," so that there is vagueness about how clear is clear), and the institutional rules that had to produce a determinate answer on a deadline. I have been trying to separate three things I think the episode runs together — vagueness in the object (a ballot that neither counts nor fails to count), vagueness in the standard (many admissible standards, no uniquely correct one), and vagueness about which of those two we are dealing with — and I want you to tell me whether that separation is real and where each theory lands on each element. For the epistemicist: if every ballot has a determinate status and the boundary between counting and not counting is sharp but unknowable, what follows about what the canvassing boards, the Florida courts, and the U.S. Supreme Court should have done? Does that make the December 12 cutoff metaphysically innocent and merely epistemically tragic, and does it sit comfortably with an equal-protection holding that turned on the arbitrariness of the standards rather than on anyone's ignorance? And can the epistemicist say anything informative about what the unknowable cutoff is — a determinate number of detached corners, say — or is the sharp line just a brute semantic fact?

For the supervaluationist: a ballot counts just in case it counts under every admissible precisification of the standard, fails to count just in case it counts under none, and where it counts under some but not others there is no fact of the matter; the vote totals therefore become vague numbers, and "who won Florida" can be indeterminate. I want to know whether that is coherent as applied to a certification and an inauguration that actually occurred, and what constraints on admissibility the supervaluationist can appeal to: must an admissible precisification be one a human being could implement in a few seconds with a punch card in hand, and if the constraints come from what counters actually do, does supervaluationism about "legal vote" collapse into the claim that the cutoff is fixed by convention — that whichever test a board applies consistently becomes the right one? Does the safe-harbor rule count as a legitimate device for selecting a precisification, or as a change of subject from who received more legal votes to whose certificate arrived on time? Then reverse the direction: what does the episode show about the two views? Does the fact that competent counters looking at the same dimpled chad reached opposite verdicts, with no principled way to adjudicate between them, support the supervaluationist's no-fact-of-the-matter, or does the fact that the ballots were physical objects with determinate properties support the epistemicist's there-is-a-fact-we-could-not-know? Is the county-to-county variation evidence for either side, or is it not vagueness at all but a difference in enacted law? And which view better explains why it was tolerable for the process to stop without anyone knowing who had received more legal votes — whether legitimacy here came from a decision procedure rather than from the truth of the matter. Please don't give me citations or a reading list; I have the course materials and what I want is the argument, so where a theory is silent, say that it is silent rather than forcing it to speak. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that names which view the episode favors — epistemicism, supervaluationism, or neither — followed by one sentence identifying the single feature of the episode that settles it for you.
