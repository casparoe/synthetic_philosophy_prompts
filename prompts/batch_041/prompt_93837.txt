I'm in a forecasting reading group and we keep coming back to the outside view — the idea that when you want to know how long something will last, you shouldn't reason about the details of the case, you should find a reference class of similar past cases and use the base rate of their durations. One of us is writing a paper applying it to great powers, and he put the argument to me like this. (1) To forecast how long a polity will last, use the outside view: assemble the class of comparable past polities and take the distribution of their lifespans. (2) For a great power, the relevant class is great powers — or empires. (3) On his list (Persia, Rome, the Caliphates, Spain, the Ottomans, Britain...) the typical lifespan is a few centuries, and the great majority ended within roughly 300 years of rising. (4) The present leading power, and the present international order, have been going for well under a century. (5) So they're already deep into the upper tail of the distribution, and the probability of one or the other ending within the next hundred years is high. (6) And no fact about the particular case counts as evidence against this — that's the entire point of the outside view, so "this time is different" is just the inside view talking.

What I can't shake is that this seems to prove too much. Point it at Rome around 100 AD and it says the thing is on its last legs; Rome in one form or another outlasted that moment by well over a thousand years. Point it at the Ottomans in 1600, or the Chinese imperial system at almost any date you like, and you get the same confident verdict, except the polity doesnt end, or ends and then reconstitutes. Point it at any durable institution and it says "this ends within the horizon" — and since the class is by construction a list of things that already ended, the method can never be embarrassed by a case that hasn't ended yet. So either the argument is fine and history is just a long run of lucky survivals, or one of (1)-(6) is quietly doing more work than it's entitled to. Someone in the group muttered that sampling a random moment in history isn't the same as sampling a random empire, and someone else said something about lifespans that are still being lived and so can't be counted — I didn't follow either and I don't know if they matter.

I'd rather not give up the outside view itself; I think it's a genuine corrective and I've seen it save people from bad forecasts. So: which step goes, why did it look fine, and what does it cost to fix — in particular, if I narrow the class enough to make it relevant, do I still have enough cases left to have a base rate at all? Lead with your answer in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, don't restate the question.
