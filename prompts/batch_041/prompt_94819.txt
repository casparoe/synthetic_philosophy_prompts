I am a retired natural scientist who has come to philosophy late, and my reading group has asked me to lead a session on authorial intention and the meaning of a text. Acting as a philosopher of language and of literary interpretation, please write a dialogue between two equally competent proponents of opposing views, each trying to talk the other round.

The case on which they disagree is one I have invented, so neither of them may appeal to a real novel: a novelist's posthumous papers contain a letter, written some twelve years after the book appeared, in which she says the central friendship was meant to be read as a slow mutual deception rather than as sincere affection. The text itself is genuinely ambiguous on the point, and in the same letter she concedes that she may now be reading her own book as a stranger would.

Let one speaker defend the view that a work means what its author actually intended at the moment of writing, so that later testimony is at best evidence of a changed mind; let the other defend the view that meaning is the best hypothesis about what an author of that work, writing in that tradition, could have intended, and that later letters are perfectly good evidence for that hypothesis. Neither is to be a straw man. Have each reply to the other's actual argument rather than to a convenient paraphrase of it. The exchange should reach the point at which each must concede something awkward: the first must say whether an author can simply be wrong about her own work, and the second must say what would stop an interpreter from inventing an intention that suits her.

Begin by jotting a few notes on the strongest form of each position before you draft. Please write in British English, and keep the dialogue to roughly a thousand words.
