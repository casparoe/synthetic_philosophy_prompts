I would like to run a prediction exercise about Immanuel Kant and the modern death penalty debate. Kant died in 1804, well before DNA evidence, before innocence projects and exonerations, and before the modern abolitionist movement, and I am interested in what he would have made of arguments he never had the chance to consider. My understanding of his position is that he defended capital punishment for murder on retributive grounds: the murderer owes his own life in return, punishment must be imposed because it is deserved and never merely as a means to some further good such as deterrence, and the punishment is not simply something the state does to the criminal from outside, since the criminal's own rational will is supposed to be what authorizes it. I have no formal philosophy background, so tell me if that picture is wrong before you build on it.

I would like you to work through two features of the modern debate rather than give me a general survey of the topic.

First, the risk of error. Any human fact-finding process has a nonzero error rate, and there are cases in which people were executed and later widely believed to have been innocent. The standard abolitionist argument from this is not that the state intends to kill the innocent, but that it knowingly runs that risk, and that a state which cannot make wrongful conviction impossible should not be in the business of executions at all. I want to know whether that distinction between intending and foreseeing does any work in Kant's framework, or whether foreseeable wrongful killing counts as a violation regardless of intent; and whether his prohibition on using people merely as a means has any grip on the wrongly executed, who are in effect sacrificed to keep the institution of retributive justice running.

Second, the question of what the punishment is keyed to. Suppose a state tried to keep capital punishment but restricted it to cases of near-certain proof, such as cases with video, DNA, a confession and several witnesses. Would Kant regard that as the correct way to reconcile retribution with the risk of error, or would he object that the severity of punishment is now tracking the quality of the evidence rather than the gravity of the crime, which sits badly with the like-for-like equality that lex talionis demands? A related question: if life without parole is plausibly a worse fate than a relatively quick execution, would substituting it for death exceed what the murderer deserves, and therefore be unacceptable on Kant's own terms?

Then I would like (a) the strongest case, using only Kantian materials, that he would have moved toward abolition, (b) the strongest case that he would have held firm, and (c) your own verdict, with a clear indication of where you are extrapolating rather than reporting something Kant actually committed himself to. If his rejection of deterrence as a justifying ground makes the modern empirical literature on whether executions deter simply irrelevant to him, say so explicitly, and if any part of the question is genuinely underdetermined by his texts, say that too rather than manufacturing a definite answer. No citations or reading list needed; I want the argument itself, and there is no need to rehash the famous scenario about a society dissolving itself and the last murderer who must be executed first.
