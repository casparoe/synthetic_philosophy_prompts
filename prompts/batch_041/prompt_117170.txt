I'm the vice-chair of my state's sentencing guidelines comission — nine members, and we vote on the new offense-severity ladder in three weeks, which is also our statutory deadline to report to the legislature. One of my colleagues has filed a formal written objection to the ladder, and the chair has asked me to write the opinion we'll put in the record alongside his, so we can publish both and let the legislature see the disagreement rather than a cleaned-up consensus. I'd like you to draft that opinion. Take the role of someone who actually works on the logic of comparative judgments — the kind of person who thinks hard about what "worse than" means and whether it has to be transitive — and start by brainstorming on the page: list the premises his argument needs, then list every escape route you can see, and only then commit to a recommendation. His argument, as near as I can put it: any two adjacent rungs of the ladder — 89 days versus 90 days in custody, a $4,000 fine versus a $4,250 fine — differ so slightly that no reasonable person would say the higher one is worse for the offender in any way that matters for desert. If worse-than is transitive, then you can walk from a $500 fine up to twelve years in prison one trivial step at a time and never reach a point where the higher rung is genuinely worse, so the top of the ladder is no worse than the bottom. Proportionality, he says, is therefore a fiction, and we should scrap the ladder and give judges wide bands. What makes it hard to wave off is that our ladder also has to order sanctions of different kinds against each other — fines, community service, supervision, custody — because it's meant to be one ordering, and the same chaining move seems to work across kinds. Two of us have floated fixes I want you to assess rather than ignore: one is a tolerance rule, where any two rungs within, say, five percent of each other get declared ties; the other is to add a third comparative relation alongside better, worse, and equal — "neither better nor worse nor equal" — so the ladder can record that some pairs just aren't comparable in that way.

What I need is the actual memo: a clear bottom line — adopt the ladder, adopt it with a specific modification, or something else, stated plainly enough that nine people can vote on it — plus the reasoning, and an honest account of the strongest thing his side has going for it and what I'd be giving up if I vote the other way. Be careful about one distinction I keep losing hold of: his argument doesn't seem to be the usual vague-predicate thing where nobody can say where the cutoff is; it's a substantive claim about which differences matter, and I want the memo to make clear whether that makes it stronger or weaker, and whether the escape routes that work against the vagueness version work here too.

Don't worry about citing literature or giving me a reading list — we're a commission, not a seminar, and nobody in the room will recieve a paper if I hand them one; argue it out on the merits. And end with a section he could actually reply to in writing, because he will.
