For my Chinese philosophy course I'm writing on the Mencius–Xunzi dispute about human nature, and I want to settle part of it with a case rather than with abstractions. Build me one thought experiment: a single ordinary person, in ordinary circumstances — no state of nature, no contrived deprivation or enhancement — in which Mencius's and Xunzi's accounts predict opposite outcomes for that person's moral development, even though both agree that cultivation is necessary and both can accept the person's upbringing as adequate. The split must come from their own mechanisms — an inborn inclination to moral feeling that develops unless it is damaged, versus desires that are morally indifferent or worse and become good only through deliberate effort, ritual, and teachers — and not from any stipulation that settles the verdict for one side; a case that assumes its conclusion fails. Hold everything fixed except the one variable you are isolating, and keep the person realistic. Then walk me through what each theory predicts here and why, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, say what happens to the disagreement if you remove or reverse that feature, and describe how a defender of whichever side comes out worse would try to redescribe the case to rescue it. The case should be one the familiar textbook examples do not already cover, and it should be clear at the end what this case shows that they do not.
