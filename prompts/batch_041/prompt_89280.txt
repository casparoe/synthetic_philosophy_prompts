I've just spent half the night arguing with my flatmate about advance directves and I want to turn it into a short research proposal before I forget the good bits. Her line: if someone with dementia says they're happy now and wouldn't have wanted the directive, we should listen to the person in front of us, because the earlier self is gone. My line: that can't be the whole story, because then anyone with a change of heart could dissolve a promise just by becoming someone new, and the whole point of a directive is to bind the later self. We went round in circles, and I think both of us were assuming the real question is whether the earlier and later selves are the same person. I've got a half-formed idea that that's a red herring. The interesting question is about authority: when the later self rejects the directive, is she making a new claim about her good, or is she disputeing the earlier self's standing to speak for her at all? And I want to split authority into two things -- authority as authorship, where the earlier self wrote the script and so gets to determine the plot, and authority as stewardship, where the earlier self is acting as a trustee for interests she may not currently have. The hunch is that advance directives look strong on the authorship model and much weaker on the stewardship model, and that most of the disagreement is really about which model is doing the work. I also want to test a twist: maybe the later self's rejection should be treated not as a rival preference but as a challenge to the earlier self's jurisdiction, and jurisdiction might be something we can assess without settling personal identity. One case I especially want to think about: the later self's rejection is itself shaped by the very condition the directive was meant to guard against. Does that make the rejection less authoritative, or does treating it as less authoritative just beg the question?
