I'm a science journalist, and I've spent a few months now reporting a piece about whether markets can be said to "know" anything — whether a price is the kind of thing you can trust like a thermometer reading, or more like a rumor that a lot of people happen to be repeating. The problem is that every economist and philosopher I interview tells me they take the efficient markets picture "with caveats," and then it turns out they mean wildly different things by the caveats, and I end up writing the same muddled paragraph every time. So I want to build a sorting instrument I can run on my interviewees. Here's what I'd like: a decision tree that takes as its starting point someone who says, fine, I accept the no-trade result — the Milgrom–Stokey thing, Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree, that Grossman–Stiglitz point that a perfectly informationally efficient market is impossible because then nobody would be getting paid to dig anything up. Assume they take these seriously as theorems rather than dismissing them, and assume they don't think the whole literature is a trick. From there, take them down a tree where each branch node is one of the theorems' own assumptions, stated as a question a trader or a philosopher could answer about the world without already knowing which answer leads where — is there a common prior, or do people start from genuinely different priors and that's just the end of it; is the structure by which people get their information itself common knowledge; is everyone's rationality common knowledge, or only their Bayesian updating; is the current allocation already one that nobody wants to move away from; is the market even in equilibrium, or is the theorem about a state we never reach; is there anything in the market besides people updating on evidence — noise traders, people selling because they need the cash, people hedging something they own elsewhere, people who trade on vibes. Some of the nodes should be concrete little cases (the insider, the hedger, the momentum chaser) rather than abstractions, so my interviewees have to actually commit to something.

Each leaf should be a named position about what markets can and can't know, plus one sentence on the price it pays, i.e. what it has to give up to hold that position. I want the tree grown from the logical space first and the names fitted in afterward, so if there's a combination of answers that nobody in the literature seems to hold, tell me, and say whether that's because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered. I'd like it to include at least the "markets are risk-transfer machines, not knowledge machines" line, the heterogeneous-priors line, the noise-trader/behavioral line, and the reflexivity line where informationally efficient prices are self-undermining, but don't pad it out with names you can't locate in that space. Something like six branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves, and if two positions can't be separated by any question you can ask, flag that rather than papering over it. Also, I should say, I can't use a reading list or citations — I'm after the shape of the disagreement, not the paper trail, so don't hand me a bibliography.

One last thing: I've been burned before by smart people telling me with total confidence that some contested interpretation of these results is just what the theorem says. So keep the settled stuff and the contested stuff visibly separate — the theorems themselves and what they're conditional on, versus whether any real market satisfies those conditions, whether having different priors counts as a mistake, and whether any of this bears on the epistemic authority of prices — and don't sell me a live fight as the consensus.
