My older brother has no training in philosophy, but he has spent years reading and arguing his way into a view about how much morality can ask of a person, and he holds to it more consistently than anyone I know. I have never been able to decide whether his position has a principle at its center or only a cluster of habits, and I would like your help in predicting what he will say about a decision our family now faces.

His verdicts, as well as I can state them:

- A child drowning in a shallow pond on his walk to work: he is required to wade in, at the cost of his shoes, his phone, and his morning.
- Skipping his daily coffee, when the same few dollars sent to a good charity would do far more good than the coffee does: not required.
- Giving away about a tenth of his income: required. Giving away half of it, though he agrees it would do more good than anything else he does with the money: not required.
- Donating a kidney to a stranger: not required, though he calls it admirable.
- Paying for his daughter's piano lessons rather than sending the money where it would save lives: permitted.
- Taking a better-paid job he would find pointless, in order to donate the difference: not required.
- Giving a hundred dollars to a local clinic's fundraising drive, when several hundred other people could each cover the gap and the drive will very likely succeed without him: not required.
- He once tried to hold himself to a far stricter standard for a year, and reports that he became miserable, distracted, and worse at his work and worse at home; he now says that a rule he cannot live under is not a rule for him.
- And he has said that he would press a button that made him permanently and noticeably poorer in exchange for ten strangers' lives, but that he does not think he is bound by anything that has to be renewed every single day, even where the daily cost is smaller.

What is coming: our neighbors' father, whom my brother knows by name and sees at the school gates, will die within the year without a treatment costing about forty thousand dollars. The family cannot pay, no fundraising campaign will come close, and my brother and his wife are the only people in a position to cover it. The money is the whole of what they have saved for a house down payment; paying it would postpone the house by roughly three years and would, I think, put real strain on the marriage. Not paying means the man dies.

What I want from you is not your own verdict. I want a prediction of his, the rules his earlier judgments are consistent with — I expect there is more than one that fits them all — what each of those rules predicts here, which one you would bet on and on what grounds, and which single further case would best separate the rules that remain standing. If his record genuinely leaves the question open, I would rather know that than have you pick for him.
