I'm giving a paper at a small workshop on probability and rationality next month and I'm more nervous about the discussion than the talk, so I want to prep properly. The room is mostly formal epistemologists who work on credence and confirmation -- the kind of people who take Lewis's Principal Principle more or less as gospel and who think of chance as whatever the best system of laws would assign, Humean style -- plus two statisticians from the stats department who are frequentists and are honestly a bit allergic to talk of 'objective chance' as a primitive, and one philosopher of physics who cares about whether the chances of statistical mechanics are the same kind of beast as the chances of a coin. So three pretty different sets of commitments staring at me, and I know at least the two statisticians have read the paper in advance and are gunning for the framing.

Here's what I'm arguing, and it's a bit against the grain: I think the Principal Principle is real but derivative, not fundamental. I want to say the fundamental norm is just deference to the best available predictor of an outcome -- roughly, if you know that some quantity or procedure is the thing that best predicts X, then your credence in X should match it -- and that chance earns its special status only derivatively, because given the best-system picture, chances happen to be the best predictors available to someone in our epistemic situation. On my view there's no seperate, irreducible rational constraint that ties credence to chance as such; the tie falls out of the general predictor-deference norm plus a substantive and contingent claim about how good chances are at predicting. I also want to use this to take the sting out of the old worry where a chance event can count as evidence against the very chance distribution that assigns it a high chance -- I think once the deference is to the best predictor rather than to chance itself, that worry loses most of its bite, since the thing you're deferring to is picked out by its track record rather than by its metaphysical status. Anyway, you get the shape of it.
