I am finishing a novel whose climactic scene is a closing address in a patent suit, and I have given my advocate an argument that strikes me as both elegant and, I suspect, ruinous. I want to know where it fails.

(1) To own a thing is to hold a right to exclude others from it. (2) A right to exclude is justified only where another's use would take the thing from me — where the thing is scarce in the way bread and land are scarce. (3) An idea is not scarce in that way: if I teach you my method, you do not thereby take it from me; we may both use it, and neither of us is the poorer. (4) So copying an idea wrongs no one, and there is nothing for a law of property to protect. (5) What we call copyright and patent is therefore not the recognition of a right but the grant of a privilege. (6) A privilege granted only for its consequences may be withdrawn when the consequences turn — when a patent puts a medicine beyond the reach of the sick, or a copyright lets an estate suppress a book. (7) By the same reasoning every copying that does good is licensed: the pirate edition that finds more readers, the imitator who employs more hands, the later writer who tells the story better. (8) Yet I cannot believe that a novelist is unharmed when another takes her characters, her sentences, and her name and sells them as his own, though she loses neither reader nor penny. (9) So there is a wrong in copying that no taking explains, and (2) must be false.

The argument, as my lawyer delivers it, seems to prove more than he wants: it would make the theft of an unpublished manuscript and the betrayal of a confidence equally harmless. I would sooner part with (8) than with (2), yet the book cannot do without (8). Tell me which step to give up, why it looked innocent, and what each alternative costs. No more than 400 words, and name the step you reject in your first sentence.
