My flatmate and I have spent most of this year in a reading group on self-locating belief, and we have ended up on opposite sides of a dispute that neither of us can now state cleanly. He holds the self-sampling assumption in something close to its standard form: other things equal, you should reason as though you were a random draw from the observers who actually exist, within some reference class you have to specify. I hold the self-indication assumption: your existence is itself evidence, so hypotheses that entail more observers are favoured in proportion. We agree about every case we discuss and disagree about every conclusion, which is the signature of two people talking past each other. His objection to me is that my principle lets a philosopher in an armchair settle whether the universe is finite; my objection to him is that his principle yields the doomsday argument. I would like the document we should have written at the outset — the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, which either of us could sign without giving up his position, and which makes our remaining disagreement exact rather than merely loud.

Please produce it in three parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims, and I want them non-trivial: not that the issues are subtle, but the two principles restated in neutral wording that each of us would accept as fair; what we agree the disputed credences are credences about, and whether the disagreement is about the world or about the normalisation of self-locating credence; what the qualification that the principles apply only other things equal is doing in each; which cases we agree genuinely bear on the question (at least the Sleeping Beauty problem, the doomsday argument, the armchair-cosmology case usually called the presumptuous philosopher, and the effect of enlarging the reference class); and what each side must concede about the costs of its own principle. Second, a disagreements table with these columns: the crux; the self-sampling side's position in wording it would sign; the self-indication side's position in wording it would sign; the case, argument or calculation both sides accept as bearing on the crux; the self-sampling side's prediction about it; the self-indication side's prediction about it; and a status column marking the crux substantive, partly verbal or verbal, with a one-line reason. Neither of us wants a verdict, but I do want it recorded, per crux, which side carries the burden and what would discharge it. I would also like the statement to include one case of our own, because I think it exposes a split within his camp: the Nightshift case, in which a coin is flipped and on heads one observer is created who lives eighty years, while on tails one observer is created who lives eighty years and, in addition, a trillion observers are created who each live exactly one second, all of them with experiences indistinguishable from one another's and none able to tell whether they are the eighty-year observer or one of the trillion. There are at least three candidate answers — one half, roughly one in four hundred if you weight by observer-moments rather than observers, and roughly one in a trillion — and possibly a fourth if identical experiences are not counted as distinct observer-moments; the statement should say which reading of the sampling assumption each answer belongs to and what each side predicts. Third, a short closing section on whether any part of our dispute is verbal (if so, which part and why), plus a single JSON object at the end with these fields: cruxes, an array of objects each having name, status (substantive, partly verbal or verbal), would_move (self-sampling, self-indication or neither) and settling_evidence; verbal_diagnosis, a string; and collaboration_outcome, a string describing what the exercise did and did not achieve.

Please state both principles in your own words rather than gesturing at what someone is supposed to have said — no citations, no reading list, no references. The whole point is that we can both put our names to the agreed claims, so where you are tempted to fudge a formulation into vagueness, either make it sharp and attribute it to one side or leave it out.
