I am a member of a small reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and one of the others keeps reaching for the same analogy whenever the question of probability in the Everett interpretation comes up; I have never been able to work out how much weight it can bear. His picture is this. A man goes to sleep and wakes in one of a number of windowless hotel rooms, all of which look alike from the inside; he has no idea which room he is in, so he divides his credence equally among them; on learning that the rooms differ in floor area, he redistributes his credence in proportion to area. That, he says, is exactly the predicament of an observer in a many-worlds universe. Every outcome of the measurement occurs, each successor of the pre-measurement observer ends up in one branch, and from the inside none of them can tell which branch that is; the Born rule is simply the rule for rational credence in such a predicament — weight your branch by its size. His conclusion is that the Everett interpretation needs no additional probability postulate, because the appearance of chance is nothing over and above self-location, precisely as it is for the man in the hotel. He concedes that he has not seen the analogy spelt out in quite this way, but insists that it is the familiar Everettian answer in plain clothes.

I would like you to audit the analogy rather than endorse or dismiss it. Begin by setting out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — saying what is supposed to answer to the hotel, the rooms, the sleeper, the process that put him in a room, the floor area, and his evidence. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and in particular which features of the hotel case it quietly imports into the quantum case: that there is a determinate fact of the matter about which room the man is in; that some assignation procedure placed him there and can be described probabilistically; that his credences are answerable to an ensemble which could, in principle, be surveyed from outside; and that 'size' is an independent property of a room rather than a restatement of the very weights we were trying to explain. Which of the resulting disanalogies matter for the conclusion he draws, and which are merely decorative?

I am especially interested in three charges. First, that the hotel story cannot even be told without a fact about which room the man occupies, and that the Everettian has no counterpart to that fact, so the 'uncertainty' is of the wrong kind. Second, that the appeal to floor area either presupposes a probability distribution over rooms, or else silently identifies floor area with branch weight, in which case the derivation of the Born rule from self-location is circular. Third, that the hotel case permits a survey of all the rooms and hence a check on frequencies, whereas the quantum case affords no external vantage point, so the analogy supplies a warrant for credence that the target cannot supply. Tell me which of these you take to be serious, which are red herrings, and whether the friend's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table; if a better analogy is available for what the Everettian is entitled to say about probability, give it. Please write in British English.
