I'm in a small reading group that's been chewing on pain asymbolia for months and we are completly stuck, going in circles. The phenomenon, as best I understand it: people with certain kinds of brain damage (strokes, and I think sometimes after psychosurgery) will report that yes, they feel the pain, they can tell you where it is and whether it's sharp or burning, but it doesn't bother them at all -- they aren't suffering, it's just not awful. I have a vague memory that some of them will smile or even laugh while you're doing it, though I might be mixing that up with something else. I've also read it has to do with the insula getting disconnected from limbic/cingulate stuff, but dont trust my anatomy.

Camp A in our group says: here we have a case where the sensory-discriminative part is intact and the awfulness is gone, so the awfulness can't be baked into the sensory quality itself. Pain is bad because of the aversion, the motivational pull, the wanting-it-to-stop; strip that out and what's left is a sensation that's basically just information about tissue damage. Camp B says: no, what asymbolia shows is that the patient doesn't have pain at all in the full sense, they have a fragment. Calling it pain with the painfulness filed off is like calling a car with no engine a car. And a third person keeps saying the reports can't carry the weight either way, because someone whose affective machinery has just been disconnected by a lesion isn't a reliable instrument for reporting on their own phenomenology, and we're building a theory of the badness of pain on a handful of old clinical anecdotes.

My hypothesis -- tell me if it's wrong -- is that this isn't really an empirical disagreement at all, it's a verbal one. Camp A means by pain the sensory state, Camp B means the whole package including the aversion, and then both ask what the asymbolic patient is missing, and of course they get different answers, because they're pointing the word at different things. But I can see two other readings. One: it's a genuine disagreement about what makes anything bad for a subject at all -- whether badness has to involve a conative attitude aimed at the state, or whether a felt quality can be bad just by being the quality it is -- and asymbolia is just the vivid case where that fight gets staged. Two: it's a disagreement about evidence, about what first-person report can establish about the structure of experience, which would make it a methodological fight and not a metaphysical one. I dont really beleive it's a factual dispute about the patients, since nobody in the group disputes the patients.

So what I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Which of these is it, or what combination, and what in each side's case is actually doing the work? Where exactly do A and B talk past each other and where do they genuinely contradict each other? And then, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here: is there a distinction, or a case, or something you could actually run on patients -- or a thought experiment that would do the same job -- that would move this, or is it the kind of standoff where nothing moves until people agree about what the word pain is for? If it's the latter I'd rather hear that than a hopeful list of future research.
