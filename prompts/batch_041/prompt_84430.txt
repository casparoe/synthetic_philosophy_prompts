Two colleagues of mine were arguing about how to read a statute, and I want the argument adjudicated as it actually went, move by move. The statute is one I have paraphrased and lightly altered, but the drafting is realistic: a consumer-protection act requires retailers to pay a rebate on any "returned appliance," and it ends with an enacted interpretive clause providing that the chapter "shall be construed to give effect to the purposes stated in section 1, and shall not be construed in a manner that defeats those purposes." Section 1 states two purposes: protecting consumers who receive defective goods, and keeping the rebate process simple and predictable. The case in dispute is a buyer who returned an appliance undamaged, having changed her mind, and demanded the rebate.

The first colleague said the words "returned appliance" settle the matter in the buyer's favour, and that the interpretive clause cannot change that, since the clause is itself text and must be read by the same method it purports to displace; read otherwise, the operative words are a nullity. The second replied that the clause tells us which reading to prefer, that purpose is what makes words determinate rather than a rival to them, and that the legislature wrote its purpose down, so nothing is being guessed. The first answered that if the clause controls, the words do no independent work and the law becomes whatever best serves the purpose — vague, undrawable in advance, and effectively made by courts; he added that the same majority could have written "defective" into the operative section and did not, so the mismatch looks like accident rather than design. The second said the clause was published with the statute, so retailers had notice, and that a court applying a word against a purpose the legislature expressly disclaimed is substituting its own policy for the legislature's; he also observed that the self-defeat point proves too much, since even the instruction to apply ordinary meaning must itself be applied purposively. The first closed by granting the clause for the sake of argument: section 1 states two purposes that here pull apart — the defective-goods purpose favours the second colleague's reading, the predictability purpose favours his own — so the clause does not decide the case but only relocates the indeterminacy one level up, leaving the ordinary canons to break the tie.

Please adjudicate each move: who has the better of it, and what, if anything, that person is missing. I do not need a general account of textualism and purposivism; judge each move on its own. Give me exactly five numbered items, one per move, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; in the fifth, also say which colleague has the better of the exchange as a whole.
