I am assembling a handout for a philosophy of mind reading group on whether dreams are experiences, and I want it to function as a diagnostic instrument rather than an exposition of the Malcolm-Dennett dispute. Please write a decision tree—a flowchart of yes/no or three-way questions—that begins with a reader's ordinary commitments about dream reports and ends at a named position on whether dreams are experiences. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of philosophers: first the questions, then the positions that occupy the resulting combinations, then a note on which combinations are unoccupied and why. Every internal node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Every leaf must carry the name of a position (use the label unnamed if no one defends it) and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. The tree must separate at least these leaves: (1) dreams are genuine experiences occurring during sleep, reported more or less accurately; (2) dreams are genuine experiences, but the dream report is a retrospective reconstruction rather than a transcript; (3) dreams are not experiences at all, and a dream report is not a report of a past experience but an avowal or expression of a disposition to tell a story; (4) dreams are not experiences at all, and the dream report is a confabulation produced on waking, so the report is the primary datum; (5) dreams are minimal phenomenal experiences without narrative structure; (6) there is no such thing as dreaming, only sleep mentation that we retrospectively label. Use these concrete cases as branch material: a sleeper woken from REM who gives a long, coherent report; a lucid dreamer who claims to have decided to raise her hand within the dream; a person who reports a dream only after being asked whether she dreamed; a sleeper whose vocalizations and movements during sleep match the actions in her subsequent report; a child who says she dreamed but cannot say what; and a person who sincerely reports a dream that is inconsistent with the sleep-lab record. At least one branch must turn on the distinction between a dream as an occurrent state and a dream as a narrative object, and at least one on whether first-person authority over dream reports is epistemic or expressive. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say whether the failure is due to the cases or to the positions themselves. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts; the instrument must stand on its own. Your entire response must be no more than 850 words.
