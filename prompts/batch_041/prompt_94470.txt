I am a high school junior, and I am writing a short story for our school's literary magazine after a discussion in philosophy club that I have not been able to stop thinking about. We were asking why we call a glass fragile even when it never breaks, and the answer seemed to be that we are not describing anything the glass is doing now, only what it would do if struck. Then our advisor described two ways the world could keep a fragile thing from ever breaking. In the first, the striking itself takes the fragility away, so that at the very moment it should have shown itself there is nothing left to show. In the second, the glass stays fragile through and through, but something else — a mat beneath it, a hand catching it — blocks the effect. He called the first case a fink and the second a mask, and asked us whether we would still call the glass fragile in each.

I would like a story of about 1,500 words built on the second case, with a person in place of the glass: a character whose defining trait is genuinely there and never once gets the chance to show itself, because ordinary circumstances always intervene at the last second. Nothing magical should be involved; the interference has to be the kind of thing that could happen in a real school or family. I want the story to end without settling whether the trait was ever real, so that the reader finds it as hard to deny the trait as to believe in it. Let the situation carry the idea, and please do not have any character explain the philosophy out loud.
