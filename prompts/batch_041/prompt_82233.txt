I have spent several years defending a package I will state in three parts, and I want an honest audit of how exposed it is. First: the meaning of a moral sentence is fixed by the practical state it is used to express, not by any proposition it stands for — its semantic job is to set a norm for the speaker's attitudes. Second: embedding is handled by treating the moral predicate, wherever it occurs, as a device that characterises a practical state rather than one that expresses it — so in "if lying is wrong, then encouraging your brother to lie is wrong," the antecedent picks out the state of disapproving of lying, and the conditional is a claim about how practical states combine. Third: validity is not truth-preservation but a constraint on coherent combinations of attitudes; an argument is valid when there is no way to hold the attitudes of the premises while failing to hold that of the conclusion. I take truth to be thin enough that I may still call moral sentences true, and I take the whole thing to be a version of the expressivism that the Frege–Geach problem was supposed to sink.

What would count against this? Sort the defeaters by how much they should move me — fatal, serious, merely awkward — and be specific about which of the three parts each one attacks. I am especially interested in whether the second part quietly converts the view into descriptivism (and, if it does, whether that is fatal or only a redescription); whether the third can distinguish the state expressed by "lying is not wrong" from the mere absence of the state expressed by "lying is wrong," and from what is expressed when one simply withholds judgement; and whether anything in the package predicts something its rivals do not, or whether it is all stipulation. Tell me which of these a defender of my view should concede rather than wave away, and finish by assessing whether the view has bought its survival through qualification. What would persuade me most is an inference or a case that my own commitments require me to treat as valid, or as incoherent, but which plainly is not.
