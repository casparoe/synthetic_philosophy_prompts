So — full disclosure — I'm a novelist, three books, none of them about mathematics, and somehow I've ended up teaching a seminar in our interdisciplinary honours program called "The Infinite." It's cross-listed with the MFA, so half the class is writing fiction and the other half have had exactly one course in logic and now think of themselves as set theorists. Finals are in two weeks and I'm writing the essay question myself. It's on Skolem's paradox, and I'm already dreading the answer I know I'll get: "countability is only relative to a model, the bijection exists outside the model but not inside it, so there's no contradiction." Which is true, and I taught it, and if that's all they write they've learned nothing. I want a question that forces them to actually push on that move, not recite it.

What I want them to have to do is say whether the inside/outside story *solves* the paradox or just restates it in fancier vocabulary. So the question should make them take seriously the standard "this is just an artifact of first-order logic" line — the idea that if you axiomatise set theory in second order instead, you get a theory whose models are pinned down much more tightly (any two of them agree up to isomorphism, or else one is just a shorter rank-initial piece of the other), so a countable model of the "reals" can't happen and the paradox evaporates. And then it should make them face the comeback, which is that a second-order quantifier is supposed to range over all subsets, and if you're allowed to just assume you know what "all subsets" means then you've assumed the very thing that was in dispute — so has the relativity actually been killed, or just moved up a floor? I also want them to say, in their own words, what an absolute, model-independent notion of "countable" would even amount to, and whether you can answer someone who thinks all set-theoretic notions are relative without begging the question against them. The good ones, I think, will notice that the same Löwenheim-Skolem reasoning applies to whatever theory you're using to talk about models in the first place, and that this is where the argument actually gets interesting rather than where it ends.

Can you write me: the question itself (one essay, 90 minutes, closed book, no citations needed, and it shouldn't be answerable by someone who skimmed a good encyclopedia entry the night before); a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme with five must-hit points, 20 marks total, and for each point a one-line note on the mistake that usually costs marks. These are mostly humanities students, so the model answer can use a little notation but shouldn't turn into a logic paper.
