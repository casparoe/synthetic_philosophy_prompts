I retired some years ago from a career in experimental geochemistry, and since then I have been trying to educate myself in philosophy of science. A small reading group I belong to has spent several sessions on the reference class problem, and I find that the usual labels—frequentist, propensity, Bayesian, and so on—do not help me see where the real disagreements lie. I understand the basic puzzle well enough: a probability judgment about a particular event seems to depend on which class of similar events one locates it in, and different defensible classes can yield different probabilities. What I want is not another survey but a diagnostic instrument, something I could put in front of a scientifically literate but non-specialist reader and use to sort positions by what they actually claim.

Please construct a decision tree or taxonomy that begins from a starting commitment and leads to a named position on the following question: when a probability judgment about a singular event depends on a choice of reference class, what, if anything, makes one choice correct? Each branch node should be a question that can be answered without already knowing the name of the position it leads to. Use concrete cases throughout: a geologist estimating the probability that a particular unstudied formation contains a certain ore; a clinician giving a prognosis for a single patient whose combination of features is rare; a court assessing the probability that a unique historical event occurred; and a machine-learning model reporting a calibrated probability for one user. The tree should distinguish positions along at least these distinctions: whether there is a unique correct reference class fixed by the world; whether the correct class is fixed by the totality of available evidence; whether the choice is pragmatic and relative to a decision; whether singular probabilities are genuine properties of the world or only summaries of information; whether the class must be the narrowest class with known frequencies; and whether exchangeability or some related symmetry can replace the choice of class altogether. Leaves should carry the name of the position where a standard name exists, or be marked as unnamed where the combination is unoccupied, and each leaf should have one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pairs of leaves the tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers are unoccupied and whether they are unoccupied because they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The reading group is small, so keep the tree to six to nine leaves. It should include the position that the problem is a pseudo-problem or dissolves under a decision-theoretic reformulation; the position that direct inference from finite frequencies is legitimate; and at least one position that treats the reference class as a feature of the modeler’s utility rather than of the world. I do not want citations, reading lists, or references to particular authors; I want the logical space itself. Where the tree depends on a distinction between objective chance and credence, make that branch explicit rather than assuming the reader accepts one side. The final product should be usable as a handout: nodes phrased as questions, arrows labeled with the answers, and leaves labeled with the position and its cost. If a leaf is a position that has no standard name, say so and give it a descriptive name.
