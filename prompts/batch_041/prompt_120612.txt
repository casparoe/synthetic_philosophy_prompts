As a retired natural scientist who has only recently begun reading philosophy of biology, I would like your considered and committed verdict on a dispute that has been nagging me. Gould and Lewontin's 1979 critique of adaptationism is famous for its architectural analogy: the spandrels of San Marco are said to be non-adaptive byproducts of the constraints imposed by the dome and arches, and this is supposed to show that many biological traits may be byproducts rather than adaptations. But I have read that the features at San Marco are not spandrels at all but pendentives, and that genuine spandrels are the triangular spaces between arches. Since the entire critique seems to be built on this analogy, doesn't the misnomer undermine the argument? If the central example is architecturally mistaken, should we conclude that the critique of adaptationism loses its force, or at least that its rhetorical power depends on a confusion? I want a definite answer, not a survey of opinions: does the biological critique stand or fall with the correctness of the architectural example? Please give your reasons, and if you think the critique survives, explain exactly what work the analogy is doing and what would have to be true for it to fail.
