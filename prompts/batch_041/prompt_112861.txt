I am trying to state the man-of-his-time defence precisely enough to argue about, and I want your credence in the resulting principle, decomposed. You should not need any literature for this: everything the question turns on is stated below, and I am not asking you to cite or name anything.

Call an act seriously wrong if it belongs to a class of acts that we now judge to be grave moral wrongs — slaveholding, colonial violence, wartime decisions with mass civilian costs. Call an agent's belief that such an act was permissible warranted if an epistemically conscientious inquirer who had the evidence and the cognitive resources the agent actually had, and who reasoned without error, could have held that belief. Here is the principle:

WBE. If an agent's belief that a seriously wrong act was permissible was warranted, then the agent is not blameworthy for that act.

Two features of WBE are worth flagging before you answer, because they are where the action is. First, it excuses the unreflective wrongdoer as readily as the reflective one: what matters is whether the belief was warranted for the agent, not whether the agent did any thinking. Second, it concludes only that the agent is not blameworthy — not that the act was not wrong, and not that the agent is not responsible in the broader sense that bears on our assessment of their character.

My question is this: what is your credence that WBE is sound, that is, that whenever its condition is met the agent really is not blameworthy, so that the defence succeeds? Treat this as a claim with a truth value, and say what would count as resolving it: for instance, a case in which WBE's condition is met and we have strong independent reason to blame the agent, or a case in which the condition is unmet and blame plainly does not attach.

Give me the number and the decomposition in these sections, in this order.

Credence. One or two sentences: the number, and a single clause naming the term you expect to dominate it.

Decomposition. At least six sub-questions, each with its own number and one or two lines of justification. Include at least these, and add any you think I have missed: (a) whether a warranted-belief condition belongs in a correct theory of blame at all, as against objective wrongness, the agent's actual careless beliefs, or the quality of the agent's will; (b) whether moral ignorance can ever be non-culpable, or whether moral knowledge in the relevant cases is always available to anyone with ordinary capacities, so that the excuse almost never triggers; (c) whether the counterfactual in WBE is determinately satisfiable in the paradigm cases — for a mid-eighteenth-century colonial landowner, say, or a wartime leader — or is merely indeterminate there, which I take to be a distinct kind of failure; (d) whether WBE has the right extension, or excuses too much, sweeping in ordinary wrongdoers who simply never considered the question, or too little; (e) whether some non-epistemic reconstruction of the defence — an excuse turning on the unfairness of demanding that an agent transcend their entire conceptual world, or on moral luck — is more defensible, and if so whether it is a rival to WBE or a supplement to it; (f) whether WBE's verdicts about the standard targets of the defence, figures such as Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus, match confident judgments or clash with them, and if they clash, whether that is a modus tollens against WBE or against the judgments. Flag any sub-question you think is not truth-apt, and say how you handle it rather than dropping it silently.
