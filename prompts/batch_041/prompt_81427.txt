I'm in a reading group that's working through the big cyclical-history crowd and we've hit an argument I can't settle. The bloke who presents each week keeps saying a civilisation is an organism: it's born, it grows, it matures, it ages, it dies. He's using it to say our lot are in late autumn and there's nothing to be done — you can't legislate yourself into spring, reform is just a dying body putting on make-up, and anyone proposing renewal has misunderstood the biology. I want to know how far the analogy actually holds before I let him have it.

So: give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the embryo, the growth, the metabolism, the lifespan, the death — then the places where it breaks. Then tell me which of those breaks actually matter for the conclusion he's drawing and which are just decoration. I'm particularly interested in what the picture quietly imports: a fixed lifespan, no reproduction or hybridity between cultures, no conscious agency, one clean boundary around the thing, senescence as internally driven rather than something that happens to it.

Then I want three pictures kept seperate, because he's muddling them. (1) The organic life-cycle one, as above. (2) The ancient one — Polybius-ish — where constitutions just rotate round a fixed set of forms (one man, few, many, and their degenerate twins) and never die of old age at all, they just come round again. (3) The one about dynasties, Ibn Khaldun's sort of thing, where the solidarity that founds a ruling house dissolves after about three generations of comfort, but can be renewed by a new religion or a hard frontier or a common enemy.

Do those three predict the same thing about a polity that's plainly decaying and then gets a new founding myth plus a foreign war? If they diverge, which one is actually doing the work in his argument, and does the "nothing to be done" verdict survive once you've picked? And if he switches to picture (2) or (3) to dodge a counterexample, does he still get to keep the gloom, or has he quietly changed his conclusion?

Last thing: is there a better analogy? Someone in the group said it should be an ecosystem going through succession rather than an individual organism, so what looks like death is really just a shift to a different stable state — does that rescue his claim, water it down into something no one would disagree with, or replace it with a different claim altogether?
