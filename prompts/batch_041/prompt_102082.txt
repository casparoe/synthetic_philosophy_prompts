I have been reading about the lessons of history — Munich, Vietnam, the so-called Thucydides trap — and I have arrived at a position I should like to have tested. My view is that a historical analogy is a legitimate guide to policy only when its causal mechanism can be stated independently of the analogy itself, so that the inference would survive the substitution of every proper name and date; where that test cannot be met, the analogy is doing rhetorical rather than evidential work. What would count against this? I want the cases and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are — including analogies that guided a decision well without any articulable mechanism, and analogies that satisfy my test yet led policy astray — together with an honest assessment of whether the view collapses into the trivial claim that good analogies are simply good reasoning, and whether its defenders have quietly qualified it into unfalsifiability. Please say which objections a committed defender of my position would themselves concede, which they would dismiss, and what the position predicts that its rivals do not. Kindly write in British English.
