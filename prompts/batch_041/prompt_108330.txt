I'd like a first-principles argument for a position I hold about decision-making under moral uncertainty. I am not asking for a survey of the literature: argue for the position, from premises, the way you would defend it in a seminar where the objection is always on the table.

Setup. An agent must choose among options. She does not know which of several moral theories is correct, and she has credences over them; crude, unsharp credences will do, and nothing should turn on their being sharp. Two kinds of downside can attach to an option, and I want them kept firmly apart:

- shortfall risk: according to the true theory, the option is suboptimal. It is permitted, but some available alternative would have been morally better.
- condemnation risk: according to the true theory, the option is impermissible. It is not merely a worse choice; it is one the true theory forbids.

I do not know whether these two labels are already in use; treat them as my coinage. If they collide with an established distinction, say so and switch to the established one, but do not let the relabelling do any work in the argument.

The position to defend: condemnation risk has lexical priority over shortfall risk. An agent should first restrict herself to options that carry no non-negligible condemnation risk — options that no theory she takes seriously forbids — and only within that restricted set should she maximize expected moral value, or apply whatever her preferred intratheoretical criterion is. When the restricted set is empty, she must fall back on something else, and the argument has to say what: either a ranking of condemnation risks, or an explicit concession that the framework has broken down at that point.

What I want from the argument:

1. The case for the priority claim. Why should being forbidden by the true theory outweigh being merely suboptimal by it, even when the suboptimality is enormous? Note that an appeal to the stringency of deontological constraints will not do on its own, since the agent does not know that the true theory is deontological: the priority is supposed to hold whatever the true theory turns out to be, including a purely consequentialist one. If you think the view has to be restricted to agents who assign some credence to a theory containing constraints, say so and defend the restriction.

2. Whether the view needs intertheoretic comparisons of value. As I see it, a selling point is that condemnation risk is a probability — the chance that the true theory forbids the act — and probabilities are comparable across theories in a way that magnitudes of moral value are not. Say whether that is right, and where the hidden cost of the manoeuvre lies, because there must be one.

3. The strongest objections, engaged with rather than merely listed. At a minimum: (a) paralysis — if the agent takes many theories seriously, every option may be forbidden by some theory, so the restricted set is empty; (b) the threshold problem — what does "takes seriously" mean, and is the view simply letting the agent's low confidence in a theory operate as a veto; (c) prohibition-heavy theories — a theory that forbids nearly everything, or one artificially gerrymandered into many near-duplicate theories, seems to acquire outsized power; (d) whether the view is really maximin or plain risk-aversion in disguise, and whether it inherits their defects, such as insensitivity to probability and sensitivity to irrelevant re-descriptions of the option set; (e) the collapse worry — if the true theory is a straightforward consequentialism then condemnation risk is zero for every option and the priority claim goes vacuous, so the view's content depends on which theories the agent happens to entertain.

4. Defeat conditions of the kind that could actually be met, not "if the true theory were known."

5. One short worked case in which the priority rule and plain expected-moral-value maximization come apart. Invent it or adapt it; concreteness matters more than novelty. Say which verdict you think is right, and why.

Do not cite literature: I want the argument, not the state of the field, and I would rather you reason than report. Do not spend space explaining what moral uncertainty is.

After the argument, give me a structured summary as a single JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "thesis": the position in one sentence.
- "premises": an array of strings — the numbered premises of the argument in the order used, one sentence each.
- "load_bearing_premise": the single premise that, if false, sinks the argument, reproduced from the "premises" array.
- "restricted_set_empty": what the view says when no option is free of condemnation risk, in two sentences or fewer.
- "objection_table": an array of objects, one per objection from item 3, each with the fields "objection" (one sentence), "reply" (two sentences or fewer), and "residual_cost" (what the reply concedes, or "none").
- "worked_case": an object with the fields "setup" (three sentences or fewer), "priority_verdict", "expected_value_verdict", "which_is_right", and "why" (one sentence).
- "defeat_conditions": an array of strings, each a condition that would defeat the position if met.
- "confidence": a number between 0 and 1, your confidence in the position as stated.
