A group I'm part of (a small research fund, about 40 people deciding where the money goes) just switched to futarchy for grant decisions, and I voted for it, but the more I think about it the less I understand what we actually signed up for. The setup: anyone can put up a proposal, then we run two prediction markets on a metric we all voted on in advance (right now it's a weighted thing combining publications, citations per dollar, and a survey of grantees) — one market pays out on the metric if the proposal is adopted, one pays out if it isn't. The proposal passes only if the adopt price beats the reject price by some margin. The metric itself can be changed, but only by a seperate vote.

What I want from you is the implicit philosophy. Some things I'm stuck on:

1) The design seems to rest on a clean split between values and beliefs — we vote on what we want (the metric), markets handle what's true (whether a proposal gets us more of it). Is that split actually coherent, or does picking the metric already decide most of the contested questions, so the market is just filling in an engineering detail? Which theory of the fact/value distinction does the design need, and how strong a version?

2) It assumes the good can be summed into one number and compared across worlds. Does it need full value commensurability, or would something weaker do — a proxy that's roughly monotone in what matters? Where exactly does it break if the metric is only a proxy?

3) The thing that really bugs me: only one of the two markets ever resolves. If we adopt, the reject branch never happens, so the price on it never gets checked against anything. What view of counterfactuals, and what view of market efficiency, does the design need for that price to mean anything? Could you run the whole thing on a deflationary view where the price is just the crowd's best guess and there's no fact of the matter?

4) Does it presuppose that legitimacy is purely about getting decisions right, rather than about everyone having a say? And if so, why do we get to vote on the metric at all — is that a leftover from consent-based legitimacy, and do the two halves fit together?

5) In a market this thin, price equals probability only if traders are roughly risk-neutral and nobody can move the price cheaply — and the people who wrote the proposal are the ones most likely to trade on it. How much is the practice leaning on those idealizations, and what happens to the philosophy if they fail?

Please also say which of these the design strictly requires versus which it just happens to fit well with, and where it's underdetermined — i.e. which rival theories would also license this setup.
