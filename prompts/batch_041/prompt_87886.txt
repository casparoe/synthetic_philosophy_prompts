The dispute over Frankfurt-style cases and the principle of alternative possibilities has now run for more than half a century, and its shape has barely changed. The cases are supposed to show that an agent can be morally responsible for an action she performs even though she could not have done otherwise: a counterfactual intervener is poised to guarantee the outcome she in fact produces, but never acts, so that by stipulation she acts on her own. If that is right, the principle of alternative possibilities — that moral responsibility requires that the agent could have done otherwise — is false, and a central support of libertarian accounts of free will falls with it.

The defenders of the principle have not conceded. Their standard reply is a dilemma. In any such case, either the agent's decision is already settled before she acts — by her prior character, a prior sign, or the deterministic structure of the scenario — in which case the case does not show that she is responsible but only that the outcome was unavoidable, and the alleged counterexample dissolves; or her decision is not already settled, in which case some alternative possibility survives, however thin a flicker, and the principle is untouched. Compatibilists respond that the dilemma is false: the surviving flicker, if there is one, is not the kind of alternative possibility responsibility requires, and cases can be described in which nothing settles the decision in advance yet no alternative is open. Each side regards the other's moves as question-begging, and the exchanges have the air of variations on a script neither party will abandon.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. My working hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that the parties are not in fact disagreeing about a single proposition. One side is asking what grounds or makes for responsibility — what the attribution rests on — while the other is asking whether a particular modal fact obtained, namely whether the agent could have done otherwise in the relevant sense; and the phrase "could have done otherwise" is being read by one side as a counterfactual about nearby possible worlds and by the other as a claim about a standing ability that the agent possesses. If that is so, a good deal of the dispute may be verbal, or may be two distinct questions pursued under a single heading. But I am not confident of this. The disagreement may instead be substantive, turning on whether the counterfactual and the ability readings can be pried apart at all; or it may be methodological, turning on whether stipulated cases can generate evidence about responsibility or only express the theorist's prior commitments; or it may be a dispute about which cases count as data.

Diagnose it. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, arguing from what each side says rather than surveying positions. If my hypothesis is correct, show how it dissolves the impasse and what, if anything, of substance remains once the two readings are distinguished. If it is mistaken, identify the real crux. As a check on whichever diagnosis you reach, consider a variant of the case in which the counterfactual intervener is removed but the agent's decision is nevertheless settled in advance by her own character, and say whether the competing readings predict different verdicts about it — if they do, that tells us which reading is doing the work. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a case that discriminates between the readings, a formal result about the relevant counterfactual, or an argument that the dispute is a standoff at bedrock and no progress should be expected.
