I'm a science-and-ideas journalist by trade, but I've been roped into co-teaching a small grad seminar this term on philosophy between the wars — the Vienna Circle, Neurath, the Bauhaus milieu, that whole moment when people thought redesigning a language and redesigning a city were basically the same kind of project — and somehow I'm the one writing the final. The students are sharp, but they've all absorbed the same three paragraphs about logical positivism being naively scientistic and politically quietist, so I want a question that makes that story impossible to just recite. What I'm after is the specific tension in Carnap: on one side there's the voluntarism, where choosing a language framework is a practical decision rather than a theoretical one, tolerance is the order of the day, and frameworks get judged by how well they serve purposes rather than by being true; on the other side there's Carnap the man — a socialist who thought a rationally planned society was more or less the point of the whole exercise, who treated metaphysics as not merely empty but as an active obstacle to a decent society, and who plainly took himself to have reasons for that rather than a taste. The crux I want them to land on is whether the question of whether society should be rationally planned is an external question in his sense — a practical decision with no truth of the matter, settled by which framework turns out more fruitful — or whether his own political advocacy needs it to be something more than that. And if it is the former, what survives of his attack on the metaphysics he despised, given that on his own account the reactionary metaphysician is also just picking a framework and getting on with it? I don't want them smuggling in the lazy answer that he was tolerant in philosophy and committed in politics and those are just different compartments; they have to say what the connection is, or admit there isn't one. One more thing: the model answer must not invent quotations or lean on sources it can't verify — paraphrase positions in plain words, and if it gestures at a text, it should say something like the 1934 essay on theoretical questions versus practical decisions or just the tolerance principle, without pretending to quote. The students can't look anything up either, so nothing in the question should hinge on a date or a title they'd have had to memorize.

Give it back to me as a JSON object with exactly these fields: "exam_question" (the question as it would appear on the paper, with a 60-minute time allowance and a 900-word cap); "why_this_is_hard" (two or three sentences aimed at me, not the students, naming the specific crux); "model_answer" (roughly 700 words, no quotations, no citations, written as a strong student would write it); "marking_scheme" (an array of objects, each with "criterion", "marks" as a number out of 20, "full_marks_move", "partial_credit_move", and "typical_failure" — the criteria must be named as specific intellectual moves, not topics, and the marks must sum to 20); "grade_boundary" (an object with "A_vs_B" and "B_vs_C", each a single sentence naming the move that separates them); and "distractor_traps" (an array of strings, each one a plausible-sounding but weak answer plus, in the same string, one clause saying why it earns little credit). Keep the whole thing tight enough that I can hand the question and the scheme to a TA without a cover note.
