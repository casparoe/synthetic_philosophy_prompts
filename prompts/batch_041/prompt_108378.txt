I teach a 300-level metaphysics of mind seminar with eighteen students, and the session on mental causation runs seventy-five minutes. The room has movable chairs and a whiteboard, and the students have read a short introduction to causal closure, supervenience, and the no systematic overdetermination premise, so they can use the vocabulary but the causal exclusion argument still lands for them as a syllogism they can follow rather than a problem they feel. I want a runnable activity that puts them inside the squeeze: a role-play or structured deliberation, with no lecture up front and no naming of the argument until the debrief, in which they must assign causal credit for a single human action—use the case of a juror who changes her vote from guilty to not guilty after an hour of deliberation—among at least four candidate causes: the neural realizer of the change, the mental state of doubt, the reason that the evidence was weak, and the social pressure of the other jurors. The rules should make the exclusion result emerge from the mechanics rather than from a speech: for instance, each candidate cause receives a share of a fixed causal budget, but any candidate that is sufficient on its own must receive the whole budget, and no two candidates may both be treated as sufficient for the same effect. I want the group to discover, in the middle of the exercise, that the mental state either collapses into the neural realizer or is left with nothing to do, and then to discover how much that result depends on the scoring rule. I do not want the exercise to stack the deck for non-reductive physicalism; I want them to feel why epiphenomenalism becomes tempting and why that temptation is supposed to be a cost. Include the roles, the materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact budget or scoring system, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the premises of the exclusion argument, the difference between excluding mental events and excluding mental properties, the distinction between overdetermination and supervenience, and the main replies—reductionism, non-reductive physicalism, emergentism, and the charge that the argument proves too much.

I am also interested in a wrinkle that I think gets flattened when the argument is taught as a syllogism. The exclusion pressure often depends on a total, non-contrastive picture of causation, on which a sufficient physical cause leaves no causal work for a distinct mental cause. If the group instead treats causes as difference-makers relative to contrast classes—neural state N rather than N*, mental state M rather than M*—then the mental cause can re-enter without overdetermination. I want the activity to make them feel the force of the total-cause picture and the escape hatch offered by the contrastive picture, and I want the debrief to be explicit that the game's scoring rule has already chosen a side. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—which groups will game the budget, where the analogy to actual causation will break, how to handle the student who insists that the mental cause is just another description of the physical cause—and be frank about which feature of the exclusion argument the activity cannot reproduce, such as the modal strength of the closure premise or the difference between causation and causal explanation, and what I will have to supply in the debrief.
