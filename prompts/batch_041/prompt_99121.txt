Two years ago I began spending Saturday mornings as a companion to residents in a memory-care unit, and the experience has made a philosophical question personally uncomfortable in a way it never was when I only read about it. Much of what I do there is powered by feeling: I like these people, I would feel wretched if I stopped, and I am aware that being the sort of person who shows up matters to my self-image. I had always assumed this was fine, or even good. Then I started reading Kant in a nineteenth-century translation (T. K. Abbott's), and the following passage, from a preface whose exact placement in Kant's corpus I cannot work out — the page I found labels it 1780, which seems impossible for a text that keeps talking about a doctrine of virtue — has stopped me cold:

"But it is not, therefore, useless, much less ridiculous, to trace in metaphysics the first principles of ethics; for it is only as a philosopher that anyone can reach the first principles of this conception of duty, otherwise we could not look for either certainty or purity in the ethical teaching. [...] But in fact, whatever men imagine, no moral principle is based on any feeling, but such a principle is really nothing else than an obscurely conceived metaphysic which inheres in every man's reasoning faculty; as the teacher will easily find who tries to catechize his pupils in the Socratic method about the imperative of duty and its application to the moral judgement of his actions. The mode of stating it need not be always metaphysical, and the language need not necessarily be scholastic, unless the pupil is to be trained to be a philosopher. But the thought must go back to the elements of metaphysics, without which we cannot expect any certainty or purity, or even motive power in ethics. If we deviate from this principle and begin from pathological, or purely sensitive, or even moral feeling (from what is subjectively practical instead of what is objective), that is, from the matter of the will, the end, not from its form that is the law, in order from thence to determine duties; then, certainly, there are no metaphysical elements of ethics, for feeling by whatever it may be excited is always physical. But then ethical teaching, whether in schools, or lecture-rooms, etc., is corrupted in its source. For it is not a matter of indifference by what motives or means one is led to a good purpose (the obedience to duty)."

I would like help understanding this passage rather than a summary of Kant's ethics in general, and I would rather you not point me toward secondary literature, since I have no library access and am not looking for a reading list. Working through it in order would be ideal. What does "pathological" cover here — does it take in warm fellow-feeling and love, or only self-interested appetite? How exactly is the contrast between "the matter of the will, the end" and "its form that is the law" doing work in the sentence about deviation? The clause "for feeling by whatever it may be excited is always physical" reads to me less like an argument than a flat assertion; is there a premise I am missing that would make it an argument, and does the word "physical" mean what a contemporary reader would take it to mean? In the sentence about "motive power," is Kant claiming that a person cannot actually be moved to do their duty without metaphysical reflection, or something weaker about the conditions under which ethical teaching is reliable? And what should I make of the claim that a moral principle is an "obscurely conceived metaphysic which inheres in every man's reasoning faculty" — is that a thesis about the content of morality or about the machinery of the mind, and does it imply that the ordinary person who has never done philosophy is nonetheless implicitly a metaphysician?

Finally, and this is the part that actually keeps me up: the last sentence quoted, about its not being a matter of indifference by what motives one is led to a good purpose. I take it the passage is telling me that my Saturday mornings, insofar as they run on affection and on distaste for my own guilt, are not morally worthy in the sense at issue. I want to know whether the passage really entails that, or whether it is only making a point about what grounds the duty in the first place, which is a different matter. If there is a tension between "no moral principle is based on any feeling" and the role that some kind of felt respect seems to play elsewhere in Kant's ethics, say so plainly and explain how a Kantian would reconcile the two without giving up the quoted claims. Where the text is merely obscure, tell me it is obscure; where it is clear but hard to accept, tell me that instead, and give me the strongest case for accepting it.
