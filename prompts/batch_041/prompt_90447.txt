I'm a retired physical chemist (thirty-odd years in spectroscopy, so I've spent my life treating time as a parameter rather than a puzzle) and I've been sitting in on a philosophy of time course at the university extension. We did the grandfather paradox and closed timelike curves last week, then I went home and watched Back to the Future again with my grandson, and now I cant stop picking at it. What I'd like from you is a real reading of the film through the philosophy of time travel, not just a list of goofs. The thing that nags at me is that it seems to run two incompatible pictures at once: it plays the photo of Marty and his siblings fading out, which tells us the past is changeable and that his own existence hangs on whether his parents get together, and then it lands us in a rewritten 1985 where the family is prosperous and Marty is still standing there, which looks instead like one fixed history that always contained his meddling. Then there's the dance, where he plays Chuck Berry and Marvin Berry phones his cousin — a loop with no origin at all. So which picture does the film actually want — a mutable timeline, a self-consistent one in the Novikov style where everything he does was always part of the record, or a branching one — and is there any way to make the fading photo and the happy ending sit together? Point me to the scenes that do the most work, and the ones you think can't be rescued at any price.

I'd also like the philosophical stakes laid out properly rather than in film-nerd terms. From the lectures I have a vague memory of an argument — the bilking argument, I think it's called — to the effect that if you really could travel into the past you could always stick around and undo whatever you'd supposedly already caused, so the kind of backward causation the film needs is incoherent; and something about personal time versus external time, so that going back needn't mean the past changes, only that the traveller's own clock runs the other way relative to the world's. Use both of those to say exactly what the film is committed to, where it contradicts itself, and whether any single coherent story could keep the best scenes intact. And finish with what you think the film gets across about time that the arguments can't — I have a suspicion that a film can show you the feel of a fixed past in a way a proof never will, but I'd like to know whether that's a genuine point or just an old man being sentimental about a DeLorean.
