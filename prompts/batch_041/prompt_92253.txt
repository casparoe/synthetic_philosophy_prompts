Write a dialogue between two philosophers who accept the same facts about the sphex wasp but draw opposite lessons from it. The facts: a female wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it at the threshold, and goes inside to inspect the burrow before hauling the cricket in; if an experimenter nudges the cricket a few inches away while she is inside, she emerges, retrieves it, drags it back to the threshold, and is once again driven to inspect the burrow — a loop that can repeat many times before she takes the obvious shortcut. Both grant that after enough repetitions her behavior does eventually change, and that re-inspecting a disturbed nest may itself be adaptive.

The first philosopher treats the case as a mirror held up to us: every apparently flexible act, human deliberation included, is a routine that fires when its conditions are met, and the wasp differs from us only in the size and nesting of its routines, so sphexishness is a matter of degree. The second insists the difference is one of kind: the wasp's routine is keyed to a fixed feature and cannot take itself as an object, whereas we can represent a routine, compare it with the end it is meant to serve, and rewrite it — and it is this loop, not the size of the state space, that makes talk of freedom non-vacuous.

Have each press the other on where exactly the line falls; on the regress objection that whatever does the revising is itself a routine, so the wasp has merely been pushed further up the stack; on whether the power to revise means while one's ends stay fixed (as the wasp's ends are) suffices for anything worth calling free will, or whether freedom requires revising ends as well; and on what evidence would settle the dispute. Neither should be a strawman, and each should answer the other's actual last point rather than restate a position.
