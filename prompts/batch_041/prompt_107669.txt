I retired from a chemistry department about six years ago — thirty-odd years of spectroscopy and crystallography, which meant a lot of group theory and Fourier analysis — and since then I've been reading alot of philosophy of science, enthusiastically and not very systematically. There's a phrase that keeps turning up in the books I read and at the little reading group I've joined, and I've started to notice how much work people get out of it: the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics. It's credited to Wigner, and in practice it's usually the last word in an arguement rather than the first move in one. One man in the group takes it to show that the world simply is mathematical all the way down, so that physicists are discovering rather than inventing; another takes it to show we'll never have a final theory, because the fit between maths and nature is a permanent miracle; a third says it's an argument for God, which I didn't follow at all. I've also seen it waved at the suggestion that mathematics is a human convention, as though the phrase disposes of that on its own. And when I offered my own field as the obvious case — that Galois's group theory, invented to settle a question about polynomial equations, turned out to be the natural language for molecular spectra and orbitals — nobody seemed to think I'd added anything, so I suspect I'm missing what the phrase is actually supposed to be claiming.

What I want isn't a defence of one reading, it's a menu. What are the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, from the ones that are nearly trivial to the ones that are heavy metaphysical bets? I'd like each one pinned down enough that I can say what would have to be the case for it to be true, and then your verdict on which are platitudes, which are serious and defensible, which are straightforwardly false, and which one the people around me most likely mean when they use it to close a discussion. Someone did once tell me the whole thing is a selection effect — that we only call a subject physics when it is already yielding to mathematics, and that we simply look away from the parts of nature that don't — and I couldn't tell whether that was a clever reply or a dodge. I've also heard the joke version, that the real puzzle is the unreasonable ineffectiveness of mathematics in economics, and I don't know whether that's a serious point or just a dig.

Please write it in American English; I've been reading mostly American journals for years now and it's what I'm used to. And please don't humour me if the question is badly posed — if it's really three or four questions wearing one coat, or if the honest answer is that the slogan does no work and I should stop letting it impress me, say so plainly rather than writing a tidy essay around it, and tell me where your confidence is thin and where you're reporting a live disagreement among philosophers rather than anything settled.
