I'm a science journalist at work on a long feature about the claim that what a person believes can change what they literally perceive — the phenomenon usually discussed under the heading of cognitive penetration. I have the colourful examples well in hand (the radiologist who finds what the referral note suggests, the diner who tastes what the label promises), and I don't need more of those, nor a literature survey. What I need is a single conceptual knot untied slowly and carefully.

Here is the knot. Any such case can be described in at least two ways that predict the very same reports. On the first, the belief altered the content of the perceptual experience itself, so the subject genuinely saw something different. On the second, the experience was exactly what it would otherwise have been, and the belief altered only the subject's interpretation, judgement, or readiness to say what they saw. Call these the perceptual hypothesis and the judgemental hypothesis.

Please explain, at whatever level of detail the material requires:

First, what each hypothesis is actually a claim about — the content of experience, the causal process that produced it, the subject's evidence, or merely the subject's dispositions to act and report — and where the difference between them is a real difference in what is happening rather than two descriptions of the same happening.

Second, whether the two are empirically distinguishable at all, and if so, by what. Are there downstream signatures that only a genuine change in perceptual content could produce — effects on adaptation or aftereffects, on discrimination performance, on tasks that don't require the belief in question — or does the reliance on the subject's own report make the distinction undecidable in principle?

Third, a case I keep returning to and can't resolve: what should we make of a perceptual effect that outlives the belief that produced it? If a subject's experience snaps back the moment the belief is corrected, that looks like the belief functioning as a context cue; if it persists after the belief is abandoned, that looks less like penetration and more like perceptual learning, or like a shifted criterion for reporting. Does the persistence or collapse of the effect after belief revision tell us which hypothesis is right, or does it merely relocate the puzzle?

Fourth, how much the mediating route matters. Must the belief influence the experience directly, or does it count as penetration when it works through attention, expectation, or years of training? What turns on insisting that the route be non-attentional, and does that insistence make the phenomenon rarer than the popular discussion assumes?

Finally, why the answer matters outside the seminar room: for eyewitness testimony, for how much weight to give expert perceptual judgement, and for the broader idea that perception is a neutral tribunal which belief can only misreport.

I want the conceptual structure, the trade-offs involved in defining the phenomenon one way rather than another, and a clear verdict on which of the two hypotheses, if either, is doing genuine explanatory work.
