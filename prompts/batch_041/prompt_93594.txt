I serve on the scientific advisory board of a small foundation that has earmarked about three million dollars over four years for "the philosophy and foundations of mathematics" — meaning conceptual work on what mathematical knowledge is and how it is secured, not logic in the technical sense and not mathematics itself. Our program officer has asked each board member for a strategy memo, and I've been putting mine off partly because the last meeting turned into an argument. The set theorist on the board insists that the only real questions are internal to mathematics, that anything a philosopher could contribute about, say, the continuum hypothesis has already been settled one way or the other by independence results and the multiverse debates, and that funding philosophers here is a way of paying people to write about mathematics instead of doing it. I think that's wrong, but I want a memo that answers him rather than one that ignores him, and I'd rather have an honest ranking than a hedge that funds everything a little. Assume the audience is philosophers and philosophically-minded mathematicians, none of whom will be given a reading list — the memo has to stand on its own reasoning.

So: which open conceptual questions in this area most deserve the money, and in what order? Here are the candidates I've been chewing on, though I don't want to be confined to them — (a) the epistemic status of computer-assisted and machine-checked proof, where the gap between what any human can survey and what the community actually accepts keeps widening, and where "we ran it and it returned true" may be doing work no epistemology of mathematics has accounted for; (b) whether set-theoretic independence signals genuine indeterminacy in the subject matter or only a choice among frameworks, and correlatively whether there is a fact of the matter about "the" universe of sets; (c) mathematical explanation — whether there is a robust, non-pragmatic distinction between proofs that explain and proofs that merely establish, and whether anyone has a criterion that survives the standard counterexamples; (d) the social epistemology of acceptance, including testimony, refereeing, and the several cases in living memory where a result was accepted on authority for years before anyone checked the details; (e) whether the applicability of mathematics to physics confers any warrant on mathematical claims, or whether the "unreasonable effectiveness" is a fact about our theories rather than about mathematics. For each question, tell me how much other work depends on its answer, how tractable it is for people whose training is philosophical rather than mathematical, whether it's already crowded or genuinely neglected, and — this is the part I care about most — what would count as evidence over the next few years that the question has moved up or down the ranking. Finish by naming what you would deprioritize and why, including at least one item from my list that you think is a dead end, and give me the criteria you're using in an explicit order with the trade-offs between them stated, so that the set theorist can see exactly where he and I are disagreeing rather than just that we are.
