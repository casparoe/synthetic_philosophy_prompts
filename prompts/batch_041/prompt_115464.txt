I have just been appointed to the restitution advisory panel of the small municipal museum where I have worked as a curator for six years, and I am the newest member, which means my first contested case arrives next week. Before I vote, I want to be sure I understand the logic the panel has actually been running, rather than the logic it says it is running.

You are a philosopher of cultural property and museum ethics, and I would like you to act as my analyst. Please begin by brainstorming the several general principles that could each explain the record below, then work through the case systematically rather than jumping to a verdict. I am less interested in a slogan than in a disciplined reconstruction.

Our panel's record, as the board has ratified it:

- Human remains excavated from a burial site during a colonial-era survey, with no permit and against the recorded objections of local elders: returned.
- A carved ancestral figure that a colonial administrator purchased from a local intermediary for a sum both sides called fair, but which the source community regards as a lineage guardian that can never properly be owned or sold: returned.
- A ceremonial staff seized as spoils during a punitive military raid: returned.
- A set of masks and regalia that the community itself later asked us to keep, arguing that its young people no longer know the practices and the objects are safer in our climate-controlled storage: retained.
- A collection of utilitarian household pottery and iron tools gathered by an anthropologist with documented consent and an export permit: retained.
- A sacred bundle that a trader obtained by threatening to cut off the community's access to the trading post unless it was handed over: returned.
- Botanical specimens and field photographs made collaboratively with a local guide, with a signed agreement: retained.

The new case: a ceremonial object of the same inalienable kind as the ancestral figure and the sacred bundle, one that the community describes as never having been legitimately transferable. It entered our collection in 1931 through an open, documented sale at a fair price, paid to a hereditary custodian who signed a receipt, with an export permit issued by the then-colonial administration. There was no threat, no raid, and no fraud that anyone has alleged. The descendants of the source community are numerous, organized, and petitioning for its return, and they say the custodian had no right to sell what was never his to sell.

Predict what our panel will decide if it is to be consistent with its own record. Identify the candidate principles the record is compatible with, state what each predicts about the new case, give me your best guess together with the reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would best distinguish the surviving principles from one another. Finally, tell me plainly what principle we would be adopting if we return the object and what principle we would be adopting if we decline, so that I know what my vote commits us to.
