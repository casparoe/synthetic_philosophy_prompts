I retired a few years ago after a career in field ecology—mostly long-term studies of small mammal populations in fragmented forests—and I have spent the last two years reading moral philosophy, especially the literature on wild animal suffering. Over dinner with former colleagues, I found myself defending the uncomfortable thought that habitat destruction might, under certain narrow welfare calculations, reduce the total suffering of wild animals, and I was struck by how quickly the conversation became a shouting match about biodiversity, ecosystem health, and whether a forest can be said to have a welfare at all. I would like to turn that discomfort into a satirical piece for a small, philosophically minded conservation newsletter, and I would like you to draft it.

The form should be a mock environmental impact statement—roughly 900 to 1100 words, with numbered sections, a findings summary, and a mitigation plan—written in the deadpan bureaucratic register of a fictional regulator, the Office of Net Ecological Welfare. Assume the project under review is a proposed conversion of a species-rich lowland forest to a plantation, with a mitigation plan involving relocation and a biodiversity offset. The target is the practice of treating ecosystem-level metrics such as biodiversity, biomass, trophic complexity, and resilience as proxies for the welfare of individual animals, together with the mirror-image confidence of some utilitarian calculations that habitat destruction is straightforwardly good because it reduces the number of suffering lives. The piece should follow a net-wild-animal-welfare assessment to a conclusion the reader will find unsettling, but the humor must come from getting the ecology and the philosophy right rather than from inventing absurdities; the document should never break frame or wink at the reader. It should engage, at least implicitly, with r/K selection and density-dependent mortality; compensatory mortality; the predation problem, meaning whether suffering caused by predation belongs in the ledger; the replaceability problem, meaning whether animals lost are replaced by others whose lives are no better, so that the net welfare change is not simply the sum of the deaths; the invertebrate blind spot; the difference between ecosystem health and individual welfare; and the question of whether extinction can be a welfare improvement for anyone. Include a dissenting footnote or sidebar from a fictional conservation biologist who is horrified by the findings, and let that objection be at least partly question-begging, so that the reader has to work out what is really at stake. Do not cite any literature, and do not include a bibliography or reading list; the satire should be legible to someone who knows the wild-animal-suffering debates and readable to a conservationist who does not.
