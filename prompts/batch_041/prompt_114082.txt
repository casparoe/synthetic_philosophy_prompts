I host a small podcast about the conceptual foundations of physics — a few hundred listeners, mostly engineers, a handful of physics graduate students, and one old college friend who is now a philosopher and who is the reason the show exists at all. Next month I am recording an episode with a philosopher of physics who has just published a book-length study of Gödel's argument against the reality of time, and I will be interviewing her for roughly fifty minutes, of which maybe twenty will be my questions. My degree is in computer science and I have never taken a philosophy course; I have read her book once and would say I followed about seventy percent of it. So please assume that the questions themselves have to carry the conceptual weight, rather than my framing of them, and phrase them so that I can read them aloud without stumbling.

Her position, as best I can reconstruct it, is this. Gödel's 1949 rotating solution to Einstein's field equations — the swirling-dust universe that is homogeneous but has one distinguished axis, and that contains closed timelike curves, so that a traveller who is patient enough can loop back into his own past — was meant to show that time is not objectively real. On her reconstruction the argument runs in three steps: if time is objectively real, there must be a single, observer-independent division of events into past, present and future, a global now; general relativity contains no such structure, and Gödel's solution makes this vivid, since the theory permits a spacetime in which a global now cannot even be defined consistently around a loop; therefore the structure that time's reality would require is absent from the world that our best theory describes. Her objection is not that the conclusion is false but that the argument fails in an instructive way. She says the step from "the theory does not require this structure" to "the world lacks this structure" is illegitimate, and that the appeal to a merely possible solution — a solution of the equations, which is a mathematical object, not a demonstrated physical possibility — is doing work Gödel never licenses. She also insists the argument, if it worked, would prove the unreality of space just as well as of time, since that same solution gives no preferred way of carving space out either. Her own positive view, which is what I would most like to get out of her on air, is that the real lesson concerns what relativity cannot express: the theory has no way of stating which events are occurring now, and she thinks the honest response is to call the theory incomplete rather than to call the now an illusion. She labels this the expressive-gap reading, and pairs it with a version of the idea that becoming is relative, so that each observer has his own present along his own worldline — though she concedes in the book that this last piece is the weakest thing in it.

Could you draft eight to ten interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that when she replies in the expected way I have something to reach for and can push once more? I would like at least one question on the modal step — why a solution of the field equations should tell us anything about what is possible in the world, and what she would count as a solution that fails to do so; at least one on the space-for-time symmetry she thinks sinks the argument, including what she makes of the reply that space and time are disanalogous precisely because only one of them is supposed to have a flowing present; and at least one on whether the expressive-gap complaint is even coherent — what a theory that closed the gap would look like, given that any theory's claims have to be written down in tenseless mathematics. I would also like something that forces her to defend the relativized-becoming part against the objection that a present which differs for every observer is not a present at all but a relabelling of the tenseless picture she rejects; something on whether Gödel actually needs our universe to rotate, since I gather observational cosmology has constrained any rotation of the cosmos very tightly, and I want to know whether that is fatal to the argument or whether he only ever needed the solution to be permitted; and something on the asymmetry I keep returning to as a layman, namely why memory runs in one direction and not the other, and why entropy increases rather than decreases, if there is no objective now. If you can work it in, I would also like one question about how a mathematical Platonist like Gödel could think a purely mathematical solution to a set of equations carries any evidential weight about the temporal structure of the actual world. Please leave out anything that would merely have her summarize her first chapter, and no biographical or what-drew-you-to-this-problem questions — she is very good at those and they would waste the hour. Finally, if there is a question on the list that you think she has no good answer to and will have to concede something on, flag it and tell me why, because that is the moment I most want to be ready for.
