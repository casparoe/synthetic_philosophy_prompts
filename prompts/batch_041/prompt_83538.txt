I have no training in philosophy and no one in my life to argue with about this, so I have been working through it alone for some months. I would like you to take the role of a professional philosopher who has spent a career on free will and moral responsibility, and who knows the contemporary literature on both sides well enough to tell me which moves are live and which are dead. Please think carefully and slowly before committing to anything: begin by listing for yourself, in your own working, every step below that could conceivably be the weak one, along with what it would cost to abandon it, and only then choose.

I came across an argument, attributed to a philosopher named Galen Strawson, that no one is ever morally responsible for anything. I have reconstructed it in my own words. Read on its own, each step seems to me convincing; the conclusion I cannot accept.

(1) Whenever you act, you act as you do because of the way you are — your character, your tastes, your values, your reasons. Had you been a different person, you would have acted differently.
(2) Therefore, to be truly morally responsible for what you do, you must be truly morally responsible for the way you are, at least in certain crucial respects, since that is what produced the action.
(3) To be truly morally responsible for the way you are, you must have brought it about that you are that way — brought it about deliberately, on purpose, for reasons you endorsed at the time.
(4) But any such deliberate self-shaping is itself an action, and so by (1) it too flows from the way you already were. Whoever did the shaping was already someone in particular.
(5) So responsibility for the shaping act would require responsibility for the way you were when you did it, which would require a still earlier act of self-shaping, and so on with no possible stopping point.
(6) Nothing can be the cause of itself; no being can be, in the relevant respect, its own origin.
(7) Therefore the chain can never be grounded, and no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything.
(8) Nothing in the argument depended on whether determinism is true; the regress runs identically in an indeterministic world. So the whole quarrel between compatibilists and incompatibilists is beside the point — the obstacle is not causation but self-creation.

The conclusion strikes me as proving far too much, in three ways. First, consider a man who spends thirty years deliberately remaking his own character — the bigot who trains himself out of contempt, the drinker who rebuilds his habits from nothing. On this argument his effort can earn him nothing, because the person who made the effort was already given, and so was the person who made him. That seems to make the whole notion of self-improvement an illusion, and I suspect that is not something the argument's author intended to claim. Second, the same reasoning seems to run for beliefs: I cannot be responsible for what I believe, since my believing flows from how I am, and I did not choose how I am. If no one can be blamed or credited for a belief, then no one can be blamed for accepting or rejecting this very argument, and I cannot see what the point of accepting it would then be. Third, if the argument is right, no one is ever truly responsible, but also no one is ever truly not responsible, and no one is ever unjustly blamed — so praise and blame, gratitude and resentment, are not merely undeserved, they are not even the sort of thing that could be deserved. And yet the complaint I keep hearing underneath the argument, that it is unfair that some people got better natures than others, seems to presuppose the very notion of desert that (7) abolishes.

I would be very reluctant to give up (1) or (6), neither of which I can see how to deny, and (8) seems to me the most interesting and disturbing part of the whole thing.

What I want from you is a diagnosis. Which single step or transition should I reject — is there a genuine flaw somewhere, or is the argument valid and the real question merely which plausible premise is least costly to surrender? If you tell me a step is question-begging or equivocates, say precisely which premise begs which question, or which word is doing double duty and in which two senses. Then explain why that step looked so good to me, and price the alternatives: tell me what I have to give up if I deny it, which of the standard positions on responsibility can stop the regress without quietly reinventing self-creation, and whether the three worries above amount to a reductio of the argument or are simply bullets its author should bite. Do not spend time on whether the conclusion would be socially harmful; I care only about whether the argument is sound and where the cost falls.

End your reply with a single final line in exactly this form, with no words after it — Verdict: [step number] — [a label of three to eight words naming the flaw or the premise to abandon].
