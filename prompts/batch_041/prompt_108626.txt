I'm a freelance science journalist — long features, mostly philosophy and the life sciences — and I'm deep into a piece on insects and what we might owe them. Here's the thing I keep running into and I need help with.

Every single person I interview does the same move in the first ten minutes, more or less on a napkin: there are something like ten quintillion insects alive at any moment, nobody knows whether any of them feel anything, so put the chance at one in a hundred — or one in a thousand, doesn't matter — and multiply it out, and the expected amount of suffering in a sprayed field dwarfs anything humans have ever managed to inflict on each other. That's the whole argument. Vast numbers against a low probability of sentience. It comes out almost word for word from campaigners, from the entomologists who are sympathetic, even from a philosopher of biology who I thought would be the skeptic in the piece.

Then I called a decision theorist to sanity-check the napkin, and he wouldn't even engage — he said it's just Pascal's wager for bugs, and once you see that, you can throw it out. Which struck me as a bit of a drive-by. But I don't have the equipment to say whether he's right, and I'd rather not print either version of this without knowing what I'm printing.

So: I want you to audit that analogy properly, as an argument, not as a dunk. Take the role of a philosopher who works on decision theory and has also read the animal-sentience literature. Think carefully here, and don't hand me the standard line that expected value reasoning is controversial — I know that, I need the specifics.

Start by brainstorming: list the structural features of the wager as Pascal actually sets it up — the proposition, the payoff, the stake, the fact that you can't just decide to believe, the infinite-regress/many-gods business — and for each one say whether the insect argument shares it, lacks it, or is somewhere in between. Then give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of God, what plays the role of the payoff, the probability, the wager itself, the many gods. Then tell me where it breaks, and be ruthless about sorting the breaks that matter for what the campaigners are using it for from the ones that are just tidiness.

Three things I specifically want you to chew on:

The probability isn't a single number. As far as I can tell it's the output of a checklist — nociception versus actual pain, opioid receptors and whether analgesics do anything, whether the animal trades off avoiding a bad stimulus against some other motivation, avoidance learning — and those criteria are contested and half of them are behavioural. So in the wager you've got a credence over one binary proposition; here you've got something that comes out of an argument by analogy. Does that change what the multiplication even means?

The many-gods problem. Does it map onto the obvious objection — fine, but what about mites, nematodes, and while we're at it plants? I've heard a reply that the many-gods problem only bites when the candidate gods want different things from you, so if every candidate mind-type points at the same actions, the symmetry doesn't matter. Is that a real escape hatch or does it just move the problem?

There's no mugger. In the wager someone is offering the deal, and there's a whole literature about how that kind of reasoning makes you exploitable. Here there's nobody offering anything — just a lot of us with flyswatters and a pesticide budget. Does that make the insect version stronger than the wager, or weaker?

Then the payoff question: does the debunk survive the audit? If the analogy collapses, does the insect argument come back to life as-is, or does it only support something weaker — a precautionary thing, or just a big research budget, since unlike Pascal we can actually go find out? And if the wager is the wrong analogy, what's the least-bad right one? Options I've seen floated are the wallet mugging, insurance, a lottery ticket, the precautionary principle about a new chemical. Pick one and tell me where it fails too.

Keep it tight, I need it for my own head more than for the page, but end with the two or three claims that are actually carrying the whole insect argument, so I know where to push when I'm interviewing.
