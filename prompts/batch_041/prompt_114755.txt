Bit of a weird one, and I'm not sure it even has a clean answer. I'm a backend engineer, no real philosophy training beyond one intro course I half-remember, and I've been reading around the WWI war-guilt fight — Fritz Fischer and the whole storm over whether Germany (or "the German leadership", or just "Germany") was to blame for 1914, the Kriegsschuldfrage, Article 231, all of that — and a friend who did a history degree handed me the following argument, which I wrote down pretty much verbatim because I honestly couldn't tell if it was clever or just broken. It's meant to show we should stop blaming nations for wars altogether:

(1) You can only blame someone for a war if that someone is an agent — if there's a fact of the matter about what they decided and intended.
(2) Decisions and intentions live in individual minds. "Germany", "the Allies", "the nation" aren't minds; they're labels for millions of people plus an apparatus, most of whom did nothing and wanted nothing of the sort.
(3) So nations as such can't be blamed.
(4) Fine — blame the individuals who actually decided: the Kaiser, the Chancellor, Moltke and the generals, and on the other side Poincaré, Grey, and so on.
(5) But every one of those individuals decided under radical uncertainty about what the others would do, and the war that actually happened — the trenches, the millions dead, four years of it — was nobody's plan and nobody's aim.
(6) And each individual's contribution was, on its own, replaceable: had any one of them decided differently, the machine plausibly rolls on anyway, because the others were already committed and the alliances were already in motion.
(7) So no individual caused it either, in the sense that matters for blame.
(8) Therefore nobody is to blame for the First World War, and "war guilt" is a category error from top to bottom.

I'd quite like to keep (1) and (2) — the thought that blame needs an agent feels right to me — and I'd cheerfully give up (8), which seems obviously false given that the war definately happened and somebody did it. But I can't see which of the middle steps is doing the damage, or whether the whole thing is actually valid and it's one of my two keepers that has to go. So please act like a philosopher of history who works on collective and moral responsibility, think it through carefully, and start by brainstorming the candidate trouble spots before you commit to one. Then tell me which step to reject, why it looked fine to me, and what each available exit actually costs.
