ok, I need help with something that's been nagging me. I keep running into dutch book arguments — in podcasts, in footnotes, from a friend who's doing a PhD — and they all sound equally crushing when someone says them out loud, but I suspect they're not all doing the same amount of work. I've wrote out six of them below in my own words (some reconstructed, some half-remembered, at least one I made up myself). Can you sort them into tiers (top tier = this really does force the conclusion on anyone who grants the setup; bottom = this is a fancy way of restating an assumption), and order them within each tier? Ties are fine if two of them are the same arguemnt. For each one: a sentence or two on what it actually gets you if it works, and a sentence or two on the most serious place to push back — including, where relevant, whether it survives someone saying "but the offer itself tells me something" or "I just won't bet". Please don't tell me they're all valid; I'm asking because I think a couple of them are doing less than they look like they're doing.

1. The plain one. If my credences break the probability axioms, there's a collection of bets, each of which I'd call fair, such that I lose whatever happens.

2. Same thing with no bookie. Nobody offers me anything: the claim is just that a violation means my own judgements about fair prices can't all be right together, so the incoherence is in me rather than in some betting set-up.

3. The one about changing my mind. Suppose I'm certain now that when I learn A I'll update by a rule that isn't conditionalizing. Then there's a set of three bets — one on A, one that only goes live if A happens, and a third offered after A has come in and I've updated my stated way — such that I lose whatever happens. (I think the whole thing leans on my being certain in advance how I'll update, which seems like a big assumption.)

4. The one about my future self. I'm certain that at midnight I'll have credence 0.8 in A. Right now I have credence 0.4. So a bookie can buy a bet from me now at 0.4 and sell the same bet back to me at 0.8 at midnight: either way I'm down 0.4. Moral: if I'm sure what I'll believe later, I should already believe it now.

5. The infinite one. Every point in my possibility space gets credence 0, but the space as a whole gets 1, and nothing stops that from being finitely additive. A bookie buys a bet from me on each point at my own price — i.e. for free — and collects 1 from whichever point turns out true. Except that's infinitely many bets, and I never actually lose anything at any finite stage. (Someone also told me that finite additivity is the whole story and countable additivity is an extra demand the betting picture never justified, which I don't fully follow.)

6. The one about vague credences. A friend says a bookie can clean out someone whose credence in A is the whole interval [0.2, 0.8] by betting at both ends of the interval. I tried to write it out and got the opposite: if she only takes a bet when it looks favourable at every probability in her set, she'll take a bet on A at 0.2 and a bet on not-A at 0.2, pay 0.4 total, and collect 1 — she's the one making money. Is the friend just wrong, or is there a version of this that works?

And finally: if you had to throw all but one of these away, which one survives? And tell me which item I've got wrong, if any.
