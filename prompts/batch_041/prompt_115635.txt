ok so context: I've been reading LW/ACX-adjacent blogs and forums for like ten years, I'm not a philosopher by training, but meta-ethics is the one I keep getting dragged into in comment sections, so this is a thing I actually care about getting right rather than a topic I'm just using as set dressing.

I want you to write me a parody, and I want the joke to be load-bearing rather than vibes. Here's what I keep noticing. Expressivism — the view that when I say lying is wrong I'm not reporting a fact, I'm giving voice to a conative attitude, roughly the way booing at something isn't a claim about it — has the famous problem that Frege and Geach are the names for. Whatever that moral phrase contributes has to contribute the exact same thing when the sentence is unasserted: as the antecedent of a conditional (if lying is wrong then getting your little brother to lie is wrong), under negation, inside a disjunction, inside a question, inside a clause of an HR policy. If it doesn't, modus ponens stops being valid for moral arguments, and the whole point of moral argument — that some moral claims actually follow from others — collapses. Then there's the negation problem on top of that: what is the attitude in lying is not wrong, exactly? And the standard patches — Hare's split between the descriptive component and the attitude, higher-order attitudes like disapproving of a disapproval, Gibbard-style norms, Schroeder-style being-for operators — all look, from the outside, like increasingly baroque bookkeeping whose function is to earn back the right to talk exactly the way everybody already talks anyway.

So here's the bit. Write the code of conduct and values page of a company whose official meta-ethics is expressivism. Invented company, obviously. Around 700 words, as numbered clauses with a short Legal and Compliance FAQ tacked on at the end.

Rules for it: deadpan, no winking, no narrator leaning in to tell me it's a joke, no line where the company admits the problem in so many words. The comedy has to come out of the real machinery. So: the clauses that work beautifully as bare exhortations, and then the same values having to appear inside conditionals and negations and questions — the supplier clause, the retaliation and whistleblower clauses, the bit of the interview rubric that says what to do with a candidate whose integrity you're unsure of, the FAQ question about whether the company's values are true, the annual attestation form. Let the patches be recognizably the real ones, so the Legal footnotes are where the laughs live. It should be funny to someone who has read any of this stuff and still followable as a comedy about compliance by someone who hasn't.

Then, clearly separated from the parody, a short note in your own voice, 150 words or so: which horn of the Frege-Geach problem the gag actually rests on, where the parody is least fair to a real expressivist — i.e. what the best version of the reply would say about that specific clause, not the reply in general — and what would change your mind about whether it's a fair hit at all. If you think the whole premise is a cheap shot dressed up as a joke, just say that instead of hedging. No sources needed, just the piece and the note.
