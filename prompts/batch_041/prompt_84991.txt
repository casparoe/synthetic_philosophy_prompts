I'm in a second-year seminar on judgement and decision making and next week it's my turn to open the session — the instructor likes us to start with something that isn't a paper, people have done little games and case studies, and I want to do a poem instead. The week's topic is satisficing and bounded rationality, and the thing I actually want the poem to get at is the part that gets skipped whenever anyone says "good enough": satisficing is a stopping rule, not a mood. You search through options until one of them clears your aspiration level, and then you stop — and the aspiration level isn't fixed, it slides with how the search has been going, dropping when nothing clears it and creeping up when things come easily. I find that alot more interesting than the version where it's just people being lazy, and it's the version where the whole thing stops looking like a failure to optimize and starts looking like a model of how anyone decides anything.

So: a poem, maybe 16-20 lines, no fixed form, in the voice of someone who has been searching for something — you pick the something, a flat, a person, the right sentence, whatever gives you room to move — where the form carries the theory instead of describing it. What I'm asking for specifically: one line that comes back more than once, and each time it comes back its standard has moved. The first time it should be pitched somewhere the speaker could never actually reach, then revised down after the search goes badly, and by the end it should be something the speaker can say and then stop on — the line going still is the search stopping, and I want that to land as relief and loss at the same time, not as a punchline about settling. Someone who's read Simon should recognize the shape without the poem ever saying his name; someone who hasn't should just find it a good poem. And please dont make the speaker into either a smug defender of the heuristic or a sad case who gave up on excellence — the point is that it's neither, and if the poem ends up leaning on one side of the argument about whether heuristics are rational adaptations to real environments or just cheap shortcuts, that's fine, just let it know it's doing that.

Start with the poem itself, no preface and no explanation before it, and then afterwards give me a short note on where you took liberties with the theory or leaned on a contested reading of it — and say how confident you are in what the poem is claiming about satisficing, and which single bit of it you're least sure about.
