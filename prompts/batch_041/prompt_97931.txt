Ok, so a friend of mine is convinced epiphenomenalism is self-refuting and built this whole argument to prove it. Epiphenomenalism, for anyone lucky enough not to have argued about this at a party, is the view that experiences are real but causally inert -- the brain does all the causing, and the feel of red just rides along without doing any work. I wrote his argument out as numbered steps and now I honestly can't find the exit. Here it is:

(1) If epiphenomenalism is true, phenomenal properties never cause anything -- not other phenomenal properties, not brain states, not anything I say or do.
(2) My reporting that something looks red is a physical event and it has physical causes.
(3) So if epiphenomenalism is true, the causes of my report are exhausted by prior physical states; the redness-quale contributes nothing to producing it.
(4) So the neural process that produced my report would have produced it anyway, whether or not the quale was there, and whatever it was like.
(5) So my report isn't sensitive to the phenomenal character it's supposedly about.
(6) A judgment that isn't sensitive to the state it's about can't be a reliable judgment about that state, and an unreliable judgment isn't knowledge.
(7) So if epiphenomenalism is true, none of my judgments about my own experience are knowledge.
(8) But I do know what I'm experiencing, at least in the basic cases -- that this looks red, that this hurts.
(9) So epiphenomenalism is false.

Fine so far, and honestly I'd like (9). But here's my problem. Step (6), plus the general shape of (3)-(5), seems to kill the barometer too. A barometer tells me a storm is coming. The storm doesn't cause the barometer reading -- the falling pressure causes both the storm and the reading. Nobody thinks the barometer can't give me knowledge of the storm just because the storm isn't the thing pushing the needle. But the steps seem to say the reading isn't sensitive to the storm, only to the pressure, so it can't be knowledge. That's crazy -- barometers obviously tell you about the weather.

So either the argument is broken or I'm definately missing something. Which step do I drop, why did it look so reasonable, and what do I pay for dropping it? And if the fix is something like "a common cause is enough for reliability, you don't need the state itself to be doing the causing" -- does that actually save epiphenomenalism, or does it only save the cheap version and leave the real problem standing? Because it feels like there's still something lurking underneath, namely why my judgments about my experiences would line up with those experiences at all if the experiences aren't doing anything. I'd rather keep (8) than (1), but I want to know what I'm buying.
