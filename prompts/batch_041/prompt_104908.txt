I write a column on science and ideas, and I have been asked to run a session for a graduate reading group in decision theory and the philosophy of probability — about eighteen people, one ninety-minute meeting, most of them fluent in expected-value reasoning but none of them specialists in the fanaticism literature. I want to build the session around a runnable game rather than a talk, because Pascal's mugging is the sort of argument that looks like a parlour trick on the page and only bites when you are the one who has to decide. The design I am reaching for is this: the group commits, in advance and in writing, to a betting policy — a rule for when to accept a gamble and when to refuse — and is then run through a sequence of offers that tests it, beginning with ordinary gambles where the rule behaves sensibly, and ending with a mugger who asks for a trivial, merely inconvenient sacrifice in exchange for a minuscule chance of something inconceivably large (a one-in-ten-to-the-hundred chance of saving that many lives, or a comparably absurd payoff), and who can always scale the payoff up until it defeats whatever probability threshold the group has written down. I want them to feel the two-way squeeze: that expected-value maximisation obliges them to accept, whilst refusing seems to require either an arbitrary cutoff, a lexicographic or otherwise non-Archimedean ranking in which some goods are simply not tradable against the enormous one, a cap on the payoff, or abandoning expected-value maximisation outright — and I want the game to make the last of those feel less like a cop-out than it usually does. Please give me the full design: the rules, the materials, a payoff scheme that works without real money (I would rather not have people staking actual cash), the sequence of offers with the numbers I should use, and a debrief that maps each round onto a specific move in the argument.

Two complications I would like the activity to expose rather than smooth over. First, the one-shot versus repeated case: a policy that accepts a single mugging appears to license accepting indefinitely many, so that the trivial sacrifices compound into everything one has, and I would like a round that makes that arithmetic visceral — ideally one where the mugger's prize starts to dominate every ordinary good the participants might otherwise have pursued, in the manner of a utility monster. Second, the fact that the mugger's probability is stipulated rather than measured — the number is asserted by the party who stands to benefit from it — and I want a round in which the group has to decide how much credence to give an unverifiable claim made by an interested party, so that the activity engages the epistemic side rather than treating the probability as given. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be candid about the one thing this format cannot reproduce — I suspect it is the sheer scale of the numbers, which no classroom payoff can make genuinely gripping, so I should like to know how you would paper over that gap and what will still not land. Finally, flag any point at which the game's own incentives start pulling the group towards a conclusion the argument does not actually support.
