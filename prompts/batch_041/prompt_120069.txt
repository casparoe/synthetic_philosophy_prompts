Modern patent systems resolve competing claims to the same invention with priority rules: the patent goes to whoever filed first, and the invention is treated as the work of a determinate individual or team who can then exclude others from it. Yet the same systems refuse patents for inventions that a person skilled in the art would find obvious given the prior art, and the history of science and technology is full of near-simultaneous arrivals — the calculus, natural selection, the telephone, the incandescent lamp — that suggest many inventions would have appeared on roughly the same schedule whoever happened to get there first. What conception of discovery does this practice presuppose: that inventions are attributable to particular minds in a way that makes the first filer's claim to exclusive control coherent, or merely that the law needs some determinate tie-breaker and is otherwise indifferent to who deserves credit?
