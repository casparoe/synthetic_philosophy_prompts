I am the rapporteur of the Constitutional Sovereignty Advisory Council, a standing body that advises our parliament on whether draft instruments of the European Union are compatible with the constitutional settlement under which our state acceded. That settlement is deliberately double-edged: a single article commits us to the pursuit of ever closer union and, in the same breath, reserves to the state those competences held to constitute its national identity. The Council has never been given a rule of priority between the two halves, and it has never articulated one.

Six draft opinions fall in the present term. I must write the sixth, and I would like an independent prediction of how the Council will decide it, on the assumption that it decides consistently with the five opinions it has already issued.

The five earlier opinions:

1. A regulation on cross-border data flows, adopted by qualified majority, which left enforcement priorities to national supervisory authorities, from which our state retained a standing opt-out, and which our government supported. Held compatible.

2. A directive imposing a single statutory minimum wage by a Union-wide formula, adopted by qualified majority over our government's objection, displacing the national wage-bargaining framework that the identity clause names, and containing no opt-out. Held incompatible.

3. A regulation pooling bank-resolution costs into a common fund, adopted by qualified majority, covering a sector the identity clause does not name, and containing a permanent opt-out our negotiators secured; our government supported it once the opt-out was in hand. Held compatible.

4. A proposal to move decisions on the corporate tax base to qualified majority, stripping our veto; taxation is named in the identity clause, there was no opt-out, and our government objected. Held incompatible.

5. A directive binding national energy policy to a Union-wide emissions cap, adopted by qualified majority, covering a sector the identity clause does not name, and providing a right of withdrawal on notice; our government supported it. Held compatible.

The sixth: a directive establishing a Union-wide duty of care for large online platforms, adopted by qualified majority over our government's objection. The sector is not named in the identity clause. The directive hands enforcement to a new Union agency rather than to national regulators. It contains no opt-out, no withdrawal clause, and no realistic route of reversal except departure from the Union. Commentators have already complained that the Council has never once addressed the democratic-deficit objection to transferring enforcement to Union bodies, not even in the opinions it approved.

Predict the Council's verdict on the sixth. Then set out the rule, or the several rules, that would equally well explain the five earlier opinions — I suspect there is more than one, and I would rather see them all than a single tidy one — and say what each predicts for the sixth. Tell me which of them you would bet the Council is actually running, and why. Finally, name the further case that would most cleanly separate the rules you have identified, and say what each rule would say about it. I am interested less in which side wins than in what the Council has unknowingly committed itself to.
