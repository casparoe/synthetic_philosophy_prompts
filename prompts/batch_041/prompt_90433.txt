I am putting together the opening handout for a graduate seminar on time, modality, and the open future, and I want the first session to be diagnostic rather than expository. Please build me a decision tree — a flowchart, not an essay — that carries a student from a single starting commitment to a named position on the old sea-battle puzzle. Take the starting point to be the bare admission that sentences about what will happen can be assessed for truth and falsity at all; from there the tree should proceed by questions that genuinely divide the positions, and only hang the labels on the leaves once the logical space has been laid out. Every internal node should be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to — nothing of the form "are you an Ockhamist?" — and each branch should be labeled with the answer that splits it. I want the tree to force three distinctions in particular: between treating the openness of the future as a semantic matter (a gap or indeterminacy in truth value) and treating it as a metaphysical matter (indeterminism, branching, or the sheer absence of fact); between views on which the truth of a future-tense claim is grounded in something already present (a divine decree, a causal disposition, a settled "thin red line" of fact) and views on which it is grounded in the future itself; and between views that merely deny us knowledge of a determinate future and views that deny the future is determinate at all. Please make sure the leaves include at least the Aristotelian position, Ockhamism, Peirceanism, a Łukasiewicz-style many-valued treatment, supervaluationism about future contingents, the thin red line, and an open-future or open-theism position, and use two cases to drive the branches rather than the sea battle alone: an ordinary prediction about a future sea battle, and a prediction about the outcome of a genuinely indeterministic process such as a radioactive decay or a fair quantum measurement.

Where two leaves come out looking alike on the cases, say so rather than inventing a distinction to keep them apart, and where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, mark it and diagnose why — is the view incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply underexplored? I would also like you to flag the point in the tree where a student's answers most depend on a contested reading of what the question is even asking, since I suspect some of these disagreements are about the semantics of future-tense language while others are about the metaphysics of time, and the tree should not quietly conflate the two. Finally, tell me where the instrument is most likely to mis-sort a student or to beg a question against one of the positions, and what argument or consideration would force me to redraw the tree — I would rather know its weak joints now than discover them in seminar.
