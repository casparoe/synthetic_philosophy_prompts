Right, this is going to be a bit long, sorry, but the payoff's worth it. Bit of context: I work on the theory end of archaeology (I'm not out there with a trowel), and I keep banging into the same fight about how you get from objects to beliefs and institutions — from pots and burials and house floors to claims like 'these people practised ancestor veneration' or 'this was a market' or 'there was a centralised authority'. Everyone agrees you can't just read a belief off a pot. The row is about what licences the leap, and I can't seperate the good moves from the bad ones.

My mate Dan built this argument for a seminar and it's been living in my head rent-free, because every step looks fine and the conclusion is mad. Tidied up:

(1) For objects to count as evidence of a shared belief or an institution, that belief or institution has to be part of the best explanation of why the objects are the way they are.
(2) The best explanation is the most parsimonious one, and parsimony is about what you posit. A shared belief is one more thing to posit; a shared institution is one more thing to posit.
(3) Now take any single object. You can always explain that one object without positing any shared belief or institution: it's one person's habit, or an accident, or the ground doing something, or later people having shifted it about. None of those stories posit a shared belief or institution at all.
(4) So for a whole assemblage: explain each object that way and you've explained the assemblage, still without positing any shared belief or institution.
(5) So a story with no shared belief or institution in it is always at least as parsimonious as one that has one, and usually more so.
(6) So you should never infer a shared belief or an institution from objects.
(7) But that's absurd — it'd kill off the archaeology of religion, of trade, of anything social, and the same reasoning would scupper geology and forensics too.

I'd definately like to keep (1), (2) and (3); they feel like the actual content. And I can't use (7) as my reason for rejecting (5) or (6), because 'I don't like where this ends up' isn't an argument and I know it. So: is some step doing something dodgy I'm not seeing, or is the thing valid and I've just got to swallow a premise — and if it's that, which one costs me least, and what exactly am I giving up about how inference to the best explanation is meant to work? I want the price tag on each option, not just the one you'd go for, and I want to know whether the fix is cheap (I mis-stated something) or expensive (I have to change my whole picture of what archaeological inference even is). Be straight with me too about whether 'inference to the best explanation' is the right frame for this at all, or whether that's the thing quietly causing all the trouble. British English please, I'm a pedant about it.
