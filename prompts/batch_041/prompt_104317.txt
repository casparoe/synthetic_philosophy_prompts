Here's the spec. Build two cases side by side, holding as much fixed as you can. Case A: a creature has the same first-order sensory state a normal perceiver would have, but nothing higher-order going on about it. Case B: the creature has a genuine higher-order representation of that first-order state, but the representation gets the first-order state wrong — it's about a state the creature isn't actually in. Make Case B the interesting one for the higher-order thought view (the Rosenthal-ish line that a state is conscious only when there's a higher-order representation of it).

Constraints: keep it realistic — a neurology ward or a psych clinic, no brains in vats, no perfect mind-readers, no exotic physics. One or two paragraphs, tops. And the higher-order element has to count as a real higher-order representation of the first-order state, not just something downstream of it that happens to correlate with it, otherwise a defender will just say "thats not a higher-order thought, thats a symptom."

Then do the check for me. For each case, spell out the first-order state, the higher-order state, what the higher-order thought view says the creature is conscious of, and what a plain first-order representationalist says instead. Tell me which single feature of the setup is doing the work, what happens if I drop the misrepresentation and make the higher-order representation accurate, and how a defender of the higher-order view would try to save it — e.g. by denying the case counts, or by tacking on an extra condition about what makes a representation higher-order. 

One thing to keep in mind: the whole point is to test whether the higher-order representation is doing any work on its own, so dont just stipulate that the creature is or isnt conscious in each case — let the verdicts fall out of the two theories, and tell me honestly if they dont.
