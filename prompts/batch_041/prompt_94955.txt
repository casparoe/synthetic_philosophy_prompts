I run grantmaking at a small family foundation — roughly $2M a year to give away, individual grants between $5k and $400k, decided by me plus a five-person board that's mostly retired business people, one economist, nobody who's read any philosophy since college. Over the past year a growing slice of our proposals has started to feel like a different species from the rest. A group wants $250k to work on a technical problem where they claim there's maybe a one-in-ten-million chance of averting something very bad and permanent. Somebody else wants a small "seed" grant for a project whose stated payoff is, honestly, civilization-scale. And then the ordinary stuff — a literacy program, a vaccine delivery charity, a land trust — where I can more or less see what we're buying. My problem is I have no rule for comparing these, and the way I've been doing it, eyeballing "how real does this feel," is just my gut with a spreadsheet stapled to it. A friend in effective altruism told me the name for what's happening to me is fanaticism and that once you take expected value seriously you basically have to fund the long shots; our economist then said something similar but concluded the opposite, muttering about utility functions having to be bounded or you get absurdities. The board also has this informal thing where we never fund anything we estimate below about one-in-a-million, and I genuinely can't tell if that's a principle or just a superstition we all agreed to.

I want you to turn whatever the philosophy of this actually says into a procedure I can run on a proposal in about twenty minutes, before I take it to the board. It needs to say, in order: what I compute; what I refuse to compute and why; what I do when the probability isn't a number anyone derived but a vibe (a one-in-ten-million is almost always a vibe); where the procedure stops with a clean no; and where it hands the decision to the board because the question is values, not arithmetic. It has to survive the objections I actually get — that any nonzero chance of astronomical or infinite value dominates everything else, that a rule which discounts small probabilities would have missed penicillin or whatever, and that a rule which doesn't discount them commits us to funding anything a clever person can dress up as a long shot. It also has to be honest about the parts that are just me laundering a risk preference as math — I suspect the "one-in-a-million floor" is one of those. And I want the escape hatches named: the cases where the procedure says fund it and I'll hate it, and the ones where it says don't and I'll hate it more.

One more thing. The founder's letter that set us up says we should prefer the many certain small goods to the one uncertain great one, and the board treats that line like scripture; I'd like to know whether that's a defensible position or just an old man's risk aversion, and how much of it I can keep while still taking long shots seriously. So run the finished procedure on three proposals: (a) $250k for a lab working on a problem with a one-in-ten-million chance of averting a permanent catastrophe; (b) $100k for a proven deworming-style program with a large, well-measured but thoroughly ordinary benefit; (c) $50k for something whose applicants describe the upside as literally infinite — they mean it, and I don't even know how to file that. Tell me what the procedure spits out for each, where it's just my values wearing a lab coat, and what the whole thing definately can't do.
