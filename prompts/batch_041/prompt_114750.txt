I've been going back and forth on supererogation and I want to pin down what I actually think, because my gut keeps doing something wierd with certain cases and I can't tell if the gut or the theory is wrong.

The picture I was taught: an act is supererogatory when it's (a) not required, (b) morally good, and (c) praiseworthy. Fine. Here's my gut on a pile of cases.

- Someone runs into a burning building and pulls out a stranger's kid. Not required, heroic, supererogatory, no question.
- Same guy does it while terrified and nearly chickens out. Same verdict. The fear doesn't subtract anything, in my book.
- A man gives a kidney to a stranger and it's pretty clearly mainly to win a long-running argument with his brother-in-law about which of them is the more decent person. I still want to call the act supererogatory. The man, maybe not so praiseworthy. This is where clause (c) starts wobbling for me.
- Someone gives 40% of their income to bednets. Not required, I'm confident it's supererogatory. But if everyone in their bracket did it, I don't think it'd be supererogatory anymore, it'd be ordinary decency. So whether an act is supererogatory depends on what other people are already doing? That bothers me and I don't know if it should.
- A doctor who could make three times the money doing cosmetic work spends her life in a rural clinic. Not required, good, praiseworthy. But I don't think I'd say she *did* anything supererogatory. She just picked a career. So is it whole lives that can be supererogatory, or only acts? 
- Giving up your seat to someone exhausted on the train. Nice, not required. Calling it supererogatory feels like inflation. Where's the line between 'nice' and 'beyond the call'?
- Passing up revenge when you have the perfect chance and nobody would blame you for taking it. I want to call that supererogatory too, even though it's a non-act. Does that break the whole act-shaped picture?
- I promise to drive a friend to the airport, then my dad has a stroke the same morning. I don't think I'm still required to drive them, and the friend would be a jerk to insist. But if I drive them anyway, that feels supererogatory now -- it was required when I made it, then stopped being required, and now doing it is beyond the call. A category that shifts mid-stream. No idea what to do with that.
- And the one that really gets me: someone who pours everything into a cause and quietly wrecks their own kids in the process. Each individual act looks like the burning-building case. The life as a whole I think is flatly wrong. So can 'supererogatory' attach to acts that are pieces of an overall wrong life? Or is my act-level verdict the mistake?

Is there a principled account that gets all of these, or close? If not, which of my verdicts do I have to give up? Specifically I want to know (1) whether praiseworthiness is part of what makes the act supererogatory or a separate fact about the agent, (2) whether the category is relative to what others are already doing, and (3) whether the good-making feature has to sit in a single act or can sit in a pattern or a life. And give me one case I haven't listed where your preferred account and the standard three-part one come apart, so I can see what's actually riding on it.
