I run a small reading group on decision theory and we've spent the term on Kelly betting and the geometric-rationality stuff around it -- the growth-optimal fraction, the difference between maximizing expected wealth and expected log wealth, the fact that a bet with positive expected value can still be one you should refuse if it risks a bankroll you can't rebuild, overbetting, ruin as an absorbing state, the whole time-average versus ensemble-average business where the single path you actually live is not the average over all the paths you could have lived. For our last session I want to do a film night instead of a paper, and I've landed on Uncut Gems -- the Safdie brothers one, Sandler as Howard Ratner, the jeweler with the opal and the debts.

What I don't want is ninety minutes of everybody pointing at the screen going "thats an overbet, idiot." I want an actual reading of the film in terms of the theory, faithful in both directions. So: what does the film dramatize or get right about geometric rationality? Where does the idea illuminate a specific choice -- the championship ring he pawns to make the first six-way parlay on Garnett, the parlay that wins him six hundred grand and then gets frozen by Arno because it was made with money he owed, the appraisal that comes in way under what he believed the opal was worth, the final parlay on Garnett's numbers that Julia has to fly to the casino to place, the win that lands and the gunshot that lands right after it. Is the last bet Kelly-rational or not, given that his bankroll is borrowed, leveraged, and partly owned by a loan shark who can and does stop his payouts? Does the fact that he wins 1.2 million and dies anyway make the film a geometric-rationality parable, or does the Safdies' refusal to let the win function as a reward actually undercut the moral? And where does the film push back on the theory -- Howard's bets aren't bad bets in the expected-value sense, they mostly win, and the opal is a talisman rather than an estimate of an edge, so the whole apparatus of edge and fraction may be the wrong description of what he's doing.

Also say where the theory is just a worse description of the film than the film is of itself -- I don't want the reading to flatten a character study about compulsion and family into a fable about bet sizing, and I'd rather you tell me honestly that the lens distorts than force the fit. But end by saying what the film gives us that the math can't: the felt texture of the bet, the social ownership of the bankroll, the fact that for Howard not betting isn't in the option set. Don't quote dialogue at me, I've seen it, just talk about scenes.
