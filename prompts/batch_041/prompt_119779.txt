I teach an undergraduate course on the philosophy of time and want to close the term with Slaughterhouse-Five. Read the novel against four-dimensionalism and the Rietdijk–Putnam argument — the case that, because simultaneity is relative and no global present is privileged, all moments are equally real; its sharpest form is the Andromeda paradox, in which two people passing each other on the street disagree about whether an Andromedan invasion fleet has already been launched. I want the reading to separate the metaphysics from the mood. Is the Tralfamadorian view eternalism, or is it fatalism in science-fictional dress — and does Vonnegut's time resemble the relativistic block at all, given that his is a Newtonian landscape of moments with a single ordering you can wander through, whereas relativity enters the argument only through the failure of any such ordering? Eternalism does not make our deliberations idle, since they are as real as anything else in the block, so if the novel slides from the claim that all moments exist to the claim that nothing can be done, mark where the slide happens and whether it matters. Then ask whether Billy's being unstuck in time is even the right picture of the block: on eternalism other times exist, but we do not visit them. If that is a confusion, say so plainly, then try the stronger reading, that the novel's real subject is trauma and memory and the block is a metaphor for a past that will not stay past. End by telling me what the novel supplies that the argument cannot — what work "So it goes" does that a proof of the reality of all moments does not — and where the reading strains, given that the book is also a protest against Dresden, which seems to require the very agency the block is said to take away.
