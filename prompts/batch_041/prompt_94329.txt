OK so I'm in this reading group on information economics and we did the no-trade theorem last week, and it's kind of broken my brain a little. The version we got is: if it's common knowledge that everyone is rational, that everyone shares the same prior, and that the current allocation is already Pareto efficient, then no amount of private information can generate trade. The reasoning being that anyone offered a trade has to ask why the other side wants it, infers they must know something bad, and the whole thing unravels before it starts. Which is obviously false as a description of the world — we trade all day, every day. So for my write-up I want to take one real episode and run it through the theorem in both directions: does the theorem actually illuminate the episode, and does the episode embarrass the theorem?

The episode I've picked is the collapse of Long-Term Capital Management in 1998. Quick version of the facts so we're on the same page: it was a hedge fund run by John Meriwether with a bunch of heavyweights, including two guys who'd just won the econ Nobel for the option-pricing work. Its whole strategy was relative-value / convergence trades — buy the cheap bond, short the expensive one, bet the spread narrows. For a few years it printed money. Then in August 1998 Russia defaulted, the spreads blew out instead of converging, the fund lost something on the order of four to five billion dollars in a matter of weeks, and the New York Fed had to convene the big banks to recapitalize it because everybody was terrified of the unwind.

So map it for me. Who are the traders, what exactly is the private information being traded on, and is the common-knowledge-of-rationality condition even remotely met here — I suspect it isn't, and I want you to be specific about which assumption fails and why. I'm especially hung up on the crowding problem, because LTCM's positions were apparently well known on the street and other funds were copying them, so its "private" information was arguably public — does that make the theorem apply with extra force (there really was nothing left to trade on) or does it make the whole framework inapplicable? And give me an honest accounting of what the episode shows about the epistemology of markets: does it vindicate the differing-priors escape hatch, or the limits-to-arbitrage story, or does it show that the theorem was answering a question nobody in markets was ever asking? Wherever the theory goes silent, say so instead of forcing it to speak — I'd rather have the gaps than a tidy story.
