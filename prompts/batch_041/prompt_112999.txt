I recently sat in on a dispute between two colleagues, neither of them a specialist in logic, and I have not been able to settle it in my own mind. What I want is a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as they were actually made, not a general essay on the paradox.

The dispute concerned a sentence one of them had written on a card, which I reproduce exactly as it stood: 'The sentence written on this card is not true.'

(1) A maintained that the sentence is neither true nor false. Truth, she said, is not invariably bivalent; there are sentences that fall into a gap, and this is one of them. B replied that this cannot be right, for the simplest of reasons: if the sentence is not true, then matters stand exactly as the sentence says they stand, and so the sentence is true. On his view, the gap theory is refuted by the very sentence it was introduced to handle.

(2) A replied that B was conflating 'false' with 'not true'. She had not said that the sentence was false; she had said that it was not true. B's objection, she said, succeeds only if 'not true' is taken to mean 'false', which is precisely the classical assumption at issue, so B is assuming what he set out to prove.

(3) B answered that this is worse than a gap; it is a circle. To rescue the sentence from contradiction, A must alter the behaviour of 'not', and she has offered no independent reason for doing so. He added that if 'not true' is not to mean 'false', she owes an account of what the third status is, and of how negation behaves in its neighbourhood.

(4) A then changed tack. The sentence, she said, is not the sort of thing that can be true or false at all: it is meaningless, or at any rate not truth-apt, and merely looks like a sentence. There is therefore no gap to worry about, because the sentence never enters the game in which truth and falsity are the two possible outcomes. B objected that they had both understood it perfectly well — well enough to derive a contradiction from it and to know exactly what it was doing — which could not be so if it were meaningless. He added that 'not truth-apt' looks like the gap under a new name.

(5) B then tried to show that the paradox has nothing essentially to do with self-reference. He wrote out an infinite list of sentences. The first said that every sentence below it was false; the second said the same of the sentences below it; and so on without end. No sentence on the list refers to itself; each speaks only of the sentences further down. The old contradiction returns: if any sentence on the list is true, the next one is false, but a false sentence of the form 'every sentence below this one is false' requires some later sentence to be true, which contradicts the first; so none is true, and so all are false; but then the first sentence, which says that all the later ones are false, is true. Self-reference, B concluded, is a red herring.

(6) A replied that the list as a whole is still a closed loop of reference, and that calling a loop infinite does not make it any less closed. B said that a loop in which no sentence refers to itself, and which has no last element, is not the same thing as self-reference; and that if A meant circularity generally, she should note that two sentences, each saying that the other is false, are perfectly consistent — one true, the other false — so circularity cannot be the trouble either. She must mean something further, and she has not said what.

I would like you to adjudicate move by move. For each of the six moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or sound, and give the reason in a sentence or two. Then do the following. First, check the two-sentence case B produces in the last move: is it in fact consistent, and what, if anything, does it show? Second, separate the disagreements that are substantive from those that are merely verbal, saying which tacit assumptions about 'true', 'not true' and 'truth-apt' each party is relying on. Third, say what each party has conceded that the other ought to have pressed harder, and what both of them are missing. Finally, say which of the two is closer to the truth overall, and why. Neither of them is a specialist, so do not grade them on familiarity with the literature; I want their reasoning assessed, not their vocabulary. You may name positions or authors where that genuinely helps, but the verdict should rest on the arguments themselves.
