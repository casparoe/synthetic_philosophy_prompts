In a graduate seminar on decision-making under deep uncertainty, a colleague shut down a dispute with the sentence "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence." The question on the table was whether, when we have no data whatever about some outcome, it is legitimate to represent our belief about it by a single number — the equiprobable one — and then compute as usual, or whether intellectual honesty requires a broader representation. My colleague intended the slogan to rule out the first option. I suspect she was right, but I cannot say precisely what the sentence asserts, and I certainly cannot say why it would license her conclusion.

What I want is a menu rather than a verdict. Set out the distinct claims that "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence" could be making, arranged from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical. For each, give the most precise formulation you can, then classify it as: trivially true; substantive and defensible; defensible only under a stated condition; or false. When a reading is true only under a condition, state the condition.

I am especially interested in readings that turn on the difference between a probability and the precision of a probability — between where a credence sits and how determinate or spread out it is — and between what the evidence does to the probability of a hypothesis and what it does to our entitlement to hold a definite probability at all, since that is the distinction the seminar dispute was really groping toward. For each such reading, say whether it genuinely supports my colleague's conclusion — that ignorance should not be collapsed into an equiprobable point — or whether it is being stretched to do work it cannot do. Finally, identify the single reading that people most likely mean when they use the slogan to end an argument, and say whether that reading is strong enough to end one.

Keep the answer self-contained: spell the readings out rather than alluding to them.
