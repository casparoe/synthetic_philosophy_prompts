The question was: set out Newcomb's problem, explain how causal decision theory and evidential decision theory each evaluate the two options, and say which theory you think gets it right and why. Here's what the student wrote:

"In Newcomb's problem you are shown two boxes. The transparent box contains £1,000. The opaque box contains £1,000,000 if the predictor, who is infallible, foresaw that you would take only the opaque box, and is empty otherwise. You may take the opaque box alone (one-boxing) or take both boxes (two-boxing). Causal decision theory says two-box: the contents of the boxes are already fixed before you choose, so taking both gives you £1,000 more than taking the opaque box alone in either state of the world, and your choice cannot causally affect what the predictor has already done. Evidential decision theory says one-box, because the expected utility of an act is worked out by conditioning on the act itself: the world in which you one-box is overwhelmingly a world in which the opaque box is full, so one-boxing scores far higher. The real dispute, then, is whether a rational agent should be guided by what an act causes or by what it reveals. I come down on the evidential side — the predictor's accuracy is a fact about the world like any other, and it would be perverse to ignore evidence about which state you are in. The causal theorist's dominance argument only looks compelling because it assumes the prediction is probabilistically independent of your choice, which is exactly what the set-up denies."
