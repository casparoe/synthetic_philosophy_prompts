My closest friend from graduate school has spent twenty years writing mitigation reports for capital defense teams, and she holds a settled, rather idiosyncratic set of convictions about when a person's conduct is genuinely his own. She insists on separating two questions that she says come apart: whether conduct belongs to the agent in the sense that makes resentment or gratitude fitting, and whether the agent should be punished. Her verdicts on the first question, accumulated over years of argument, are these.

A man whose arm was gripped and guided by another so that he struck a stranger is not the author of the blow.

A woman who kills during a psychotic episode, taking her victim for a demon and being unable to entertain the thought that she is mistaken, is not the author of the killing.

A man who embezzles from his employer out of a greed he has nursed for years is fully the author of the embezzlement, even though a colleague had secretly arranged that a drug in his coffee would have dissolved any last hesitation had he shown any; he showed none, and acted on his own greed throughout.

A man who emerged from an operation with a surgically induced urge to set fires is not the author of a fire he set, even though he was able to resist the urge, sometimes did resist it, knew it was wrong, and could have called for help instead. He experienced the urge, she says, as something that happened to him rather than as a reason he acted on.

A man who has chosen his nightly drunkenness, never regrets it, and misses his daughter's recital because of it is the author of the missed recital, even though at the moment he could not have chosen otherwise.

Next month I am bringing her a case I have built specifically to test her, and I would like you to predict what she will say. A man with a long history of violent jealousy undergoes an experimental procedure that installs in him an aversion to violence so complete that, once it engages, he cannot bring himself to strike anyone; there is no wavering, no struggle, and at the moment of provocation it is not within his power to do otherwise. The procedure is voluntary; he endorses it completely before and after, says that he is at last himself, and would consent again. It works. Some years later his brother-in-law provokes him viciously, and he does not strike, which he meets with relief and gratitude. Over the same period the same installed aversion leaves him unable to tolerate a deceit he had long practiced, and he ends an affair and keeps faith with his wife at considerable cost, calling this the best thing he has ever done.

Will she say that the restraint and the fidelity are his own, and that he is owed gratitude or credit for them? I care less about the verdict than about the rule. Set out the rules that fit everything above, say what each predicts here, tell me which she is most likely running and why, and name the further case that would best separate them. If her record leaves the question genuinely open, say so, and tell me what principle she would be adopting with each verdict.
