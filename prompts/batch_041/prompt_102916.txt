I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years now, and I've developed a habit of collecting argumentative moves I can't quite file under an existing name; this one has been nagging me the longest. The move goes like this. Someone claims that their explanation of a body of evidence is the simpler one, and leans on that simplicity as a reason to believe it — often as the main reason. Press on what "simpler" means and the verdict turns out to depend entirely on which features of the situation are taken as primitive and which as derived, that is, on a descriptive vocabulary that was never stated. Switch vocabularies and the ranking flips: what is one short clause under a scheme that treats intentions as basic becomes a sprawling conjunction of coincidences under a scheme that treats base rates as basic, and the people making the argument will slide between the two schemes as it suits them without flagging the switch, so that the same fact is a suspicious coincidence under one description and exactly what you'd expect under the other. When you object, the reply is usually a shrug: of course simplicity is relative to a language, there is no God's-eye notation, so demanding a language-independent notion of simplicity is naive. But the same people keep using "simpler" as though it were a language-independent mark of truth, which is precisely what the shrug was meant to defuse. It feels like the argument borrows the authority of language-independent simplicity while taking cover behind language-relative simplicity, and I genuinely can't tell whether that is a real fallacy or just my confusion.

I know this sits near things that already have names. From the sequences and from older philosophy of science I know about Occam's razor, about Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity, about minimum description length, and about the green/grue puzzle, where one body of evidence equally supports two predicates and the tie gets broken by which predicate counts as entrenched. I have also met the reassurance that the choice of description language cannot matter much, since any two universal languages yield complexity measures that differ by at most an additive constant. My trouble is that this reassurance seems to answer a different question: the constant is exactly where the live disagreements sit, because the cases people actually argue about are small and finite, and calling a predicate "entrenched" seems to rename the vocabulary rather than justify it. So what I am after is the carving, not a label. Is there an established name for this specific package — winning a simplicity comparison by means of a silently chosen vocabulary, then retreating to language-relativity when challenged? If it is really just grue, or really just the reference-machine dependence of Kolmogorov complexity, or really just the ordinary point that simplicity is a subjective prior rather than evidence, say which and why, and where the pattern I have described comes apart from each of them. And if nothing established fits, coin a term and define it, with a few boundary cases — look-alikes that should not count, and innocuous-looking cases that should — so I can recognize the real thing when it appears.
