I have been the rulings and errata editor at a mid-sized tabletop card-game studio for three years, and I am finally trying to write down the procedure my team should follow so that our official answers stop depending on which of us happens to open the ticket. Roughly forty rules questions a week arrive from players and tournament judges, of which a dozen or so need an official answer; a bad answer costs us a tournament's worth of goodwill, and an unnecessary errata costs us a print run. The three of us who answer tickets know the comprehensive rules backwards but have no philosophical training at all, and our lead designer is only in the office on Fridays, so anything that needs a design decision tends to sit for a week. What I want from you is an ordered procedure — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which it stops with a definite no, and the point at which it must be handed to a person because it is a question about what we want the game to do rather than about what the rules already say. It has to fit on one page, be runnable by a non-philosopher in about fifteen minutes, and not require the reader to know any metaphysics.

The reason I think this needs philosophy rather than more careful reading is a pattern in our tickets. The naive way to read any ability is as a conditional: a card is disposed to produce its effect when its trigger condition occurs just in case it would produce that effect if the condition occurred. That is precisely the analysis that philosophers of dispositions have spent decades dismantling, and our tickets are full of the counterexamples. There are cases where the trigger itself removes the ability before it can do anything — a creature that grows when dealt damage, and a card that strips the abilities of any creature dealt fire damage, both watching the same damage event. There are cases where the ability is plainly still there and the effect is nevertheless blocked, as with a global effect that stops triggered abilities from triggering for a turn. And there are cases where the effect appears without the ability ever having been present, as with copy effects that reproduce an ability's result after the source has left play or lost the ability. I want the procedure to keep the distinctions that actually do work for us — above all between an ability being absent and an ability being present with its manifestation blocked, since other cards care about that difference, and between the effect occurring and the ability triggering, since other cards care about that too — and to drop the ones that are idle in our setting, where abilities are stipulated by printed text rather than grounded in any causal basis. Please also be honest about the converse: in a game like ours the simple conditional reading may be the correct semantics for the text, in which case the interesting cases are gaps in the text rather than failures of the analysis, and the procedure should say so instead of importing a philosophical conclusion wholesale. Where the metaphysics genuinely does not settle the question, mark that as an escalation rather than papering over it, and where a step is really a design choice dressed up as a conceptual test, say which.

Once you have written the procedure, run it end to end on three tickets: the fire-damage pair above, where the trigger and the ability-removal are the same event; the silence effect, together with a creature that has a static bonus-granting ability and a second creature that gains a triggered ability only after the silence has already resolved, and a variant where the ability's text imposes a further condition that must hold both when the trigger occurs and again when the effect resolves; and the copy effect, where the result is reproduced after the source is gone. For each, give the answer the procedure yields, the reasoning in the procedure's own vocabulary, and the one-line explanation we would put in the public ruling. At least one of the three should come out in a way players will hate — pick whichever it is and say so plainly, and then say what we are tempted to do instead and what it would cost us. Finish with a short list of what the procedure cannot do, the kinds of tickets that will slip through it, and a note on when the right output is a ruling and when it is errata.
