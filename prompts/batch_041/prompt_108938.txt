I've been in a reading group thats been stuck since March on the last notebook — the one full of remarks about Moore's claims to know things like that he has two hands and that the earth had been around long before he was born, the notes published as On Certainty. We've split into two camps and we're going in circles, so what I want is a diagnosis of the fight itself, not another round of it.

Camp A: these commitments aren't known, aren't justified, don't get doubted, and aren't even believed in the ordinary sense — they're more like the river-bed that the rest of our talking flows over, and someone who demands a justification for them has misunderstood what a justification can do. Camp B: that's quietism with a nicer name. If there's no justification, then I don't know the things I'd ordinarily say I know, and Camp A has just redrawn the word "knowledge" around the problem. But when we press Camp B on what does justify them, the only answer anyone can give uses the commitments themselves, and we all agree that's circular.

My hypothesis, and I'd rather you test it than agree with it: the two camps are answering two different questions under one heading. One question is what these commitments do — what role they play in making a practice possible at all. The other is what their status is — whether we're entitled to them. I think most of the arguement is about the first and most of the conclusions drawn are about the second. Someone else in the group goes further: she says "hinge" is being used in two senses, one where a hinge is whatever the practice couldn't get going without (take it away and the surrounding talk stops meaning anything), and one where a hinge is just whatever we can't bring ourselves to doubt (take it away and you'd be a very strange person, but the practice would carry on fine). She thinks the literature keeps proving the second while claiming the first.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really — verbal, a genuine clash about what rationality and knowledge are, or two sets of people answering different questions? And does her can't-get-going-without / can't-bring-myself-to-doubt distinction do real diagnostic work, or does it just relabel the camps?

One test case, because it's where we got stuck: someone who says, sincerely and with no drama, "sure, I might be a brain in a vat" — and then goes on doing physics perfectly well, uses the stairs, eats lunch. Does that person doubt a hinge, or not? If they do, then "can't be doubted" was never true of anybody and the whole thing looks like an empirical claim about human psychology that happens to be false. If they don't — because those words aren't doing anything in their life — then say what makes the difference, and whether that difference is normative or merely behavioural.

Finally, be straight with me. Given your diagnosis, what would actually move this thing — a distinction, a case, some empirical work on what people can and can't doubt, or nothing at all, because the camps are doing different jobs and should stop pretending to disagree? If it's the last one, just say so.
