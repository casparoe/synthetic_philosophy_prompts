I serve on the medical policy committee of a mid-sized health plan, and we have to decide, case by case, which conditions count as diseases for coverage and clinical-guideline purposes. Our committee keeps deadlocking between two camps, and I need a written decision procedure we can actually run.

The naturalists among us hold that a condition is a disease only if some part or process of the body fails to perform its biological function, whether or not anyone is troubled by it. The normativists hold that a condition is a disease only if it is disvalued—harmful, unwanted, or impairing—whether or not any biological function has failed. We have tentatively adopted a two-gate test: a condition counts as a disease only if both gates open, so there must be a dysfunction and the condition must be disvalued. But we cannot agree on the order of the gates, on how to settle the dysfunction gate when the notion of function is contested, or on what to do when the gates disagree.

On the dysfunction gate, three rival readings are on the table and nobody can choose among them: (i) a function is what the trait was selected for over evolutionary history; (ii) a function is what the trait typically contributes to survival or reproduction; (iii) a function is simply what the trait typically does in a reference population, so that dysfunction amounts to statistical deviation from the norm.

On the disvalue gate, the hardest question is whether the disvalue must be warranted by facts about the condition itself or whether it may come from social reaction—stigma, prejudice, or a mismatch with prevailing expectations—since a condition that is unwanted only because it is stigmatized seems different in kind from one that is unwanted because it hurts.

Please write the procedure. I need the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a definite no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than facts. Then run it on four cases: (1) an incidental, symptomless anatomical variant, such as an extra rib found on a scan; (2) a condition defined by a cutoff on a continuous measurement—say a bone-density or blood-pressure threshold—where moving the cutoff changes who counts as diseased; (3) a trait that is disvalued mainly because it is uncommon and stigmatized, such as left-handedness in an era that pathologized it; and (4) a person who seeks treatment for a trait that lies within the normal range, such as a student requesting medication to improve focus or a short adult requesting growth hormone. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the committee will not like, be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure cannot do. Our members are a mix of clinicians and lay appointees, we meet monthly, our decisions govern coverage, and the medical director signs off, so the procedure has to be usable by non-specialists in about fifteen minutes per case.
