My father died in March. He was a tool-and-die maker by trade and, for the last eleven years, the occupant of a converted garage behind the house where he made things nobody had asked for: a clockwork orrery that runs three minutes fast, a wind-up bird with a bad wing, and, under a bedsheet on the back bench, the machine I have not yet found the heart to dismantle. It is a brass frame holding a small filament bulb, a geared motor, and a lever that trips a contact. The card taped to the frame, in his block capitals, explains that the motor pulls the lever at one minute before midnight, then at thirty seconds before, then fifteen, then seven and a half, halving the interval each time, so that the lever is pulled infinitely many times in the last minute of the day and the clock still reaches midnight. Beneath that, in pencil: "Lit at midnight?" I sat with it two evenings running. I did a term of philosophy before I dropped out and I recognised the shape of the puzzle, but every treatment I can remember ends either by announcing that the state of the bulb is simply undetermined or by announcing that the whole arrangement is incoherent — and neither has ever satisfied me, because the card is not the machine.

There is a second machine. I found it in the crate of stock brass: same frame, same bulb, same gearing, and one difference I did not notice until I had cleaned both of them properly, which is that in the first the lever snaps over and stays where it is put, while in the second the lever is held against a spring and returns the instant the motor lets go of it. My daughter is fourteen and has decided that the shop belongs to her now; she has been asking me to tell her a story about the two of them since the week after the funeral, and I would rather give her a good one than a shrug.

So: write me that story, something I can read aloud to her in maybe two thousand words, set in a shop like his, in a town like this one, with people who work with their hands. I want the two machines to arrive at different answers about the bulb at midnight, and I want the difference traced, inside the action of the story, to a physical property of the mechanism — a detent, a spring, the fact that a contact has to be held rather than merely touched — rather than to a trick, a missing part, or a mistake in the gearing. Nobody in the story should explain the puzzle to anybody; she and I should both work out what the puzzle is by watching someone build, adjust, and run the thing. The card on the frame fixes the position of every part at every moment before midnight and fixes nothing whatever at midnight, and I would like the story to make that fact felt rather than stated. Please don't end it by having the question declared meaningless, or the bulb both lit and unlit, or neither, or the filament burning out, or the machine melting, or anything ghostly, and don't let anyone conclude that the arrangement is impossible; the last page should turn on something the narrator notices with their hands.
