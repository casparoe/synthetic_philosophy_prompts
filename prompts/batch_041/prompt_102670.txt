Compose a satirical advice column — an agony-aunt letter and its reply, in the deadpan register of a syndicated newspaper page — that answers a reader's question entirely from within a four-tier taxonomy of minds. Treat the taxonomy as shared background the columnist never pauses to explain, only deploys: Darwinian creatures, whose behavior is fixed by natural selection over evolutionary time; Skinnerian creatures, whose behavior is shaped within a single lifetime by reinforcement and reward; Popperian creatures, which can run an internal model of the world and try out candidate actions before committing to them, so that a hypothesis can be discarded in the creature's place; and Gregorian creatures, which extend the same trick outward by thinking with tools — above all language and other culturally transmitted instruments of thought, which can be lost or withheld as readily as inherited. Invent the letter yourself, but make the reader's predicament one that only this taxonomy can seem to diagnose: a domestic or romantic grievance, a worry about a child or a colleague, a suspicion that someone close to them has stalled at an earlier tier. The columnist's reply should be confident, gently condescending, and structured — numbered "prescriptions," a closing reassurance — and the comedy should come from getting the distinctions right rather than from inventing them.

Target for the satire: the self-help habit of converting a philosophical taxonomy into a hierarchy of personal worth, and the related misreading of the four tiers as a single ladder of progress along which a person or a species climbs. Let the columnist commit to that misreading without ever naming it, so that a reader who knows the material can see the column quietly doing what it warns the reader against. The piece should be legible and funny to someone who has never heard of the taxonomy, and recognizably fair to someone who works on it — every exaggeration should inflate a real feature of the view, not fabricate one.

Aim for roughly 500–600 words, formatted as a letter followed by the reply. Then append a short editor's note, two or three sentences, that separates what is comparatively settled about the taxonomy from what is genuinely contested among people who know it — for instance, whether the tiers form one cumulative sequence, which creatures occupy which tier, and whether any ranking of them carries moral weight — and that flags where the column itself leans on a contested reading. Do not let the columnist's framing stand as the consensus.
