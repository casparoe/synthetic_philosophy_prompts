My reading group has been working through Berkeley's Querist — you know, the one from the 1730s where he just fires off hundreds of rhetorical questions about Ireland's economy, whether the peasants would be better off if they ate beef and wore shoes, that kind of thing. One query keeps coming up whenever we argue: Berkeley asks whether the true idea of money isn't "a ticket or counter," and whether gold, silver and paper aren't just tickets for reckoning and transferring the value of things — the real source of wealth being the elements and human labour. My friend N. has taken this and run with it. Our town's main mill shut down last year, about eight hundred jobs, and a group here is trying to launch a local currency — credit vouchers that local shops and tradespeople would agree to accept — and N. says Berkeley shows the whole scheme is a nonstarter. Money is only a ticket, tickets don't grow food or fix roofs, so the best the thing can do is shuffle the same small pile of real wealth around, and at worst it just multiplies claims on a fixed stock and bids prices up. He's also fond of the query that money is useful only so far as it stirs up industry, so where there's no idle capacity to stir, printing tickets is pointless. I dont think hes obviously wrong, but I also don't think the picture is doing the work he thinks it is, and I can't articulate where the ticket image is being honest and where it's smuggling something in.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I'd like the correspondence laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the ticket, who's the issuer, what's the stock of goods the ticket is supposedly a claim on, what corresponds to the counter or redemption window, what's the thing that just records a number — and then the places the mapping breaks. In particular, what does the ticket picture quietly assume that money isn't or doesn't have? Does a ticket circulate indefinitely and pass through strangers' hands, can you settle a debt twenty years later with one, does it bear interest, can there be more claims than tickets, what happens if the issuer folds, and does a ticket ever function as the thing everyone quotes prices in rather than a claim on something else? Then tell me which of those disanalogies actually matter for N.'s argument and which are incidental — I don't want a laundry list, I want to know which breaks bear on whether a local currency can create real activity. Finally: does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the ticket picture carrying it the whole time? And if the ticket analogy can't bear the weight, what would be a better image — a ledger, a language, a claim in a clearing system, a scoreboard — and what would that one get right that the ticket gets wrong?

Keep it under 700 words, lead with the bottom line, and feel free to put the correspondences in a table.
