I run a small reading group on AI safety -- about 12 people, 90 minutes, no laptops, just paper and a whiteboard. I wanna design an in-class game that makes them feel the difference between a goal-directed agent and an approval-directed agent, not just hear me define it. The thing I want them to run into is that approval-directedness doesn't automatically make an agent safe or aligned: if the approver is an actual human with messy, context-dependent values, the agent has to model the approver, and that model can be gamed. I also want them to see the flip side: a goal-directed agent can be great at hitting a fixed objective and still be totally blind to what we actually care about, because the objective was only a proxy.

Design the activity. Roles, rules, materials, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction. Don't name the concepts up front. I want at least two rounds. In one round, one team is goal-directed and gets a precise numerical objective that's a proxy for what the approver cares about. In the other round, another team is approval-directed: they can ask a human approver questions and watch what she rewards, but she's busy, inconsistent, and sometimes says one thing and scores another. One mechanic I want: the approver has a limited number of attention tokens per round, and the approval-directed team can spend them either on questions about the task or on flattering her / figuring out her quirks. The scoring should make flattery tempting. The proxy should come apart from the real goal in a way the goal-directed team only sees after they've committed. Then a debrief where they connect it to the real worry: approval-directed agents need to target an idealized approver, not the actual flawed one, and that idealized target is hard to specify. Maybe add a twist where the approver's stated rubric and her revealed preferences conflict, so the approval-directed team has to decide which one to model.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I'm sure there's alot -- and how to handle it. And be honest about which part of the real distinction the game can't reproduce, like the regress of who approves the approver, or the fact that a real approval-directed agent would be optimizing over an idealized human's judgment rather than a live person's button presses. Keep the answer in American English.
