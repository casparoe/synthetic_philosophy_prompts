My friends Ines and Marcus got into an argument after our little reading group the other night and its still going. The reading was Hofstadter on the self as a strange loop, the GEB guy, plus the later book where he argues the I isn't confined to one skull. Neither of them is a professional philosopher, they're just both stubborn, and by the end I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through it move by move and sort out who's right about what, and what each of them is missing? No need to cite anything, I just want the arguments adjudicated.

Round 1. Ines said the whole picture is circular. For a loop to be a self, the loop has to represent itself; but a representation of a self is already a self doing the representing. So saying the self is a strange loop just gives the phenomenon a cooler name and calls it an explanation. Marcus said she has it backwards. Nothing in the machinery has to be a self: you've got symbols that are just patterns of activity, and because the system is complex enough, some of those symbols end up denoting the system itself. The I is one of those symbols. Self-reference comes out of dumb parts the way life comes out of chemistry. That's not circular, he said, that's just reduction.

Round 2. Ines said fine, then the word loop is doing no work. She offered a test: a program that prints its own source code refers to itself and nobody thinks it's conscious; a thermostat whose next setting depends on its current one is a loop too. Loops are everywhere. So either the strange loop is cheap enough that quines qualify, in which case so what, or strange secretly means has a self, and then we're back to circularity. Marcus said the difference is the tangling, not the looping. In a quine the levels never actually touch: the part that prints the source is fixed, nothing at the upper level rewrites the lower and gets rewritten back. In a brain, the symbol that stands for the whole system is produced by that system and feeds back into it, so the describing and the described are the same ongoing process, and it's not frozen, it runs and it gets used. He then added a point I couldn't tell was good or not: that the self-symbol has to earn its meaning by being deployed, that the system uses its own I to model other people, predict them, run analogies between itself and them, so the symbol ends up tracking the very pattern that generates it. That's why a thermostat doesn't get one, he said: it has a state, not a symbol it thinks about minds with.

Round 3. Ines said that's a functional-role story, and it makes the self a representation the brain has, which is exactly the problem. Representations can be wrong, partial or missing: anosognosia, split-brain cases, and Hofstadter's own line that the I is a story the brain spins about itself. If the self is a story then the account is an error theory, there's no self, just a self-model. Which is fine, she said, but then saying the self is a strange loop is misleading, because you've swapped the self for a symbol of it, and that was the very thing she accused him of doing. Marcus said that's a false choice. The pattern isn't a stand-in for the self, it is the self, the way a storm isn't a stand-in for the weather. Then he brought in the distribution claim: since it's a pattern and not a lump of tissue, the same I can be partly instantiated in more than one brain, which is why he thinks something of a person survives in the people who knew them. He even said that's why losing a grown-up is a bigger loss than losing a newborn, because there's more pattern and it's copied into more heads. Ines bailed out there. She said the distribution claim shows he isn't explaining the self, he's redefining person to mean pattern, so of course the pattern turns out to be the person. And the loss comparison she called ghoulish and unargued: if the pattern is lossy and slightly different in every head, then either there's one person per head, so many partial people, or there are none, and he can't say which without begging the question. Marcus said at least his version doesn't need a ghost, and that she seems to want a self that can't be wrong about itself, which is a strange demand.

So: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, tell me who's right about which specific point, and say what both of them are missing. Write it in British English please.
