I'm inclined to think that constitutional entrenchment — bills of rights, supermajority requirements, and similar constraints that a current majority cannot easily undo — is best justified by the Ulysses-contract analogy: a democratic people binds its future self against its own anticipated passions, much as Odysseus had himself lashed to the mast. What would count against this justification, sorted by how decisive each consideration is? I want the disanalogies taken seriously rather than the generic worries about judicial review. In particular: Odysseus's restraint is imposed by a constraint he does not control, whereas a constitutional limit is enforced by officials who are themselves part of the bound people — does that make constitutional precommitment more like a promise than a physical restraint, and is a promise by a shifting collective even coherent? Does the analogy presuppose a corporate agent with a stable preference ordering and an identifiable authentic self, and can that be defended without circularity? Does it face a regress, since whoever binds the people must in turn be bound? And can it cut the wrong way, since a later majority may be better informed and cooler-headed than the framers, so that the constraint blocks reason rather than passion? For each, tell me whether a defender would themselves concede it, whether it is fatal or merely awkward, and whether the analogy, once qualified enough to survive, still does any work beyond the truism that some decisions are better than others.
