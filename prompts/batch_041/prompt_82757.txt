I sit on the board of a small historical association in a town of about six thousand people, and we have now spent three meetings arguing about a wording problem that has turned out to be much harder than anyone expected. For our bicentennial we are commissioning a roadside marker and a small permanent exhibit, and the centerpiece is meant to be our founding story. As everyone here grew up hearing it, the town was founded in the 1820s by a woman who arrived alone, bought her land from a family already settled nearby, and drowned in a flood a few years later, leaving behind a strongbox of gold that has never been found. When we finally laid the evidence out on a table, it was thinner than the story. The earliest written mention anyone can produce is a memoir by a local minister written some seventy years after the events, and he had an obvious interest in making the town look older and more romantic than it was; as far as we can tell, everything else descends from him. There is a creek that carries the woman's surname, a gravestone with the right first name and no dates, and two families who insist that their grandmothers told them the story. One board member argues that since nobody has disproved any of it, we should print it as history, and adds that all history begins as legend anyway. Another wants to label the whole thing a legend and be done with it. Neither answer satisfies me, but until now I have not been able to say precisely what is wrong with either.

What I would like from you is a principled way of deciding where a story like this belongs, and then an actual recommendation about what we should put on the marker. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list; I want the reasoning itself, applied to these facts, so that I can defend a decision to the rest of the board and to the families who will be insulted by whichever way we go. Two things in particular leave me stuck. First, the scraps of evidence we do have are not independent of one another — they may all be downstream of a single interested writer — and I cannot tell how much that should cost us. Second, the strongest thing the story has going for it is that nobody would have had a reason to invent it, which on some days strikes me as the most important consideration there is and on other days as worthless, since a town does have a reason to invent a flattering founder.

For calibration, I keep being told that archaeology vindicated the Trojan War while King Arthur remains a legend, and I have heard the Exodus invoked on both sides of what sounds like the same argument, so I would like to know whether those are really one question or three, and what my local dispute has in common with them. Finally, please tell me what to say to the colleague who believes that the absence of disproof settles the matter; I would rather have an argument than a slogan.
