I write about philosophy for a living but I also run a small continuing-ed seminar on philosophy of language, and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on the Frege-Geach problem for expressivism — the thing where moral sentences are said to express attitudes rather than beliefs, and then everyone notes that "murder is wrong" can sit inside a conditional like "if murder is wrong, then telling on your friends is fine," where nobody's expressing an attitude, and the expressivist still owes us an account of what it means and why modus ponens goes through. Trouble is, every halfway decent student can recite that summary, so I need the question to force them into the genuinely hard part. Two jobs for it. First, make them treat the negation case seperately from the conditional case — "murder is not wrong," or "it's not the case that lying is wrong" — and say why negation is the nastier one: a conditional at least has a reading where the whole thing expresses a conditional attitude, but a bare negation has no attitude to express and yet has to come out inconsistent with the unnegated claim. Second, give them a fix to knock down. Here's the one I want them to evaluate: an embedded moral sentence means whatever attitude you'd be in if you asserted it, and when it's negated you're just expressing an attitude toward that attitude. Make them say where that either collapses into plain descriptivism or leaves the inconsistency unexplained.

Write the question, a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the moves each must contain and the errors that should cost marks. Keep it all in continuous prose — no headings, bullets, bold, or tables, just the way you'd write it in an email.
