I write about law and ideas for a general-interest monthly, and for the past two years I've sat on the advisory council of a small public-history foundation that is preparing a permanent gallery on the international tribunals held after the Second World War, at Nuremberg and Tokyo. The curator has circulated a draft framing statement that presents the proceedings as the birthplace of international criminal law. A retired appellate judge on the council wants us to strike that and say plainly that they were victors' justice: the offenses were defined after the acts were committed, and the victors' own conduct — the area bombing of German cities, the Soviet invasion of Poland in 1939, the Katyn massacres — went unexamined. The council votes Friday, and the chair has asked me, as the only non-lawyer at the table, to draft the decision memo.

Please write it. I need a concrete recommendation the council can vote on, with specific language for the wall text rather than a survey of the literature, and I need the reasoning in plain English — I'm not a lawyer by training, though I don't want the doctrinal points softened. In particular the memo should confront the retroactivity objection at its strongest, in the plain form that no one may be punished under a law that did not exist when he acted, and then deal honestly with the tu quoque problem: the tribunal's usual answer is that the charter bound the court it created and that aggressive war was already condemned by customary law, and the memo should say whether that answer survives once the unprosecuted Allied conduct is put on the table. It should also take a position on whether Nuremberg and Tokyo can be presented as a single institution or must be treated as two — the Japanese proceedings, with their majority reasoning and the separate opinions dissenting from it, are the harder case for our purposes — and it should reach a view on whether the trials are best described as unlawful yet morally justified, or as lawful from the start.

Finally, and this matters to me more than it may to the others: give the case for the position I end up rejecting at enough length and with enough force that the retired judge will recognize it as his own rather than as a straw man. If the memo's bottom line is that the foundation should keep the birthplace framing, the judge should still be able to say the memo understood why he wanted to tear it down.
