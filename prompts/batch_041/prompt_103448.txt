So my old MFA program has roped me into giving the commencement address this spring, and I need you to write it. It's a small room — maybe sixty grads, their families, a few faculty who've sat through a decade of these and can smell a cliché at fifty paces. I'm a novelist, twenty-odd years in, and I don't want to stand up there and do the standard never-give-up-on-your-dream number, because honestly I've watched too many friends grind for a decade on a book that was never going to work, and I've also watched a couple of people quit one page from the end of something great. So I want the speech to actually be about this, not just gesture at it.

Here's the spine I'm after. The idea that once you've paid a cost — time, money, years of your life — it should have no weight at all in what you do next; only the future counts, the past is spent, the textbook line. That's the sunk-cost thing everyone knows, and I basically believe it. But the whole reason I got asked is that I spent nine years on a novel that nearly killed me and then sold badly, and I still can't honestly tell these graduates it was a mistake, and I want the speech to sit in that discomfort instead of resolving it cheaply.

There's also a pushback I half-remember and want you to take seriously, because I think it's where the real thinking is: that honoring a sunk cost can be rational. That the years you've put in are evidence about what you actually value, not just dead weight. That sticking to a thing because you've already stuck to it is a way of holding yourself to a commitment you'd otherwise talk yourself out of. That some projects are so tangled up with who you are that cutting your losses is really just becoming a different person, and that's a cost the spreadsheet doesn't see. I don't know if that's a real argument or just fancy rationalizing, and I suspect the honest answer is some of each. Use whichever parts of it hold up and don't pretend the others do.

So: write me the address. Eight, maybe ten minutes spoken. It has to land for the writers in the room without losing the parents and grandparents. Don't let it turn into a lecture on decision theory — the philosophy should be doing the work underneath, shaping what the speech can and can't say, not being announced from the podium. And whatever it ends on, it can't be false. If the real conclusion is persist, but know why, say it in a way I could actually say out loud to these people. Also work in one concrete scene from the nine-year novel — you can definately invent the details, I'll swap in the real ones.
