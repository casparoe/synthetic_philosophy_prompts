I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, though I spend most of my working life on data modeling and schema design, which may be why the following question has lodged itself in my head. I have been reading about the notion of a merely verbal dispute — the suggestion that some disagreements which appear to be about the world are in fact disagreements about how a word is being used, and that they would dissolve if the parties translated their claims into a shared vocabulary. I want to test that notion against a case I think I understand: the International Astronomical Union's reclassification of Pluto in 2006.

The case as I understand it is this. Pluto was discovered in 1930 and was treated as the ninth planet for most of a century. In 2006, prompted in part by the discovery of Eris and other distant bodies of comparable size, the IAU adopted a definition on which a planet must orbit the Sun, be massive enough that its own gravity has rounded it, and have cleared the neighbourhood of its orbit. Pluto meets the first two conditions but not the third, and was reclassified as a dwarf planet. The decision was contested at the time and remains contested; a number of planetary scientists preferred a definition built around a body's intrinsic physical properties rather than its orbital environment, and under that definition Pluto would still count as a planet. The public, for its part, treated the demotion as a loss.

I would like you to do two things.

First, analyze this episode through the lens of verbal disputes and metaontology — the study of what, if anything, classificatory and ontological disputes are really about. Map the dispute carefully: who the disputants are, what each of them meant or wanted "planet" to mean, whether the disagreement was over facts about the solar system or over the correct use of a term, and whether the public counts as a disputant at all or merely as an audience. Then apply the standard test for a merely verbal dispute — roughly, whether the disagreement would survive the parties' agreeing on every underlying fact; in my own idiom, whether translating both sides into a common schema would leave every row unchanged — and report what it yields here. Finally, address a wrinkle I suspect the standard framing does not anticipate: the dispute was not settled by discovering which of two existing meanings was correct, but by an institution stipulating a new meaning and putting it to a vote. Does the metaontological vocabulary of verbal disputes and of adopting a framework have room for a dispute resolved by stipulation, or is this a case the framework never contemplated?

Second, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the episode show about the theory? Does it support the claim that such classificatory disputes are verbal, does it show that at least some of them are substantive in a way the theory underrates, or does it suggest the theory is asking the wrong question?

Where the theory is silent, please say so rather than forcing it to speak. I am after a careful carving of the case, not a flat verdict about whether the dispute "really" was verbal.
