I teach in a small master's program in language documentation — the kind where students spend a summer in a village recording the last fluent speakers of a language that has never been written down, then spend a year turning those recordings into something an archive can actually hold. In June I've been asked to give the commencement address to our graduating class, and I want to build it around an idea from Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach that has quietly organized my methods course for years. Hofstadter separates the information carried by any message into three levels: a frame message, the bare structural signal that announces itself as a message and asks to be decoded; an outer message, the implicit instructions carried by pattern and structure that tell the receiver how to build the right decoding mechanism; and an inner message, the content the sender meant to transmit. What makes this more than a taxonomy for our students is that in our line of work the frame and outer messages are exactly the parts that die with the last speaker — the conventions, the grammar, the unspoken sense of what a story is and when it is finished — so what an archivist actually manufactures is not the transcript but the decoding key, written for readers who will never meet a speaker.

Here is the particular problem I want the speech to solve, and I would like you to treat it as a real constraint rather than a flourish. The ceremony is in our auditorium, maybe 250 people: the graduates, who have lived inside this material for two years; their parents and grandparents, most of whom have never heard the word morpheme and have come to watch someone they love cross a stage; a few colleagues; and — the part I keep circling — four or five elders from the community whose language the students documented, who have traveled a long way to sit in that room. The address will be interpreted live into that language by one of the graduating students, so for a portion of the audience my words arrive through a decoder I can neither inspect nor correct, and I will never learn what of it survived the trip. I want the three levels to be the architecture of the speech, not a concept mentioned once and abandoned: it should be honest that a commencement address has a loud frame message (this is a ritual; decode it as encouragement) and a thick outer message (the genre's conventions tell you how to hear everything that follows), and I want its inner message to be something a listener could not have predicted from those first two, because a speech whose whole content is already carried by its frame has said nothing at all. There should also be a moment, and not a comfortable one, when I acknowledge that the elders may be decoding a message quite different from the one my students believe they have been sending all year.

Write the address: ten to twelve minutes spoken, warm rather than solemn, roughly 1,500 words. No citations and no quotations from Hofstadter — I will gesture at the idea, not lecture on it — and nothing that requires anyone in the room to have read the book or to know what an ergative is. There is one story I will probably use, and you may use it too: a student's transcript of what her consultant introduced as a story about a journey read, once the field notes were stripped away, as a simple travelogue, when the whole point of the telling — who had rights to which valley — lived in the outer message, in everything the teller assumed her listener already knew. It has to land for the families first, because they are the ones who will remember it.
