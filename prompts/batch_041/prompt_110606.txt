I don't have a philosophy degree, but I run a monthly continuing-education seminar for adults at our local library, and we've spent the last few sessions on a question I can't stop turning over: can one endless future be better than another endless future, when neither ever gets a final score? A friend in the group keeps saying that a future with infinitely many good days must beat a future with none, and I can't tell whether that's a real insight or a trick of language. For the final exercise this term I want a single essay question that a student can answer in 90 minutes without having read the technical literature, but that also can't be answered by reciting the usual slogans about different sizes of infinity or by gesturing at a famous wager. To keep it self-contained, build the question around this toy case, which I'll print on the exam sheet: a future is an infinite sequence of days, each day scored 0 or 1. Future Zero has 0 every day. Future Sparse has 1 on days 1, 2, 4, 8, and so on, and 0 on every other day. Future Dense has 1 every day. The question should ask students to compare these three futures under three candidate principles: first, the long-run average welfare; second, the limit of the running total; third, a tail-insensitivity principle that treats two futures as equally good if they differ on only finitely many days. They should then say which future, if any, is best, whether any of the three principles can deliver that verdict, and whether the failure is a defect in the principles or a sign that the question itself is ill-posed. I want the question to force them to notice that Sparse has infinitely many good days and yet the long-run average ranks it with Zero, while the running-total limit ranks Sparse and Dense together at infinity; and to ask whether there is a principled way to count infinite value without collapsing into either cardinality or density. If it helps sharpen the exam, you may add a fourth future with only one good day and then zero forever, so students can test whether tail-insensitivity really treats that as no better than Zero.

Please write the exam question itself, a model answer of about 700 words that a strong student could produce in the time, and a marking scheme worth 20 points. The marking scheme should name the specific moves that separate an A answer from a B answer—for instance, whether the student distinguishes a principle that is extensionally adequate from one that is decision-guiding, whether they see that tail-insensitivity is not the same as indifference to the infinite tail, and whether they can explain why adding one more good day to Sparse does not change its cardinality but does seem to matter. It should also list the common mistakes that should cost marks: treating all countably infinite sets as equally valuable, assuming that because a sum diverges no comparison is possible, and confusing the long-run average with the limit of the running total. Don't include citations, reading lists, or references to named authors; the question has to work for someone who cannot look anything up. Finally, please state how confident you are in the model answer and the marking scheme you give, and single out the one point in the answer you are least sure about.
