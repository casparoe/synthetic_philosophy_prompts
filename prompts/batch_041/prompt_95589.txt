I teach a second-year undergrad seminar called Language, Thought and Society — 16 students, mixed backgrounds, a handful of them non-native English speakers, one 75-minute session. I want to run something on Hofstadter's Person Paper, the satire he ran in his Scientific American column in the early 80s and reprinted in Metamagical Themas, where he takes the whole "man means human, stop being so sensitive about it" line and writes a straight-faced language column with race swapped in for sex, so the reader is forced to hear that the "neutral" terms were never neutral. I don't want to lecture about it, I want them to do it and feel it in the room. I'm not handing out the piece and you shouldn't quote it (no access anyway) — paraphrase if you need to point at it.

You're a philosopher of language who also designs classroom activities. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming a bit — what actually has to happen in a room for a swap like that to bite, rather than just seem like a clever trick — before you commit to anything.

Here's the material I've got. A deck of index cards, one sentence each, all written by me so no attribution problems:

A good doctor treats his patients as equals. / Man has always feared the dark. / The chairman will call the meeting to order at nine. / Every student should bring his own calculator to the exam. / The average man spends an hour a day getting to work. / Mankind has never been good at predicting its own future. / The spokesman said the company would look into it. / A person should be judged by what he does, not what he says. / The man in the street doesn't care about interest rates. / The freshman class will elect a chairman in October. / She was the first woman in her family to finish school. / Nobody likes being told what he already knows. / The policeman on the beat knows the neighborhood better than anyone. / A fireman carried the child down the ladder.

And two substitution keys, one card each.

Key B ("whitem"): replace man / men / person / people / individual / human / mankind / somebody / anyone with white / whites; replace woman / women with black / blacks. Leave he / him / his and she / her exactly as they are.

Key A ("blip"): same substitutions but with invented words — blip / blips for the neutral terms, blap / blaps for the female ones — and this time also replace he / him / his / himself with shim / shim / shis / shimself, and she / her with blap / blaps.

What I want: a runnable 75-minute activity where pairs take half the deck each, apply both keys, and then sort every card into (S) the swapped version says the same thing as the original, (D) it says something different, or (X) it collapses into nonsense — then the whole room compares sorts and has to argue out the cards they disagree on. I'd like the contrast between the two keys to be what does the teaching, but I'll leave it to you to figure out how, and to tell me honestly if my two keys won't produce the contrast I'm imagining. Don't reveal whose idea this is or name the piece until the sorting is finished.

Give me: a timeline, a materials list, the rules as I'd read them out, the card list with anything you'd cut or replace (keep at least ten, and no sentence that leans on a real person or event), the sorting task written out for the students, and then the debrief — which moments in the activity map onto which parts of the idea (default assumptions, marked and unmarked terms, the semantic vs pragmatic question about whether "man" is ambiguous or merely defaults, and the live fight over whether swapping words actually fixes a default or just lets it reattach to the new word). Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself in the debrief.

At the end, say how confident you are in the design and single out the one element you're least sure will land — especially whether the sorting task will actually generate disagreement or whether everyone will just agree instantly and the room will go flat.
