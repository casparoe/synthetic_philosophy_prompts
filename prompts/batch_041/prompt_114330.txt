I am in the upper sixth and have spent this term trying to work out what I think about consciousness. I have arrived at a position I would now like to have attacked. I am not asking for a reading list or for a survey of what others have said; I want the arguments themselves, and I want your own verdict.

The position is that consciousness is best understood as a user interface. When I taste coffee, something certainly happens — I am not denying that there is experience. What I deny is that introspection reports it accurately. On my view, the qualities I seem to find are real states of my brain whose properties are misrepresented to me: they seem simple, ineffable and immediately given, when in fact they are structured, relational, and the output of a great deal of work that leaves no trace of itself. In the same way, the subject who seems to have them — one thing, present throughout, owning the experiences — is a summary rather than an entity, a model the system builds of itself. The seeming is the whole trick: because nothing in the system signals that a construction has taken place, the result arrives looking like something received rather than something made.

I want to keep three claims separate, because I suspect they stand or fall differently:

(1) The felt qualities are real events with misrepresented properties.
(2) The unity and the ownership of experience are likewise misrepresented: there is a self-model, not a self that has the experiences.
(3) There is nonetheless something it is like to be a system like me. I refuse to say that experience itself is an illusion, because an illusion has to appear to somebody, and if there is no appearing then the word has quietly lost its meaning.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. What would count against each of the three claims? Which findings, arguments or intuitions should actually move me, and which are merely awkward? Please rank them, from the ones that would be close to decisive down to the ones a defender can absorb, and tell me which costs the defender of such a view is already conceding.

Two things worry me in particular. The first is whether I have simply made the view safe. Everything I might point to — blindsight, the split-brain cases, the way attention and change blindness show that what I report seeing is not what reached my eyes — gets absorbed as further evidence of construction, and I cannot imagine what could happen that would count the other way. Is there anything that could, or is the view unfalsifiable in principle? If it is, does that matter here, or is it a fair price for a view about how things seem rather than about what there is?

The second is a pair of cases I have invented and cannot place. Suppose we found a system with every piece of machinery that is meant to produce the illusion — the self-monitoring, the self-model, the reporting — and it behaved in all respects like something with experiences, yet there was reason to think nothing was going on for it at all. That looks to me like evidence against (3) rather than against (1) or (2), which would leave my position oddly untouched. Conversely, suppose there were a creature that behaved and reported exactly as I do, but whose introspective access had been severed, so that its reports were produced by a mechanism quite separate from the states being reported on. Does that support me, or does it merely show that I can only ever test the machinery of report, and so am always checking the one thing I have said is unreliable?

For what it is worth, I think I would be more disturbed by a demonstration that the view cannot even be stated without using the very notions it is meant to explain than by any experiment.

Please take a definite position. Tell me which of the three claims you would give up first, which single consideration is the most serious threat to the position as a whole, and what you would conclude if it turned out the way I fear. I would much rather have your verdict and its reasons than a list of options.
