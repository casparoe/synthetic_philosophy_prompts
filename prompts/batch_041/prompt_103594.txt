ok so I've been chewing on this for a while and I want you to write me an essay, the kind of thing I'd post on a forum and then spend the next day regretting. Topic: the old fight between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony. You know the shape of it — reductionist says you need independent grounds to think a given speaker is reliable before you're justified in believing what they tell you, and that justification is built up piece by piece; anti-reductionist says no, you're entitled to a default presumption that people are telling you the truth, defeasible when something looks fishy but not something you have to earn from scratch each time. Take a side and defend it. I don't want the "well they're each right in their own domain" mush.

Here's the wrinkle that I think makes this worth writing about instead of a textbook recap, and I want you to actually wrestle with it. Both positions were cooked up for a world where producing testimony is relatively costly and comes from people whose incentives you can at least partly see. Now imagine the opposite: a world where testimony-shaped text is free to generate, fluent, confident, well-formatted, and impossible to check one piece at a time, coming from sources you can't interrogate or even properly individuate. Call the distance you can actually check a claim before you run out of time or expertise its verification horizon. In that world, what happens to the debate?

A few things I want you to hit, since these are the bits I keep getting stuck on. First, the reductionist seems to owe us a non-circular story for how you ever calibrate trust in the first place, given that almost all your evidence about anyone's reliability is itself testimonial — how bad is that bootstrapping problem, really, and does it sink the view or just make it slower? Second, the anti-reductionist owes us an account of why a default presumption isn't just gullibility wearing a nice suit, especially when the default gets farmed by whoever can produce the most fluent text. Third, there's that familiar phenomenon where you realize you can't out-argue a domain's experts on their turf, so you quietly stop trying to evaluate and just defer — roughly, epistemic learned helplessness. Is that a failure of reductionism, a vindication of it, or just what anti-reductionism looks like when it's working? And fourth, does any of this even change if the testifier isn't a person at all — is "testimony" still the right category, or is the whole frame a category error once the source has no stake in being believed?

Don't lean on reading lists or citations, I just want the argument. And please actually reach a verdict — tell me which view you'd bet on and why, not a survey.
