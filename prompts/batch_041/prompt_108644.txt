In a reading group I keep meeting the same exchange, and I would like to be able to adjudicate it rather than just feel the pull of both sides. One person argues that insect welfare deserves serious weight because insects are so numerous that even a very low probability of their being sentient yields an expected quantity of suffering that dwarfs the suffering of the animals we already take seriously. Another replies that this is just Pascal's wager with bugs, or the mugging case in which a stranger threatens to torture enormous numbers of people unless you hand over your money — the same structure, and we already know that kind of argument should not move us, so insect welfare can stay a niche concern. How far does the analogy actually hold? Give me the correspondence: what plays the role of God, of the wager, of the infinite payoff, of the probability you cannot check. Then give me the places where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in whether the probability of insect sentience is the sort of thing that can be estimated and revised on evidence, unlike the probability of a particular god; whether it matters that the payoff, however large, is finite rather than infinite; and whether the many-gods objection has a real analogue here, given how many candidate minds, thresholds, and lineages there might be. Separate the disanalogies that bear on the dismissive conclusion from the ones that do not. Then tell me whether the dismissal survives, or whether the analogy was carrying it all along, and what a better analogy for the scale argument would be.
