In discussions of how we can know anything about the distant past, a courtroom analogy keeps surfacing: the historian is a detective or a judge, the surviving sources are witnesses, and a historical claim is a verdict reached through cross-examination, corroboration, and scrutiny of a witness's motives. A colleague leans on it to argue that our knowledge of events like the Persian Wars, or of figures like the historical Socrates, can be as well grounded as a court's finding, so sweeping skepticism about the distant past is overblown. I want the analogy audited rather than endorsed or dismissed. Set out the correspondence table — what plays the role of the witness, the testimony, cross-examination, corroborating evidence, chain of custody, the standard of proof, and the verdict — and then mark where the mapping breaks down. Please build in a distinction the courtroom picture tends to flatten: between a source treated as testimony, a narrative offered by someone with intentions who can distort or deceive, and a source treated as a trace, unintended residue like a fingerprint, a pollen sample, or an archaeological layer that asserts nothing yet can still function as evidence. Does the analogy have room for traces, and if traces are added, does that strengthen or weaken what it can carry? Close with a definite verdict: does the analogy genuinely support the anti-skeptical conclusion, or was it doing the argument's work for it, and what analogy would serve better?
