After a departmental seminar, two graduate students, Marta and Dev, got into an argument I couldn't referee, and I'd like your help settling it. The case we had been discussing is this: a small city, under pressure to cut costs, signs a thirty-year contract with a cheap power plant whose emissions are known to cause a mild but lifelong respiratory condition in most people who grow up there — nothing life-ruining, just chronic discomfort and some lost days of work. The costlier alternative would have kept the city poorer, with the result that most current residents' parents would have moved away before ever meeting, so those particular residents would never have been born. Marta said the contract cannot be wrong in the way it is usually described, because no one is worse off: every person who will grow up with the condition has a life clearly worth living, and the only alternative for each of them is not existing at all. Dev said that cannot be the end of it: one can call an outcome a worse world to have made without pointing to any single person who lost out, and the fact that no one can complain does not mean nothing was done wrong. Marta replied that "a worse world" is doing all the work, and that she cannot see what it is supposed to mean apart from being worse for someone; she added that if impersonal badness counts, then any choice that leads to a child being born with a life worth living could be called wrong, which is absurd. Dev answered that the city case is not absurd because there is something bad about the outcome — the condition, and the money — and one does not need a victim for there to be something bad; the absurd cases only look absurd because nothing bad happens in them. Marta then said that if the badness is real but victimless, Dev owes an account of who, if anyone, has standing to object, and Dev said standing and wrongness are different questions. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move: say which of these points are right, which are wrong, and what both of them are missing.
