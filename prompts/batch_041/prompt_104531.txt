I've basically talked myself into Humeanism about laws of nature -- the Lewis-style best system picture, where you take the whole mosaic of local matters of fact, find the true systematization of it that scores best on the strength/simplicity/fit trade-off, and those axioms just are the laws. No governing, no necessary connections between distinct existences, no extra modal glue holding the regularities up. And I don't merely tolerate this, I think the anti-Humean is positing a kind of ingredient we have no independent grip on at all.

So what would count against it? I want the actual defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, not a list of famous objections with names attached. A few things I keep chewing on and would like you to take seriously rather than wave away:

- The fundamental/effective split. If the true fundamental dynamics turns out to need horrendously disjunctive axioms, while some higher-level systematization scores better on the balance, does the Humean have to say the higher-level thing is the law? That seems to sever lawhood from whatever's actually doing the causal work at the bottom.
- Boundary conditions. Say a future physics hands us a dynamical equation plus a wildly intricate special initial condition. If the single simplest true systematization just folds the initial condition into its axioms, does that turn a boundary condition into a law? And if the Humean has no principled way to say where the line falls, how bad is that?
- Chance. Tying chance to the best system's frequencies seems to let later facts undermine the present chances -- the undermining-futures thing. Is that a technical wart or a real objection?
- Symmetry principles and conservation results that physicists treat as explanatory constraints on what's possible rather than as summaries of what happens.
- The old intuition that laws support counterfactuals and explain their instances; if laws are just systematized regularities, does the account lose that or merely redescribe it?
- Cheap-looking cases: a world with one lonely particle that never moves, or two that never interact. Does the best-system account get the intuitive verdict about whether there's a law there?
- Underdetermination: two equally simple and equally strong systematizations that disagree about what the laws are. What does the Humean say the law is, and is there an answer that isn't just a shrug?

For each, tell me whether the Humean should themselves concede it as counting against them, or whether it's something they can deflate -- and if they deflate it, what the deflation costs them elsewhere. I'm especially interested in whether the mosaic-plus-best-system idea is actually exposed to anything, or whether it's been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's a genuine problem for a view of this kind or just the price of doing metaphysics. I'll say up front that the one that would move me most is the fundamental/effective split, so don't soft-pedal it.
