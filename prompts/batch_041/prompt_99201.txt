I'm a high school junior working on a philosophy paper whose assignment is to take a thinker from the past and ask what he would have made of a problem that only arose long after his death. I've chosen Leibniz, and the problem I want to put to him is the modern puzzle about personal origins: could a particular human being — take me as the example — have been conceived from a different sperm and egg? A number of contemporary philosophers answer no, on the grounds that the very gametes I came from are essential to my being me, so that any world containing a different pair of gametes contains a different person rather than me. Others find this absurd, since the child produced from those other gametes might have had all of my traits, memories, and history, and it seems strange to insist that this is enough to make him someone else.

I would like you to predict what Leibniz would have said about this puzzle. I already know the broad outline of his metaphysics: that every individual has a complete concept containing everything true of it, that God surveys possible worlds and actualizes one of them, and that he wrote about alternative versions of particular people figuring in worlds that were not actualized. What I want is that machinery applied to a case he never considered. Would he say that I could have come from a different sperm and egg? Would his answer coincide with the modern essentialist's "no," or would it rest on an entirely different reason — for instance, on the completeness of the individual concept rather than on the identity of the gametes? I am especially interested in whether the alternative versions of people in his other possible worlds are the same individuals under different circumstances or merely similar substitutes, which some readers take to make him an ancestor of the view that other-worldly versions of us are counterparts rather than ourselves. If that reading is right, I want to know what it does to the sperm-and-egg question, since a counterpart is not obviously the person I am.

Please be explicit about which parts of your answer report views Leibniz actually held and which parts are your extrapolation, because I don't want to attribute to him anything he never maintained. Open with your verdict in a single sentence, then defend it. Keep the entire response under 450 words.
