The surprise exam paradox: a teacher announces that there will be exactly one exam in the coming week and that the students will not be able to predict, in advance, on which day it will be held. The students reason backwards — it cannot be Friday, since by Thursday evening, with no exam yet, they would know; hence not Thursday; and so on — and conclude that the announcement is false, whereupon the exam is held on Wednesday and genuinely surprises them. I want the most important implications of this, not another verdict on whether the students' reasoning is valid. What does the paradox show about the general practice of announcing unannounced events — a regulator's promise of a no-notice inspection, a firm's policy of random audits, a teacher's standing threat of a pop quiz — in particular, whether such an announcement can be true, and whether it can be known by those it addresses? Does it bear on the principle that knowing something entails knowing that one knows it, and where exactly does the students' backward induction go wrong: is it the same reasoning that solves finite games of perfect information, or a different one? Finally, work out the variant in which the day is fixed before the announcement by a fair random draw sealed in an envelope, so that it cannot respond to the students' reasoning: which of the paradox's implications survive, which collapse, and what does that show about what the paradox was ever about?
