Heres a view I've talked myself into and I want to know how exposed it is. The impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the ones showing you can't have a predictor that's calibrated within groups and also has equal error rates across groups, unless the groups have the same base rates — strike me as facts about partitions and weighting choices rather than facts about fairness. Every one of those criteria is stated relative to a grouping, and the grouping is something we choose and can re-choose; slice finely enough (condition on the score itself, say) and the disparity either disappears by construction or gets pushed into the weights. So the theorems constrain what you can ask of a predictor given a taxonomy, not what justice permits. The positive upshot, as I see it, is that the thing to attack is the base rate, i.e. the history that produced it, and that where you can't move the base rate you should just own your choice of who's errors you're willing to make instead of pretending a criterion made the choice for you.

What would count against this? I want the real defeaters, ranked, not the ones that just say the view is politically convenient. In particular: which of my moves is load-bearing, and would it survive if base rates turn out to be partly downstream of the predictor's own use (feedback loops seem to break the given-base-rate framing the theorems lean on); is there a criterion that isn't partition-relative — something individual-level, or stated purely in the joint distribution — that we'd still care about and that's also unsatisfiable; and honestly, is the view vacuous? If any disparity can be dissolved by re-slicing and re-weighting, have I said anything at all? Tell me which of these a defender of my view would have to concede rather than deflect, and what would actually move me.
