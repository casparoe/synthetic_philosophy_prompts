I am helping to revise the submission guidelines for a philosophy of science journal, and the section on our empirical-theory track is meant to implement a broadly Popperian demarcation criterion. The current draft states that a paper's central hypothesis must be falsifiable, meaning that it must entail at least one observable prediction which, if it failed, would show the hypothesis to be false, unless the author can independently justify a modification to an auxiliary assumption; a hypothesis that can be reconciled with any possible observation by an open-ended series of auxiliary adjustments does not satisfy this requirement. A senior colleague has objected that this guideline is self-undermining. His argument is that the guideline itself is not falsifiable: if a paper appears to satisfy it but later turns out to be unfalsifiable, we can always preserve the guideline by adding an auxiliary clause explaining that the paper was not a genuine instance of the kind of hypothesis the guideline was intended to cover. Therefore, by the guideline's own standard, the guideline is unscientific and should be removed. I am inclined to think he has a point, but I also suspect his argument proves too much. Is the colleague right that a falsificationist editorial policy cannot be consistently applied without collapsing into the very unfalsifiability it condemns? If not, where exactly does his argument go wrong, and how should the guideline be reformulated so that it does not fall to the same objection?
