I am a novelist, and for the better part of a year I have been reading my way slowly through Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics in one of the standard English translations — not as a scholar but as a scavenger, because the book I am writing follows three generations of a family that deals in second-hand goods, pawns objects, and lends at interest, and its central scene is an exchange in which two people walk away insisting that they are now even. It is for that reason that Book V, chapter 5 has me stuck. Aristotle there argues that exchange must be proportionally equal, that commodities must somehow be commensurable, and that money is the device that renders them so; but the way he grounds the whole business seems to me to pull in two directions at once, and I cannot decide whether the strain is in him or in my reading. Here is the stretch I keep returning to: "Hence all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food. ... It is therefore necessary that all commodities shall be measured by some one standard, as was said before. And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will. ... Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality. If there were no exchange there would be no association, and there can be no exchange without equality, and no equality without commensurability. Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes."

What I want from you is a careful reading of this passage, aimed at someone who is not a classicist and has no appetite for footnotes. I would particularly like you to sort out what look to me like three separate puzzles. First: in what sense is demand the standard "in reality" while money is only its conventional "representative" — is this a claim about where value actually resides, or merely a claim about which instrument does the measuring, and does the distinction survive scrutiny? Second: if money "does not exist by nature but by custom" and can be "altered and rendered useless at will," how can it also be what makes things commensurable and reduces them to equality — is the commensurability grounded in nature, in need, or in the convention itself, and which of those three does Aristotle actually need for the argument to work? Third: what is he conceding, exactly, when he grants that things "so different" cannot become commensurable "in the strict sense," and does the escape hatch of a measure that is "sufficiently accurate for practical purposes" rescue the account or quietly abandon it?

I should add that the passage sits in a chapter that also treats money as "a guarantee of exchange in the future," a promise that can be held against a need that has not yet arisen, and I would be grateful if you would say how that forward-looking function bears on the puzzles above — whether it strengthens the idea that money is a mere convenience of trade or gives it a standing that the appeal to custom seems to deny. Please argue with the text rather than summarize it, tell me plainly where you think Aristotle is entitled to his conclusion and where he is not, and where you see a genuine tension, resist the temptation to dissolve it into a tidy synthesis; I would rather have the difficulty left standing and well described than smoothed away, since the difficulty is precisely what I mean to put into the novel.
