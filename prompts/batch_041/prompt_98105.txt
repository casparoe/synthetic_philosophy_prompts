I have been trying to determine when it is rational to follow the crowd, and my verdicts on a range of cases do not obviously cohere. I would like you to identify the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that captures them, say what it predicts for cases I have not listed, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

My verdicts are these. Choosing the crowded of two adjacent cafés, knowing nothing else about either, is rational, because the crowd is evidence about the food. In a hiring committee that votes in sequence, the third member privately judges a candidate mediocre but votes to advance her because the first two favored her, and I count this rational, if uneasily so. A doctor prescribes the standard first-line drug although a study she trusts indicates a second-line drug is better for this patient's subtype, deferring instead to what her colleagues do, and I count this irrational. Buying a stock because its price has been rising I count irrational. Buying the same phone as all my friends, though I slightly prefer another, because I can share chargers and get help, I count rational. Switching to a popular but no better keyboard layout, merely because it is popular, I count irrational. Following a convention such as driving on the right, or using the dominant file format, where the only thing that matters is that everyone does the same thing, I count rational. Buying a product because the checkout line is long, having later learned that the line began with one enthusiast who brought friends, who brought friends, and that no one in it evaluated the product independently, I count irrational. A junior analyst repeating a senior colleague's forecast she privately doubts, so as to avoid blame if it fails, I count irrational.

The principle I keep reaching for is that following others is rational exactly when their choices are evidence about some fact I care about, and irrational when they are only evidence about what others will do. This seems to misclassify at least one of the cases above, and I suspect it cannot survive contact with the rest; the cafés, the hiring committee, the phone, and the convention seem to want different clauses, while the doctor, the stock, the keyboard, the queue, and the analyst seem to want a single one.

Please test that principle against the cases, repair it as far as it can be repaired, and if it must be replaced, offer the account you think does best, noting where it buys coverage of my verdicts at the cost of extra machinery. Say what it implies for one further case of your choosing that I have not mentioned, and identify the single verdict of mine it most clearly forces me to abandon.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
