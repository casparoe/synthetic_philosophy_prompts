A friend keeps insisting that the presumption of independence is a fact about the object -- that a complicated enough claim, like the 10^100th digit of pi being a 7, really is as good as a fair coin, and that any formalization worth the name should say so. I want the other case made, and made properly rather than surveyed. Argue that the only defensible version of the principle says something about the reasoner instead: there's no short certificate of dependence, in a description language fixed in advance, and that's all the principle can amount to. Let the relativity to the language carry the weight. If the same object can be described two equally natural ways and comes out independent under one and blatantly rigged under the other, then a heuristic argument is only ever as strong as the description you picked, and the real work is choosing descriptions rather than justifying credences about mathematical facts. Give one toy case where two natural descriptions of the same string, graph, or program pull the credence in opposite directions, then say honestly how much of the heuristic-arguments programme survives -- does it just collapse into we're uncertain because we're bounded, or is there still a real sense in which one heuristic argument is better than another?
