Ok so this is a slightly odd request, but bear with me.

I've been reading rat-adjacent blogs and forums since about 2009, and through a series of unforced errors I'm now the one who runs the submissions queue for a small group blog (six contributors, a few thousand readers, we publish twice a week). The problem is that a solid third of what lands in the queue is some version of the same post: consciousness doesn't do anything; deliberation is just post-hoc rationalisation; your reasons for believing things are confabulated; the feeling of understanding is decorative. Sometimes they're good, sometimes they're obviously circular, and the comment section always ends up relitigating the same question -- if the thing is doing no work, how did the author manage to write three thousand words about it? I've read enough to know this is the paradox of phenomenal judgment, but I've never seen anyone write down a way to actually check a draft aginst it. And my co-editors aren't philosophers, so I can't just say "watch out for self-stultification" and expect it to land.

So: I want you to turn the epiphenomenalism debate's central problem into a procedure my co-editors and I can actually run on a draft. The deliverable is a checklist or decision procedure in words -- the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts, and where all it can say is "survives, but only in a hedged form". It has to work for someone who doesn't know the literature and doesn't have time to read up, and it has to run in about fifteen minutes on a draft we're deciding whether to publish that week. If the procedure needs some machinery to work -- different ways of being causally idle, or the difference between explaining a correlation and explaining it away -- then build that machinery into the procedure itself rather than pointing at where to read about it. Nobody on the masthead is going to look anything up.

Then run the procedure on three drafts, showing the steps and where each one lands:

1. A draft arguing that conscious experience never causes anything and that we should stop mentioning it in explanations of behaviour.
2. A draft arguing that people's stated reasons for their views are post-hoc rationalisation, so the reasons aren't doing the work they're advertised as doing.
3. A draft I actually want to publish, arguing that introspection is unreliable enough that we should basically ignore people's reports of their own mental states. This is my own position and I'd love the procedure to vindicate it, so if it doesn't, don't fudge it -- show me exactly where it bites and what the honest version of the claim would be.

Also tell me what the procedure can't do: the drafts it will misjudge, the ones where the verdict depends on how much coincidence you're willing to swallow, and the points where the person running it is really just making a judgment call that no checklist can make for them.

Please write the whole thing in British English -- we have a style guide and I will definately get yelled at otherwise.
