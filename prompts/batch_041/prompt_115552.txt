I am a retired experimental scientist — thirty-five years of field campaigns, instrument design, and the analysis of very noisy records — and for the past four years I have been reading philosophy of probability with more seriousness than I expected to bring to it. I have come out the other side with what I took to be a settled position, until I tried to write it down. I would like you to audit it rather than introduce me to it. Specifically: tell me which of the following items genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only look as though they do, how severe each real conflict is, and what would have to be surrendered in each case. I have no interest in a reading list; I want to know what in my own position has to move.

Here is what I hold.

One. A degree of belief is not a private feeling that I inspect and report; it is a disposition. To hold a credence of x in a proposition is to be prepared to take either side of a wager on that proposition at odds of x to one.

Two. If my odds across a family of related propositions fail to obey the ordinary rules of the probability calculus, then someone who knows my odds can assemble a set of bets on those propositions such that I lose money whichever way the world turns out. I regard this as decisive. It is what I would say to anyone who asked why they should bother with the calculus at all.

Three. An argument of the same shape settles the question of learning. When I acquire evidence, my new odds must be my previous odds restricted to what the evidence leaves open. Any other habit of revision is defective in exactly the way described in Two, and I can see no principled difference between the two cases.

Four. Coherence is the whole of the requirement. Two people whose odds are each internally consistent have made no error, however differently they respond to identical evidence; there is nothing further for them to get right. Probability as I use it in my scientific work is a different commodity altogether: the long-run frequencies the world itself supplies, as objective as the mass of the electron and no more answerable to my opinions.

Five. There are propositions I will not wager on at all, and not because I have no opinion about them. I would not take either side of a bet on whether I am alive in 2045. I would not bet on the survival of my daughter's marriage. I would not stake a meaningful sum on whether the Cascadia subduction zone produces a great rupture in my lifetime, because in the world where I collect, the money would not matter to me.

Six. Some of my credences are genuinely imprecise. For the proposition that the mechanism I identified in a mouse model is the mechanism at work in human disease I have a range and not a number, and forcing it to a point would be a fiction.

Seven. I buy lottery tickets when the jackpot is absurd and I insure my house, both at odds I would describe as unfavourable to me, and I do not believe this reveals any inconsistency in my odds as stated in Two.

Where does this come apart? Rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, distinguish the ones that are merely apparent from the ones that bite, and for each genuine conflict say what has to give and what it costs. If a single revision would resolve more than one of them, tell me which, and tell me what else it would take down with it.
