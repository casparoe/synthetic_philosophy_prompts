Last night a friend and I got into it for like two hours about meritocracy — he kept saying that if someone works harder and ends up better off then they deserve it, full stop, and I kept saying that "deserves" is doing all the work and nobody can actually cash it out. Anyway it turned into a whole thing, and now I've somehow agreed to present my own take at the public policy school's Thurday seminar, which is going to be a gauntlet: it's mostly empirical economists who study intergenerational mobility and who think "desert" is a word people reach for when they can't measure anything, plus two political theorists — one Rawlsian who will hear everything I say as a clumsier version of the arbitrariness-of-the-natural-lottery point, and one luck egalitarian who cares intensely about keeping desert and responsibility apart and will pounce the second I blur them.

Here's what I'm actually going to argue. The usual meritocratic defense is desert-based, and the desert claim is empty, because any claim of the form "she deserves X for effort E" needs a counterfactual baseline — what she'd have produced or become absent that effort — and there's no normatively neutral way to fix that baseline; every candidate baseline smuggles in the very distribution we're trying to justify. I'm calling it the baseline-smuggling objection, and I think it's stronger than the standard arbitrariness point because it doesn't depend on determinism at all — it bites even if you think people are the genuine authors of their own effort. Then I pivot: meritocracy might still be defensible, but only as a coordination device with good incentive properties, not as a just desert-based distribution, which means we should stop dressing it up in desert language. So it's half critique, half partial rescue, which I suspect is the worst of both worlds for this particular room.
