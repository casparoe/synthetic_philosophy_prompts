I'm about two hundred pages into a novel and I've written myself into a corner I can't reason my way out of, so I'm hoping you can help. The plot: a public inquiry — five members, all careful, decent people — has to settle a set of linked questions. Did the minister mislead the House? Was the misleading material to the decision? And therefore, was the decision itself improperly taken? Each member on their own is scrupulously consistent; you'd never catch one of them believing a contradiction. But they vote on each question seperately, and when you tally it up the panel as a whole affirms that the minister misled, affirms that the misleading didn't matter, and denies that the decision was improper. Which is daft, and it's the hinge of my third act.

A friend who did a philosophy degree tells me not to make such a meal of it. She says this is nothing special — it's just Arrow's theorem wearing a different hat. Swap voters for propositions, preferences for beliefs, transitivity for consistency, and you get the same impossibility result in new clothes. She thinks all the hand-wringing about groups being irrational is a fuss about a relabelled theorem. I don't know if she's right, and it matters enormously for how I write the thing: if she's right, my panel's inconsistency is a parochial feature of the procedure I happened to invent; if she's wrong, I've accidentally put something structural at the heart of the book, and the whole tone of the ending changes.

So — how far does the analogy actually hold? Lay out the mapping for me: what plays the role of the voters, the alternatives, the preference orderings, that independence condition I keep reading about. Then tell me where the two come apart, and which of the disanalogies matter for the point my friend is using it to make. I'm especially interested in three things. First, is the sentences-versus-preferences difference cosmetic or load-bearing? Second, my questions are supposed to be connected — the conclusion is meant to follow from the premises — and I want to know whether that connectedness has any counterpart in the preference story or whether it's doing work the analogy can't see. Third, the chair quietly chooses the order in which the votes are taken, and I'd assumed that was a plot device I'd invented for tension; is it actually something the result forces on me? Then tell me whether my friend's deflationary conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there's a better analogy for what's really going on here, what it is.

Plain terms please — I'm a novelist, not a logician, and definately not a mathematician.
