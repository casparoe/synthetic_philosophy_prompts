I've somehow ended up as the referee in a two-year fight between two people in my department, and I'd rather they just wrote the document they keep almost writing at each other.

The fight: one of them is a heterophenomenologist of the Dennett stripe. His line is that in a science of consciousness we take the subject's reports seriously as data, but the data are what the subject says and believes, and what we explain is the belief and its causes — we don't have to treat the subject's own account of their experience as authoritative, and we're free to explain it away. The other is a neurophenomenologist. Her line is that this is a method for never studying consciousness at all: trained first-person description — the reduction, disciplined reporting, intersubjective checking of the descriptions — gives you data that no third-person method can get, and the Dennett move just quietly swaps out the explanandum for something else.

They're now at the stage of argueing about whether they're even arguing about the same thing, which is where I'd normally say: fine, write it down.

So — draft the joint statement these two could actually both put their names to.

a) The agreed claims: what a science of consciousness is trying to explain, what counts as data, what a verbal report is evidence for, and what each of them is not committed to. I suspect each of them is carrying around a cartoon of the other's position and I want the cartoons killed explicitly. In particular, give me a statement of the fictionalism charge — heterophenomenology treats the subject's experience as a useful fiction — that the heterophenomenologist can accept as a fair statement of the charge before he rejects it.

b) The genuine disagreements, each written so the person who holds it would accept the wording. If a formulation is one the other side would call a strawman, it isn't done yet.

c) The cruxes, the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody. My guesses: whether trained phenomenological description is a skill that produces intersubjectively checkable data, and whether there could in principle be evidence that distinguishes the claim that the subject experienced X from the claim that the subject believes they experienced X. If the second one comes out as no, then say plainly which part of the fight is verbal and which part isn't.

d) For each crux, a concrete case, experiment or argument that both of them would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. And what each of them would count as evidence against their own position — not against the other's.

e) One forced application, because I think it will smoke out whether the disagreement is real. What does each framework say about a system that produces fluent first-person reports of experience — a chatbot, or a model trained on human descriptions of seeing red? He has a story ready, he always does. Hers is the interesting one: either the method tells you something about whether there's anything it's like to be that system, or it tells you it can't, and I want that said outright rather than hedged.

Format: a numbered agreed-claims section first, then a disagreements table with a column for what would settle each one. Something I can hand out in a seminar — a few pages, not a treatise. No literature reviews and no citation lists; I don't care what anyone else said, I care that these two can sign it.
