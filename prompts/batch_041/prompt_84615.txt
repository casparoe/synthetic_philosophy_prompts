I am a doctoral candidate in political philosophy, working on the formal representation of individual rights, and I need help producing a document that two camps in my field have never managed to produce for themselves. I will set out the dispute as I understand it; treat my framing as the common ground rather than as something to be checked against a literature you may not have.

The dispute concerns the impossibility result that links three conditions. The first is an unrestricted domain of individual preference orderings. The second is the weak Pareto principle, on which a strict unanimous preference is socially decisive. The third is what is standardly called minimal liberalism, on which at least two individuals are each decisive over at least one pair of alternatives, where an individual is decisive over a pair when his or her strict preference between those two alternatives settles the social preference between them. The result is that no social decision rule satisfies all three at once.

One camp treats this as a genuine and durable discovery about the moral landscape. On this view, the thought that each person has some matter that is decisively their own and the thought that unanimous preferences ought to prevail are both attractive, and the theorem shows that they cannot both be had in the general form we want them. The other camp treats the result as an artifact of a defective formalization. On this view, being decisive over a pair is not what we mean by having a right; a right is better represented as the assignment of a game form, that is, as a specification of who chooses what, with the social outcome following from the choices made, and once liberty is modeled in that way the contradiction dissolves, leaving at most a conflict among rights rather than a conflict between rights and Pareto.

I would like you to write the joint statement that a committed member of each camp could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. I want the claims they both accept set out first: what the theorem does and does not establish, what would count as a resolution of it, and where the burden of argument lies on each side. Then the residual disagreements, each stated in wording that the side holding it would accept as its own and that neither side could dismiss as a caricature. Then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. For each crux I want a concrete case, argument, or formal consideration that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about how it comes out written down in advance. Where you judge part of the disagreement to be verbal rather than substantive, say which part, why, and what the parties would have to agree on for it to be settled. And say whether either camp has available a move that the other would be obliged to accept as a genuine advance.

My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than adopt, is that the deepest crux is whether the formalization of liberty is meant to describe the concept we already have or to replace it, and that a second is whether every right entails decisiveness over some pair of alternatives. A third, which I find harder to state, is whether the conflict is between rights and Pareto at all, or only between one right and another. If I am wrong about which of these are the cruxes, say so. To give you a sense of the kind of case I have in mind: a situation in which an individual's decisiveness over a pair is generated entirely by a preference about someone else's affairs seems to me the sort of thing both sides would have to treat as evidence about whether decisiveness can be doing the work of a right, and I would want each camp's verdict on it recorded.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose. No headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; the agreed claims, the disagreements, and the cruxes should be carried by explicit sentences rather than by layout, the way one would write a long and careful letter. No references or citations are needed; I know the literature and I am not asking you to point me at it.
