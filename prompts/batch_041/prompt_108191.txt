I convene a fortnightly reading group of fourteen people, mostly retired, among them two former nurses and a hospital chaplain, and it has fallen to me to run the next session. My own background is thirty-four years in experimental physics; philosophy is a recent enthusiasm and a humbling one, and I keep reaching for the vocabulary of controls and confounds, which I gather is not how these things are usually done. The topic I have proposed is the authority of an earlier self over a later one, approached through advance directives: what is it, exactly, that lets a person who will no longer exist dictate terms to a person who will, and why should the second of them be bound? I would like a runnable activity rather than a lecture, of roughly ninety to a hundred minutes, in which the group collides with the difficulty rather than being told about it. My one firm design requirement is that nobody should be able to spend the whole session on the sympathetic side: I want each participant first to compose a short advance directive for a future self, then to be placed in the position of a later self living under someone else's directive, and finally to be required to argue for enforcing the directive on the very person whose position they have just occupied. Give me rules, materials, roles, a timeline with rough minutes, and the wording of whatever the facilitator reads aloud, since I am an amateur with a flip chart and an egg timer. Nobody in the room will have read any of the literature and I would prefer the session not to depend on it; nothing that requires knowing who said what, where, or when.

The debrief is the part I care most about and the part I am least equipped to write. It should map specific moments in the activity onto the structure of the problem: the candidate grounds for the earlier self's say-so (that it is continuous with the later one, that it authored it, that it cannot bargain with it, that it bore the costs of producing it), and why each of them either proves too much or proves too little. I want the asymmetry arguments to be ones the participants themselves reached for during the activity, so that the debrief can quote them back. One thing I would like the session to make visible is a suspicion of mine that the usual comparison to Ulysses and the mast cheats, because in that story the later self is grateful, and here he is not; if the group's intuitions lean on that analogy, I want the design to expose the gap. As a scientist I also want a control condition: a parallel group deliberating the same case except that the later self endorses the directive, so that the debrief can isolate what the disagreement is actually contributing. Tell me whether that can be built into the same session without wrecking it, and if not, what the cheapest honest version of it would be. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it when it does, and be blunt about which feature of the underlying problem the activity cannot reproduce at all and that I will have to supply by talking — I would rather know in advance where the simulation runs out than discover it in front of fourteen people, one of whom will certainly have a real relative in mind.
