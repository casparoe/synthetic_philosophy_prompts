For a graduate seminar on the limits of liberal principles I have been trying to formulate, as cleanly as I can, an argument that the principle of tolerance is self-undermining, and the argument now looks to me too strong: it seems to show that any tolerant order must either be destroyed by its enemies or become indistinguishable from them. I would be grateful if you would tell me where it fails, or, if it does not fail, which premise I should abandon and what abandoning it costs.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) To tolerate a view or a practice is to refrain from using coercive state power to suppress it on the ground that one disapproves of it.

(2) A regime is intolerant, in the relevant sense, precisely when it uses coercive state power to suppress a view or a practice on that ground.

(3) A tolerant regime therefore tolerates intolerance: it does not suppress intolerant views and movements on the ground that it disapproves of them.

(4) Intolerant movements are, by their nature, directed at capturing state power and using it to suppress views and practices they disapprove of.

(5) A regime that tolerates such movements thereby permits their ascent and is thus complicit in the eventual destruction of tolerance.

(6) Hence a regime that intends to remain tolerant must not tolerate intolerance; it must suppress intolerant movements.

(7) But suppressing an intolerant movement is using coercive state power to suppress a view or a practice on the ground that one disapproves of it.

(8) By (2), then, a regime that suppresses intolerance is itself intolerant.

(9) Therefore the only way to preserve tolerance is to be intolerant, and the principle of tolerance cannot be consistently maintained.

What persuades me that the argument proves too much is that nothing in it is peculiar to tolerance. Substitute free expression for tolerance, and the premises about speech that would abolish speech, and the same conclusion follows; substitute personal autonomy, with the case of the person who would contract into servitude, and it follows again; substitute democratic self-rule, with the party that would use elections to end elections, and it follows a third time. If the argument is sound, it convicts every liberal principle of the same incoherence.

A colleague proposes that the tolerant regime's suppression of intolerance is grounded not in disapproval of the movement's views but in the preservation of the conditions of tolerance, and that (2) is therefore not engaged. I cannot see that this helps: what the movement undergoes is coercion, and coercion of a view or a practice on account of what it is seems to be exactly what intolerance consists in. The colleague's escape route would work only if the phrase in (2) about the ground of the suppression were doing genuine work, in which case (2) is not a definition at all but a substantive thesis, and quite possibly a false one.

I should say that I would most like to keep (1) and (2), which have the look of stipulations, and that I am unwilling to accept (9). So: which step do I reject, why did it look innocent, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If the answer is that I must give up one of my two stipulations, tell me plainly what I lose.
