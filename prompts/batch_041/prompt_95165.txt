I have been collecting my own verdicts about identity statements and would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, a reading list, or the names of positions to look up; I want the principle that fits my verdicts, stated precisely enough to be applied. I have invented the names below so that we are not working with the well-worn examples.

(i) A reader of mine loved a novel by Celia Marsh and knows nothing of its author beyond the name on the cover. She has also read a biography of a woman called Marian Halloway and knows nothing of the biographee beyond that name. She has never had occasion to connect the two. When she learns that Marsh was Halloway, I say she has learned something about a person, not something about two names. If you told her that the discovery was really about words, she would think you had missed the point of her surprise.

(ii) Ten minutes later, in possession of the same fact, the sentence Marsh was Halloway has gone flat for her; it now does what Halloway was Halloway does. I still want to say the sentence says the same thing in both moments: it is true in exactly the same circumstances, and it was true before she learned it. So informativeness is not part of what the sentence says. (I have a stubborn impulse to say that the two moments contain different sentences. I think the impulse is indefensible, but I would like it addressed rather than ignored.)

(iii) A colleague is known at her office as Dr. Vance and at her climbing gym as Ruth. A new hire has heard both names, has no beliefs that link them and none that keep them apart — two names, no pictures behind either. When she learns that Vance is Ruth, I say she has learned something, and that her earlier assumption that there were two people was false rather than meaningless. Note that there was no descriptive content in either name for the discovery to trade on: nothing she knew about Vance and nothing she knew about Ruth that could have been compared.

(iv) A third acquaintance is certain that Marsh wrote novels and nothing else and that Halloway wrote essays and nothing else — both false of the one woman, who wrote both. For her, Marsh was Halloway is informative, and I say still true: her false background does not make the sentence false, and does not make her surprise fraudulent. But her surprise is a different thing from the reader's in (i), and I want to know what the difference is.

(v) A traveller has been calling a town Ashby for a week, having seen the name on the station sign. Asked what Ashby means to her, she would say the town with the cathedral, and the cathedral is in the next town over. I say that Ashby on her lips names the town she has been in all week, not the town with the cathedral: a false description does not fix what she is talking about. But I also say that if someone told her that Ashby is the town with the cathedral, she would learn nothing whatever — the sentence would merely rehearse her own error, and would be false into the bargain.

(vi) Imagine someone who knows every fact about the woman in question — her life, her writing, the biography, the novel, all of it — but has never been told which of the two names attaches to her. I say Marsh was Halloway would be news to this person, and news about the names. Set beside (i), where I said the discovery was about a person and not about words, I cannot see how to hold both, and I would like to be told which to give up.

(vii) I am also convinced that if Marsh was Halloway is true, it could not have been false: there is no possible world in which the author of the novel and the subject of the biography are two people and both are that woman. So the principle must explain the informativeness of a sentence that could not have been otherwise.

Left to myself, I would say this: a is b is true just when the two names name the same thing, and it is informative for a hearer just when the two names give her two different ways of reaching that thing. I suspect the second half is too vague to count as a principle, and that (v) embarrasses it, but I would rather have it tested and repaired than replaced outright.

So: is there a principle that gets all of this, or close? State it, say which of my cases forces each clause, tell me what it predicts for cases I have not listed, and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot keep. Two further cases I cannot settle myself and would like the principle to pronounce on: a man who names the same cat twice, having forgotten the first ceremony, and who then learns that the two names are names of one animal — is that informative as in (i), flat as in (ii), or a third thing? And an identity statement in which one term is a demonstrative rather than a name — that woman is Dr. Vance, said by someone who knows the name only from correspondence and is looking straight at Ruth. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the principle you settle on, and single out the one point in it that you are least sure of.
