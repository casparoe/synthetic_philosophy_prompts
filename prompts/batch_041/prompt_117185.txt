im putting together a session for my reading group on machine translation and i want to close with a poem instead of yet another thinkpiece about it. the thing im after is Hofstadter's old complaint, at least as i understand it: that a system can get all the words right and still not know what the sentence is doing. translation is constrained at a dozen levels at once -- sound, rhyme, register, idiom, connotation, which noun the pronoun is actually pointing at -- and something that only tracks word-for-word correspondences will come out fluent while quietly dropping the rest.

so: a poem that is itself a translation chain, in four stages, a game of telephone where everyone ends up nodding.

1. a short poem in a strict fixed form. your call which -- rhyme, refrain, fixed meter, a line that keeps coming back -- but the form has to be load bearing. somewhere in it, one idiom or one word used in a specific sense where the sense matters, and one pronoun or one "this" whose referent you can only pin down from the previous line.
2. a faithful rendering. same content, same images, form gone: rhyme dropped, refrain flattened, the idiom replaced by a plain statement of what it literally says. it should read like something a careful person made on purpose, not a botch.
3. a fluent rendering. now the form is back and the grammar is immaculate and the rhyme lands and it sounds like a real poem, but the sense has slipped: the wrong sense of the ambiguous word got picked, the idiom has been taken literally, the pronoun now points somewhere else. nothing ungrammatical, nothing that sounds off. someone who never saw stage 1 should like it.
4. a back-translation into the original form, which is now a perfectly good poem about a slightly different subject.

two hard rules. the same small set of content words has to survive into every stage -- the vocabulary stays, the relations between the words change -- and no stage may sound broken or silly. the whole point is that the loss is invisible unless you hold the versions side by side, so there's no gibberish and no awkwardness to hide behind. the drift should be the kind of thing you only catch on the second read.

then close with two lines in the voice of the system itself, saying what it thinks it just did. perfectly sincere, and wrong about what the poem was about in a way that's obvious to anybody who read stage 1.

someone who works on translation should be able to point at exactly which level got dropped at which stage and be right about it. someone who doesn't should just find it a nice poem until the ending makes them go back and re-read. pick something small and domestic to write about -- a kitchen, a letter, weather -- the losses are subtler that way. seperate the stages clearly so i can read them out loud in order.
