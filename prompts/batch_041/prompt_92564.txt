You are a philosopher of mathematics who also writes verse, and you have taught the literature on supertasks — infinite sequences of acts completed within a finite time — often enough that the standard moves have gone stale for you. The poem you are about to write is meant to close a graduate seminar on the mathematics and philosophy of the infinite: it should land with people who know the debate well and are tired of hearing it rehearsed. Before writing, think the problem through and set out a short plan — a few lines naming exactly what the lamp setup stipulates, what it leaves open, and which formal device will let the poem embody that structure rather than merely describe it. Then write the poem.

The subject is the lamp that is switched on and off ever faster, each flick taking half the time of the one before, so that infinitely many switches are packed into the final stretch of a finite interval — and the question of whether the lamp is on or off once the interval closes.

Requirements:

1. The form must do the work. A geometric shrinking — stanzas, lines, or word-counts that halve as the poem proceeds — is the obvious device: the poem converges on a limit it never prints, and the blank at the end is where the verdict would have to go. If you have a better device that does the same job, use it, and say in one sentence why it does.

2. Refuse the verdict. The poem should make a reader feel both the pull of "it must be one or the other" and the reason the description has no last word: the sequence of switches has no final member, and nothing in the earlier states forces anything about the limit. The literature divides between reading the setup as a reductio — no such run of acts is possible — and reading it as underdetermination: the acts are fine, and the description simply never fixes the outcome. Let the poem know both readings, and let the tension stand.

3. Take the physics seriously. A real lamp has inertia: a filament cannot follow switching whose spacing shrinks to zero, and a thermal lamp driven that way settles toward a steady glow set by its limiting duty cycle — neither on nor off, but something in between. So the idealization and the device come apart. If your lamp is a physical object, its state at the limit is read off the physics; if it is a mathematical object, the state at the limit must be stipulated, and the stipulation is a choice rather than a discovery. Be exact about which lamp you are writing.

4. You may give a voice to the thought that at the limit the lamp is both on and off, or neither — but the poem must show what that costs (bivalence, or the principle that the state at a time is determined by the states before it), not offer it as a tidy exit.

5. No names, no dates, no quotations, no citations. Whatever the poem needs, it must build.

6. Keep it under thirty lines. Meter or free verse, as the shrinking allows.

The test: a specialist should find nothing to wince at, and a reader who has never met the problem should close the poem understanding how a finite interval can hold infinitely many acts — and why "on or off?" may be the wrong question to have asked.
