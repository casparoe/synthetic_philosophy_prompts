Bit of an odd request, so bear with me. I spent thirty-five years as a chemist (physical organic, then a long stretch in industry), and since I retired I've been reading philosophy -- mostly philosophy of science at first, but I've fallen down the AI safety hole and I can't get out.

Here's a view I've more or less talked myself into, and I'd like you to try to knock it down. The proposal goes roughly like this: if you want an AI to answer a question that's too hard for any human to check, you don't have the AI answer it directly -- you have two AIs argue opposite sides in front of a human judge, and the judge picks the winner. The claim is that this can be more reliable than just asking, because a lie has to survive an opponent who is payed to tear it apart, while the truth only has to be defended. So in principle you get trustworthy answers to questions that neither the human nor any single model can verify. I find this wierdly compelling -- it's the closest thing I've seen in this literature to an actual mechanism rather than a wish.

So: what would count against it? And I mean really count against it, not the lazy version where you tell me the judge might be dumb or biased. Assume the judge is smart, honest, and trying. I want the specific places where the asymmetry between attacking and defending breaks down.

Three things I'm half-aware of, which I want you to take seriously rather than wave at:

First, the case where the honest debater is in the same boat as the judge -- they know the answer is true but can't produce a chain of reasoning short enough for the judge to follow, while the dishonest one can produce a beautiful, false, checkable-looking argument. It seems to me that on some questions the liar has the easier job, and I want to know which questions those are and whether they're exotic or everywhere.

Second, the worry that a debater can bury the flaw in a step the judge has no way to inspect. The argument is broken, but the break is hidden, and the judge's feeling of having understood is exactly what the debater is optimizing for. This seems to me to turn the whole thing into a contest about the judge's psychology rather than about the question.

Third, and this is the one that bothers me most as an experimentalist: the two debaters usually come out of the same training process. If they share a blind spot, the contest never probes it, and the judge's approval quietly becomes the thing being optimized for. The judge is inside the loop, not outside it. That seems different in kind from ordinary fallibility.

Then sort them. Which are fatal, which are merely awkward, which are just the price of any method? Which ones would a defender of this proposal happily concede and still keep the view? Is there a version of the claim left that's still interesting once every concession has been made -- or has it been qualified into something unfalsifiable, and if so, is that actually a problem here, or is it just the normal situation for a claim about what's possible in principle?

A few smaller things I'd like folded in. I have a vague memory that there's a formal result behind all this, something about the setup handling problems in a complexity class far beyond what the judge could handle alone -- PSPACE, maybe? Correct me if I've mangled it, and tell me how much weight the whole proposal puts on that result. If the formalism only covers idealized debaters with endless patience and a judge with endless time, that seems to me like a big deal, but I've been wrong about such things before.

Also, what does the honest debater do when the answer is just a long arbitrary string -- a password, a factorization, a specific number -- where there's nothing to argue, only to check? Does the picture survive that, or does it quietly need the answer to be a claim rather than a datum?

And a fair question about me: is my suspicion just an old lab habit? In my line of work the adversary was nature and it didn't give a damn about rhetoric -- you ran the experiment and the answer came back yes or no. So the idea that you can squeeze truth out of a contest between two motivated talkers strikes me as possibly backwards. Tell me whether that instinct is doing any real work, or whether I'm just pattern-matching from a field where the judge couldn't be fooled.
