I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years, and I keep returning to futarchy and decision markets. Take the concrete procedure rather than the slogan: an organization picks a single scalar metric of welfare, fixes a time horizon, and for each proposed policy runs a conditional prediction market that pays out according to the metric's value under the policy versus under the status quo. If the market's conditional price says the policy will raise the metric, the policy is adopted; otherwise it is rejected. The values are supposed to be settled by a vote on the metric, and the facts by the market's bets.

What philosophical commitments does that procedure presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not a reading list. What theory of value does it require—welfarism, consequentialism, a commitment to commensurability? What theory of legitimacy does it assume—that a policy is legitimate because a market predicts it will maximize a metric, rather than because it was deliberated or consented to? What theory of rationality and evidence does it assume about traders—that they are risk-neutral expected-utility maximizers, that prices are efficient, that conditional markets can estimate causal counterfactuals? Which rival views does it quietly rule out: value pluralism, deliberative democracy, deontological constraints, prioritarianism, the idea that some values are incommensurable? Where is the procedure underdetermined, so that several theories would license it? And where are its parts in tension—for instance, does the separation between voting on values and betting on facts survive the fact that the choice of metric is itself a value-laden and strategically contestable decision, or that the market's conditional price is not a pure counterfactual estimate because the policy's adoption depends on that very price? I am especially interested in whether the practice presupposes risk-neutrality on the part of traders—since otherwise the market price is not a probability but a risk-adjusted price—and whether that is a substantive philosophical commitment or a merely convenient modeling assumption. I am not asking whether futarchy would work empirically; I am asking what it would have to believe. Finally, if we replaced the presupposed view with a rival—say, a pluralist, deliberative, or deontological one—what would have to change in the institution's design? Answer in under 800 words.
