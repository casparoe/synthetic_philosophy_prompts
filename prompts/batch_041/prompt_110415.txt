I chair the board of a six-hospital nonprofit system, and I need a written opinion I can put in the board packet before our special meeting Thursday night. Eleven days ago a ransomware crew got into our billing and imaging systems, they've posted a sample of patient records to prove they mean it, and they want $4.2 million by Friday. Our cyber insurer's outside counsel is pushing us to authorize a payment -- the insurer would cover most of it, it's the fastest route back to normal operations, and the alternative is more patient data dumped. Our CISO is pushing the exact opposite: pay once and we're a payer forever, and so is every hospital that looks like us. One trustee wants us to adopt and publish a blanket no-pay policy. Our general counsel keeps muttering about sanctions exposure. I have to bring the board something coherent instead of five people talking past each other.

Here's the part that's actually hard and I don't want a memo that papers over it. Those are two seperate decisions and they pull in different directions. Whether we pay by Friday is about this incident, and by Friday the incident has already happened -- the crew already picked us, already encrypted everything, the boxes are already filled as far as I can tell. Whether we adopt and publicize a no-pay policy is about the next five years of targeting decisions made by people we will never meet, and that decision only matters if the policy changes their behavior, which is precisely the thing nobody knows. One of our trustees keeps calling this our Newcomb's problem and I honestly can't tell if that's clever or just annoying. Tell me whether the analogy is right, where it breaks, and whether the break actually changes what we should do.

The empirical picture from our consultants: they think targets that pay are something like 30-40% more likely to get hit again within 18 months. They also say the sample is garbage, because the hospitals that pay are probably the ones that were easier to hit in the first place, and the ones with good backups and a real IT budget are the ones who tell them to get lost. So I'm not even sure that number is about paying at all, or about being a soft target. Meanwhile the general counsel wants the policy worded as "we do not pay, except in extraordinary circumstances," which to me is an escape hatch any halfway competent adversary can see straight through. A commitment with a visible exit is not a commitment, and I'd rather we either make one or admit out loud that we haven't.

So please write the opinion. No citations, no reading list, no philosophy seminar -- I need something a trustee who's a retired CFO will actually finish. Pick a side, give me a concrete recommendation with dates and a named owner for each action, and give me the strongest version of the case for paying rather than the cartoon version. American English. Lay it out like this:

- "Bottom line" -- about 150 words, your recommendation on both decisions up front, including what we do about Friday's deadline.
- "What we're actually deciding" -- about 250 words separating the two decisions and explaining why they come apart.
- "The case for refusing, publicly and now" -- about 400 words.
- "The case for paying" -- about 400 words, the strongest version, not a strawman.
- "On the Newcomb comparison" -- about 300 words: where it holds, where it breaks, and whether the break changes your recommendation.
- "What would change our mind" -- about 150 words.
- "Recommendation and next steps" -- about 250 words, with dates and owners.
