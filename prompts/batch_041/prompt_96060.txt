ok so I've basically come to beleive Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes. you know the shape of it: the unit of appraisal isn't the lone hypothesis but a whole programme with an unfalsifiable hard core you methodologically decide not to abandon, wrapped in a protective belt of auxiliaries you're licensed to keep tinkering with, and the real question isn't "is it falsified" but whether the programme is progressive — keep generating novel predictions that pan out, showing heuristic power — or degenerating, i.e. only ever patching up anomalies after the fact and playing catch-up to a rival. I like that it lets me say the Newtonians were rational to keep going through the anomalies, and that abandoning a programme can be rational long before it's contradicted. It also seems to capture something the naive falsificationist story just loses.

what would count against it? I want an honest map of its exposure, ranked, not a textbook recap. specifically:

what is "novel fact" even supposed to mean, and does any reading of it leave the criterion with teeth? if novelty is just "a consequence nobody bothered to check yet," then every programme is progressive forever and the distinction does no work; if it's strict temporal novelty — the prediction has to be made before the fact is known and not used in constructing the theory — then a lot of episodes we're confident were good science come out looking bad. and there's the relative reading where novelty is novelty with respect to a rival programme. is the view forced to pick one, and does the one it needs survive?

can I identify the hard core at the time, from the inside? if the core is defined by whatever the community has decided not to give up, doesn't the criterion slide into being descriptive rather than normative — able to tell me, after the fact, which programme was progressive, but unable to tell me what to do now? that's the one that actually keeps me up, so please take it seriously and tell me what a defender's best move is to get the normativity back rather than just reasserting it.

the no-deadline problem: if a degenerating programme can always be revived later by one good novel prediction, is there ever a point at which it's rational to walk away? or does it collapse into anything-goes? and if it does, is that fatal or a bullet a pluralist can bite?

collapse into a rival: suppose a Bayesian confirmation story, or the Laudan-ish problem-solving picture, can reproduce all the verdicts I care about on the historical cases, without the hard-core/protective-belt machinery. does that machinery earn its keep or is it decoration?

self-application, and I don't want this dodged. the methodology of research programmes is itself a research programme. has it been progressive on its own lights, or has it been in a defensive crouch ever since, rewriting its own criteria each time an anomaly lands? and if the reply is "the meta-criterion is exempt," what makes that principled rather than a special plea?

also tell me which of these a committed Lakatosian would actually concede counts against them versus which they'd wave off, and rank the whole lot from fatal to merely awkward. and include what you think the real test cases are — episodes where doggedly defending a core looks clearly rational, ones where it looks like plain stubbornness, and ones where you can only tell in hindsight. what I'd personally find most convincing is a case where the methodology's verdict is obviously wrong about something I already have firm opinions about, so lead with that if you've got one. no reading list needed, I don't need citations, just the argument.
