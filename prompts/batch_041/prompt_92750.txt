I'm a novelist by trade — about two-thirds through a book set in eleventh-century Baghdad, and one of my characters has fallen in with the Ash'arite theologians, so I've spent the better part of six months reading about occasionalism and causation and I keep starting arguments about it at dinner parties. On the side I present a very small podcast (mostly an excuse to interview people I'd otherwise just email), and next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent her career defending a thoroughgoing occasionalism in the Ghazālīan vein: no created thing has any causal power of its own, God is the sole agent, and what we call the laws of nature are really just God's settled habit of producing the same effect on the same occasion — the fire doesn't burn the cotton, God does, and the fire is only the occasion. She's formidably good at deflecting objections, so what I want from you is eight to ten interview questions that force her to do the actual work rather than restate the thesis, plus, for each one, a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want them ordered so they build, and I'd rather they got properly in the weeds than stayed polite.

A few things I specifically want covered, though use your judgement on the rest. One: the "habit" move — is God's settled habit a genuine metaphysical posit, or just a redescription of Humean regularity with a theistic label glued on? Press her on whether the view is empirically distinguishable from plain naturalism, and have a follow-up ready for the reply that the regularity is reliable because it's grounded in God's will. Two: the argument that there's no necessary connection between distinct existences, so the fire can't necessitate the burning — and then the objection that the very same premise seems to cut against God's own causation, since God and the effect are also distinct existences; anticipate whatever move she makes about God's power being a different kind of thing. Three: the problem of evil and divine authorship of sin — if God is the sole agent, is God the author of every atrocity? — and whether the creature's acquisition (kasb) of its own act is doing any real work or is simply a label pasted over the difficulty; follow up on the likely "it's a mystery, not a fifth kind of causation" reply. Four: the practical stakes — why pray, why take medicine, why blame anyone, if nothing created has any causal power? and what she says to someone who finds that answer pastorally hollow. Five: whether she thinks Ghazālī himself actually held the position or was only arguing dialectically against the philosophers to show where their reasoning leads, and whether that scholarly dispute matters for her own defence of the view. Six, to close: what would count as evidence against occasionalism — I don't want her to say "nothing could", and if she does, I want the follow-up that makes that costly. And do keep them as questions a novelist could actually ask out loud without sounding like I'm reading off a seperate crib sheet — I'll be the one asking, after all, and I'd like to sound like I've understood it rather than just memorised the terms.
