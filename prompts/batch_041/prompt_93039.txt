Next month I'm doing the interview segment for a small history-of-science podcast I co-host with a friend, and the guest is a historian who has spent her whole career arguing that the Middle Ages, the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution aren't discoveries about the past so much as administrative artifacts of the modern research university -- she calls them instrumental fictions. Her view isn't just "periods are fuzzy." She has a two-part test for when a period label earns its keep: the changes it bundles have to be abrupt relative to other changes of similar size in the same domain, and the bundled changes have to be causally linked to one another rather than just happening to occur within a few centuries of each other. On her telling the Renaissance fails both tests, while the Scientific Revolution fails the second but probably passes the first, which is why she thinks one of them is a harmless convienience and the other is an actual distortion of the record. I want eight to ten questions that force her to work rather than restate that thesis. She is very good at getting through interviews by saying that periodization is just a tool and not a claim about the world, so a lot of what I need are questions that make that line harder to use.

Specifically, I need at least one question each on: (a) the fact that she still writes "early modern" in her own book subtitles and blurbed a colleague's book on the twelfth-century renaissance -- is she exempting her own usage, and if so on what principled grounds? (b) her favourite analogy, that periodizing history is like carving the spectrum up into colours: I want to press whether that analogy actually helps her, since colour words arguably track real facts about our visual systems in a way that "Renaissance" doesn't track anything; (c) whether her critique is self-undermining in the historicist way -- the suspicion of grand narratives is itself a product of a particular moment in the discipline, so why doesn't that relativize her claim the way she thinks it relativizes theirs? (d) the merely verbal worry: if these labels are fictions, isn't the whole textbook fight over 1453 versus 1492 versus 1500 a verbal dispute, and if so why isn't her objection verbal too? (e) what she would actually do in a department meeting -- a dean wants a line in "early modern Europe," a TA has to teach a survey with Renaissance in the title; does her view have any upshot beyond relabelling? (f) her proposed replacement, problem-histories that follow a question across centuries without asking when it started, and whether that just smuggles in a new periodization (the long whatever) along with a new canon of whose problems count; and (g) what would count as evidence against her -- what would she have to see in the archive or in the data to say, alright, that one really was a period? For each question give me a follow-up aimed at the answer she's most likely to give, and flag any where you think she has a genuinely strong reply, so I don't walk into it flat-footed. No softball stuff about how she first got interested in history.
