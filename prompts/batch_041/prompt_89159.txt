I'm putting together the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on history and memory, and I need one essay question to go with a model answer and a marking scheme. The problem is that the exam has turned into a recitation exercise: my students can all reproduce the standard framing in which memory is social, present-oriented, and identity-constituting while history is critical, distance-taking, and reconstructs the past as past, and they can all add that the two are "in tension." I want a question that makes that framing useless as an answer — one where a student who has nothing but the framing will write something fluent and visibly incomplete. The students get seventy-five minutes, no notes, no internet, and they should not earn credit for naming theorists; what I care about is whether they can do the reasoning on the page.

To force that, I want the question built around a piece of apparatus that I hand them in the exam itself, so they cannot have memorized it. Here is what I have in mind, and please keep it. Distinguish two offices that a community's relation to its past can serve: an evidential office, in which the community's account is answerable to what happened and stands or falls with the record, and a constitutive office, in which the account is doing work in the present by making the community the kind of thing it takes itself to be. Then attach a condition I will call the convergence requirement: a community may retain an account that the record contradicts only if it can say what the account is doing for it that a corrected record could not do, and only if it stops offering the account as a claim about what happened. The case I give them is a fictional mill town whose oral tradition about a 1919 labor confrontation diverges from the county archive, with the twist that the archive was compiled by the company that owned the mill — so the students cannot simply assume that the record is the neutral party and memory the interested one. The question should make them decide rather than survey: given that inversion, when, if ever, should memory give way to the record, and when should the record give way to memory; and is the convergence requirement the right way to draw that line, or does it smuggle in the assumption that memory and history are answering the same question?

Please write the exam question itself, phrased as it would appear on the paper, then a model answer of roughly 700 words, then a marking scheme totaling 25 marks built around five must-hit points. For each point, give the marks it carries and a one-line note on the specific way students typically miss it — I want the real failure modes, not generic remarks about lack of clarity; for instance, treating the constitutive office as a license to keep believing something false, or treating a written record as neutral just because it is written down. Close with a short paragraph on where the line between an A and a B answer falls, phrased in terms of what the B answer does and does not do. The model answer should show what a strong student would actually produce under time pressure: it should not read like a literature review, and it should not depend on any citations.
