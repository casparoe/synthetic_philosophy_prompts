Next term I am presenting a paper at our joint philosophy–religious studies colloquium, and what I need from you is not a defence of the paper but a Q&A preparation sheet. The thesis I defend is this: once one takes seriously the canonical Buddhist identification of karma with intention (cetanā) and the further claim that the karmic order operates automatically, without a legislator or judge, Buddhist ethics is best reconstructed as a form of consequentialism — but one that fits none of the familiar act, rule, or motive divisions, because the consequences that carry the normative weight are principally the agent's own subsequent mental states, and those states are at once the locus of the good and the object of the practice. I call the resulting view self-registering consequentialism and claim it is a genuine third position rather than an unstable hybrid. My argument proceeds in three moves: first, that the automatic character of the karmic order rules out any genuinely deontic reading, since there is no one to whom the rule is addressed; second, that the reflexivity of karmic consequence rules out standard act-consequentialism, since the agent is never merely a node in a distribution of impersonal value; and third, that the practice of dedicating or transferring merit shows the tradition already treats the good as something that can be moved across the boundary between persons, which is exactly what a consequentialist needs and exactly what the virtue-ethical reading cannot supply. I am confident about the second move and least confident about the third.

The audience is not neutral, and the whole point of the sheet is to prepare me for the objections this particular room will raise rather than the ones I would get from a Buddhist studies conference or from a utilitarian reading group. Roughly half the room are analytic consequentialists from our own department: two work on aggregation and interpersonal comparability, one on population axiology, and all of them will regard any appeal to a self-registering good either as a concealed egoism or as a category mistake, and will want to know how value can be commensurable across lives if the karmic ledger is not. The other half are historians of Theravāda Buddhism who are suspicious on principle of applying the consequentialism/deontology/virtue grid to pre-modern South Asian material at all, who will know the commentarial and Abhidharma literature far better than I do, and who will suspect that I have quietly naturalised a juridical metaphor into a causal law in order to make the view do consequentialist work. There is also a visiting decision theorist who will almost certainly press me on whether merit-making, on my account, is simply the optimisation of a proxy that can be gamed — ritual merit-making for its own sake being the obvious failure mode — and whether that is not a familiar reward-tampering problem in pre-modern dress. I should add that I am willing to give ground on the third move, and would rather not give ground on the first.

Please draft the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each phrased the way the person who will raise it would phrase it — the comparability objection in the vocabulary of the axiology people, the anachronism objection in the vocabulary of the historians, the gaming objection in the vocabulary of the decision theorist — with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is weakest, and an explicit statement of what I should concede at the outset rather than defend. Tell me which single objection I ought to pre-empt in the talk itself, and which of my three moves is most exposed. Finally, include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
