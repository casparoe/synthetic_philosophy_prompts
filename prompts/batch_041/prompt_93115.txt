**Question.** Explain the money-pump argument against intransitive preferences. Then explain how a structurally similar argument can be run against an agent whose preferences reverse over time. In your answer: (i) set out the pump and say precisely what it is supposed to show about the agent's preferences; (ii) explain the role played by the assumption that the agent cannot bind or commit itself in advance; and (iii) say whether the same conclusion follows in the diachronic case as in the synchronic case, and why or why not.

**Student answer.**

The money-pump argument starts from an agent whose preferences over three options are cyclic: it prefers *a* to *b*, *b* to *c*, and *c* to *a*. Put the agent at *c*. Because it prefers *b* to *c*, it will pay a small sum to trade up to *b*. Because it prefers *a* to *b*, it will pay again to trade to *a*. And because it prefers *c* to *a*, it will pay a third time to return to *c*. It ends up exactly where it began, minus the three payments, and the whole cycle can be run again. The conclusion drawn is that such preferences are irrational. The force of the argument is that transitivity is not an optional extra we might drop while keeping everything else: a transitive ranking is exactly what is needed for the expected-utility representation of preference, and an agent who violates it can be systematically exploited by anyone who knows its ranking.

Two replies are standard. The first distinguishes preferences from choices: the pump shows that the agent will make a sequence of pairwise trades, not that the underlying ranking is defective. The "resolute" chooser refuses to reopen the question at each later stage and sticks to an initial plan, and so cannot be pumped. The second reply says the argument only bites if the agent is willing to trade at each step and cannot bind itself in advance; a "sophisticated" agent that anticipates the whole sequence reasons backwards and declines the first trade, so the pump never gets going. Both replies concede that the pump is a real threat unless the agent has some capacity of this kind.

Now the diachronic case. Here the agent's ranking of the same two options reverses as time passes: at t₀ it prefers a small reward now to a larger reward later, and is even willing to pay to commit itself to the larger later reward; by t₁, with the small reward within reach, it prefers the small reward now. This is really the same structure as the case above. If we write out the agent's preferences at a single time, they turn out to be intransitive — it ranks the early option above the late option, the late option above a commitment device, and the commitment device above the early option — so the diachronic pump is just the synchronic pump applied to a ranking that shifts. The role of the "cannot commit" assumption is therefore the same in both cases: it is what stops the agent from stepping off the treadmill.

The lesson is accordingly the same in both cases. The money pump shows that intransitive preferences are irrational, and a rational agent must have preferences that admit a transitive representation. In the diachronic case the extra ingredient is only that the agent cannot bind itself; absent commitment it will keep paying to switch, so the argument establishes that time-inconsistent preferences are irrational in precisely the way that cyclic preferences are.
