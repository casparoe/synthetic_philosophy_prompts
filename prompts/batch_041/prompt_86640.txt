I recently spent a long evening arguing with a close friend about whether technology can be political in its own right. She kept insisting that tools are neutral and that only people can be held responsible for how they are used, while I found myself convinced that certain designs do political work regardless of anyone's intentions, and neither of us could persuade the other. I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week my guest is a philosopher of technology who defends a position she calls the politics of the default. On her account, an artifact is political when its design quietly settles a question that ought to have remained open, by treating one kind of user as the unmarked norm and everyone else as a special case. She illustrates the idea with objects such as a public bench whose armrests make lying down impossible, a soap dispenser that seems not to register some skin tones, and a voice assistant that falters on non-native accents, though she insists that the point is not about the designer's intentions but about what the object's design presumes. I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions for her. I want the questions to press her view rather than allow her simply to restate it. Please include at least one question on how she distinguishes an artifact that is political by design from one that becomes political only through use; one on how she answers the objection that her position is technological determinism in disguise, since she appears to let objects have political effects independent of human choice; one on whether her criterion can avoid counting every artifact as political, given that every design must presume some user; and one on what evidence could count against her view. For each of those four, add a follow-up question keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I am not left flat-footed when she replies. The questions should be specific enough that a listener new to the debate can follow, but not so general that she can answer them with a slogan. Please write your response as continuous prose, in the form of an email to me, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; I would rather have the questions woven into sentences than set out as a numbered list.
