Okay so I've been in a running argument with my book club about sunk costs, and it's gotten to the point where I think we're arguing about four different things while calling them all "the sunk cost fallacy." Here's my current split, tell me if it's wrong. (1) The boring textbook one: past costs carry no information, so letting them into a forward-looking expected-value calculation is just a mistake. (2) Sunk costs as evidence: the fact that I've already put four years in is a signal about the project's quality, or about my own disposition — people who finish things are the ones who finish things — and that's a screening/signaling story, not obviously irrational. (3) Sunk costs as a precommitment device: you make flinching costly so you don't bail at the first dip, and if from the ex ante perspective you'd have chosen to be the kind of agent who honors sunk costs, then honoring them looks rational from behind that veil, which is basically the resolute-choice move in decision theory wearing a hoodie. (4) Sunk costs as narrative: the "waste" isn't disutility, it's a broken story about who you are, and my friend insists this is doing all the real work while everyone pretends it's (1). I also suspect there's a fifth thing hiding in there about organizations, where the person who paid the sunk cost and the person deciding whether to continue are different people, which makes the glib "just ignore it" advice way less obvious — nobody wants to fund year one of a research program if year two always gets cancelled.
