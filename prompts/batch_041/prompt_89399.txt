Last term I sat in on a reading group on moral responsibility, and a friend of mine — call her R — produced an argument that she said she could not refute and that I have not been able to shake since. It runs as follows. (1) To blame someone is to address a demand to them, as one member of the moral community to another, that they live up to a standard. (2) One cannot in good faith make such a demand of another while one is not oneself trying to meet the standard in question. (3) Trying to meet a standard means actually meeting it when one can, so a single deliberate violation shows that one was not, at that moment, genuinely committed to it. (4) Hence anyone who has deliberately done the very thing they are now blaming another for cannot in good faith make the demand. (5) Such a person therefore lacks the standing to blame. (6) But for any standard one might invoke, one has at some point violated it; nobody is perfect. (7) So no one has the standing to blame anyone for anything. (8) Blame offered without standing is not blame at all but mere criticism or cant; only one who has standing may properly blame. (9) Therefore no one may properly blame anyone, and holding one another to account is a fiction.

I want to resist (9) — it plainly proves too much, and it would dissolve the very accountability I care about — yet I also think there is something right in (2) and (5): the hypocrite's reproach does ring hollow, and the hollowness seems to attach to the reproacher rather than to the truth of what is said. So which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I cannot tell whether I am hunting for a flaw in the reasoning — an equivocation, a slide in scope — or whether the argument is valid and one of the early premises is simply too strong. If I must choose, I would rather keep (5) than (9), but I want to see the price. Does the price differ for a wrongdoer who is sincerely struggling to reform as against one who is serenely unrepentant, and does it differ when the blamer is the victim rather than a bystander? Please name the step to reject, explain why it looked plausible, and then say what each of the going positions on hypocrisy and blame — that hypocrisy forfeits standing, that it leaves standing untouched, and that it vitiates the address while leaving desert intact — must pay to reject it. Keep separate what people who work on this take to be settled and what is genuinely contested among them; I do not want a contested view handed to me as the consensus.
