My sister and I are winding down our dad's carpentry business and splitting it between us — tools, the van, the client list, a little cash — and it's been a nightmare of who-deserves-what, so I asked a friend who did a master's in econ how people are supposed to divide something like this fairly. He said there's a proven answer, the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution: you find the most each side could possibly get if the other just rolled over completely, draw a straight line from the no-deal point to that everyone's-best point, and wherever that line crosses the edge of what's actually achievable is the fair split, the point where both sides end up with equal gains. He said it's what you get from Nash's axioms once you drop independence of irrelevant alternatives, that it's the one real fairness solution (Nash's only counts as fair when the situation is already symmetric), and that because it has this monotonicity property you should just use it whenever you're dividing something up. This sounds too tidy to me, and "fair" is carrying a lot of weight in that sentence. How much of it actualy holds up? What would someone who works on bargaining theory wince at, and is equal gains even the right way to describe what that point does? Skip the history and who-proved-what, I just want to know whether the picture is right.
