I'm a science journalist, I've spent most of this year on a long feature about the many-worlds people — the ones who take quantum suicide seriously — and I've been using one source as my sanity check throughout, a philosopher of physics who's been sharp on all the classical cases. Her calls so far, roughly in her words: the standard teleporter (scan you, burn you, rebuild you on Mars) kills you, and the guy who steps out on Mars is a new person who's wrong about being you. Gradual replacement is the opposite — swap your neurons one at a time for functionally identical parts and nothing is lost, that's still her. A perfect copy made while you're still standing there is a seperate person, and if someone kills you a minute later you died, the copy didn't rescue anybody. And split-brain stuff — cut the corpus callosum, let the two halves drift into wanting different things — she says that leaves you with one strange person, not two. What she's never given me a line on is fission, the brain-halves-into-two-bodies case. I never asked, and at this point I don't want to tip my hand.

Next week I'm meeting a guy who wants to actually run the thing. Setup: each round, a spin gets measured; on "up" he gets a lethal injection, on "down" nothing happens. The twist is he's tuned the field so the surviving outcome has an amplitude of maybe a billionth of the other one — tiny, but not zero — and his whole pitch is that he'll still be there afterwards, because he can only ever be in the branch where he is, so the amplitude doesn't matter. He's also got a variant where in the up branch a machine builds a fresh copy of him from a scan taken a moment before, so that branch still contains someone who remembers being him.

What's she going to say about the main version, and about the variant? What I actually need for the piece: what rule is she running — is it that survival requires a unique continuer, so any duplication is death, or that the same process continuing is what counts and branching is just fission, or something about credences and self-location — and which of her earlier calls really pin that down versus which ones only look like they do. It matters alot to me which of the remaining rules each new case would come out under, and if I get one more question with her, which case should I put to her to tell them apart? I'd rather not waste it.
