Self-sampling vs self-indication, and why the sampling unit does all the work

Two assumptions get used when we reason about our own place in the world. The self-sampling assumption says: reason as if you were randomly selected from all the observers who actually exist in your reference class. The self-indication assumption says: given that you exist, reason as if you were randomly selected from all possible observers -- so a world containing more observers is a likelier place to find yourself, and your own existence is evidence about how many of us there are.

They come apart in the case people call the presumptuous philosopher. A coin decides whether the world contains one person or ten billion. You show up. The self-indication assumption says it's almost certainly the ten billion branch, and that you should be nearly certain you're one of an enormous crowd. Self-sampling says the crowd is irrelevant, since in either world there's exactly one of you. The presumptuousness is the bit that follows: if your existence is evidence that the world is already huge, then things that make it huger are things you have overwhelming reason to think are already true of the world you're in, and you end up with the population-ethics conclusions everyone finds absurd.

My point is that self-indication isn't one assumption until you say what the sampling unit is: observers, or observer-moments? The usual formulation says observers, but the intuition motivating it -- treat your own position as a random draw from the positions on offer -- doesn't obviously privilege whole lives over moments. If the unit is the moment, you get a likelihood proportional to the number of observer-moments in the world. Two consequences:

1. Duration boost. A world where everyone lives twice as long has twice as many observer-moments, so it's twice as likely a place to find yourself, other things equal. So self-indication plus moments says your existence is evidence that minds are long-lived. That looks like a free lunch -- nobody thinks turning up alive tells you anything about lifespan -- but I can't find where it goes wrong.

2. The two versions disagree by orders of magnitude. World A: one person, 100 years. World B: 100 people, one year each. Same total person-seconds, same total amount of experience. Observer-version says B is 100 times likelier. Moment-version says they're equally likely. So the choice of unit isn't a formality, and the presumptuous philosopher argument looks much worse under the moment version, because now you're not just favoring more people, you're favoring more lived time.

So my claim is that the presumptuous philosopher isn't a reductio of self-indication; it's an argument for fixing the unit as the observer, and it looks like a reductio only because we slide between the two units without noticing.

What I want from you: is the duration boost real, or am I double counting something? Is there a principled reason to take the observer rather than the moment as the unit, or is that just picking whatever gives you the answer you wanted? And I'd like you to seperate what's actually settled here from what's a live dispute among people who work on this -- I don't want to stand up and present some contested fix as if it were the consensus view.
