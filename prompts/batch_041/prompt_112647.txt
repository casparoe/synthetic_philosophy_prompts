I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size company and I've some how ended up as the person who decides how much rope our internal AI agents get. We ship a new agent or a new capability every couple of weeks now -- the current batch is a refund agent that drafts support decisions, a bot that comments on pull requests, and a thing that writes our weekly ops summary out of the logs. Right now the answer to how much freedom each one gets is basically whoever argued loudest in the design review, and I want to stop doing that.

Someone pointed me at this four-level picture of minds and it's been stuck in my head since. As I understand it: level one creatures are just wired up, hardcoded responses, no learning (Darwinian). Level two can learn from what actually happens to them, trial and error, reward and punishment (Skinnerian). Level three can run the trial and error inside its own head first, so a bad option can die in simulation instead of dying for real (Popperian). Level four does level three but also leans on external stuff -- tools, notation, other people, written procedures -- to think way past what one brain can hold (Gregorian). Each level has everything the level below it has, plus one new move.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my team can actually run, per capability, to work out which of those levels we should be building at. Not which level is coolest -- the cheapest one that works, plus an explicit note for when the answer is that we shouldn't build the capability at all. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure terminates with a clear verdict, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because it's a values or risk call rather than an engineering call.

A few things it has to handle honestly. Some capabilities genuinely can't be trained -- there's no usable signal, or the feedback shows up too late to matter -- and I suspect my team reaches for fine-tuning or a bigger model when the real answer is a hardcoded rule or a checklist a human fills in. The reverse happens too, someone writes an if-statement ladder when the thing obviously needs to learn. I also want the procedure to be upfront about which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as engineering, so I can tell the difference when I'm signing off.

Constraints: I need to run it in about an hour per capability, with one engineer and one PM in the room, no philosophy background required, and I have to be able to hand the output to our safety reviewer as a one-page justification. If a step needs data we don't have, the procedure should say whether to go get it or fall back to something dumber.

Run it on three cases and show your work: the refund agent, where the failure mode is paying out money we shouldn't; the PR commenter, where the failure mode is being annoying and being wrong in public; and the ops summarizer, where I honestly suspect the answer is going to be boring and I'd like to be told I'm wrong if I am. At least one of the three should come out as a verdict I don't want to hear. If all of them land where the enthusiastic version of me would have put them anyway, the procedure isn't doing any work.

And tell me where the thing breaks. Which capabilities will it misclassify, what kind of team will misuse it to justify what they already wanted to build, and what does it structurally not cover? I assume it says nothing useful about multi-agent coordination or about who's accountable when the agent is wrong, but tell me what else falls outside it -- and whether any of those gaps are big enough that I should not adopt this at all and go find a seperate approach.
