What does a theory of when an assertion is OK to make actually have to deliver? Not which account I like — the requirements list. The cases it has to get right, with the case behind each requirement; the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse; the things it has to stay consistent with; and which of those are genuinely non-negotiable versus just the house style of one camp. If some pair of the requirements can't be satisfied together, tell me which pair — I half suspect that's where the whole fight actually lives. And if you had to pick the single requirement that does the most work in ruling candidates out, which one is it.

Please don't just recycle the stock examples, I've read all of those. I want a list that can actually handle a few cases I've been chewing on. One: a support tech tells a caller "yeah thats the known bug, reinstalling the driver fixes it" when she only thinks it's probably the bug — but the caller can undo it in two minutes if she's wrong. The usual stakes line says higher stakes raise the bar; here the stakes are low and reversible, and I want the requirements to say whether that's supposed to matter and why. Two: a company spokesperson reads out a statement she doesn't believe and couldn't verify herself, but which the company has vetted — if the knowledge account is right then either she's breaking the norm or she isn't the asserter, and I want the desiderata to force a clean answer about who the asserter even is when someone speaks for an institution. Three: someone says the meeting's at three mostly to stop a colleague asking again, and would be totally relaxed about being wrong — is that a bad assertion, a different speech act, or fine? Four: asserting something to a hearer who can't do anything with it and won't remember it.

Also build in the stuff from outside the armchair: that we push back on assertions by asking how the person knows, that we retract assertions when we learn we were wrong but don't usually retract beliefs for merely pragmatic reasons, and the experimental work suggesting ordinary speakers are happy to call assertions fine even when the speaker doesn't know. And I care about the distinction between being warranted to assert and being warranted to believe, and between either of those and a hearer being warranted in relying on what they're told.
