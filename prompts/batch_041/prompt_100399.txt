I'm a junior in a political theory seminar on money and politics, and I'm stuck on something that came up when a guest speaker from a state ethics commission visited. I've been going back and forth with my freinds about it. She described the actual regime in her state and it's lopsided in a way I can't stop thinking about. Direct contributions to candidates are capped at a low number and have to be itemized, but lobbying is basically uncapped and regulated only by disclosure: you register, you report what you spend and who you meet, but you can spend as much as you want and meet anyone. Independent expenditures are also uncapped and only disclosed. There's a one-year cooling-off period for former legislators before they can lobby, but it doesn't cover senior staff or strategic consulting, so people just become consultants for a year. So the same system treats a small check to a candidate as a corruption risk worth capping, but a multi-million dollar lobbying campaign aimed at the same official as something we just need to know about, and it treats a former legislator's own lobbying as suspect but their advice to a lobbying firm as fine. What philosophical commitments does that split presuppose? I don't mean the legal doctrine, I mean the underlying theory: does it assume corruption is only quid pro quo and not access or ingratiation? Does it assume voters have a right to know but not a right to equal influence? Does it assume money is speech in one place and property in another, or that contributions are owed to candidates while lobbying is just petitioning? And is the split actually coherent, or is it just a historical pile-up of different theories attached to different parts?

What I'd really like is for you to reconstruct the implicit theory of the practice -- what view of political equality, representation, and the point of elections it only makes sense under, which rival views it quietly rules out, and which parts are underdetermined so that several theories would license the same rules. Then tell me what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with a strict equal-influence view or with a libertarian view that says all spending is speech. Also, is there a reading where the split is not inconsistent but actually presupposes a single theory, or does that distinction collapse? I'm writing a paper on this and I want to avoid just repeating the standard takes, so please be concrete about which parts are strict presuppositions and which are just things the practice happens to fit.
