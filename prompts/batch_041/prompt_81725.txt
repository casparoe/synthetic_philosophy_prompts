I organize a monthly philosophy discussion group in the back room of a pub: about a dozen adults, no formal training, and none on my part either — I have simply been chewing on this for a year. Next month I want to spend an hour on the difference between contextualism about knowledge and pragmatic encroachment, and I need a plan for how to explain it, not an explanation of it. Specifically: the order the pieces must go in so that the stakes-dependence of knowledge lands as a discovery rather than a stipulation; the one case that should carry the whole hour and what it has to have and lack (I am tempted by the bank-on-Saturday case everyone reaches for, and by courtroom standards of proof, and I suspect both are wrong for this group); the misconceptions I should expect and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off — I already expect them to hear contextualism as relativism, and to hear encroachment as "you may believe whatever helps"; and the single closing question that would show me they can tell apart the three things I watch them collapse into one another, namely the meaning of "knows" shifting with the conversation, the fact of whether someone knows shifting with what is at stake, and the truth of a knowledge claim depending on who is judging it. I would like the carrying case to be third-person, where the person doing the attributing is neither the person whose knowledge is in question nor the person with the most at stake — say, someone deciding whether to tell a friend that she knows enough to act — and I want a second, smaller case in the plan where the conversation turns skeptical while nothing practical is at stake at all, because that is what pulls the first of those three ideas apart from the second. Tell me what to leave out at this level and to say so out loud, and how to keep the hour from turning into a vocabulary lesson in which they leave with the labels and nothing else.
