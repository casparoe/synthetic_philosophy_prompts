Take on the role of a philosopher of probability who knows Keynes's work inside out and is willing to stick their neck out. Before you answer, spend a moment working out how the relevant distinction actually functions.

Here's the distinction. Keynes separated a proposition's probability from what he called its weight — roughly, how much relevant evidence stands behind that probability. Two propositions can sit at the exact same probability while one leans on a mountain of evidence and the other on a shred. Subjective expected utility treats that difference as either invisible or as something allready folded into higher-order probability; Keynes thought weight was a genuine, choice-relevant dimension of evidential support.

Constraints: realistic (a clinic, a farm, a workplace — not a lab toy or a sci-fi setup), one paragraph for the scenario itself, no numbers anywhere, and the weight gap has to come from how much relevant evidence there is, not from the two bodies of evidence being about different subject matters. Don't stipulate the verdicts — they should fall out of the theories.
