A colleague of mine defends what she calls the perceptual view of the emotions: fear stands to danger, and anger to offence, roughly as sight stands to colour. She draws three consequences from it — that an emotion can justify an evaluative belief much as a perception can justify a belief about one's surroundings; that being afraid of a harmless spider is no more irrational than seeing the lines of the Müller-Lyer figure as unequal; and that two people who feel differently about the same thing resemble two people with different eyesight more than they resemble two people who disagree. Lay out the correspondence she is relying on, saying what plays the part of the sense organ, the stimulus, the content, the correctness condition, and the illusion, and noting what the perceptual side of the analogy imports that the emotional side may not have. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, which of the breaks bear on which of her three claims, and whether any of the three survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy for what emotions are doing is available, say what it would be.
