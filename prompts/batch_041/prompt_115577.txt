My friend and I argued for like two hours last night and now I can't tell who's right. He's been reading a bunch of AI safety stuff and his line is that strategy can never be a moat: whatever one agent works out how to do, any other agent thats roughly as capable can just do the same thing, so the only lasting advantages are in resources, not in cleverness. He calls it the strategy-stealing assumption, and he says it's the same move as the proof that in certain games the first player must have a winning strategy even though nobody can actually produce one. His conclusion is that trying to keep dangerous capabilities locked up is basically pointless.

What bugs me is that the law doesn't seem to believe him. Patents only give you a monopoly if you disclose the invention well enough that a skilled person could build it — which assumes that the moment it's known, it's copyable. Trade secrets are the reverse: you're protected only as long as it stays secret, the protection dies the second it goes public, and a rival who works it out independently has done nothing wrong — which assumes secrecy really does hold and that some know-how just doesn't travel. And non-competes assume the know-how walks out the door in a person's head.

So what does each of these practices presuppose about what a strategy actually is — a detachable lump of information, or something inseparable from the particular agent and the position it's in? Can one coherent view of knowledge and capability license all three, or are they quietly contradicting each other? And are there strategies that stop being worth anything once they're stolen, or that can't be stolen at all because they only work for whoever's holding them? If I swapped out the view the law seems to assume for the opposite one, what would actually have to change?
