Ok so this is a weird thing to be hung up on but I've been thinking about it for like two months straight. My therapist told me flat out that guilt is the healthy one because it's about something you did, and shame is the toxic one because it's about who you are, and that shame is basically just guilt with bad aim. It landed at the time. Then I spent a week at my partner's family's house. Nobody there ever says "I did a bad thing." They just go quiet, and dissapointed, and you'd rather be yelled at. And yet they're more honest and more considerate with each other than my family is, and my family runs on quiet guilt and nothing ever actually changes. So now I don't believe the therapist version, and worse, I don't even know what I'm asking when I ask which emotion is "better."

So instead of giving me an answer, I want the spec. Give me the list of requirements any adequate account of the guilt/shame distinction — and of the claim that one of them is morally better — would have to satisfy. The cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the findings from psychology and anthropology it can't just wave away, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. Some stuff I want on the list, so you have to deal with it: (1) shame about things nobody knows about, where the "audience" is entirely imaginary — is shame essentially social or can you internalize the witness? (2) guilt with no victim and nobody who could forgive you — survivor guilt, guilt over being the lucky one in a family. (3) shame that isn't about anything you did at all, like being seen naked or being bad at something or being poor, which seems to break the tidy "shame = self, guilt = act" story. (4) people who are ashamed of feeling guilty — a family or a unit where guilt reads as weakness or as ratting everyone out. And (5) the culture-level question, because that's the one I actually care about: some places run on blameless postmortems at work, some run on parading people around in shame, some places have no exit so you can't ever leave the people who know you, and some are anonymous enough that shame does nothing. Can a culture actually choose? And is the choice about what people are taught to feel, or just about the size and mobility of the community, in which case there's no real choice at all?

Flag any two requirements on your list that I probably can't have at the same time — I suspect there's a nasty one where you can't get shame's power as an enforcement mechanism without its tendency to grab the whole self and attach to things people can't change. Say which ones are non-negotiable and which are just commitments of one camp, and if a single requirement does most of the work of knocking out popular views, point at it.
