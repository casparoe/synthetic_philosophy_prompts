The debate below is set out in full, so that it can be assessed on its merits rather than by appeal to the literature. Write an essay that settles it.

The debunking argument. (1) Our moral beliefs are produced by cognitive dispositions that natural selection installed because they promoted reproductive fitness. (2) Selection for reproductive fitness is indifferent to moral truth: it rewards whatever issues in adaptive behaviour, whether or not the corresponding belief is true. (3) Therefore the causal history of our moral beliefs does not make them likely to be true, and we are not justified in relying on them.

The parity reply. This reasoning debunks our logical, mathematical, and ordinary empirical beliefs as well, since those are equally the output of evolved dispositions. No one will accept that everything is debunked; so the argument proves too much. Until an asymmetry between moral and non-moral beliefs is produced, it fails.

The relocation reply. There is an asymmetry, and it favours the realist. What natural selection shaped was not the content of moral beliefs but a disposition to internalize and enforce the norms of one's community. The content of any particular moral belief is therefore acquired rather than inherited: the agent reads off the norm that surrounds her. The case is exactly parallel to language, where the language faculty is plainly an adaptation while the content of any particular grammar is acquired from the environment rather than selected for. Nobody takes the evolution of the language faculty to impugn the correctness of the grammar a child ends up speaking. By the same token, the evolution of the norm-internalizing disposition casts no doubt on the truth of particular moral beliefs.

The counter. The analogy fails at the crucial point. In the language case the input — the speech of the surrounding community — has an independent claim to be a reliable guide to what is being learned, since the facts in question simply are facts about that community's usage. In the moral case the input is the local norm, and the local norm has no comparable claim: what a community enforces varies with ecology, with the distribution of power, and with the costs of detecting defection. Relocation therefore does not save the realist; it only moves the debunking from the faculty to its input. Our moral beliefs are only as reliable as the norms we happen to have been raised under, and there is no reason to suppose that those norms track the moral facts.

A realist might add that adaptive success is at least weak evidence of truth, since some norms are adaptive precisely insofar as they approximate the moral truth. Assess this addition: is it an independent consideration, or a restatement of what the argument was supposed to prove?

Now consider a case designed to sharpen the dispute. On an isolated island, the inhabitants enforce a rule requiring that a fixed fraction of every catch be surrendered to a central store; the rule is enforced because it stabilizes supply through bad seasons. The islanders do not merely comply — they sincerely believe that withholding is morally wrong, and they say so when asked. Suppose the store is no longer needed, or suppose a neighbouring island enforces the opposite rule with equal conviction. Does the islanders' moral belief retain any claim to truth that survives this genealogy? The relocation reply says that it does: the belief is the product of an ordinary learning process applied to a social input, and a learning process is not impugned by the fact that the input varies. The counter says that it does not: a learning process is only as good as the reliability of its input, and here the input is a convention whose content was fixed by adaptive convenience.

Write an essay that settles this dispute. It should do the following.

- State as precisely as you can what the relocation reply must establish in order to succeed, and what the counter must establish in order to succeed.
- Identify the crux — the sub-question on which the two sides divide — and say what would count as argument or evidence for each answer to it. If you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal, about what is to count as a moral belief, say so and say why.
- Reach and defend a verdict on whether the relocation reply rescues the epistemic standing of moral belief, or whether the debunking merely changes its target. Do not end on a non-committal note. If the answer depends on which moral beliefs are in question, or on which theory of moral truth is in play, say precisely which, and defend the line you draw.
- Say what would change your verdict, and why that consideration would be decisive.

Engage the arguments as stated rather than through appeal to the literature; there is no need to cite sources or to supply a reading list.
