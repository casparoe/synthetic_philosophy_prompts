I'm in a graduate reading group on value theory, and we spent most of our last session arguing about whether "better than" is transitive. The dispute was touched off by the familiar population-ethics spectrum — a long sequence of outcomes, each of which differs from its neighbour by a tiny improvement in one respect and a large worsening in another, so that adjacent items seem clearly ranked while the two ends seem clearly ranked the other way — but I don't want another recitation of that example. What I want is a careful explanation of the conceptual machinery underneath it: what a spectrum argument is actually supposed to establish, why we are initially so confident that betterness is transitive, and what precisely the spectrum gives us reason to doubt.

Please build the explanation around a small case of your own invention — six or so options, deliberately not the standard population examples, ideally something mundane such as apartments, job offers, graduate programs, or wines — and walk through it step by step: state the pairwise verdicts, show exactly where the cycle or the breakdown arises, and then diagnose what has gone wrong. Along the way I would like you to untangle four things our group kept running together. First, the difference between intransitivity and incomparability, and where the notion of parity fits: the thought that two items may be genuinely comparable even though neither is better than the other. Second, the difference between an argument that the relation "better than" is not transitive and an argument that our judgments about one particular spectrum are jointly inconsistent, with some account of how the second could be true while the first is false. Third, whether the spectrum is best understood as a version of the Sorites paradox, as a point about vagueness (for instance, the idea that a difference small enough cannot make a difference to a comparison), or as a substantive claim about the structure of value itself. Fourth, the distinction between acyclicity and transitivity, including whether a failure of transitivity on its own is enough to license a money-pump or exploitation argument against someone who holds the intransitive verdicts.

Then set out at least three ways a defender of transitivity might respond — denying one of the argument's premises, insisting that there must be a sharp cutoff somewhere along the spectrum even if we cannot locate it, or reconceiving the relation so that the offending judgments are not verdicts about betterness at all — and say which response you find most defensible and why. I would also welcome a short closing remark on how the structure you have described reappears in the population case (the repugnant conclusion and the mere-addition structure), though keep that brief. There is no need for citations, references, or a bibliography; I want the anatomy of the problem rather than a survey of who has said what.
