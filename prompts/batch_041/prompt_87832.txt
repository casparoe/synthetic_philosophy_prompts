So my reading group has been going through the pramāṇas, the four sources of knowledge Nyāya lists — perception, inference, comparison (upamāna) and testimony (śabda) — and one guy showed up with an argument that the list is too long. I keep feeling it cheats somewhere but I cant put my finger on where. Here it is, tidied up a bit:

(1) For a source of knowledge to count as its own pramāṇa, the valid cognition it yields has to arise by its own distinctive route, not by a route already on the list.
(2) When I learn something from what you tell me, here's what actually goes on: I hear the sounds (perception), I parse the sentence and grasp what it means, and I come away believing it.
(3) But hearing and understanding the sentence only gives me knowledge if the speaker is genuinely reliable — if what she says tracks how things are.
(4) And my reason to think she's reliable is always some observed correlation between her assertions and how things turned out, i.e. an inference from a mark to what it marks.
(5) So the cognition I get from testimony is produced by perception plus inference, with nothing left over.
(6) So testimony isn't a distinct pramāṇa; it's inference wearing a coat.

Then he runs the same move on upamāna and says we're down to two.

My problem is this. I pretty much agree with (2) through (4) as a description of what happens, and I've got zero interest in denying (4) — obviously testimony is only as good as the speaker. But I can't swallow (6), and not just out of loyalty to Nyāya. Because when I point the same machinery at inference itself it seems to work just as well: inference needs me to perceive the mark and remember the pervasion, and my confidence in the pervasion rests on, well, more observation. So if (1)-(5) is a good argument it seems to eat inference too and spit out perception-only, which even the Buddhist reductionist doesn't want. Either the argument is broken or we should all be perception-only, and nobody is.

Which premise do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd most like to keep (3) and (4) — I'm not going to pretend testimony floats free of the speaker's reliability — and I'd like to keep the four-pramāṇa list if I can. Tell me which step is doing the damage and why it looked fine until now.
