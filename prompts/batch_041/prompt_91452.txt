I'm in a small reading group on AI risk — four of us, mostly ML grad students, we meet in a coffee shop that's always too loud and one guy brings a whiteboard marker he never uses — and we keep rehashing the same fight every week, so I want to actually write it out as a dialogue. The fight is between Priya, who thinks anything short of a worst-case guarantee is just cope, and Tom, who thinks worst-case reasoning is a thought-stopping device that would forbid ever doing anything. It comes up because of this specific imagined case we keep circling: a lab that could deploy a model to speed up its own alignment research, where the internal evals suggest something like a 1-in-10,000 chance per deployment-year of a catastrophic failure mode, and the payoff is maybe a 2x speedup on the research. Priya says you don't trade a 1-in-10,000 chance of the end of the world for a 2x speedup, full stop. Tom says you take that trade every day of the week and anyway "the end of the world" isn't a number you're allowed to put on one side of a ledger. Tom keeps quoting the line about how worst-case-only policies would mean never getting out of bed; Priya keeps saying that's exactly right for some beds.

Write me the dialogue. Both of them should be genuinely sharp — Priya is not a scaremonger and Tom is not a reckless accelerationist, and neither one gets to win by being the reasonable one. I want them actually in the weeds with each other, not talking past each other. Some threads I'd like them to pull on: what exactly the "worst case" is quantified over (all logically possible worlds? worlds consistent with our physics? adversaries bounded by the same compute we have? an adversary who has read our source code and our evals?), and whether "guarantee" means a proof, an empirical bound, a conservative policy, or just a vibe. Let one of them press that the worst-case person can't actually name the worst case — every safety property is proven relative to a threat model, so the "guarantee" is only as strong as a modeling choice somebody made on purpose, and pushing the threat model wider doesn't make you safer, it just makes you vaguer. Let the other counter that "the probabilities are unknown" cuts against deployment at least as hard as it cuts against caution, and that a proof you can check beats a probability you're guessing at. I also want them to hit the asymmetry thing: why worst-case reasoning feels mandatory for irreversible harms but obviously silly for crossing the street, and whether that asymmetry is a real structural fact about the world or just how human risk perception is wired. And I'd like them to run into the distinction between a guarantee that holds for all inputs, one that holds for all input distributions, and one that holds for all adversaries in some class — because I suspect that's where the two of them are actually disagreeing without noticing.

Have them either converge on something neither walked in with, or else land precisely on the point where the disagreement is stuck and say why it's stuck there. Don't have them resolve it by someone suddenly getting vague or agreeable.
