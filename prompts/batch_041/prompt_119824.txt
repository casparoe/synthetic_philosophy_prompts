An agent's preferences over three options run in a cycle — it prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A — and are otherwise unremarkable: stable over time, fully known to the agent, and the agent always takes the option it most prefers among those available. It also prefers more money to less, other things equal. It begins holding A, then is offered three trades in sequence, each for a fee small enough that the incoming option is still preferred to the outgoing one, money included: A for C, then C for B, then B for A. It accepts all three and ends up holding A with less money. It could have signed in advance, at no cost, an enforceable commitment to refuse any such trade, and being fully aware of the sequence in prospect, it would have. Note that its ranking of whole histories is not cyclical: it strictly prefers the history in which it trades never to the one in which it trades three times.

What is your credence that this agent is irrational, in the sense that there is a genuine requirement of practical reason that it violates? I put it near 0.35 and a friend puts it near 0.85, so I care at least as much about the decomposition as about the number. Give a probability and a one-line justification for each sub-question you take the answer to turn on — for instance, whether a cyclical ranking is a defect in itself, independent of any pump; whether a foreseeable sequence that leaves one worse off is a defect even when every step is licensed by the agent's preferences at the moment of choice; whether immunity under a costless commitment device dissolves the charge; whether the proper unit of assessment is the choice, the sequence, or the preference structure; and whether "irrational" is doing pragmatic work here (predictable exploitation, self-undermining) rather than per-choice work — then show how the terms combine. Close by naming the term your number is most sensitive to, the argument that would move it most, and the sub-question my friend and I most likely disagree about.
