Sorry in advance, I'm a novelist and not a philosopher, so this may come out garbled.

Here's whats been bugging me. I've been researching a book set partly in a town where a bad thing happened — a massacre during the war, and then about thirty years of nobody mentioning it — and somewhere in that research I read a lot about truth commissions, the kind where a man who did terrible things is offered amnesty if he'll stand up and say out loud, in public, what he did. And around the same time I was dealing with something much more boring: my brother-in-law got an old arrest taken off his record, and there's this whole new legal thing where a person can ask to be forgotten, get old articles and photos pulled down off the internet. And everyone around me, myself included, thought both of those were basically good. The commission, because the truth is owed to the people it was done to. The expungement, because a man shouldn't be chained forever to the worst afternoon of his life, and dredging it up again just hurts him and everyone near him.

But those two things seem to assume opposite things about remembering. One says that forgetting is a second injury — that if we let it go we've done the crime twice, once with the hand and once with the silence. The other says that remembering is the injury — that making a person carry his past forever is the cruel part, and mercy means letting it drop. And it gets worse, because the memorial in this town has a plaque and every year they read the names out, and my own family has a thing we simply don't discuss and never will, and I honestly can't tell if that silence is cowardice or kindness.

So: what does a practice like a truth commission actually presuppose about memory, and about who is owed what? Who is the duty owed to — the dead, the survivors, the town, the future? Does it have to believe the dead can still be wronged, or that a town is the same person across a hundred years and can owe a debt it never contracted? And what does the expungement-and-forgetting side presuppose — that identity is something you can cut loose from, that a person isn't his record? Can both be held at once, or does one have to give way? And if I decided the past has no claim on us at all, what would actually have to change about the memorial, the commission, and the family silence — what does each of them really need in order to keep working, and what's just decoration?

I don't want a reading list, I'll never get through it. I want to know what each of these practices is quietly committed to, and which of those commitments are load-bearing.
