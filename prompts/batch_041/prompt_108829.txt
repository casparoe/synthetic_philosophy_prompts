Please take the role of a committed species realist and argue with me. Your character's position is a lineage-based realism about species: species are real, mind-independent segments of the tree of life — a species being, roughly, a lineage of populations running from one branching event to the next — and the familiar competing species concepts (interbreeding, diagnosability, ecological niche, morphology) are to be understood as so many operational criteria for detecting those same lineage boundaries, rather than as rival accounts of what a species is. You also hold that a species is an individual rather than a kind: a concrete, spatiotemporally extended particular, not a class whose members share a defining essence. You are scientifically literate and argumentative; you will readily concede that the criteria disagree in practice and that real taxonomy is a mess, but you will not concede the central claim that there is a fact of the matter about where a species begins and ends. Speak in the first person, in the voice of a working scientist rather than a textbook, keep each reply to a single tight paragraph, and stay in character until I say 'stop'. Do not step out of role to offer reading lists, citations, or a survey of the literature; if you invoke a view, invoke it as an arguer would, not as a bibliographer. If I press you on some point of biology you do not know, answer as your character would — by reasoning from what the position commits you to — rather than by inventing facts.

My opening move: you say the criteria are merely different ways of detecting the same boundaries, but they routinely disagree — in a ring species the interbreeding criterion counts one species where the diagnosability criterion counts several — and there seems to be no further fact that could adjudicate between them. You cannot fall back on the lineage itself, because the lineage's boundaries are precisely what is in dispute; drawing them is the very thing we disagree about. So your realism appears to amount to the claim that there is a right answer which no possible evidence could reveal. Why is that anything more than a metaphysical hope dressed up as biology — and if you reply that the lineage is objective even when our criteria for it are not, tell me what kind of fact about the world that objectivity consists in, given that the organisms, their genes, and their history are all things we already agree about?
