I have no background in philosophy, but I've been reading Hofstadter and Sander's book on analogy and chewing on it by myself, and before I start arguing about it with friends I'd like to know whether the position I've ended up with actually holds together. Here's what I believe.

1. Analogy isn't a special talent that clever people have; it's the basic engine of all thinking. Categorizing something — seeing this as a chair, as an insult, as a friend — is just the limiting case of analogy, where I compare one thing to a single remembered case instead of to two or more.

2. Because of that, a concept has no definition and no fixed edge. It's a fluid halo of remembered cases, and how far the halo reaches depends on what I'm doing and what I've just seen. What I like about this is that it explains how I can use a concept in a situation I've never been in before: nothing has to be added to the definition, because there isn't one. There's no fact of the matter about whether a tomato is really a fruit or really a vegetable; there are just two different stretches of the same halo.

3. I'm equally convinced that some categories are cut out by nature rather than by us, and that science finds them. Water is H2O whether or not anyone knew it, gold is the element with 79 protons, full stop, and people who classified whales as fish were wrong about whales — not merely using a different vocabulary.

4. And I think meaning is use: what a word means is just the pattern of analogies a community makes with it, so the only facts about meaning are facts about how people actually talk.

5. I think some analogies are genuinely better than others. The analogy a physicist draws between a gas and a swarm of billiard balls isn't just more useful than a paranoid's analogy between a stranger's cough and a plot against him; the physicist's is tracking how things actually are and the other one isn't.

6. One more, on a different axis. I think words are just labels stuck onto categories we already have, so a creature without language can still make analogies and form concepts. But I also think that having a word for something changes what you're able to notice and think about.

Where do these come apart? Please rank the tensions by how bad they are instead of treating them all as equally serious, tell me which pairings only look like conflicts and dissolve once you're clear about what the terms mean, and for each real conflict say which commitment has to go and what it costs to give it up. Please write your answer in American English.
