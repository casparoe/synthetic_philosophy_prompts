The essay:

Title: Why Bradley's Regress Is Not About Relations

Bradley's regress is usually introduced as an argument about relations. If two things, a and b, are united by a relation R, then either R is a further item alongside a and b, in which case something must unite a, R, and b, and so on without end; or R is not a further item, in which case it is hard to see how it does any unifying. I want to argue that this is the wrong diagnosis. The regress is not a fact about relations. It is a fact about a certain conception of unity, and the conception can be replaced.

Suppose we say that a complex is unified when its constituents are related. This sounds like a truism, but it carries a hidden commitment: that relatedness is itself a further item in the complex. For if relatedness is a constituent, then the complex a-R-b contains three items, and we may ask what unites them; if relatedness is not a constituent, we have not yet said what unifies the complex, since we have merely restated that it is unified. The regress is generated entirely by the demand that unity be an item in the thing unified — that is, by the assumption that whenever a complex has a feature, there must be a constituent that is that feature.

This assumption is a residue of substance-and-attribute metaphysics, in which every truth about a thing must be grounded in something the thing contains. Drop the assumption and the regress loses its grip. We can say that a and b are unified by R without treating the unifying as a fourth thing: being unified is a mode of the complex, not a member of it, in the way that being a heap is a mode of a pile of stones rather than one more stone. The regress then becomes a regress of analysis rather than of being. Each attempt to spell out in constituents what unity consists in generates a further constituent to be spelled out, but nothing in reality is added.

The same structure appears in the unity of the proposition. A proposition is not a list of its constituents, and if we say that some further item binds them together, we are off again. My suggestion is that the puzzle here and the puzzle about relations are one puzzle, and that it dissolves in the same way.

The payoff is a diagnosis. Bradley's regress has the same shape as the rule-following regress. There, each attempt to say what following a rule consists in invokes another rule, and the moral is that understanding is primitive. Here, each attempt to say what unity consists in invokes another unity, and the moral is that unity is primitive. Neither regress refutes anything; both expose the limits of a certain kind of analysis.
