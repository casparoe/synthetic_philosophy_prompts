My friend sits on a municipal heritage commission that has spent two years fighting over a statue of a nineteenth-century benefactor whose fortune, everyone now concedes, came partly from a trade none of us would defend, and over a long dinner we discovered that we disagree about something more basic than whether the statue stays. Her position is that commemoration is always addressed to the living: a monument, a street name, a plaque is a way for a present community to say something about itself, and any appeal to what the dead are owed is either sentimentality or a category mistake, since the dead have no interests left to consult. I said that Sophocles' Antigone is the standing counterexample to that, and she said Antigone is about burial, not commemoration — that a young woman performing the rite over her brother's body, once, in secret, and dying for it, has nothing to do with the question of what a city puts on a pedestal. I'm writing the reading note for the commission's packet, so I need an actual reading of the play through the ethics of commemoration, and I need it to be more than the Creon-is-the-state, Antigone-is-conscience version I was taught at school, which is true enough and useless here.

What I want settled: is Antigone's act a case of commemoration at all, and if the modern debate defines commemoration by durability, publicity, and address to a political community — the definition that makes contested heritage a coherent topic — does the play show that the definition leaves something out, namely the thought that the dead are themselves the addressee and the living are only custodians of an obligation they cannot waive? Take the play seriously in both directions. Where it supports the reading, use what it actually stages: Ismene's refusal and what it implies about whom the obligation binds; the chorus's ambivalence, which never quite becomes endorsement of Antigone even when it pities her; the fact that she is sealed in a vault rather than executed, so that her death is administered by the same city that denies her brother a grave; Creon's reversal, and the play's suggestion that the wisdom arrives too late to be of any use. Where it resists the reading, say so — if the tragedy is really about the collision of two institutions rather than about any claim the dead have on us, or if Antigone's insistence looks more like a demand for recognition from the living than an address to a corpse, that matters more than a tidy verdict. End by telling me what the play supplies that our commission's terms of reference cannot: what a monument is for, if the dead are listening. If the analogy I'm pressing is confused — if funerary rite and public monument are different enough in kind that the comparison does no real work — say so in your first sentence and don't write the essay anyway. Otherwise lead with the reading's thesis in your first sentence, then the argument, and skip the preamble.
