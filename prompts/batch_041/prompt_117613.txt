I have been trying to work out my own view about how much rule-following a community may properly demand of the people inside it, and I have been going by gut on cases rather than by any settled theory. Here is what I actually think, case by case.

A cloistered monastery requires its members to rise at four, keep silence at meals, and submit to the abbot's judgment on matters ranging from diet to correspondence. I think that is entirely permissible, and not merely because the monks agreed to it.

The same monastery requires a member to break off contact with any relative who has left the order. I think that is impermissible, even though the rule is of the same kind, imposed by the same body, on the same consenting adults.

A city requires its employees to live within the city limits. I think that is impermissible, even though municipal employees are free to resign.

A homeowners association fines owners fifty dollars for leaving the trash bins at the curb past eight in the morning. I think that is permissible.

The same association, in the same documents, forbids owners from displaying signs for or against a candidate during the two months before an election. I think that is impermissible.

A hospital requires surgical teams to read a checklist aloud before an operation and disciplines those who skip it. Permissible.

A small town has an unwritten norm that everyone attends everyone's funeral, and those who stay away find themselves quietly dropped from the informal network of rides, casseroles, and babysitting. I think that is impermissible, even though no rule is written and no one is formally punished.

A country requires every eighteen-year-old to give two years to national service, military or civilian. Impermissible.

A commune pools all members' earnings and pays each the same stipend. Permissible.

An employer requires its staff to sit for fifteen minutes of group meditation every morning. I cannot make up my mind.

A religious school that accepts public funds requires its teachers to affirm the school's statement of faith. I lean toward impermissible, but I suspect I am being moved by the public funds rather than by the rule itself.

My tentative principle is this: a community may demand a rule of you to the extent that the rule governs conduct inside a practice you are free to leave, and may not to the extent that it governs conduct outside that practice or raises the cost of leaving. I have been calling the first sort bounded rules and the second sort portable ones, and my hunch has been that bounded rules are a community's own business while portable rules are not.

That principle handles some of my verdicts well. It explains why the monastery may set the hour of rising but may not make a monk cut off his mother: the first rule is bounded, the second portable. It explains why the commune may pool income while the state may not conscript two years: the commune can be left and the state cannot. It may even explain the funeral norm, on the ground that what the town does to those who skip is precisely to raise the cost of staying. But it also runs my verdicts on the trash-bin fine and the residency requirement straight through in the wrong direction. The trash-bin rule is enforced by a penalty I could escape only by selling my house, and I think it is permissible anyway; the residency requirement costs a city employee nothing but a resignation to escape, and I think it is impermissible anyway. If exit cost is what matters, I have those two backwards. So I suspect I am tracking something else as well, perhaps whether the rule is a condition of membership in a practice rather than a condition laid on the member, or perhaps something about who is doing the demanding, or perhaps about how visible and irreversible the demand is. I do not know which of these is doing the real work, and I would rather be told than guess.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough that I can see where they part company. For each clause, tell me which case in my list forces it. Then tell me what the principle says about at least three cases I have not listed, one of which should be a case in which the same rule is imposed once by a private association and once by a state, so that I can see whether the identity of the enforcer is doing any independent work. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. If saving every verdict requires a principle with an ugly extra clause, say which verdict I should give up instead, and why I should trust the argument over my gut there. If there is a single verdict of mine that, revised, would simplify the rest of the picture most, tell me which one it is. Please write in American English.
