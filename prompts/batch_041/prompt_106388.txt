Question 3. In the literature on the norm of assertion, three candidate norms are standardly distinguished:

(T) One may assert a proposition p only if p is true.
(B) One may assert p only if one believes p.
(K) One may assert p only if one knows p.

Answer all four parts.

(a) (6 points) State the relations of entailment among (T), (B), and (K), and say whether any of the three is idle given the others.

(b) (10 points) A critic argues as follows: since knowledge entails truth, any assertion that satisfies (K) satisfies (T), and any assertion that violates (T) violates (K); the knowledge norm therefore excludes nothing that the truth norm does not already exclude, and is idle. Are the critic's premises true? Does the conclusion follow? Justify your answers.

(c) (9 points) Some writers hold that the norm of assertion is constitutive of the practice of assertion rather than merely regulative. Explain the distinction, and say what, if anything, would be lost by treating the norm as merely regulative.

(d) (10 points) Consider the following case. An autonomous system is deployed to issue public weather warnings. When its sensors cross a fixed threshold it emits the sentence, "A tornado will touch down in your county within the hour." The system has no beliefs or knowledge in any ordinary sense; it registers a sensor reading and emits the sentence. Its warnings are true 94% of the time, and evacuations prompted by them reliably save lives. Assume, for the sake of argument, that the emissions are assertions. What does each of (T), (B), and (K) imply about the permissibility of these assertions, and what bearing, if any, does the case have on the choice among the three norms?

The student's answer:

(a) The three norms state necessary conditions on permissible assertion. Because knowledge entails both belief and truth, (K) entails (T) and (B). (T) and (B) do not entail each other: one may believe a falsehood, and one may fail to believe a truth, as when an examinee utters a sentence she is certain is false but which is in fact true. (K) is thus the strongest of the three, and the one most exposed to counterexample.

(b) The critic's premises are true. If knowing entails the truth of what is known, then every assertion that violates (T) also violates (K), and every assertion that satisfies (K) satisfies (T). But the conclusion does not follow from those premises. The entailment runs in the wrong direction for the critic's purposes: because (K) entails (T) and not conversely, it is (T), the weaker norm, that adds no exclusion of its own once (K) is in place. (K) excludes everything (T) excludes and more besides — namely assertions of true propositions that the speaker does not know. So the critic has shown, if anything, that the truth norm is idle, not the knowledge norm. The knowledge theorist can add that the additional exclusions are precisely the ones that matter: what is objectionable about the lucky guess is not that it might have been false, but that the speaker did not know.

(c) A norm is constitutive when it partly defines the activity it governs, so that flouting it renders the utterance a defective move rather than merely a forbidden one; a norm is regulative when it is a rule that the activity happens to carry. The distinction shows up in how counterexamples are read. Against a regulative norm, a counterexample is a case in which the norm's verdict is outweighed; against a constitutive norm, it must be a case that is still recognisably an assertion. In practice, however, a constitutive norm is simply a norm that cannot be outweighed: it is the weightiest kind of regulative rule, one that takes priority over every other consideration within the practice. The distinction is therefore one of degree rather than of kind.

(d) The belief norm has no purchase here, since the system has no beliefs. The truth norm is satisfied whenever a warning is accurate, which on the stipulated figures is most of the time. The knowledge norm is never satisfied, since the system does not know anything. But the warnings are useful and save lives, so the case counts against the knowledge norm: it shows that a practice of assertion can be valuable even when the norm it is measured against is systematically violated, and hence that the knowledge norm demands more than assertion can reasonably require.
