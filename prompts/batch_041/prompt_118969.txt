I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to think through the claim that statistical machine translation — systems trained to predict text, which produce fluent translations without any model of what the words are about — is in some important sense shallow, and that this shallowness survives however good the output gets. Please take the role of someone who works on both the philosophy of understanding and the engineering of these systems, think carefully, and brainstorm before you commit to an answer.

Assume I've drawn up a list of candidate criteria for deciding whether such a system understands a sentence it has translated. Rank them in tiers by how much evidential weight you'd give each as a sign of genuine understanding, and say in a sentence or two why each lands where it does:

1. Round-trip fidelity: translate into the target language and back with an independent system; count understanding as present only if meaning survives the round trip.
2. Counterfactual substitution: swap a source word for a near-synonym with different connotations and require that the translation shift in the corresponding way.
3. Justification: the system must be able to defend a particular lexical choice by appeal to the nuance of the source rather than the frequency of the phrase in its training data.
4. Garden-path interference: a preceding sentence that primes a false reading should distort the system's translation the way it distorts a human's, and no more.
5. Productive ambiguity: on a pun or a deliberate double meaning, the system should either preserve both readings or flag the choice, rather than silently collapsing to one.

Treat the last two as my own constructions rather than anything standard, and tell me if either is incoherent as stated. I also want you to name the criterion most likely to be passed by a system that plainly does not understand, and the one most likely to be failed by a system that does. Close by saying how confident you are in the ranking as a whole and singling out the step you are least sure of.
