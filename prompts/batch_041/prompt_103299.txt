For a book I am writing about how categories end up shaping the people they describe, I have spent the better part of a year reading the philosophical literature on psychiatric classification, and I now understand, in its own terms, the argument that human kinds are interactive: that classifying people can change the people classified, which in turn changes what the classification ought to say, and round again. What I still lack is an honest translation of that argument into the vocabulary I actually use when I report on machine learning, and a clear-eyed reckoning with what the translation destroys.

Please restate the looping-effects picture, together with the surrounding debate over whether a psychiatric category can be valid in anything like the way a category of disease is supposed to be, in the language of statistical learning under feedback. I am fluent at the level of a well-read reporter rather than a practitioner: I can follow training distributions, distribution shift, concept drift, labels and label noise, retraining, and feedback loops, and I have read the recent work on performative prediction and strategic classification, in which the act of predicting alters the very target the model is trying to predict. Take the translation as far as it will honestly go. Map the looping effect onto the feedback dynamics; map the worry about validity onto the worry about whether a model can estimate anything stable once its own deployment perturbs the distribution; and map the contrast between categories of people and categories of things onto whichever distinction in my vocabulary comes closest.

Then the part I actually want: the residue report. For each element of the philosophical picture, say whether it survives intact, survives only in a distorted form, or has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I am most interested in three candidates, and I would like you to rule on whether each is a genuine residue or merely a gap that better formalism would close. First, the claim that the classification does not merely cause a shift in the target but partly constitutes the kind. Second, the claim that the classified are interpreting themselves, so that the loop runs through meaning rather than through incentives alone. Third, the normative charge that the category is doing something it should not, which appears to have no home in a purely predictive framework. I would also like you to tell me whether the psychiatric case is genuinely special or is just ordinary measurement reactivity or indicator-gaming under a grander name; if the philosophers have a stronger claim than that, I want to see exactly where the extra strength comes from.

I would rather have a translation that fails honestly at a few points than one that succeeds by quietly assuming the answers, so mark your own fudges and tell me which of the three residues, if any, reflects a real shortcoming of the machine-learning vocabulary rather than a philosophical claim that was doing less work than it appeared to. No citations needed; I am after the mapping and the ledger, not a reading list.
