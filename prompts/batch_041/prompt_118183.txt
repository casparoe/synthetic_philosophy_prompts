I sit on the long-term stewardship committee for our state's abandoned-mine reclamation fund — the setup is that a pot of money from a mining severance tax has to cover acid-drainage treatment basically forever, so the committee has to pick a discount rate to convert an endless stream of future treatment costs into a single number today, and then decide how much we're actually obliged to bank now. We've been deadlocked for two meetings, and on Saturday I got into a four-hour fight about it at a barbecue with my friend Dana, who's an economist.

Her line is that the rate should just be the market rate, call it 4% real, because that's the opportunity cost of tying the money up, and any lower rate is "a hidden tax on the current generation." My line was that at 4% a dollar of treatment cost in the year 2200 is worth essentially nothing today, so we'd be rationally choosing to hand the next dozen generations poisoned water, which can't be right, and that the whole point of a permanent fund is precisely that we're not allowed to do that. Where it got genuinely in the weeds is that our actuary has floated splitting the rate into two seperate pieces: a "pure impatience" piece, which he wants set to exactly zero, and a "non-existance" piece — a discount for the probability that by year t there's no state, no fund, and nobody owed anything — which he wants set at something like 0.5–1% to capture political and institutional risk over centuries. Dana says that's a shell game: a non-existence discount does the exact same arithmetic work as impatience, so renaming it doesn't launder it, and anyway the whole notion of "owing" something to a generation that might never exist is confused. I think there's a real difference between "I care less because it's later" and "it might not happen at all," but I couldn't articulate it under pressure, and now I have to actually write something. (One other member keeps muttering that we should just use a declining schedule with lower rates for the far future, but nobody has explained to me why that wouldn't be arbitrary.)

Could you draft the short opinion I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? I need a concrete recommendation — a specific rate or rate schedule and what we should bank this year, or at minimum a defensible formula with the parameter values you'd plug in — plus the reasoning behind it. And I want the strongest version of Dana's opportunity-cost point engaged head-on rather than waved away, since two members are likely to vote with her. If you think the actuary's two-piece decomposition is the right frame, say so and tell me how to defend it against the shell-game charge; if you think it's a mistake, say that instead and give me the version I should be pushing.
