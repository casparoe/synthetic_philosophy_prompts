my roommate's in law school and she keeps explaining the standards of proof to me over dinner, and honestly the more I understand it the less it makes sense. Here's the setup as I've got it: a cop can stop you and pat you down if she has reasonable suspicion, which is a low bar; to arrest you she needs probable cause; to convict you a jury needs proof beyond a reasonable doubt; and if the victim just sues you instead, they only have to show it's more likely than not — so the exact same act, with the exact same evidence, can get you acquitted in one courtroom and held liable in the next. Four tiers, one body of evidence, and the tier seems to get picked by how bad it would be to get it wrong: convicting an innocent person is the worst, wrongly arresting them is less bad, wrongly stopping them barely registers, and wrongly losing a lawsuit is just the cost of doing business. The part that really gets me is that my roommate also says the law won't let you off for not knowing what the law is — everyone is treated as knowing the rules whether they've ever read them or not — but if you deliberately bury your head in the sand and avoid learning some fact, a judge can instruct the jury to treat you as if you knew it all along. So in one place not-knowing counts as knowing, and in another place knowing is a bar set so high that the cop, the prosecutor and the jury all seem to count as knowing different things about the same man.

What I want to know is what theory of knowledge all this presupposes. Does the law actually think that whether you know something depends on whats practically at stake for you — that the very same person with the very same evidence can literally know something in the civil case and fail to know it in the criminal one? Or does it only need the weaker claim that what you should *do* on the basis of a belief shifts with the stakes while knowledge itself sits still, so the four tiers are just four tolerances for error and nothing epistemic is happening at all? Which views of knowledge does the arrangement quietly rule out, and which ones would license every bit of it? And is it even internally consistent — I have a nagging feeling the head-in-the-sand rule and the everybody-knows-the-law rule are pulling in opposite directions, but I can't tell whether thats a real contradiction or just two unrelated things wearing the same word. Last thing: if somebody insisted that knowledge never varies with practical stakes, what would actually have to change here — the labels on the four tiers, or the verdicts themselves?
