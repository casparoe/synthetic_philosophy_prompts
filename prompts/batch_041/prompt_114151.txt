I convene a final-year seminar on the history of economic thought, and the closing third of the course is given over to the socialist calculation debate: Mises's challenge of the early 1920s, Hayek's reformulation of it in the 1930s and 40s around the division and dispersion of knowledge, and Lange's market-socialist reply, with its Central Planning Board adjusting prices by trial and error until excess demand is eliminated. My students can recite the standard arc without hesitation — Mises holds that rational calculation is impossible without prices for capital goods, Hayek holds that the deeper problem is that the relevant knowledge is dispersed, often tacit, and never given to a single mind, and Lange answers that a board can set the prices while managers are instructed to price at marginal cost and minimise average cost. What none of them can do is tell me whether a computationally enhanced version of Lange's board — one that receives mandatory, real-time, machine-readable returns from every firm and household and solves the equilibrium system directly rather than groping towards it — actually answers Hayek's objection or merely restates it in silicon, and what would have to be true about the knowledge for the answer to go through. That is the crux on which I want the final essay to turn.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. The scheme should keep the calculation problem and the knowledge problem apart, so that it distinguishes a candidate who sees them as two objections from one who collapses them into a single charge; it should penalise the reflex answer that a sufficiently powerful computer simply solves the matter, unless the candidate states what would have to be true about the knowledge, the incentives, and the entrepreneurial discovery process for that to work; and it should be built around must-hit points, the marks each carries (total 25), and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong. I want the question answerable in a ninety-minute sitting, and pitched so that it cannot be answered by reproducing a summary of the debate.

The model answer should be explicit about where the historical participants genuinely disagreed as opposed to where later commentary has smoothed them into a comfortable consensus — for instance, whether Hayek's objection is really distinct from Mises's or only a refinement of it, whether Lange's board is internally coherent as a pricing mechanism, and whether the incentive critique is a fair reading of Lange or an addition by his critics. Finally, I want both the model answer and the marking scheme to mark the boundary between what is settled among specialists in this debate and what remains genuinely contested, and not to present a contested reading as the consensus; I would rather see a candidate concede that a point is disputed than assert it as established.
