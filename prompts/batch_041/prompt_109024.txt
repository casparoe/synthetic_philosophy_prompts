I retired a few years back — I was a physical chemist, thirty-odd years of thermodynamics, so I came into this thinking I already understood the Second Law better than any philosopher ever would — and since then I've been working my way through philosophy of physics. It's been humbling.

Anyway, I just finished a popular book about time (I won't say which, I don't want to color your answer) and the chapter on the arrow of time has been nagging at me. Here's the gist of what it says:

The laws of physics are all perfectly symmetric in time — run Newton's or Maxwell's or Schrödinger's equations with time going backward and you get a perfectly legal description of the world. Yet the world we actually live in is plainly not symmetric: coffee cools, eggs break, we remember yesterday and not tomorrow. The explanation for all of this is the Second Law of Thermodynamics. Entropy — roughly, disorder — always increases, and it's this increase that gives time its direction. The past looks different from the future for exactly one reason: entropy was lower in the past. Time's arrow just is the arrow of entropy. Strictly speaking nothing is truly irreversible — a shattered egg could reassemble itself — it's just so staggeringly improbable that we never see it happen, and that tells us that the direction of time is really a kind of illusion, an artefact of our own ignorance of the microscopic details. If you could somehow know the position and velocity of every particle, past and future would look identical to you, and there would be no arrow at all. This also explains memory: remembering is just a process that raises entropy, which is why it can only run one way.

Something feels off and I can't put my finger on it. My gut says that "it's all just an illusion" bit can't be the boring settled truth the author makes it out to be — but I've also spent my whole career treating the Second Law as bedrock, so maybe I'm just emotionally attached to it doing more work than it actually does. A friend of mine who did a PhD in philosophy mentioned something about a "past hypothesis," that we have to assume the universe simply started out in a very low-entropy state, which I'd never heard of, and I suspect that's relevent to whether the book is being fair to the physics.

So — is this a fair summary? Go through it more or less sentence by sentence and tell me what a philosopher of physics would nod along to, what they'd wince at, what's a harmless popularization, and what's straightforwardly wrong. And the author draws a conclusion at the end, that the direction of time is "really" our ignorance — does that follow from anything true in the rest of the summary, or is it smuggled in?
