Consider the debate protocol for scalable oversight in AI safety. In the version I have in mind, two AI systems are given the same question and asked to argue for opposing answers; a human judge, who cannot solve the question on their own and may have no domain expertise, reads the arguments, may ask follow-up questions, and then decides which side is right. The judge’s decision is used both to grade the episode and, in some proposals, to train the debaters. The protocol is supposed to scale oversight: it lets a weak judge supervise a strong model by forcing the model’s claims into an adversarial exchange where falsehood is harder to defend than truth.

I want an account of what philosophical commitments this practice presupposes. Start by reconstructing the implicit theory of the protocol: what it assumes about truth, justification, and rationality; what it assumes about the human judge’s epistemic capacities; what it assumes about the relationship between winning a debate and being correct; and what it assumes about the stability of deception under cross-examination. Then distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. For instance, does it need the strong claim that the truth is uniquely defensible in adversarial argument, or only the weaker claim that a truthful debater has an advantage over a deceptive one? Does it need a correspondence theory of truth, or is it compatible with deflationism, pragmatism, or a verificationist view? Does it presuppose that there is a fact of the matter about the question being debated, and if so, what kind of fact? Does it presuppose that the judge can recognize valid reasoning without being able to produce it, and is that assumption coherent given the competence-without-comprehension literature?

Then identify the rival views the practice quietly rules out. For example, does it rule out the possibility that a sufficiently capable deceptive model could win a debate against an honest one? Does it rule out the view that human judgment is so opaque or biased that no judge can reliably track the better argument? Does it rule out the view that oversight requires the overseer to comprehend the domain rather than merely adjudicate? Does it rule out a purely consequentialist or purely procedural account of the protocol’s justification?

Finally, say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it, and tell me what would have to change in the protocol if we replaced the presupposed view with a rival one. For example, if we denied that adversarial argument selects for truth, what would the protocol need instead? If we denied that a weak judge can track validity without domain competence, what would scalable oversight have to become? Be concrete about the design changes, not just the philosophical labels.
