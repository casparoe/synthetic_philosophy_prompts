Please explain the conceptual issue that has come to be called expected-value fanaticism, and explain it from the inside, as a structural problem, rather than by summarizing the surrounding debate.

Start from this case. A button costs c to press and, with probability p, brings about an outcome of value V; otherwise it brings about nothing, and 0 < c < V. Expected-value maximization says to press whenever pV > c. Because V is unbounded — there is no largest amount of good that an outcome can contain — for every fixed cost there is some probability small enough that pressing is required. Two features of the case are essential to me. First, the structure is sign-symmetric: the reasoning that sends you chasing an enormous long shot also obliges you to pay a great deal to avert an outcome of value −V that occurs with probability p. Second, nothing here needs infinities or astronomical magnitudes; unboundedness by itself seems to do the work.

What I want is the anatomy of the difficulty.

(1) State exactly which assumptions about value and about rational preference, taken jointly, generate these verdicts, and say why infinity and astronomical size are red herrings.

(2) For each of the natural repairs — bounding the value scale; imposing a floor below which probabilities are simply neglected; treating the probabilities as imprecise or indeterminate rather than tiny; giving up the continuity condition that makes a preference ranking representable by expected value; or simply accepting the fanatical verdicts — say what the repair buys, what it costs, and whether the repaired preferences remain representable as the expected value of something.

(3) Test the repairs against a decomposition. Take the floor repair, on which probabilities below some threshold are neglected. I can offer you one chance in 10^9 of V, or a thousand independent chances of one in 10^12 each. If the first is negligible and the second is not, is that a principled distinction or an artifact of how the options are packaged? And if it is an artifact, what becomes of the repair?

End by saying where you take the crux to lie, and what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move you to a different verdict about which repair is least costly, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
