I have a colleague who works on probability in the Everettian, or many-worlds, interpretation of quantum mechanics, and for some time now I have been trying to reconstruct the rule behind the credences she assigns in branching scenarios. Her verdicts so far are these. In a measurement with two branches of equal amplitude, one successor of hers in each, she assigns a credence of one half to each outcome. In the Sleeping Beauty puzzle — a fair coin determines whether she is woken once or twice, with amnesia between the wakings and nothing to distinguish them — she answers one half on waking, and insists that the second waking adds nothing. In a measurement with three branches of equal amplitude, two of which realise the same macroscopic outcome and the third a different one, she assigns two thirds to the outcome realised twice. She has twice declined to answer when I asked her about cases in which the amplitudes of the branches are unequal, and I have begun to suspect that the symmetry of her examples has been doing some of the work.

Consider, now, a measurement with three decohered branches, of amplitudes √0.4, √0.4 and √0.2, the first two realising outcome A and the third outcome B, with exactly one successor of hers in each branch. What credence would she assign to A? I am less interested in the figure itself than in the rule she appears to be running, in the other rules that fit her three verdicts just as well, in which of those rules the new case separates, and in what further case would settle the matter between them. I should like your best guess at what she would say, together with the reasons for preferring one reconstruction over another; and if the rule you take her to be running is one you think she cannot sustain, say what she would have to give up in order to hold the alternative. There is no need to cite any literature — I am after the reasoning.
