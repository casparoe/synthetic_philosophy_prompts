I am running a two-hour workshop for the board of a small housing cooperative. The members are intelligent non-philosophers who must regularly decide both collective policies and matters they regard as private to individual households. I want to teach them Sen's liberal paradox, the conflict between minimal liberalism and the Pareto principle, not as a curiosity but as a diagnostic tool for spotting when a decision procedure is incoherent. Plan the session for me: the order in which the ideas must be introduced so that the impossibility feels like a consequence rather than a trick; the one concrete cooperative case that should carry the whole thing (I want to avoid the standard literary example; use something like a dispute over whether a member may keep a pet rat in their own unit alongside the cooperative's collective preference about a building-wide exotic-pet policy, but make the preference structure actually generate the cycle); the misconceptions they will bring or form, for instance that the paradox only arises from selfish or bizarre preferences, that minimal liberalism is just a veto, or that the Pareto principle is obviously sacrosanct, and exactly where in the sequence to head each off; one question I can put to the board at the end that would show they can use the paradox rather than merely recite it; and what I should explicitly leave out at this level, such as the full social-choice formalism, the distinction between weak and strong Pareto, and the precise conditions of Sen's theorem, and why.
