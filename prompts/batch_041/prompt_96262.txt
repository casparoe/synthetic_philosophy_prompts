I run the registration desk at a small museum — we handle maybe six of these a year, always under deadline pressure becuase a loan or an insurance valuation is hanging on it — and I keep fighting with our conservators about whether something that's been heavily restored is still the same object. A chair comes back with three new legs and a new seat, a painting gets relined, a bronze gets its patina stripped and redone. Our current rule is basically that if the accession number still applies, its the same object, which is circular and everyone knows it.

Write me a procedure I can actually run, grounded in the endurantism vs. perdurantism debate — whether a thing is wholly present at each moment or is a four-dimensional whole with temporal parts. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with "different object, re-accession," and where it has to hand the call to a person because it's a value judgment rather than a fact — flag which steps are judgement dressed up as procedure. Our director signs off on any re-accession and hates them, so I need to know what I'm asking her to decide. Then run it on two cases: a 17th-century oak chair with three replaced legs and a replaced seat, and a painting relined and retouched over about 30% of its surface. One of the runs should give me an answer I don't like, and tell me what the procedure can't settle — especially where the endurantist and perdurantist answers come apart.
