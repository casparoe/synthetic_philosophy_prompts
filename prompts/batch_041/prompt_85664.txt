I am trying to find out whether there is an established name for a pattern I keep running into, and, if there isn't, what the pattern really is. Here is a case. I asked a colleague whether a proposal of mine stood any chance; she said the chair had been very keen on the earlier drafts. I understood at once that the proposal was dead. If I had said to her, "you told me it was dead," she would have said, "I never said that, I said he liked the earlier drafts" — and she would be telling the truth, and not the kind of truth that conceals a lie, because she genuinely does not know what the chair thinks. Yet she would never have chosen that sentence if she had not wanted me to leave the room knowing the proposal was dead. It happened the other way round too: in a review I once wrote that someone had "made real progress on the documentation," which was true and was the strongest true thing I could say, and I expected him to hear the rest as weak; had he asked me whether I thought the rest was weak, I would have said, honestly, that I had said nothing about the rest. So three things seem to hold at once — the wording was chosen because of the conclusion people would draw from it, the conclusion was drawn, and neither speaker nor hearer would call the conclusion something the speaker said or meant. Is there a name for content that travels this way, and if there is not, what should it be called? I am told this is simply implication, or hinting, and that does not seem right: with an ordinary hint the speaker is answerable for what she was driving at and knows she is, whereas here the point is that she is not, and her disclaimer is sincere rather than tactical. What I want is the carving rather than the label — what exactly the phenomenon is, what it is not, where its edges lie relative to the nearest things that do have names, and what to call it if nothing fits. Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
