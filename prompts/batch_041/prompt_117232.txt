I have recently moved out of research and into an operational role, and the move has turned the Principal Principle from apparatus into a live problem for me. I now sit on a team that issues probabilistic hazard forecasts — monthly numbers that downstream agencies are, by policy, required to treat as the probability of the event and to fold into their own planning credences. The standard statement of the principle (your credence in A, conditional on the objective chance of A being p, should be p) is meant to capture precisely the relation I now find myself on the far side of. Two features of the work make me doubt that it can be taken at face value, and I want an essay that adjudicates the question and commits to an answer.

The first is reflexivity. Our number is not a chance that holds independently of what people believe about it: when we publish a high probability, agencies act in advance, and their acting changes the frequency of the event, so the quantity that is supposed to govern credence is itself partly a function of the credence it governs. The second is self-undermining. Our forecasts come from a model we already know will be replaced, and we can see now that in some futures the successor model will assign a different probability to this very event — and the successor's number is the one that will have been right. So the chance we are telling people to defer to is one the world may retrospectively contradict, and in those futures the agents who deferred to us will have deferred to something that was not the chance. If it helps to have the general shape of the second worry: if facts about chance are settled by the total history of the world, the total history can make a present chance-claim false, and the principle then appears to demand two different credences in the same proposition, one before the undermining event and one after.

Write the essay for a reader who knows the principle but not the literature surrounding it. Give the strongest case for reading it as a conceptual constraint on what 'chance' can mean rather than as a norm of rationality; give the strongest case against; then reach a verdict and defend it. Say specifically what the reflexive and self-undermining cases show. Do they call for a patch — restricting deference to information that does not itself reveal the chance, or requiring deference not to a single chance but to the complete theory of chance, the whole distribution over outcomes, so that later information no longer shifts what you are deferring to? Do they show that the principle is at best a defeasible heuristic, to be overridden whenever the forecast feeds back into the outcome? Or do they show that 'the objective chance' figures in the principle as a role — whatever a better-informed agent would defer to — in which case the principle constrains much less than it appears to? I do not want a survey of positions; I want to know which of these you think is right, and why the others fail.
