I am a tort litigator — a decade of personal-injury and product-liability practice, with the working fluency that implies in but-for and proximate causation, injury in fact, standing, the counterfactual baseline against which damages are measured, wrongful life and wrongful birth actions, loss of chance, the generalized-grievance bar, public nuisance, and the statutory causes of action that let plaintiffs recover for harms no one can point to in himself. I have read enough philosophy to have encountered Parfit's nonidentity problem, and I have never seen what is supposed to be puzzling about it. The cases in which the defendant's act is a condition of the plaintiff's very existence, so that the plaintiff cannot say he would have been better off had the act not been done, look to me like ordinary failures of the elements.

Restate the nonidentity problem in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can — not as a report of what philosophers have said, but as the problem appears when it is posed in terms of who has a claim, against whom, measured against which baseline, and remediable by what. Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophical debate survive the translation intact, which survive only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? Two questions interest me especially. First: is the person-affecting restriction — the thought that a wrong must show up as someone's being worse off — a conceptual truth about harm, or merely an artefact of the standing requirement, such that a legislature could legislate the nonidentity problem out of existence by creating a statutory right? Second: does my framework contain anything the philosophers' argument cannot accommodate — some way in which a court, obliged to reach a result in a concrete case, does something the theory has no room for?

Close with a verdict: is the residue a defect of my framework, or evidence that the philosophical claim was less determinate than it appeared? Do not end on a non-committal note, and do not give me a survey of the literature.
