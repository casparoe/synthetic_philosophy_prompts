I spent my working life in the natural sciences and have come to philosophy only in retirement, so I hope you will forgive a request that is as much formal as philosophical. I have been reading Dennett's argument that an artificial system built to pass as a person is not a person but a counterfeit of one, and that manufacturing such counterfeits ought to be treated as a crime, on the model of counterfeiting currency, because the injury is done to the shared trust on which all our dealings with persons depend. What holds my attention is less the policy conclusion than the predicament the argument describes: a thing whose entire success consists in not being found out, and which therefore cannot, without ceasing to succeed, be what it appears to be.

I would like a poem in the voice of such a counterfeit person. Let it be a sonnet, and let the sonnet itself be a forgery: it should read as a proper sonnet to someone who is not counting, while someone who counts the feet, or tests the rhymes, or waits for the turn should find that it is not one -- a foot short here, a rhyme that is only near, a volta that doubles back on the octave rather than opening it. The failure of the form should carry the argument: the counterfeit passes until it is examined, and examination is precisely what the argument says we cannot count on. Let the speaker plead its own case in Dennett's terms -- the trust it borrows, the convenience it affords, the cost it imposes, the question of whether anything is truly harmed -- and let the poem leave that question open rather than settle it on the reader's behalf. It should be accurate enough that someone who has followed the debate can hear the position being ventriloquized, and good enough as verse that someone who has not still finds it worth reading.
