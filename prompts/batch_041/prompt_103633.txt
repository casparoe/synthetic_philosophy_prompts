I am assembling a case for a project on escalation of commitment, and I would like you to construct it to my specifications. The target is a situation with the same structure as the dollar auction — two parties alternately commit more, and each ends up worse off than if neither had begun — but one in which the familiar diagnosis is unavailable, because neither party ever treats what has already been spent as a reason to continue. Please observe the following constraints. The setting must be ordinary and familiar to a high school student, such as a school club, a family undertaking, or a part-time job. The whole scenario must fit in one paragraph. There must be no auctioneer, no literal dollar, and no sum of money being bid upon, so that what the parties commit must be something such as time, effort, or standing. The prize must be indivisible, in that only one party can end up with it. The scenario should open after both parties have already committed something, so that the question of whether to begin never arises. And both parties should be aware of the dollar-auction pattern and still proceed. Every step of the escalation must be defensible as the step that maximises expected value given what the other party is expected to do next.

After the scenario, please do the following. State what a sunk-cost account of escalation predicts about the case, and show that it does not fit, since no one is reasoning from what is already spent. Then walk through the escalation step by step and show why a strictly forward-looking decision-maker takes each step, including the step that continues after the parties have recognised the pattern. Identify the feature of the setup that is actually doing the work — my guess is that it concerns the difference between the certain loss incurred by stopping and the gamble accepted by continuing — and say exactly what happens to the case if that feature is removed. Finally, tell me how someone who maintains that the parties are simply being irrational would attack the case, and whether that attack succeeds on its own terms. I am less interested in a verdict about the parties than in a demonstration that the case meets each specification, so please be explicit wherever the case would fail one of them.
