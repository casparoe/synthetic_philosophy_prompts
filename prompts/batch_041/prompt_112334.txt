I'm a third-year in political theory, supposed to be drafting the second chapter of my dissertation on immigration ethics, and I keep getting stuck on the same move that everyone I actually talk to makes — not the move the literature prepares you for. My roommate, who works in immigration policy and reads a lot but isn't a philosopher, put it to me over dinner and I didn't have a clean answer: a country is basically a big house. You own your house, you decide who comes in. Nobody thinks you've done something wrong when you lock the door at night or tell a stranger he can't crash in your living room, and nobody thinks you need a treatise to justify it. So why is it a moral scandal when a state does the same thing at the border? He thinks this settles the open-borders question more or less by itself, and I've run into versions of it in a couple of substacks and an op-ed — one of them said turning away an immigrant is no more in need of justification than a homeowner turning away a door-to-door salesman — so it isn't just him being glib.

What I want from you is a real audit rather than a gotcha. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the house, the owner, the deed, the lock, the threshold, the guest, the household rules — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. Two things I keep circling. First, whether the analogy quietly assumes the very thing it's meant to establish, namely that there's a determinate owner at all; a colleague in my department keeps saying a state has jurisdiction over its territory rather than title to it, which sounds like the same worry from another angle, though I can't tell if that's a substantive point or a lawyers quibble. Second, the fact that nobody alive actually bought or built the place: most of us were born inside and inherited our standing as co-owners, which either rescues the analogy (inheritance is a perfectly good form of ownership, families pass houses down all the time) or wrecks it (inherited standing to exclude is exactly the sort of thing we stopped treating as self-justifying a century or so ago). And there's a related asymmetry that nags at me: a homeowner can shut the door on a stranger, but a state also taxes, conscripts, and imprisons the people already inside — if you can't exclude you don't own anything, or so the argument goes, but then the state starts to look less like a homeowner and more like a landlord with a private army.

So: separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the conclusion he's drawing (exclusion is morally unremarkable, therefore no justification is owed) from the ones that are just decorative, and tell me whether the conclusion survives once the load-bearing ones are on the table. If the house is the wrong model, is there a better one — a private club with a membership committee, a landlord, a trustee holding land for beneficiaries, a lifeboat — and what does the better analogy end up licensing, if anything? I'd like to use this in the chapter's second section, so an explicit mapping is more useful to me than a general tour of the immigration debate.
