I have been keeping a list, in the back of a notebook, of arguments that all have the same shape: someone claims that a belief of ours cannot be denied, because the denial would presuppose the very thing it denies, or would have to use the thing it is trying to do without. I am told this family of arguments is called transcendental, though I am not sure the label earns its keep, and in any case I care more about my own verdicts on the individual instances than about the lineage. These ones convince me. That my words mean something cannot be denied, since the denial would itself be a meaningful string of words: the position that nothing means anything is not so much refuted as unsayable. That my memory is at least sometimes reliable cannot be doubted, since any doubt I raise about a particular memory has to be conducted with memories — of the occasion, of the evidence, of the rule I am applying to it. That I am now thinking cannot be denied, since the denial is an instance of the thing denied. And that some statements are self-contradictory cannot be rejected, though here my confidence feels different in kind, more like a rule of the game I am playing than a discovery about the world, and I cannot tell whether that difference matters. These ones do not convince me, even though they look like the same shape. That there is a world of material objects behind my experiences cannot be established by pointing out that doubting it requires me to treat my experiences as appearances, and so to presuppose something of which they are appearances: at best that shows I cannot help believing it, not that it is so, and I can hold that a belief is unavoidable and still ask whether it is true. That other people have minds cannot be established by observing that doubting is a practice I could only have learned from others, which seems to assume what it is meant to prove. That nature is uniform cannot be established by observing that I could not have come by the expectation of uniformity except through experience, which tells me about the history of the belief rather than about its truth.

There is one entry I keep going back and forth on, and it is the reason I am asking rather than simply sorting the list. It runs: that time is real cannot be denied, since any act of denying it takes time, and orders its own parts as earlier and later. One of my instincts is to put it with the convincing ones, since the denial seems to use the very thing it denies; the other is to say that this is exactly the sort of argument I have just been rejecting, and I cannot get the two instincts to sit down together. I have been arguing about it with a friend who thinks the whole family is worthless and another who thinks it is the only kind of argument worth having, and neither of them can tell me why the memory entry and the external-world entry come out differently for me when they look so alike.

So what I want is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures the pattern above — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not listed, with the particular verdict that motivates each clause, and an honest account of which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I have a tentative principle of my own to test: an argument of this shape works only when the thing denied is a precondition of the denial being intelligible as a denial — when the denial saws off the branch it is sitting on — and not when the thing denied is merely a precondition of experience, or of my holding any beliefs at all, or of my having come by my beliefs as I did. If that will not do, I should like to know whether it can be repaired or must be replaced. Please also say what the surviving principle predicts about the time argument, and about two cases I have not yet put to myself: that I am the same person who began this inquiry, and that there is a fact of the matter about what I mean by a word, independent of what I would say if questioned about it. I am not asking for a reading list, or for who said what; I want the principle, its verdicts, and the cost of holding it.
