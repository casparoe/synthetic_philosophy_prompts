I am submitting the following draft for a graduate seminar in philosophy of economics. I would be grateful for detailed comments.

Title: Revealed Preference and the Limits of Laundering

Contemporary welfare economics often treats revealed preference as a way of avoiding controversial claims about utility. If an agent's choices can be represented as maximizing a preference relation, we may treat the chosen option as better for the agent, and we need not say whether utility is a feeling, a brain state, or a formal index. I want to argue that this maneuver succeeds only if we distinguish two questions. The first is definitional: what makes a preference relation the agent's own? The second is normative: why should satisfying an agent's preferences make the agent better off? Revealed preference can answer the first by fiat, but it cannot answer the second without smuggling in a substantive theory of welfare.

My central example is the commuter. The commuter reliably chooses the train over the bus when both are available, but only because the train allows her to continue working; when she is on holiday, she reliably chooses the bus, because it is slower and more scenic. Suppose both choice patterns satisfy the usual consistency conditions within each context. If we model her as having a single preference ordering over transport modes, we must either treat the context as an argument in the alternatives or treat her as having inconsistent preferences across contexts. Neither is obviously wrong, but the choice has normative consequences. If we treat context as part of the alternative, then her preference is not for the train over the bus but for train-plus-work over bus-plus-work. I call this preference laundering: redescription of context-dependent choice as choice over a richer commodity space, thereby rescuing consistency at the cost of explanatory and normative clarity. Laundering is more accurate in one sense, but it makes welfare comparisons depend on how we individuate alternatives. If we instead treat her as having different preferences in different contexts, we need a theory of which context is authoritative for welfare.

I then introduce a distinction between thin and thick revealed preference. A thin revealed preference is a choice disposition inferred from a single class of contexts, with no commitment about its stability or its relation to the agent's reflective attitudes. A thick revealed preference is a choice disposition that the agent endorses, or would endorse under ideal conditions, as expressive of what she cares about. The standard revealed-preference approach is often thin, but welfare economics frequently needs thick preferences, especially when it recommends paternalistic or corrective policies. Thickness cannot be read off choice alone. It requires either an independent account of endorsement or a normative stipulation.

The problem is sharpened by the fact that ordinal utility is unique only up to monotone transformation. If welfare is preference satisfaction, then any monotone transformation of a utility function represents the same preferences and hence, on the thin view, the same welfare ordering. But many welfare comparisons, such as the claim that a policy benefits one person more than another, require cardinal or at least interpersonally comparable information. Revealed preference by itself does not supply this. This is often treated as a technical inconvenience rather than a conceptual limit. I want to argue that it is a conceptual limit: the information revealed preference provides is ordinal and intrapersonal, while welfare economics often needs more.

I consider three responses. The first says that thickness is unnecessary: welfare is just preference satisfaction, and preference is just choice. But this makes welfare economics revisionary in an unattractive way. If the commuter chooses the train because she is harried and cannot think, her choice may reveal a thin preference without revealing what she cares about. A theory that counts this as a welfare improvement seems to miss something. The second response says that we should idealize: use the preferences the agent would have if fully informed and deliberating. But idealization introduces a metric of its own. We need to know which departures from actual choice count as distortions and which count as expressions of value. The third response says that we should abandon preference-based welfare altogether and use a list of objective goods. That may be right, but it is a larger break than many behavioral welfare economists want.

My tentative conclusion is that revealed preference is best understood as a constraint on welfare claims, not a foundation for them. It tells us which claims are behaviorally implementable or testable, but it does not tell us which claims are normatively authoritative. The commuter case shows that the same behavioral pattern can support different welfare judgments depending on how we individuate alternatives and contexts. So the real work is done by an account of what makes a preference the agent's own, and that account is not supplied by revealed preference itself.

I would be grateful for comments on three things. First, accuracy: does the essay misrepresent the standard revealed-preference approach, or does it conflate ordinal and cardinal issues? Second, interest: is the thin/thick distinction doing genuine work, or is it just a restatement of the old distinction between actual and ideal preferences? Third, specificity: is the commuter example coherent, and does the argument about individuating alternatives succeed? Please be sharply critical rather than merely balanced. If you think the conclusion is wrong or overstated, say which argument or consideration would change your verdict, and identify where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
