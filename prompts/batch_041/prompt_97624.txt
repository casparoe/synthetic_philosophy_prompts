Please take the role of a philosopher specializing in the epistemology of disagreement, and think carefully before answering; it may help to first brainstorm the candidate decision rules that fit the evidence I give you.

I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. A staff engineer on my team, whom I'll call Priya, has become our informal referee for disputes between two engineers who share exactly the same evidence — same logs, same metrics, same code — but reach different conclusions. Her rulings so far, all concerning whether the engineer who disagrees should move toward the other's view:

1. They disagree about an empirical prediction neither can test any time soon; she says each should substantially lower their confidence toward the midpoint.
2. They disagree about whether an optimization converges, and one of them has reworked the algebra and can exhibit a specific error in the other's derivation; she says the one who found the error should not budge at all.
3. They disagree about whether a pipeline satisfies an ambiguous business requirement; she says they should split the difference.
4. They disagree about a factual claim that one of them has already checked with a quick decisive experiment that came out in their favor; she says that one should stay put.

Next week's dispute: same evidence on both sides, about an empirical claim that more data cannot settle. But the engineer we're asking about has independently re-derived the result by hand — without finding any error in the colleague's work — and this same engineer has been on the winning side of the last eight disagreements between the two, a fact both of them accept.

Predict Priya's ruling and give your reasoning. I'm most interested in (a) the candidate rules that fit her four rulings equally well, (b) what each predicts here and which you'd bet on, and (c) the one further case that would best separate those rules.
