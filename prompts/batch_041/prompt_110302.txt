I've come to hold the view that the wealth and poverty of nations is caused, more than anything else, by institutions — the rules and organizations that shape the incentives to invest, innovate, and trade — and that the historical record supports this through natural experiments, above all the long-run effects of colonial settlement patterns. Try to defeat the view: what observations, arguments, or results should count against it? Sort the defeaters by how much they should move me, from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones an institutionalist should concede rather than deflect. I want to know what the view predicts that a geography or culture story does not, and whether the natural-experiment evidence identifies the effect of institutions on prosperity or only the effect of something institutions are correlated with — an unhealthy environment that deterred settlers, say, and still suppresses income today. Give me an honest verdict on whether "institutions" has been stretched far enough to make the thesis unfalsifiable, and if so whether that is a vice or just the price of a broad claim in the social sciences. Finally, keep apart two claims I suspect are being run together: institutions as an explanation of the differences we observe between countries, and institutions as a guide to what a poor country should do now. Where they come apart, say which one the evidence actually supports.
