I want you to try to break an argument I've half-convinced myself of, about which diet kills fewer animals. The argument runs like this: if the harm that counts is the number of animals killed, then the standard plant-based recommendation may be backwards. A pasture-raised cow is one death spread over hundreds of meals, whereas a chicken is one death per handful of meals, so measured per calorie, chicken looks worse than beef. And because harvesting grain and vegetables kills field animals, mice and birds and insects among them, in large numbers, a diet of crops carries a body count that is merely less visible than the slaughterhouse's. Add the further thought that most sentient beings are wild and that wild lives may be net bad, and it stops being obvious that expanding cropland is good for animals. My tentative ranking, then: pasture beef least harmful, plant foods in between, chicken worst.

What would count against this? Rank the objections by how much they should move someone who already grants the empirical premises, say which premise each one attacks, and mark the ones that are merely awkward rather than decisive. Tell me whether the wild-animal premise is load-bearing or decorative, and what a defender of the position ought to concede. Name any consequence the position is committed to that its holders would rather not accept. Finally, is there a formulation of it that predicts nothing and cannot be refuted, and if so, is that a fatal defect or an honest response to empirical uncertainty?
