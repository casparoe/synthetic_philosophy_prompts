So I'm TAing for an upper-level seminar this term — mostly phil of mind, plus a couple of ethics people — and I've got a stack of short-answer questions to grade on moral status and computation, and honestly it's eating my weekend. Was hoping you could take one of them and give me a grade plus the comments you'd actually scribble in the margin. We grade on a pretty loose scale (0-5, or a letter if thats easier), and the whole point of the question was to see whether they can hold two objections apart and say how much each one actually does, so I care way more about the reasoning than about whether they hit the right jargon.

Here's the setup I gave them. We'd introduced a criterion in lecture that I called the reflexive-valence criterion: a system is a moral patient just in case it computes a representation of its own states as good or bad for it, in a form that's available to guide its later processing. Note it says nothing about what the system is made of or how its parts are wired — it's meant as a claim about the computation, not the architecture. Then I put up two objections. The unfolding objection: any function that a recurrent system computes over time can also be computed by a purely feedforward system that "unfolds" the recurrence into layers, so the two have identical input-output behaviour but different internal causal structure, which is supposed to make life hard for any criterion that ties moral status to a particular kind of internal organization. The attribution objection: in practice our judgments about which systems are sentient are driven by observable cues — behavioural similarity to us, anatomy, projection — rather than by any inspection of the computation, so what we call the boundary of sentience might just be the boundary of our attribution heuristics. The question asked them to explain both objections and then say how much each should move someone who accepts the criterion.

The student's answer is below. What I want from you: a grade, plus the specific marginal comments that justify it — point by point, what's right, what's wrong, and what's sloppy-but-forgivable, so that I can defend the grade to the student if they push back. Please don't just hand me a vibe, and don't let a confident tone do the arguing for them. Also flag anything that would cost them more than a point or two.

Student's answer:

"The reflexive-valence criterion says a system matters morally when it computes a representation of its own states as good or bad for it and can use that representation downstream. It's a nice criterion because it captures the thought that a creature has interests only if things can go well or badly for it, and it makes moral status turn on computation rather than on biology, which is what you want if you're going to think about artificial systems at all.

The unfolding objection goes after the architecture side of this. Take a recurrent network R that computes some function across time. You can build a feedforward network F that computes the same input-output mapping by unrolling R's recurrence into a stack of layers, one per time step. F and R behave identically on every input, but F has no recurrent connections. That leaves the defender of an architecture-based criterion with a dilemma: either F is also a moral patient, in which case the recurrence was doing no work and we should have stated the criterion purely in terms of input-output function, or F is not a moral patient, in which case two behaviourally identical systems get different moral status and no possible test could ever tell them apart. I think this is fatal to the reflexive-valence criterion, because once you see that any computation can be re-implemented in a way that strips out whatever internal structure you cared about, you can't tie moral status to computation at all.

The attribution objection is really the same worry wearing different clothes. It points out that we never inspect anyone's computation; we look at cues — does it flinch, does it have a brain like ours, does it seem to mind. And if our sense of where the line falls is a fact about us rather than about the systems, then the boundary of sentience isn't something we're discovering, and the criterion can't be a claim about what a system is really like. At best it's a claim about when we're licensed to treat a system as a patient.

So overall I don't think the criterion survives. The unfolding argument shows that the underlying computation can always be redescribed away, and the attribution objection shows that even if it couldn't, our attributions wouldn't track it anyway. Both objections land in the same place: the criterion ends up describing us rather than the world."
