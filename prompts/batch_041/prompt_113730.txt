A while back a friend of mine in the cog-sci dept got me hooked on Bongard problems -- you know, those 6x6 grids where the left six figures share some rule and the right six don't, and you have to find it. I ended up spending a whole weekend on the archive and now I'm trying to convince my seminar that they're a better window onto what concepts are than half the standard thought experiments, so I said I'd put together a handout ranking the main philosophical accounts of what's actually going on when someone solves one.

Here are the accounts I've got so far, in my own paraphrased words so nothing hangs on who said what. (1) A Bongard problem tests perception, not concepts: the solution is a gestalt switch, and whatever proposition you eventually verbalize is a post-hoc description of a perceptual reorganization rather than the thing doing the work. (2) They're evidence for a holistic picture of concepts: since adding or removing a single figure can flip the rule entirely, what counts as 'the' rule is always relative to the whole problem context, so concepts have no context-independent core. (3) Solving them is analogical mapping all the way down -- you're never testing a hypothesis directly, you're noticing that this triangle-arrangement is 'the same as' that triangle-arrangement in a different guise. (4) Bongard problems are really about a language of thought: the hard part is having the right primitives and composition rules, and the difficulty of any given grid is just a measure of how far its rule sits from your stock of predicates. (5) A deflationary one I want to include for contrast: they only look deep because we've restricted the evidence to six examples, and any classifier with a decent feature space will crack most of them, so they tell us about search, not concepts.

What I need from you: put these five into tiers by how illuminating they are as accounts of what Bongard problems reveal about concepts -- most illuminating at the top -- with a sentence or two on why each lands where it does, and flag any two that you think can't really be told apart on the evidence. The reason the ranking matters for me practically is I'm deciding which one to lead the handout with, so if one is deep but basically unfalsifiable, say so.
