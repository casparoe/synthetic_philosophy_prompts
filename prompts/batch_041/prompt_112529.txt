Here's the half-formed idea. Everyone fights about Pascal's wager on the probability side: either the probability of God is too small or too unmeasurable to do any work, or the many-gods point shows there's a crowd of rival hypotheses each carrying an infinite payoff, so the argument stops discriminating between anything. I want to come at it from the value side instead, because I think that's where the real problem is. The wager needs the payoffs — eternal bliss, eternal damnation, ordinary finite goods — to be comparable inside a single value space, and it needs infinite value to be a magnitude that swamps any finite difference no matter how small the probability attached to it. But that isn't a fact about God, it's a structural assumption about rational preference, and it's exactly the assumption that standard expected-utility machinery makes you pay for. The usual representation results want preferences to be continuous, or at least Archimedean: no outcome so good that it dominates arbitrarily long chains of small improvements. Infinite payoffs are precisely what breaks that. So my suspicion is that the wager isn't a straightforward application of expected utility theory; it's more like a counterexample to it, and the interesting question is what you have to give up on the axiom side to get the wager back, and whether giving it up costs you something you wanted elsewhere.

Stated as sharply as I can manage right now, the thing I actually want to test is this: is there a single decision rule, or a single set of axioms on rational preference, that (i) delivers the wager's verdict in the plain two-hypothesis case, (ii) doesn't deliver absurd verdicts in structurally similar cases — where the payoff is merely enormous but finite, or where the infinite payoff attaches to some hypothesis nobody had thought of — and (iii) is motivated independently of wanting the wager to come out true? My conjecture is no: that the wager's force comes entirely from there being exactly one infinite-valued hypothesis, and that every principled generalization either lets the many-gods crowd back in or collapses the rule into just maximize the best case, which nobody wants. But I could be wrong in an interesting way, and I'd rather the proposal be upfront about that than pretend I already know.
