Last Saturday a friend of mine — a set theorist who thinks that most metaphysics of the infinite is a failure of nerve about formalism — and I argued until two in the morning about Thomson's lamp. His line was that the lamp is Grandi's series wearing a lampshade, that divergent series are not mysterious, they are just not summable by the methods we happened to learn first, and that anyone who finds the case paradoxical has never met a summability method. I found myself defending something more specific than I had gone in with, and now I have to defend it in public: next month I give a work-in-progress talk to our philosophy of mathematics and physics reading group, and I would like a prep sheet for the question period.

Here is the position I will be defending. (1) A supertask determines an outcome exactly when the sequence of states it generates converges in a structure that the device itself supplies — the topology appropriate to the magnitudes the device is built to track. (2) Thomson's lamp supplies no such structure: a two-point state space with instantaneous toggles has no convergence, so "on or off at two minutes" is not a question with a hidden answer but a question with no answer, and nothing follows about the consistency of the world. The step that says the lamp must be either on or off simply assumes that every lamp history terminates in a state, which is precisely what this case denies. (3) The vase of balls is the same phenomenon in disguise: the reading that asks which balls remain converges, to none, while the reading that asks how many remain does not, and the felt paradox is the discontinuity of cardinality with respect to that convergence. (4) Where a physics does supply the structure — say a spacetime in which an observer's past contains an entire infinite future-directed timelike half-curve of infinite proper length, so that a completed infinite task is available at a single event — the criterion yields a verdict rather than a shrug, which I take to be a point in its favour.

The audience is five regulars. A dialetheist who works on contradiction at the limits of thought and holds that the lamp is a genuine contradiction derived from sound premises, so the honest response is to accept that it is both on and off, not to declare the question ill-formed. A constructivist mathematician for whom "the completed sequence" is a rule rather than a totality, who will say that talk of a state at the accumulation point is a category mistake and that my convergence criterion smuggles in exactly the classical notion of a limit he rejects. A philosopher of physics who works on relativistic spacetimes and thinks physical realizability is the entire question; he holds that where the physics supplies the structure the physics supplies the answer, so my criterion is either trivial or false, and he will want to know whether the constructions survive the energy conditions and whether anyone who can act on the result can know it. A deflationist who holds that the lamp is merely underdescribed, that the puzzle dissolves without any criterion, and that my topology is a solution in search of a problem. And a Humean-leaning philosopher of science who will say that on my view the state at two minutes is fixed by an infinite sequence none of whose members produces it, so the outcome is a brute fact with no causal history — which he regards as worse than a contradiction.

Write me the prep sheet. Give me the objections I will actually get from these five, ordered by how much damage they would do rather than by how famous they are, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who would raise it. For each: a reply I can deliver in two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and a line on what to concede up front and what to hold. Add the question I am hoping nobody asks. Tell me which of the five is most likely to leave unconvinced, and what my thesis would have to give up to satisfy him.

If the framing is confused, say so rather than assembling a prep sheet around it. In particular, if the convergence criterion is just the deflationary line restated with notation, or if the dialetheist is right that I am stipulating a contradiction out of existence, or if the task as I have posed it cannot be done well, tell me plainly; I would much rather hear it from you now than from the front of the room.
