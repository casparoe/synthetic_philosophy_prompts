I am a novelist who teaches a graduate seminar on historical fiction, and for the past two years I have been trying to reconstruct the life of one illiterate woman from the four documents that mention her in a provincial archive — a deposition, a marriage record, a bill of sale, and a will. My students slide constantly between two registers: the historian's claim to know how ordinary people thought and felt, and the novelist's claim to enter a head the archive never recorded. I want the final exam to make that slide visible.

Please write one essay question, for a two-hour closed-book exam, that cannot be answered by reciting the slogans of history from below or by summarising a few famous case studies. It should force students to confront three things. First, that the surviving sources for the poor and the powerless are overwhelmingly the sources produced by institutions that were interrogating, punishing, or taxing them, and what that asymmetry does to any inference about ordinary experience. Second, whether a single case can carry the explanatory weight that microhistorians place on it — the difference between a case that is typical, a case that is merely documented, and a case that is interesting precisely because it is deviant — and whether the program's defenders can have it both ways. Third, whether history from below is a method, a subject matter, or a political commitment, and whether those three can come apart, so that a study of elites might qualify under one reading and not the others.

Then give me a model answer of about 600 words, and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain and the errors that should cost marks. Students may cite figures and works, but marks should attach to the moves rather than the names. Include among the penalised errors the one my novelists keep committing: treating a vivid reconstruction of an interior life as though it were an inference from the evidence.
