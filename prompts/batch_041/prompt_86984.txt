I've been arguing with a freind about whether facts you already knew can confirm a theory (I think philosophers call this the old evidence problem) and I want to know what principle my gut verdicts actually add up to. Here they are, in roughly the order I thought of them.

- My car has clicked on left turns for six years. Never doubted it, wouldn't have taken a bet against it. A mechanic says it's a worn outer CV joint and that this is the usual cause of exactly that click. I'm now a lot more sure it's the CV joint. The clicking was zero news to me.
- Same click, same car, but this time I invent "it's the CV joint" myself, just as a fancier name for the noise. I know nothing about CV joints. No boost at all, obviously. Naming a symptom isn't a theory.
- I have years of stock trades in a journal. I notice they all fit "sell when the moon is waning." I feel nothing. The rule was built out of the entries, so of course it fitts them.
- Same entries, but a rule somebody else wrote before seeing them happens to fit them exactly. That does impress me, quite a bit.
- I've known forever that the office printer jams on Tuesdays. Someone says "the reason is that it's Tuesday." Nothing.
- A fact I've known forever but never once used for anything: my grandmother was born in the same small town as my grandfather. Then I read that a particular migration route in the thirties ran through that town, and the old fact suddenly feels like evidence for it.
- Two friends tell me the same thing, but one got it from the other. Not twice as confirmed. Two friends who've never met, same thing. Definitely twice as confirmed.
- If I put numbers on it my confidence that the car clicks is 1, certain. And I also think conditionalizing on something you're certain of can't move the probability of a theory. But the CV thing really did move it. I don't want to give up the numbers and I don't want to give up the boost.

My tentative stab at a principle: a fact confirms a theory just in case, had I not already known the fact, learning it would have raised my confidence in the theory. Test that against the list, repair it where it breaks, and tell me what it says about two cases I haven't thought about -- a theory that explains a known fact but predicts nothing else whatsoever, and a fact I only just now noticed was implied by a theory I already believed. If the only way to keep every verdict on my list is one clause per case, say which verdict I should just give up instead. And I'd rather have the principle in a sentence or two than a menu, if that's possible.
