I'm a glaciology PhD student who's been sitting in on a philosophy of science seminar this term, and I'm hooked in a way I did'nt expect. A friend of mine who did a masters in philosophy and now writes a blog gave me his take on the Humean view of laws: 'Humeans think laws are just descriptions of regularities, nothing more. Anti-Humeans think laws govern things, but saying a law governs is like saying gravity reaches out and grabs the planets -- it's the same mistake as thinking a cause pushes its effect. So the whole debate comes down to parsimony: the Humean won't posit extra necessary connections, the anti-Humean adds a further layer of law-things on top of the facts and pays for it in spookiness. The catch is that Humeans can't make sense of counterfactuals or of why induction works, so its a package deal -- clean ontology, no explanatory power.'

That last line feels too neat, but I can't tell where the seams are. Go claim by claim: what would a serious Humean accept, what's a caricature, what's plainly wrong, and is he right that the best versions of Humeanism aren't just the old regularity theory? Does his parting shot about clean ontology versus explanatory power actually follow from anything true in the summary? And tell me what would change your mind -- if you think a committed Humean would sign off on more of this than I expect, say so, and say what would make you reconsider that.
