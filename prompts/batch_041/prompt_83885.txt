I'm a journalist who writes about ethics and technology, and there's an arguement pattern I keep running into that I don't have a name for, and it's driving me alittle crazy. It goes like this. Someone insists there are things you simply may not do -- torture a prisoner, lie under oath, kill an innocent person -- full stop, no exceptions for good consequences. Fine. Then someone asks the obvious thing: but what if torturing this one prisoner stops the guards from torturing fifty others? What if killing this one innocent stops a massacre? And the person won't budge. They say no, that is exactly what you may not do, and it has nothing to do with the numbers. Then comes the objection I can't stop thinking about: so you'd rather let fifty people get tortured than get your own hands dirty once? You're treating the rule as more important than the very thing the rule exists to protect. The rule against torture is somehow forbidding you to prevent torture. It's like a fire code that won't let you burn down one building to stop a fire from spreading to the whole block.

That last turn is the part I want a word for. I've heard people call rules like this absolute, or inviolable, or side constraints, but that is naming the rule being attacked, not the attack. I've also noticed cousins of it in other places: you can't break a promise in order to keep someone else from breaking a bigger one; you can't lie to head off a lie that would do more damage; a lawyer can't commit a small crime to prevent a bigger one. Is that all one puzzle or several?

What I actually want, more than the label, is the shape of the thing. Is it a genuine paradox -- the view contradicting itself -- or just a counterintuitive result people don't like? Does it only bite when the rule is absolute, or does it show up for rules that admit exceptions too? How is it different from the nearest ideas that already have names -- the one where the right act is whatever produces the best overall outcome, and the one about limits on what you're allowed to do to people even when violating them would help everyone? And if there honestly isn't a single established term for exactly this, say so, then give me the best name you can and define it, with a couple of cases that clearly fall inside it and a couple that fall just outside.

Plain English where you can, please -- I'm not a philosopher -- but don't flatten the real distinctions. American spelling. And at the very end, give me one line I can paste into my notes that begins Verdict: and names the phenomenon in a phrase or two.
