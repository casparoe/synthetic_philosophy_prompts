I'm trying to get straight on something in the demandingness objection to consequentialism, and every time I read the standard stuff on it I come away feeling like two seperate complaints have been stapled together. One complaint is about the ledger: consequentialism says keep giving until the marginal cost to you matches the marginal benefit to someone else, so it eats your projects, your relationships, your whole life. The other complaint isn't about the size of the requirement at all, it's about its mode: consequentialism makes morality into something that can be *demanded* of you -- addressed to you, enforced, resented when you fall short. Call that the address reading. What I want is an explanation of the issue, made sharp and then actually used. Are these one objection or two? What is each one really a complaint about -- the content of the requirement, the agent's psychology, or the interpersonal standing of morality itself? And which of the familiar replies engages which reading: agent-centred prerogatives, satisficing, rule or indirect versions, separating the obligatory from the blameworthy, "ought implies can" plus some premise about what's motivationally available to a person? My suspicion is that the address reading is the load-bearing one, that prerogatives only answer the ledger complaint, and that they maybe make the address complaint worse, since a prerogative is still a permission handed to you by morality. Tell me if that's right and, if it isn't, exactly where it goes wrong. One more thing I want out of this: is there a principled account of how much morality can demand in the address sense -- does the notion of what can be demanded bottom out in reciprocity, or in some second-personal standing to hold each other to things -- and once you've got that account, does it help the consequentialist or sink her? I don't want the literature, I want the distinction drawn properly and then put to work.
