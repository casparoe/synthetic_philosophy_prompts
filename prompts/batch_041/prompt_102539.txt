My friend has this argument that information cascades are basically a reductio of deference, and I can't find the loose thread. It goes: (1) when I go along with a crowd I'm treating other people's choices as evidence about whats true. (2) Thats the same thing I'm doing when I defer to experts, or to a consensus. (3) But in a cascade each later person's choice is caused by the earlier choices, not by any evidence of their own. (4) So in a cascade the agreement of the many carries no more information than the choice of the first person or two. (5) The first person or two are a vanishing fraction of a big crowd. (6) So when a big crowd agrees, the size of the crowd is essentially worthless as evidence that they're right. (7) Almost everything any of us believes, we believe because a big crowd agrees -- the shape of the world, that germs cause disease, that the war ended when they say it did. (8) So almost everything we believe rests on worthless evidence. (9) So we don't know almost anything. She says (7) is obviously true and won't give it up, so the real lesson is that testimony and deference are far weaker than philosophers pretend. I think she's wrong somewhere but every move looks locally fine to me. Where does it actually break? I'd like to keep (7) if I can, and if I have to give something up I want to know what it costs me. She's also got a variant she likes better: the first two people in her story both read the same preliminary report before they chose, which she says is what real crowds are like, and she says it makes things worse. Tell me whether that variant actually changes anything or just restates the same problem in different clothes. And does the argument work the same way against deferring to one expert instead of a crowd, or does the whole thing secretly depend on there being a crowd?
