I am a first-year doctoral student, and this term I have been asked for the first time to grade the midterm for an undergraduate course in philosophy of mind. The unit I am marking covers the experimental philosophy of action, which happens to overlap with a side project of my own, and the experience has been humbling: several of the answers I felt most confident about turned out to contain errors that I noticed only on a second pass. I would therefore like you to act as a careful senior grader and as a philosopher of action who knows the empirical literature on judgments of intentional action well, and to help me assess one student's script. Please reason carefully before committing to a grade: begin by isolating the distinct claims the answer makes, evaluate each on its own merits, and only then settle on a holistic judgment. Do not reward fluency or confidence.

Here is the examination question:

In a well-known experiment, subjects read that the vice-president of a company has proposed a new program to the chairman. The vice-president explains that the program will increase profits but will also, as a side effect, harm the environment. The chairman replies that he does not care about the environment at all and wants only to maximize profit; the program goes ahead, and the environment is harmed. Asked whether the chairman harmed the environment intentionally, most subjects say yes. In a second version everything is the same except that the program will, as a side effect, help the environment; here most subjects say the chairman did not help the environment intentionally.

(a) Describe the pattern of judgments and explain why it is thought to be puzzling.
(b) Explain what this pattern is generally taken to show about the concept of intentional action, and assess how strong the evidence is.
(c) Consider a variant in which the side effect violates no moral norm but does violate a minor conventional or aesthetic norm (for instance, a chef who knows that his new dish will, as a side effect, break a rule of presentation that critics care about, though no one is harmed or wronged). Would you expect the same asymmetry? Defend your answer.
(d) Does the pattern show that the ordinary concept of intentional action is a moral concept? Justify your answer.

And here is the student's answer:

This is the Knobe effect. In the harm condition the great majority of subjects say the chairman harmed the environment intentionally; in the help condition the great majority say he did not help it intentionally, with only a minority judging that he did. What makes the pair puzzling is that the chairman's mental states are held fixed across the two conditions. In both versions he is equally indifferent to the side effect, he knows it will occur, and he proceeds for the same reason — profit. So the difference in judgment cannot be tracking any difference in what he believed or desired; it must be tracking the moral status of the outcome.

The standard lesson, then, is that the ordinary concept of intentional action is sensitive to moral considerations. The chairman wanted the environment to be harmed — he knew it would happen, and he chose to go ahead — and that is why ordinary speakers are so willing to describe the harm as intentional. In the help case the same willingness is absent. As for the strength of the evidence, it is very strong: the asymmetry has been replicated many times and shows up across a wide range of populations. Knobe's own view, it should be noted, is that the asymmetry is a pragmatic artifact rather than a genuine feature of the concept: on his account the word 'intentionally' carries a conversational implicature, and subjects are misled by the framing of the question rather than reporting how their concept works.

In the non-moral variant I would not expect the asymmetry, because the effect is specific to moral badness. If nothing is harmed and no one is wronged, there is nothing for the moral judgment to attach to, so subjects should judge the side effect unintentional in both directions. This is just what one would predict if the effect reflects a general bias toward blaming people for bad outcomes.

Yes, the pattern shows that the ordinary concept of intentional action is a moral concept, and this is now the consensus among experimental philosophers. It follows that philosophical analyses which define intentional action without reference to morality — say, in terms of belief and desire, or in terms of an agent's reasons — are refuted by the data. If the very same behaviour counts as intentional or not depending on whether we disapprove of it, then there is no fact of the matter about whether the chairman acted intentionally.

Where the student has presented something that is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic as though it were the settled consensus, say so plainly; and hold yourself to the same standard, distinguishing throughout between what is established and what is disputed rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus. Finally, tell me whether the answer as a whole deserves more or less credit than its strongest paragraph would suggest, and what the student would have needed to do to earn full marks.
