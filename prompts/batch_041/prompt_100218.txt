I teach a graduate seminar on analogy, categorization, and creative cognition, and the final unit is on the fluid-concepts tradition that Hofstadter and his students built around the letter-string microdomain — the setting in which a solver is handed one string transformation as a seed, the textbook case being abc → abd, and must then transform a second, target string in a way that counts as analogous. The pedagogical point of the unit is that the seed by itself does not fix a rule: the concepts a solver recruits — the letter, the rightmost position, the same letter, the successor of a letter, a run of identical letters — are themselves up for grabs, and the cases worth studying are those in which the target's structure pulls those concepts out of alignment with the seed's, so that the bridge between seed and target has to be rebuilt rather than merely instantiated. That slippage, rather than any single correct output string, is what I want the exam to test.

I am writing one essay question for the final, and I want it to be unanswerable by reciting the usual contrast between rigid rules and emergent computation, or by reproducing the familiar abc → abd / ijk illustration together with its canonical answer. To force the issue, I want the question to keep abc → abd as the seed but to set the target as aaa — a string of three identical letters — so that the student is pushed to notice that (i) the seed's transformation is multiply realizable and underdetermined by the seed alone, (ii) the target recruits a different carving of the situation, one organized around sameness and repetition rather than around three distinct letters in an ascending run, and (iii) a rule-based architecture would simply commit to one reading and report it, whereas a fluid architecture would be expected to hold several bridges in parallel and let their relative strengths shift as the reading develops. Please write the exam question itself (roughly 120 words, addressed to the student, and making explicit that no single output string is what is being graded), a model answer of about 700 words that actually performs the analysis — working through at least three defensible readings of the target, naming the concept each one reifies, and saying what slippage each requires — and a marking scheme worth 20 marks that separates an A from a B answer by naming the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific misreadings or shortcuts that should cost marks.
