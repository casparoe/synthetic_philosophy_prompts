I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or the theory of computation, and I'm assembling a set of writing exercises for myself. I'd like one piece of deadpan satire: an internal engineering design document of the sort a senior engineer circulates at a mid-sized company before a large rewrite, in which the author proposes replacing the company's prediction and recommendation stack with a universal predictor built on Solomonoff induction. Aim for roughly 800 words, structured as an RFC with the usual sections: Summary, Motivation, Design, Alternatives Considered, Risks, and Rollout Plan. The document should be entirely straight-faced. No winking, no asides to the reader, no sentence that admits it is a joke.

The humor has to come from the theory being reported accurately by someone who understands it just well enough to misapply it at engineering scale. For the jokes to land, these points need to be right:

- Kolmogorov complexity is defined only up to an additive constant that depends on the choice of reference universal machine, so a claim that one hypothesis is simpler than another is not machine-independent at any finite length. The document should treat that constant as a line item it can absorb.
- Solomonoff induction is not computable. The document should schedule it.
- The convergence and dominance results are asymptotic, concern total accumulated error over all future data, and say nothing about per-step accuracy or any finite-time bound. The document should promise quarterly targets anyway.
- The guarantees hold for environments that are computable, or at least approximable from below, and they come from the weighted mixture over hypotheses. The document should treat the computability of the world as an empirical finding, supported by the observation that its logs compress.
- In practice, model selection and compression are done with finite, hand-chosen hypothesis classes and off-the-shelf compressors. The document should explain away its benchmarks against a general-purpose compressor, and it should do so in the wrong direction, treating a loss as evidence that its reference machine, rather than its plan, is the problem.

I also want the piece to claim that this design derives Occam's razor from first principles rather than assuming it, and I want a reader who knows the material to see what that claim costs. The Risks section is where the genuine objections should live, translated into project language — an undecidability result filed as a schedule risk, that kind of thing.

Constraints: invent the company, the author, and anyone quoted; keep it in the register of an internal document, so no citations, no references to specific papers or researchers, and no named real people. A reader who has never heard of Solomonoff induction should still be able to tell from the document's own logic that something is being oversold.

Two things on top of the piece itself. First, open with a short note, three or four sentences, on where the parody takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading; I would rather see where it is tendentious than have it presented as settled. Second, if I have misdescribed any of the theory above, or if a straight-faced RFC is a poor vehicle for this, say so plainly rather than writing around it. I am an engineer, not a philosopher, and I would rather be corrected than humored.
