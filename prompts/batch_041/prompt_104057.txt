I'm writing the final for a third-year course I run called Chance and Ignorance — it's the principle of indifference, credences, priors, and the bit of measure theory they've all forgotten by week 6. I need one essay question, 20 marks, about 45 minutes of exam time, and I need it together with a model answer and a marking scheme, because I'm the only one teaching it and I don't want to be inventing the standard on the fly while I mark.

Here's my problem. They all turn up already knowing Bertrand's chord problem. You know the one: random chord of a circle, three natural ways to pick it — two random endpoints on the circumference, or a random radius with a random point on it, or a random point in the disk used as the midpoint — and you get 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 for the chance the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. They can recite it in their sleep. What none of them can do is say what it shows. So the question can't be "discuss Bertrand's paradox", because I'll get three numbers, the sentence "the principle of indifference is inconsistent", and nothing else.

So I want the question built around two cases instead, neither of them the chord thing, so they have to do the work rather than remember it.

Case 1 — I made this one up, so nobody's seen it. A machine makes ball bearings. All you know about the ones it produces is that each diameter lies somewhere in (0, 2 cm]. No distribution, no further information, that's it. What's the probability that a bearing's diameter is at most 1 cm? If you reason "no reason to prefer one diameter to another", you get 1/2. Do the same thing over volume — volumes run over (0, 4π/3 cm³] — and you get P(volume ≤ π/6) = 1/8. Do it over surface area and you get 1/4. Three flat priors, three answers.

Case 2 — pick a point uniformly on the surface of a sphere, then condition on the point lying on one particular great circle, say the meridian at longitude 0. What's the probability it's within 30 degrees of the north pole? Get there by conditioning on a very thin wedge around that meridian and you get about 0.067. Instead say "the point is uniform along the great circle", measuring position by central angle, and you get 1/6. Same conditioning event, two answers.

What I want the question to force them to do: (a) say what precisely goes wrong in each case, and whether it's the same thing going wrong twice or two different things; (b) say what someone who still wants to defend the principle of indifference should say — is it dead, or does it just need to be stated more carefully, and if so what does "more carefully" actually come to; (c) say whether there's any principled way to pick the right paramterisation, and when that would and wouldn't work. And I want Case 1 to be the one they have to push hardest on, since they can't pattern-match it to anything.

Deliverable: the exam question itself, written like a real exam question and not like a lecture; a model answer of roughly 600-800 words, pitched at a good third-year student who has actually thought about it; and a marking scheme that gives me the specific moves a top answer has to make, with marks attached, total 20, plus, for each of those moves, a line on how a weaker answer typically muffs it. Please seperate the moves that actually distinguish an A from a B from the ones that just show they were awake — I'm trying to write something that doesn't hand everyone a 14.

Two things to keep out of the model answer. Don't let it land on "the principle of indifference is inconsistent, so Bayesianism is in trouble" — that's exactly the answer I'm trying to stop them giving. And don't let it say the three chord methods are all wrong; each one is a perfectly well-defined experiment, which is half the point of the whole topic.
