Although I have no academic training in philosophy, I have been reading about Quine's argument for the indeterminacy of translation on my own, and I keep running into a particular summary of it that I do not know how to evaluate. A friend of mine who took several philosophy courses in college explained the argument to me as follows:

Quine asks us to imagine a linguist who has to translate a language from scratch, with no interpreter and no prior contact with the people, and who hears them say "gavagai" whenever a rabbit runs past. The obvious translation is "rabbit," but Quine says the evidence can never decide between that and "undetached rabbit parts," or "rabbit stage," or "rabbit-hood in general," since every observation that would confirm one of them would confirm the others. He therefore concludes that there is no fact of the matter about which translation is correct, and that a translation manual is a convenient fiction we project onto the natives rather than a discovery about them. Quine was a behaviorist, so for him the only evidence that could ever exist was the sounds people make in observable situations, and this is what makes the indeterminacy permanent: no further investigation could remove it. The upshot, he thought, is that we should abandon the idea that words have meanings at all; and because he had already rejected the distinction between analytic and synthetic truths, there was nothing left for a meaning to be. He extended the same point to our own language, holding that even our own words are indeterminate in what they refer to.

I would like to understand the real position rather than a slogan, and I have no way to check this account against the original. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful reader of Quine would accept, which are the kind of simplification that is harmless in a lecture but misleading once you press on it, and which are simply mistaken? Where the summary draws a conclusion -- that there is no fact of the matter, that meaning is not real, that we should stop speaking of meanings -- I would also like to know whether that conclusion actually follows from the parts of the summary that are accurate. I am inclined to think the point about evidence is hard to dispute, but the leap to "meaning is not real" strikes me as too quick, though I may be missing something. Finally, where the summary goes wrong, I would be grateful if you could say what a defender of Quine would put in its place, so that I come away with a correct version rather than merely a list of errors. I am not asking for reading recommendations; I would just like to know what the argument shows and where this telling of it goes off the rails.
