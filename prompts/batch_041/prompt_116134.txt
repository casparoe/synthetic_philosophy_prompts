ok, so I'm a first-year cognitive science MA and I've been having this running argument with my flatmate (who's a hard determinist and honestly exhausting about it), so before my seminar presentation next week I want to write my position down and see if it actually holds together. Here's where I've landed, roughly:

(1) I'm a compatibilist. Determinism is probably true and I don't lose sleep over it. The 'could have done otherwise' framing has always seemed to me a dead end — I don't need a gap in the causal order to be free.
(2) But I do think an action is only really mine, in the sense that matters, if it flows from my conscious intention. I want the conscious decision to be among the actual causes of the movement, not a bell that rings after the train has already left.
(3) I take Libet more or less at face value: the readiness potential starts building well before subjects say they decided to move, so the conscious intention isn't what gets the movement going. I know people complain that the timing reports are retrospective reconstructions, but I don't think that definately rescues the conscious-initiation picture.
(4) I think Libet's own escape hatch is basically right though: the veto. Even if I can't consciously start an action, I can consciously cancel one that's already brewing, and that's where my freedom really lives.
(5) None of this touches moral responsibility, in my view. Responsibility is about being reasons-responsive, being the kind of thing you can argue with, not about which neuron fired first.
(6) I've also read that the readiness potential is less dramatic than it sounds — something about it being an artifact of averaging, slow random drift that crosses a threshold, and that it turns up even in trials where people never move. I find that weirdly comforting.
(7) And in the actual Libet setup I'd say the subjects' movements are free in the only sense worth caring about — they endorsed what they did and nothing was forcing them.

Does this hang together? My instinct is that it mostly does and that people who say it doesn't are smuggling in libertarian assumptions, but I'd rather have someone actually go through it than just reassure me. Which of these pull against each other, which pairs only look like they're in tension and dissolve once you look, and how bad are the real ones? I want them ranked by severity, and for each genuine conflict I want to know the cheapest repair and what it costs me — what I'd be giving up. If it turns out one of these has to go, say which one and why, and if two of them are really the same commitment wearing different hats, point that out too. Please don't just tell me to become a hard determinist, that's not on the table.
