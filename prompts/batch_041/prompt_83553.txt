I'm a second-year doing joint honours in philosophy and history of art, and I've got an essay coming up on the statue and the clay — my tutor calls it material constitution, which as far as I can tell is just the posh name for whether the lump of bronze and the statue are one object or two, whether the statue survives being melted down or squashed, that sort of thing. What's bugging me is that my art history seminars keep handing me what look like live versions of the puzzle and nobody there seems to notice. Conservators work to rules like minimal intervention and reversibility; they'll argue for weeks over whether to strip the old varnish off a picture or leave the yellowed layer as part of its history; they'll replace a rotten stretcher, a lost finger, a rusted armature, and still say the work has come through intact. Then the very same people will tell you that a chemically identical cast pulled from the original mould, or a hand-painted facsimile, is definately not the work and never will be. And then there's the kind of piece that's basically a set of written instructions: at each venue a team repaints it on the wall from scratch, it gets painted over again when the show ends, and everyone including the insurers agrees it's the same work every time, with the instruction sheet as the thing that actually needs conserving. Meanwhile the auction house puts seven figures on the work and three on a perfect replica. I've been going round in circles about this with my flatmate who's doing fine art, so I'd rather have it argued out properly.

What I want is the metaphysics that's actually doing the work in all this. Does conservation practice presuppose that the artwork is something which can survive the total replacement of its parts, so the material is just the current vehicle — or does the whole ethic of minimal intervention and reversibility only make sense if the material is somehow constitutive of the work, so that every cleaning is a partial destruction? Are those two halves of the practice consistent with each other, or is the profession quietly running one metaphysics for paintings, another for instruction pieces and a third for the market? Tell me which bits of the practice strictly require a particular view of the work/material relation and which would be just as happy under rival views, and then what would actually have to change in conservation if you took the opposite line — say that the work just is its material and nothing survives replacement, or that the work is a repeatable type with no privileged token at all. No reading list please, just the argument — and please don't fob me off with the line that it's all just convention, I know that's on the table, I want to know what the convention commits them to.
