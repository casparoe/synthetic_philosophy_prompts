I'm working on a long piece about whether it makes any sense to say that one person is better off than another — the old line that comparing two people's welfare isn't really a scientific question because there's no fact of the matter — and I need a centerpiece case for it. Every case I build is too easy for a skeptic to wave off, so I'm outsourcing the job. Here's the spec.

Build me a thought experiment in which two seperate people's situations get ranked in opposite directions depending on how you compare them, set somewhere real — a clinic deciding which of two treatments to fund, an insurer, a court working out damages, a workplace. One paragraph for the scenario itself. No trolleys, no ticking bombs, no utility monsters, no sci-fi, nobody immortal, no brain-in-vats, and no paralysed-saint-versus-rich-depressive cliché. Also don't make either person simply wrong about their own life; if someone's mistaken about themselves, I want that to be the contested bit of the case, not part of the setup.

The two methods I want in conflict are (a) the preference route — compare by what each would choose, or by which of the two lives each would rather be living — and (b) the objective list route — compare by health, income, opportunities, whatever belongs on the list, regardless of what they say about it. I definately need a real conflict, not a rounding error: one method says the first person is worse off, the other says the second is, and that flips who gets the scarce thing. Neither option should dominate the other, so the Pareto crowd has nothing to say.

Here's the part I actually care about, because it's where my drafts keep falling over. I want the preference comparison to pass a test I've been calling the return-trip test: run it in both directions. Both people get all the facts, including each other's, and each is asked whether they'd rather live their own life or the other one, from the inside, for the rest of it. The comparison only counts if both answers, going both ways, land on the same life — if A picks A's life and B also picks A's life, fine; if they split, the comparison is junk and my skeptic wins by default. So: a case where the preference ranking passes the return-trip test and the objective-list ranking still disagrees with it. That's the hard part and it's the whole point of the case.

Then walk me through it. What does each method say, and why? Which single feature of the case is doing the work? What happens if you take that feature out — does it collapse into the boring case where both methods agree? And tell me straight how the loser fights back. If the objectivists lose, I assume they'll say the happy one has adaptive preferences or doesn't know what she's missing — is that a fair hit on my case, or a dodge? And what's the strongest version of the complaint that the preference route is just smuggling in a shared scale? Keep the whole thing under a page.

One warning: I'm using this to argue that the no-fact-of-the-matter line is too quick, so don't cheat. The verdicts have to fall out of the two methods, not out of you stipulating them or rigging it so one side looks like an idiot. If it turns out the case can't be built under these constraints, say so and tell me which constraint is the one that breaks.
