I teach a graduate seminar on risk and uncertainty in machine learning, cross-listed between philosophy and computer science, and we have just spent three weeks on the difference between expected-value reasoning and worst-case reasoning: maximin and minimax regret, the sense in which a guarantee that holds in expectation over a distribution tells you nothing about any particular deployment, and the fact that claims of robustness are usually quantifier claims in disguise. My students can now recite all of this, and I am fairly sure that none of them feels it. They treat average-case and worst-case as two columns in a table rather than as two different things to be afraid of, and the formal reading is not going to fix that. I want a satirical piece to assign alongside it, so that the distinction lands somewhere in the gut before it lands on the exam.

Please write, in the deadpan house style of a formal insurance contract, an endorsement to a Model Deployment Assurance Policy issued by an invented mutual insurer, together with the covering letter from its underwriter. Roughly 800 to 900 words: numbered clauses, a short definitions section, a schedule of exclusions, and an actuarial note or two. The object of the satire is the practice — common enough in the deployment of machine-learning systems, and defended in earnest — of certifying a system on average-case or expected-performance evaluations and then presenting the certificate as a safety guarantee; the humour should come from taking that practice entirely at its word and drafting the contract it implies. Keep it deadpan throughout: no winking, no narrator stepping outside the document, no jokes that depend on the reader recognising a paper or a person. An invented signatory, an invented regulator, and an invented insurer are all fine; nothing in the piece should rest on a real institution or a real text.

Three things should carry the technical weight, since I want this to be legible to a specialist as well as funny to my students. First, the central guarantee should be a quantifier mistake made binding: the policy promises that for every deployment environment there exists some configuration of the insured system that would have performed adequately, and the insurer reserves the right to specify that configuration after the environment has been observed — which turns an empty claim into one that reads, at a glance, like the strong claim, that a single configuration performs adequately in every environment. Second, the policy should incorporate the Stipulated Adversary rule, which I define here because I am inventing it: under the rule, any party seeking to establish that a system fails must draw its inputs from the evaluation distribution the developer supplied, and losses arising from inputs outside that distribution are the policyholder's responsibility, on the reasoning that the distribution defines the environment rather than the other way around. Third, the schedule of exclusions should exclude exactly what a reader would have assumed insurance was for — losses concentrated in the tail, losses in deployments large enough to matter, losses arising from inputs nobody anticipated — on the ground that such losses are not insurable, with the premium justified by an assumption, stated plainly in the actuarial note, that the deployment distribution equals the evaluation distribution, so that any divergence is an error of the policyholder's. If one footnote can be made literally correct and quietly damning at the same time — a bound that holds with probability one over a distribution the seller selects is compatible with failure in every single deployment — so much the better. The piece should be readable by someone who has never taken a statistics course, and a reader who has thought hard about alignment should be able to reconstruct, from the fine print alone, the argument that is being satirised.
