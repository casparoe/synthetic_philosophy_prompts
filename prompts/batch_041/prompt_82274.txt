I'm in a political theory seminar that spent the first half of the term on futarchy — Robin Hanson's proposal that a polity vote on the values it wants, settling on a welfare metric, and then let conditional prediction markets decide which policies actually advance that metric, with a proposal passing if the market prices its expected welfare higher than the status quo. Our discussions kept snagging on the same three worries: Tyler Cowen's objection that values and beliefs cannot be cleanly separated, since any metric we can actually name already encodes contested judgments about what matters; the manipulation problem, and whether someone with enough capital to move a market price also tends to be someone with enough power to move the outcome the market is predicting; and the suspicion that the genuine political act is the choice of metric, which futarchy hands back to the same electoral process it claims to have depoliticized. We are now reading science fiction as political theory, and I have been assigned Asimov's original Foundation trilogy. I have read it twice and cannot stop reading it as a futarchy story, though I suspect it is a hostile one, and I want a serious critical reading rather than an appreciative one.

Start from the obvious parallels and then tell me where they break. Terminus is steered by a single aggregate metric — the length of the coming dark age, which psychohistory has already fixed at a thousand years rather than thirty thousand — and the whole arrangement rests on the same epistemic wager as any decision market: that individual choices are noise while aggregate behavior is forecastable. But nobody in the books ever votes on the metric; Hari Seldon sets it, and afterward the Second Foundation quietly maintains it, so the values half of the proposal is simply missing and the system runs on technocratic authority rather than on any aggregation of preferences. The Plan is also public in a way that a market price is not: the Foundation knows a crisis is scheduled and expects a recording to explain, after the fact, how it was supposed to be resolved, which makes the prediction performative rather than merely informative. And the Second Foundation both forecasts and covertly intervenes — exactly the manipulator who trades in the market he also moves, which futarchists usually wave away by saying that such a trader can be arbitraged. Then there is the Mule, the black swan no conditional market could have priced, whose existence forces the Plan's custodians to abandon forecasting for direct intervention.

I would like a reading that is faithful in both directions: one that does not reduce the trilogy to an illustration of a mechanism, and one that does not bend futarchy into whatever makes the novels look prescient. Concretely, I want your verdict on whether Asimov is dramatizing a prediction-market polity or quietly demonstrating that such a polity needs an unaccountable enforcer standing behind it; whether the Mule is an argument about model risk or about something else entirely; and whether the later books, in which a rival plan is chosen by one man's intuition rather than by any aggregate judgment, amount to an explicit repudiation of the whole idea or are making a different point about who gets to name the metric. Where the trilogy resists the futarchic reading, say so plainly, and where I am overreading, tell me that too. Close by saying what a defender of futarchy ought to concede after reading Asimov, and what Asimov notices that the futarchy literature tends not to. No need to cite sources or recommend further reading — I want the argument, not a bibliography.
