I have been sitting in on the ethics committee at the hospital affiliated with my department for the past year while working on a dissertation in philosophy of medicine, and I have also been reading my way into the literature on the definition of death, and my honest impression is that this debate has stalled rather than that it is merely hard. The positions are stable and I can state them. On one side, death is treated as a biological phenomenon: a human organism dies when it irreversibly loses the capacity for integrated functioning as a whole, and the whole-brain criterion — irreversible loss of function of the brain including the brainstem — is simply the best available test for that event, so that the law recognizes a fact rather than creating one. On the other side, the very same bedside facts are read as evidence that the criterion is a stipulation rather than a discovery: bodies that meet it are ventilated, thermoregulate, heal wounds, fight infections, mount stress responses, and in reported cases have been sustained for years and have even carried a fetus to delivery, which looks like the behavior of a living organism rather than a corpse; on this reading 'brain death' is a status we confer for moral, legal, and practical reasons, prominent among them the need for a bright line that permits procuring organs while honoring the dead donor rule. What strikes me is that neither side is short of facts, and neither side has moved. In committee the practical version of the dispute recurs constantly: whether to tell a family that the patient is dead or that the patient is dying, and whether the answer to that should turn on a biological finding at all.

My working hypothesis — and I want it treated as a hypothesis, not a premise — is that three different questions are running under one word. There is a metaphysical question about what kind of entity a human organism is and what it takes for it to cease to exist; an epistemic question about what would justify a diagnosis; and a normative question about when it is permissible to stop treatment and to take organs. Because the dead donor rule ties the third to the first, the metaphysical question is made to carry the weight of all three, which is exactly the condition under which a conceptual dispute stops being tractable: no one can afford to concede a premise whose loss would license something they regard as murder, or as the abandonment of a living patient. If that diagnosis is right, the impasse is structural rather than accidental.

I would like you to diagnose this properly rather than survey it — I know the standard positions and I am not asking for a reading list or citations, so please work from the positions themselves. Is this a verbal dispute that would dissolve if we distinguished the death of the organism from the death of the person, or a substantive one in which the sides really do disagree about the nature of living things? Are they answering different questions under one name, disagreeing about which cases count as data (do the ventilated body's continued functions show that it is alive, or are they organ-level and mechanical, and is that a finding or a decision about what counts as integration?), or resting on starting intuitions about whether what matters is biological life or psychological capacity, which neither side can argue for? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work — and if the pivotal notion of 'integration' turns out to be functional or teleological rather than observational, does that make the dispute conceptual through and through? I am especially interested in whether the distinction some clinicians draw between permanent and irreversible loss of function does any real work here or merely moves the stipulation one step back, since permanence sounds like a fact about the world but reads like a fact about our intentions. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a revision of the dead donor rule, a separation of the determination of death from the permission to procure, a demonstration that the concept is a cluster whose criteria are selected by purpose and that there is therefore no fact of the matter to be had — or an argument that no progress should be expected here and why.
