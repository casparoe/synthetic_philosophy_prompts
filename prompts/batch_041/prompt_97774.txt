I'm in a final-year seminar on decision theory (12 of us, 90 minutes once a week) and the prof has made each of us run one session on a topic of her choosing — I've been handed risk-weighted expected utility, which I sort of get and sort of don't. The way she explained it: you take a gamble, line the outcomes up from worst to best, and then instead of weighting each outcome by its plain probability you weight the step up from one outcome to the next by a risk function applied to the chance of doing at least that well. So a coin-flip gamble paying nothing or £100, for someone whose risk function is r(p) = p squared, comes out as 0 + r(0.5)·100 = £25 instead of £50. If your risk function sits below the diagonal you're pessimistic, the good outcomes get discounted, but you still never violate stochastic dominance and your preferences stay transitive — it's not just coded as a bias. What I actually want from you is a game or in-class activity that makes the group *feel* that, the way you feel a coordination failure in a public-goods game rather than being told about it.

So: rules, materials (cheap — paper, tokens, a whiteboard, whatever), a payoff scheme that works without real money (we can play for points that buy raffle tickets for a decent bottle of wine, or candy, or nothing at all if you can make it work with pure points), and a rough timeline for a 90-minute session with 12 people. The idea needs to show up in what happens, not in what I say afterwards — so no explainer up front, they should walk into it cold and only hear the name in the debrief. Then give me the debrief: map specific moments in the activity onto the formal parts (the ranking of outcomes, the risk function, where the expected-utility calculation would have diverged, why the diagonal-below case is pessimism), tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and be straight with me about which feature of the idea the activity simply can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply in words.

One thing I care about: the part I find hardest to convey is that it's *rank-dependent* — the same outcome can get weighted differently depending on where it sits in the ordering of a gamble, which is exactly what plain expected utility with a concave utility function can't do. So please build in at least one pair of gambles that share a common consequence (or a common-ratio pair) so there's somewhere for that to show up, and at least one comparison where an expected-utility-with-concave-utility agent would be predicted to choose differently from what the group will actually do, so the debrief has something concrete to point at. And I'd rather you be honest that the activity can't settle whether the risk-weighted way of choosing is *rational* — it can make the pessimism feel natural and internally coherent, but it can't rule out that the same choices are just a concave utility function in disguise, or distinguish this model from prospect theory or disappointment aversion, since those fit the same choices. If the activity can only get them to the point where they stop thinking of it as a mistake, thats fine — just say so.
