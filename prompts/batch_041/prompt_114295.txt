I'm a novelist, and I've reached the point in a manuscript where I need a joke — a real one, not a placeholder — for a character to tell at a dinner party. She sits on a small municipal heritage committee, the kind of body that spends its evenings on whether a Victorian lamp-post may be relocated, and she is the sort of mild Burkean who cannot look at a proposal to demolish something without asking who built it and what they were trying to hold back; she is also the youngest person on the committee, and no one can decide whether that makes her a radical or a reactionary. I've been reading Burke on and off for a year, mostly for the texture of the prose — the image of a society as a partnership among the dead, the living, and those not yet born — and I keep circling back to the principle, usually called Chesterton's fence, that one should not pull down a fence until one knows why it was put up. A friend of mine insists the principle is nothing but a licence for inertia, and I don't think that's right; I want a joke that proves him wrong by being funny. So: write me a joke built on that principle.

The trap to avoid is that most jokes on this theme are political rather than philosophical — they amount to saying that reformers are reckless, which is unkind, untrue, and boring, since the principle is neutral: it is a claim about what we can know and about who bears the burden of proof, and it can be aimed at inherited arrangements as easily as at proposed changes. I'd like the joke to turn on that neutrality, and on the slightly vertiginous fact that the principle can be applied to itself — that a rule instructing us to preserve what we do not understand may end up preserving the very ignorance that protects it. If it helps, here is the setup I have been playing with: a village fence that no one can account for, whose only surviving record of purpose is the minute of an earlier inquiry, which concluded that the fence should stand until its purpose could be established. Use that, alter it, or discard it.

The punchline should be funny to someone who genuinely knows the idea — the burden of proof, the limits of what any single generation can see about why its institutions exist — and not merely a nerd joke that name-drops a famous image. Please don't put quotations in anyone's mouth, real or invented, and don't reach for anything Chesterton or Burke is supposed to have written; I want the humour to come out of the structure of the thing. Give me two versions with different punchlines, and after each, one sentence explaining what a person who knows the principle is actually laughing at.
