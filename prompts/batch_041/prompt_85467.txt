I've been trying to work out a view about everyday conventions and I want you to try and break it. Treat yourself as a moral philosopher who works on social norms, coordination, and what strangers owe each other — someone who has actually thought about this rather than someone who will list the standard positions and hand me the menu. Think it through carefully before answering.

Here's the view. Call it the local-convention default. When you're somewhere, the mundane conventions of that place — how much you tip, whether you queue or crowd, whether you cross against the light — generate real moral obligations for you, not just prudential reasons to avoid dirty looks. And they bind visitors as much as locals: "that's not how we do it back home" is no defence, even though the visitor never signed up for the local scheme and gets nothing out of it except not being resented. The ground of the obligation is that these conventions are, mostly, the terms on which the people around you are willing to share space, and ignoring them is a way of free-riding on their forbearance. I want to keep it narrow: I'm not saying every local custom binds (obviously not the ones that require you to be cruel or servile), and I'm not saying you have to adopt local attitudes or tastes, only that you play along with the behavioural conventions while you're there.

What I want from you: don't tell me it's "an interesting position" and lay out the options. Take a side. Give me the things that count against it, ranked from what should actually kill it down to what's merely awkward, and tell me which of them a defender of the view would themselves have to concede rather than brush off. Then give me your verdict — does it survive, does it need to be narrowed into something smaller, or is it just a dressed-up version of "be polite"?

Some things I want you to actually think about, because I suspect the view is doing different work in the three cases and that this might be where it brakes:

- Tipping vs queueing vs jaywalking. Queueing looks like a coordination convention — everyone's better off if we all do it. Tipping looks like a transfer: it moves money from customers to workers and the local wage bargain is built on it. Jaywalking is a rule almost everyone ignores when it's safe, so the "convention" seems to be that the law is mostly for show. If one principle is meant to cover all three, does it survive, or does it turn out to be three different principles wearing one coat? Is there a version that only covers coordination conventions and quietly drops the other two, and is that version still worth holding? I've been calling the two types coordination conventions and ransom conventions in my notes — tell me if that distinction does any work or if it's a distinction without a difference.
- The visitor's ignorance. A visitor often doesn't know the local convention, and sometimes there isn't a settled one — the locals disagree, or the norm is contested and shifting. Does the obligation come in degrees with how knowable and how settled the convention is? Does that rescue the view or make it trivial?
- Conventions that aren't exactly harmful but are pointless or rigged. If the norm persists mainly because it lets one group extract from another, does "these are the terms of cooperation" still apply, or does the view collapse into ordinary fairness reasoning and stop being a view about conventions at all?
- What the view predicts that its rivals don't. If a plain harm-based morality already tells me what to do in every one of these cases, my view is idle. Give me a case where the convention does real work on its own.

Also: is there a risk this thing is unfalsifiable? If every counterexample gets answered with "ah, but that convention is harmful, or unsettled, or optional," say so, and say whether that's fatal or just what a defeasible principle looks like.

What would move me most is a case where following the local convention is clearly wrong and the wrongness has nothing to do with the convention being harmful — if that's possible, I think I have to give something up. If it isn't possible, say that instead of inventing one.
