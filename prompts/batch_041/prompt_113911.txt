I'm in a graduate seminar on machine learning and ethics, and last week's session was on inverse reinforcement learning — the trick of inferring an agent's reward function from the behavior of a human demonstrator, on the assumption that the demonstrator is more or less acting optimally with respect to some reward over states or trajectories. One classmate made what I think is a too-quick move: she said that this finally gives us a way to learn what people really want without having to trust what they say about themselves, since what someone chooses reveals their preferences while their self-reports are unreliable. A second friend half-agreed with her but offered the case of someone who deliberately arranges not to find something out — asking a friend to order for them, avoiding a medical test, eating the cake before checking whether it is a fasting day — and I genuinely could not tell whether that was an instance of the problem or a distraction from it, so I would rather see what example you would reach for. I have to bring something short to discussion on Thursday and I would like it to be a concrete case rather than a general complaint about the limits of behavioral inference.

Please give me one example — as specific as a small scene, with a person, a situation, and an observable pattern of behavior — in which the IRL-style inference from behavior to preference goes through cleanly, in the sense that the recovered reward function fits every choice the person makes, and yet is the wrong description of the person: the reward it licenses predicts something about what this person would do under some counterfactual, and we can see that the prediction is false. What interests me is the case where the trouble is not that the data are sparse or noisy, so that more observations of the same kind would not help, but where something in the setup of the inference misdescribes what the person is doing. Once you have the example, say explicitly which assumption of the standard setup it exploits — for instance an assumption about the demonstrator's beliefs, or about whether everything a person cares about can be traded off against everything else on a single scale, or about the choosing itself being the thing that carries the preference — and explain why that assumption is doing real work rather than being a technicality that a practitioner could patch in a footnote. Then tell me what would have to be added to the model to get the person right, and whether the result would still be recognizably inverse reinforcement learning or something else wearing its name.
