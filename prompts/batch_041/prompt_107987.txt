I'm a novelist, and each year I take one session in a graduate seminar on memory and narrative. This year my session falls in the week the students have been set Margalit on the ethics of memory and Rieff on the case for forgetting, and I want my hundred minutes to do something the reading can't do for them: I want sixteen of them to feel the two pulls cross. By that I mean the moment when letting a memory go looks like a betrayal of the people one belongs to, and at the same time the faithful keeping of it looks like a way of holding a wound open on purpose — and I want them to feel that these two pulls come from different places in a person, rather than from a muddle that a bit more thinking would clear up. The reading will have handed them the vocabulary; I want the session to hand them the experience, so that the vocabulary has something to attach itself to.

Design that activity for me. One thing I'm sure of: I don't want a debate, with a team for Margalit and a team for Rieff, because that turns both views into positions to be performed, and the students will perform them rather than inhabit them. I want everyone in the room to end up jointly answerable for a decision that costs something, and I want the mechanics to do the work — by the time I open my mouth they should already have found out that what they owe their own is not the same as what they owe strangers, and that the difference doesn't feel like a moral failure even while they can see that it is one. Two things I'd ask of the design. At some point the group has to hand a memory over to somebody who wasn't there, so that keeping a thing alive and losing it turn out to be the same gesture. And something has to be genuinely lost, irreversibly, inside the fiction, so that the loss is a fact of the session rather than a topic of conversation in it. Nothing may be drawn from the students' own lives; I'll invent the material, and I want the fiction complete enough that no one is tempted to volunteer a family story.

So: the materials I need to print, the rules, the sequence with rough timings, and the debrief. In the debrief I want the mapping spelled out — this moment was the obligation to one's own, that moment was the price of transmission, that moment was the case for forgetting — along with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what I should do when it does. Be straight with me about the feature of the idea the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply in words afterwards; I'd rather know in advance than find out in the room.

Write it in British English, as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. A long letter to a colleague is the shape I have in mind. And if what I'm asking for can't be done in a hundred minutes with sixteen people, or if the design I've described would inevitably tilt the room toward one of the two views, tell me so plainly instead of giving me a polished version of the thing I asked for. I'd rather rebuild the session than run something that only appears to work.
