I am preparing the final examination for a second-year undergraduate course in philosophical theology, and I need a single essay question on omnipotence. The difficulty is that the topic is drowning in a stock answer: students recite the stone paradox, invoke the thesis that omnipotence extends only to the logically possible, and stop. I want a question that cannot be answered that way.

To force genuine argument, I would like the question organized around a proposal that is not part of the standard textbook discussion. The proposal is this: that omnipotence properly includes the power of self-limitation, so that an omnipotent being could, by its own act, place itself under a constraint it could not afterwards remove — for instance, making a promise it would then be genuinely unable to break — and that this act of self-binding would count as an exercise of power rather than a surrender of it. On this view the stone paradox loses its sting: the being could create a stone it cannot lift, or impose a limit it cannot escape, without ceasing to be omnipotent, because the inability is self-imposed and was itself within the being's power to avoid.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. The question should require students to do at least the following: (i) state precisely what the self-limitation proposal is and what conception of omnipotence it presupposes; (ii) decide whether an unbreakable self-imposed constraint is compatible with the being's remaining omnipotent, and defend that decision rather than asserting it; (iii) say whether the proposal rescues omnipotence or merely redefines the term so that the paradox can no longer arise — that is, whether it is a substantive thesis or a verbal maneuver; and (iv) assess whether the proposal has any independent motivation, explaining anything we independently want to say about divine power, promises, or freedom, or whether it is purely ad hoc. A strong answer should also consider whether a "power to bind oneself" is a coherent power at all, or whether the very idea of a power that terminates in its own elimination is self-undermining.

The marking scheme should be built around the specific moves that distinguish an A answer from a B: name each move, give the marks it carries out of a total of 20, and add a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs candidates those marks — in particular, the difference between showing that the proposal is coherent and showing that it is true, and the difference between a definitional victory and a philosophical one. The question should be answerable in 45 minutes and should require no reading beyond what was covered in the course. Please avoid asking for names, dates, or citations; I will supply those myself.
