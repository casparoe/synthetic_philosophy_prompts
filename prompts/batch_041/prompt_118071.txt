I have been trying to get straight on the question whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and I have just read a long chapter in a general-audience introduction to ethics that claims to settle it. The substance of the chapter, in my own words, runs roughly as follows:

Genuine moral dilemmas are impossible, and this is now close to a consensus. The argument is simple. A moral code tells you what to do; a code that told you both to do something and not to do it would contradict itself, and a self-contradictory code cannot guide anyone, so it would not be a moral code at all. Kant saw this first: because moral requirements must be universalizable, a real clash of duties would be a contradiction in the moral law itself, which is why he denied that duties can ever genuinely collide. The dramatic cases, then, are not dilemmas but hard choices or failures of knowledge -- Sartre's student torn between staying with his mother and joining the Resistance, Sophie made to choose which of her children dies. Learn more, or rank your values correctly, and one option will turn out to be the one you were required to take. Anyone who insists that both options were truly required must accept an inconsistent morality, and that is why the defenders of dilemmas end up surrendering universalizability and drifting into particularism or relativism. Even the formal logicians agree: the standard systems for reasoning about obligation are built on the principle that whatever is obligatory is permitted, which is the same thought in symbols. The whole dispute is at bottom the old quarrel between a morality of rules and a morality of character -- Kantians and Aristotelians -- and it dissolves once one notices that "ought" implies "can": if you cannot do both, you were never obligated to do both, so there is nothing left over to conflict.

I do not trust this, but I cannot put my finger on why; it feels too easy, and the closing move in particular seems to assume the very thing at issue. I am not after a reading list or a bibliography -- I want an audit I can act on. Where it attributes a view to a named figure, tell me whether the attribution holds and whether the reason given for it is the reason that figure actually gives. One thing the chapter never mentions, and which strikes me as the distinction the whole debate should turn on: a case in which I cannot tell which of two duties binds me, as against a case in which neither duty gives way. Tell me whether that distinction is doing real work here, or whether I am importing it from somewhere and it is a red herring.
