I'm a science-and-ideas journalist — features, the odd radio essay — and I've been roped into running next month's session of a small discussion salon I go to: about a dozen people, all bright non-specialists (a GP, a retired immigration judge, two software people, a vicar who is frankly more secular than I am), ninety minutes, one whiteboard, and no set reading beforehand, which was my own stupid promise of "no homework". I've ended up with Molinism and the idea of middle knowledge, mainly because I spent an afternoon interviewing a philosopher who kept saying, as if it were the most natural thing in the world, that God knows what you *would* freely do in situations that never actually arise, and I nodded along in a way I now regret. I know roughly what I'm aiming at: I want them to leave with the three-way division of God's knowledge — the stuff that's true no matter what, the stuff that's true only because God decided to make this world, and the awkward one in the middle — and, more importantly, I want them to feel why the middle one is the load-bearing beam and why its enemies think it's load-bearing precisely because it's unsupported. What I want from you is not the explanation but the plan for one. What has to be in place before anything else can be said, and what depends on what, so that the "middle" bit feels like it was forced on us rather than introduced as a bit of theological kit; the one example that should carry the whole session and what it has to have and what it must *not* have — I keep reaching for something like two worlds identical down to the instant before a choice, differing only in what the chooser does, but I can't tell if that's the right carrier or whether I need an actual person with an actual temptation (and please say why not, if not); the misconceptions they'll bring in or manufacture on the spot, in the order they'll surface — my guesses are "if God knows it then it wasn't free", "this is just God being a very good judge of character", "middle means halfway along in time", and "so it's predestination with extra steps" — and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, because I'd rather not spend the last twenty minutes doing damage control; and finally one question I can put to them at the end that would actually show whether the idea has landed rather than whether they can repeat my whiteboard back at me.
