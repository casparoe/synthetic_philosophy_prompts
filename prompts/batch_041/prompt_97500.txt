I am a high school student, and I have been asked to write a poem for the final meeting of our philosophy club, where each member reads something they have composed. I have chosen to write about the distinction between option luck and brute luck -- between a bad outcome that one can be said to have knowingly run the risk of, and one that simply befell a person -- and I would like the poem to be a villanelle. The reason for that form is that its two refrains keep returning whether or not they fit, and I want the refrains to be the two halves of the distinction, so that the poem's own structure keeps forcing the question of which one applies. Ideally, a reader who already knows the debate should be able to tell which refrain is which without being told.

The speaker should be someone whose losses are genuinely arguable rather than a clear case: a person who took a risk that many reasonable people take, and who also began from circumstances they did not choose, so that neither label sits comfortably. I would like the poem to arrive, somewhere in the middle, at the thought that the very same misfortune can be redrawn as either kind of luck depending on where one places the boundary of the choice -- that the line between the two is not discovered so much as drawn -- and I would like the closing quatrain to bring the two refrains together in a way that holds that difficulty open rather than resolving it. The poem should also register, without turning into a sermon, the complaint that a view built on this distinction seems to have little to offer the people who are merely unlucky, including those who made their own situation worse. The tone I am after is plain and unsentimental rather than lofty, and I would prefer slant rhyme to forced rhyme if the two conflict. It should be accurate enough that the teacher who runs the club, who has read the literature, would not wince, while still being a poem that the other students, who have not, would enjoy hearing read aloud.
