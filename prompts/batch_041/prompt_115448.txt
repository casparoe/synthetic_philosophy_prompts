"The kalam argument comes from medieval Islamic theology — al-Ghazali is the classic source — and was revived in the late twentieth century by Craig. It runs: (1) whatever begins to exist has a cause of its existence; (2) the universe began to exist; (3) therefore the universe has a cause of its existence. Craig then argues the cause must be uncaused, beginningless, immaterial, enormously powerful and personal, which is what we mean by God.

Premise 1 is defended by induction: everything we have ever observed that begins to exist has a cause, so it's reasonable to think everything that begins to exist has one. This is a very strong premise, since it's confirmed by all our experience, and you'd need a counterexample to refute it. The only serious attempt is quantum mechanics, where particles seem to come into existence uncaused, but that's a misreading: those events are caused by the quantum field.

Premise 2 is supported in two ways. First, philosophical arguments against an actual infinite: Hilbert's Hotel shows that an actually infinite number of things leads to contradictions, so an infinite past is impossible. Second, the scientific evidence: the Big Bang model shows the universe began to exist about 13.8 billion years ago, and the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin theorem proves that any expanding universe must have had a beginning. So philosophy and physics agree.

The obvious objection is 'who caused God?', but that's a misunderstanding. The argument only says whatever begins to exist has a cause, and God doesn't begin to exist. It doesn't claim everything has a cause, only things that begin to exist, so there's no regress. Craig also holds that God is timeless without creation and enters time at creation, which deals with the problem that a cause has to be temporally prior to its effect.

Another objection, associated with Grünbaum and others, is that the argument equivocates on 'cause': in ordinary cases a cause brings something about out of pre-existing materials, whereas the universe's cause is supposed to work without any materials. But this only shows the universe's cause is different from ordinary causes, not that it doesn't exist; creation ex nihilo is exactly what the theist is claiming.

Finally, even if the argument works it only gets you a cause of the universe. You need further arguments — fine-tuning, religious experience, miracles — to reach a being who is good, or who answers prayer. So kalam is a first step, not a proof of the God of the Bible."
