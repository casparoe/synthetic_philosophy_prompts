A friend and I just spent three hours arguing about accuracy-first epistemology, and I want to know whether our disagreement is about the theory or just about the numbers. By accuracy-first epistemology I mean the program that takes the sole fundamental epistemic good to be accuracy — how close one's credences are to the truth — and tries to derive every norm of credence, including probabilism and conditionalization, from a single injunction to maximize expected accuracy under a strictly proper scoring rule. My credence that the program succeeds — that is, that every genuine norm governing credence follows from accuracy considerations alone, with no independent evidential, coherence, or pragmatic value smuggled in — is about 0.35; my friend's is about 0.75.

Give me your own credence in that same proposition, but more importantly decompose it. I want a probability and a one-line justification for each of the sub-questions the number rests on, at minimum: (a) that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good; (b) that strictly proper scoring rules capture what we mean by accuracy; (c) that the accuracy-first derivation of the synchronic norm, probabilism, is sound and non-circular; (d) that the diachronic norms — conditionalization, reflection, and the rest — can be derived without some extra assumption about evidence or learning; and (e) that the remaining candidate norms, such as norms of suspension of judgment or the alleged epistemic value of understanding, need no independent value. Add any sub-question you think I've left out. Then tell me which term you are least confident in, how sensitive the final number is to it, and which single sub-question most likely separates someone at 0.35 from someone at 0.75. Finally, say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would move your number most, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.

Return the answer as a JSON object with these fields: proposition (the claim, stated precisely enough to have a truth value); credence (a number between 0 and 1); sub_questions (an array of objects, each with name, probability, and justification, one line each); combination (how the sub-probabilities yield the final number, in one or two sentences); least_confident_term (the name of that sub-question); sensitivity (what the final number does if that term moves by 0.1 in either direction); disagreement_source (which sub-question most likely explains the 0.35-versus-0.75 gap); and what_would_change_mind (the argument or evidence that would move you, and where your reasoning is weakest). Write in American English.
