I'm a third-year student in a course on moral responsibility, and I've been trying to get my own verdicts about moral luck into some kind of order rather than filing cases under the headings we were given. Here is where my instincts actually land. I'd like to know what principle, if any, holds them together, what it costs, and which verdicts I should abandon.

1. Two couriers, equally tired and equally distracted by a phone call, run the same red light at the same speed. A child happens to be crossing at the first junction and not at the second. I think the courier who killed the child is more to blame — not merely more unfortunate.

2. Two accountants are equally ready to defraud a client. One is assigned an account that makes the fraud possible; the other is never in a position to steal and never does. I think the second is not blameworthy at all, even though his character is no better.

3. Two people equally tempted to falsify an expense claim both do it. One does it in a week when nothing has gone wrong; the other in the week his rent doubles and his partner leaves him. I think the second is less to blame.

4. Two people tell the same lie to escape the same trouble. The first can see, without effort, a way of telling the truth that would have cost him very little; the second cannot think of any way out but the lie — not from laziness, but because nothing occurs to him. I think the first is more to blame.

5. Two people each embezzle once, at nineteen and at forty-five, and both live honestly afterwards. I think the forty-five-year-old is more to blame.

6. Two people betray a friend. One does it easily; the other only after a long struggle and against everything he cares about. This is the case I keep going back and forth on: sometimes I think the one who struggled is more to blame, because he knew exactly what he was doing; sometimes less, because he had to give up something in order to do it.

The principle I have been trying out is this: blame is proportionate to the defect of will that an act reveals, and luck enters only as evidence about the will, never as a constituent of it — with a rider that blame is reduced when the choice was difficult. I can see two problems with it. The rider does all the work on cases 3, 4 and 6, and the rider is exactly the kind of constitutive role for luck that the main clause forbids, so the principle looks to me like two principles wearing one coat. And the main clause forces me to drop case 1, since the killing reveals nothing about the courier that the near miss does not. My question is whether a single principle can keep 1 without surrendering what the rider does for 3, 4 and 6; and if it cannot, which verdict should go. Please state the principle precisely enough that I can apply it without asking you what proportionate means, give the case that motivates each clause, and say plainly which of my six verdicts it cannot accommodate.

I would also like its verdicts on two cases I have not settled. The first varies case 1: the child who runs out in front of the courier turns out to be his own daughter — does that change how much he is to blame, or only how much he suffers? The second is a case of what I have been calling near-miss luck: a driver who has been reckless on the same road fifty times without incident and finally kills someone, compared with a driver who is reckless for the first time and kills someone. Same act, same outcome, different history. If you find you need two principles rather than one, say which you would keep if you could keep only one, and give me a case of your own that would settle the choice. I already know the familiar headings these cases are usually filed under, so please don't spend the answer on the taxonomy; what I want is the principle, its price, and the verdicts it forces on me.
