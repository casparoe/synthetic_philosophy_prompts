A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about a small perceptual demonstration, and we ended up further apart than we started. The demonstration is easy to state: a spot flashes briefly at one point on a screen, and a fraction of a second later a differently colored spot flashes some distance away. At the right spacing and timing, you do not see two flashes. You see a single spot traveling between the two positions, and its color changes abruptly partway along the path. The strange part is the timing people report: asked afterwards, they say the change happened in the middle of the journey, before the second spot was ever shown. It is hard to see how the visual system could have had the second spot's color in hand before the second spot existed.

My friend reads this as evidence that experience is edited after the fact: the earlier moment was lived one way and its record was then rewritten, so that what we consult when we report is not what we underwent. I read it as evidence that there was never a finished earlier moment to rewrite, that the episode is a single construction assembled from whatever arrives in time, and that the question of what it was like before the assembly was finished has no answer. We could not make the two readings disagree. Every test we invented was either another report, which both readings predict identically by construction, or a physiological event, which neither reading forbids, since the quarrel is not about the wiring but about what the wiring amounts to. He offered one candidate I could not dismiss: a probe delivered between the two flashes, a masking pattern or a magnetic pulse or a third spot, that the subject demonstrably cannot report but that nonetheless shifts the color later attributed to the first spot. I answered that a shift in what is later attributed is precisely what a rewrite of the record would produce, and equally what a single late assembly would produce, so the probe buys nothing.

I have a year of protected time ahead of me and no laboratory, and I would rather spend it on whatever here is genuinely load-bearing than on whatever merely looks deep, a distinction I no longer trust myself to draw. So please set an agenda rather than settle the argument. Let the goal be progress on the cluster this demonstration sits inside: the timing of experience; whether a constructive account of perception leaves room for any distinction between what was experienced and what is merely remembered or reported; and what downstream actually turns on the answer, whether the search for neural correlates, the reading of reaction-time data, or claims about when pain or sight begins in creatures that cannot tell us. Which open questions in that cluster matter most, in what order, and why? I want the reasoning more than the list: for each, why it is upstream of the others, how much someone with conceptual tools and no laboratory can do with it, and what kind of result would move it up or down the order. Tell me also what you would deprioritize even though it looks central.
