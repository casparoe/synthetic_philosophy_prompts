I'm putting together a seminar on privacy and I want to start with the spec rather than the theories, becuase every time we read a new one we end up arguing about wether it's even answering the right question. So: write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of what privacy is and why it matters has to meet. Not the theories themselves -- the requirements list. For each requirement, the case or consideration that forces it, and then say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just something one camp happens to care about.

A few things I want the list to have to deal with, since these are the ones that keep breaking the theories we've read:

- the person who says they've got nothing to hide and genuinely doesn't care who sees their bank statements or their search history. Does the theory say they're right, wrong, or answering a different question?
- the case I keep calling the Open House: a town where every household, freely and unanimously, installs always-on cameras feeding a public archive. No one is watched by anyone who couldn't already see their house from the street. No one objects, no one is harmed, nothing is hidden that wasn't already visible. Is anything lost here? If the theory can't even ask the question, that's a mark against it.
- the inference case: every piece of data is public and every person consented to each individual disclosure, but the combination predicts something about you that you never told anyone and never would have. Is that a new privacy event, or just arithmetic on public facts?
- the privacy paradox: people say they care and then trade it away for a coupon, over and over, and it's not obviously just weakness of will.
- privacy after death, and privacy for groups -- nobody left to be harmed, or a whole collective whose members each individually have enough privacy while the group has none.

And one distinction I'd like the list to force a theory to make: is there a real difference between being seen and being modelled -- between someone watching you and a system having a working prediction of you that never needs to watch you again? If that distinction is real, some requirement on the list has to have a slot for it.

Then say explicitly whether some of these requirements can't be satisfied together. If the list as stated is unsatisfiable, that's the result I most want -- don't paper over it. Also tell me which single requirement does the most work, i.e. knocks out the most otherwise popular accounts. Skip the scorecard against the named theories, I'll do that in class.
