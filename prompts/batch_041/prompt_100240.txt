I'm finishing a term in a graduate reading group on hedonism, and the sessions keep circling back to the heterogeneity problem: pleasure seems to name such a motley assortment of things — the taste of a ripe peach, the moment a proof clicks into place, the relief when a fever breaks, the satisfaction of a grudge finally paid off, the warmth of being welcomed home after a long trip — that it's hard to say what, if anything, they all have in common, and if nothing does, it's unclear what the hedonist is even singling out as the sole good. Our readings have trotted out the usual candidates: a distinctive felt quality present in every pleasant experience; a certain attitude toward a content, roughly being pleased that something is the case; and a deflationary line on which the term is held together by nothing but overlapping resemblance, the way we are told the word game is. In a mood to stir things up, one of us proposed that pleasure might instead behave like healthy or sharp — said of many different things, but with some one of them serving as the anchor from which the rest take their point — and I want the closing poem to put that suggestion under pressure without ever resolving it.

So: write a ghazal-like poem, seven to nine autonomous couplets, each ending on the same short refrain (let the refrain be the word pleasure or a two- or three-word phrase built on it), with a rhyme immediately before the refrain that changes from couplet to couplet and with the opening couplet rhyming on both its lines. I want the form itself to carry the argument. Let each couplet be a self-contained pleasure that could stand alone, so that the only thing actually binding the poem is the recurring refrain — and let the refrain's sense drift from couplet to couplet, so a reader feels it meaning something different each time while the word itself stays fixed. If you can, let the shifting rhyme words be the candidates for what unites the pleasures — feeling, liking, wanting, seeming, and their friends — so that each rhyme captures some of the couplets and visibly fails the others. Keep all three options in play (common feeling, common attitude, mere resemblance), along with the focal-meaning hunch, and end without declaring a winner, since that is honestly where our discussion stalled. It should be accurate enough that someone in the group recognizes their own position sitting inside it, and good enough that someone who has never read a word of the debate wants to keep it.
