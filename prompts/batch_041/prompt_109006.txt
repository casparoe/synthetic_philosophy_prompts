Quick favour — I'm putting together a handout for my ethics reading group. We've spent about six weeks circling the demandingness of morality and I'm sick of the discussion collapsing into "well obviously you're not *obliged* to donate a kidney" every single time. What I want instead is a decision tree that sorts people into positions, so that at least we find out where we actually disagree.

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on supererogation and the limits of duty. Before you start writing, brainstorm the logical space for a bit — I'd rather you work out which questions genuinely seperate the positions and build the tree around those, than copy out a list of isms. Think carefully about which of the questions actually come apart from one another and which are just the same question in different clothes.

Deliverable: a flowchart, top to bottom, each node a single question phrased so that someone can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to (so no nodes like "are you a satisficing consequentialist?"), each leaf labelled with the name of a position plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Say explicitly which pairs of leaves your tree can't separate, and don't paper over it — if two views answer every question on the tree the same way, flag them as a merged leaf.

Branches I think need to be on there, though rearrange them if my order is wrong:

- whether there is any act that's morally better than the alternatives but not required at all;
- if yes, *why* it isn't required: because there's simply no duty in the first place, or because you have a permission not to do the best thing available;
- whether an act can be supererogatory for one person and flatly required for another (think: the lifeguard who swims out versus the passer-by who does; the parent who gives the kidney versus the stranger who does);
- whether omitting a supererogatory act can ever make you an appropriate target of criticism or blame even though it wasn't wrong;
- whether "supererogation" is a property of acts at all, or just a way of talking about agents and their virtues;
- whether the mirror image exists — acts that are permitted but that we're right to criticise;
- and whether the category is relative to a particular moral code (some codes have slack, some don't) or a feature of morality as such.

Two distinctions I want the tree to make room for, because I think they're doing a lot of quiet work in these discussions and I've not seen them laid out cleanly:

1. Acts that aren't required of *anyone* versus acts that aren't required of *this* agent only because of what they cost *them* — the same act might be supererogatory for you and merely decent for me if I happen to find it easy.
2. Acts whose point is the good done versus acts whose point is the risk or sacrifice borne — I think these come apart in the grenade case and possibly nowhere else.

Concrete cases I want to be able to route through the tree: the soldier and the grenade; the stranger's kidney versus the sibling's; giving away most of your income for the rest of your life; and a one-off heroic act that costs nothing because the agent happens to be unusually good at it.

Aim for about eight or nine leaves. I don't need citations, reading lists or names of philosophers — just label the positions and describe them, and where nobody defends a combination the tree throws up, say so and give me your best guess as to why (incoherent, just unattractive, or nobody's bothered).
