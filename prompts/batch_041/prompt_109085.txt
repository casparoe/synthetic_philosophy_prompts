I am drafting a short paper on Parfit's teletransporter and would be grateful for detailed comments before I revise it. The argument, in outline, is as follows.

Parfit asks us to imagine a machine that records a person's body and brain in complete detail, destroys the original, and transmits the record to Mars, where a new body is assembled from local matter and wakes up with all the original's memories and dispositions. Parfit's verdict is that the question of whether you survived is empty: everything that matters, the psychological connectedness and continuity he gathers together under a single heading, is fully preserved, and identity was never what we were tracking. His sharper point is that psychological continuity comes in degrees and can branch, whereas identity is all or nothing, so identity cannot be what matters.

My thesis is that Parfit is right that identity is not what matters and wrong about what is. What the teletransporter preserves is continuity of psychology; what it destroys is the continuation of the particular stream of experience I now occupy. Call that perspectival continuity. What I care about when I care about surviving, I claim, is perspectival continuity, and the two come apart precisely in the teletransporter case.

Parfit himself notes that the machine can be modified so that it does not destroy the original. I want to press that variant harder than he does. Suppose the scanner builds an exact replica on Mars and, because of a fault, leaves me alive on Earth. The replica is related to me by exactly the same psychological continuity as in the original case, so on Parfit's account everything that matters is present in it to the same degree. But I am still here, and it is obvious to me that the replica is not the one who will feel my future toothache. Now let the fault be repaired: the destruction of the original adds nothing to the replica and subtracts nothing from the psychological continuity. If the replica is not the continuation of my stream of experience while I am alive, killing me cannot make it one. So psychological continuity was not what I was tracking when I cared about surviving; perspectival continuity was.

I expect the reply that this simply reinstates the further fact about personal identity that Parfit denies, and I have two things to say to it. First, perspectival continuity need not be a further fact in his sense: it is not a mysterious ego but just the plain fact that experiences are had from a point of view that either continues or does not, and unlike psychological continuity that fact is not a matter of degree. Second, if the reply is that the faulty machine case is simply a branching case, and branching is exactly where identity fails while what matters survives, then the burden shifts back to me to explain why branching should be of any consequence to the person who is branched from.

I would like comments on four points. First, is my reconstruction of Parfit accurate and fair, or have I made him easier to refute than he is? Second, is the faulty machine argument sound, or does it presuppose the conclusion that a person's stream of experience cannot be duplicated? Third, what is Parfit's strongest reply, and does it succeed? Fourth, is perspectival continuity a genuine distinction or merely a relabelling of the further fact he rejects?
