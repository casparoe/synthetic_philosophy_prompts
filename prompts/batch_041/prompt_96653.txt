I am assembling a specification for a theory of rational choice under unbounded stakes, and I want the list of requirements before I read any more proposed solutions to the family of cases that includes Pascal's mugging and the St. Petersburg game. Write the desiderata: what such a theory has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, a verdict on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of a particular camp, and an explicit flag on any subset that cannot be satisfied jointly.

Build the list around four cases, which I think pull in different directions.

The mugger. A stranger with no track record offers no mechanism and makes no testable prediction, and says that unless I hand over my wallet he will have an enormous number of people tortured. He would say this whether or not he can do it, so the utterance itself carries no evidential weight, and the case turns entirely on the prior I am supposed to have on his power, an event with no available reference class.

The open-ended prize. A lottery with a one-in-a-billion chance of an outcome that is not merely large but unbounded: for every finite multiple of any ordinary good, the prize is better than that multiple. Expected utility is defined here, and it swamps every alternative, so the trouble is not driven by a minuscule probability.

The never-ending prize. A lottery whose winning outcome consists of infinitely many positive stages in sequence. Standard expected utility is not merely enormous here but undefined, since the formula is stated over real-valued utilities; I want that stated as a genuine requirement on the theory, not waved through as a technicality.

The queue of claimants. Infinitely many mutually exclusive strangers, each offering an unbounded or infinite payoff, so that there is no best option to select and no way to satisfy them all.

I take the following to be candidate requirements, and I want you to assess them rather than accept them on faith: agreement with ordinary expected-utility reasoning on bounded, finite problems; the ability to return a verdict, or a principled refusal to return one, on every problem in the class; non-fanaticism, meaning that ordinary practical reasoning is not dominated by tiny-probability unbounded payoffs; invariance under re-description of the outcomes and under rescaling or re-zeroing of the utility scale; tractability for an agent who cannot enumerate the options or compute the expectations; robustness to exploitation by a claimant who benefits from my compliance; and action-guidance that does not require me to adopt credences I cannot sincerely form, over events for which no reference class exists.

Then tell me which pairs of these cannot be held together, and which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the obvious candidate families (straightforward maximisation of expected utility, bounds on utility, discounting of small probabilities, and refusing to assign probabilities at all). Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases that motivate them; I do not need a scorecard.
