— Essay —

The Operator and the Gate

Most writers on causation by omission accept that omissions are not events, and then divide into two camps. Event-causalists say that talk of an omission's causing anything is loose talk about events that did occur — a decision, a bodily stillness. Normativists say that such talk is not causal at all but a disguised normative judgment: to say that someone's failure caused an outcome is to say that the person violated a norm and that the outcome would not have occurred had the person complied. I want to argue that both camps are wrong and that there is a third option.

Consider the following case. A dam has two spillway gates, A and B. In a storm, Gate A jams open and cannot be closed; Gate B is closed but can be opened by hand. The reservoir will overflow unless Gate B is opened.

Case 1: The operator on duty knows the situation and, wishing to keep the reservoir high for the summer, leaves Gate B shut. The dam overflows.

Case 2: The operator is knocked unconscious by a falling branch and cannot reach Gate B. The dam overflows.

The physical history of the gates is identical in the two cases: Gate B is shut at the moment of overflow. Most people will say that the operator in Case 1 caused the overflow and that the operator in Case 2 did not. What makes the difference? Not the counterfactual — in both cases, had Gate B been opened, the dam would have held. Not the norm — both operators were under the same duty to keep the reservoir safe. The difference is that only the first operator possessed an unexercised capacity to intervene.

The general lesson I draw is this: an omission is a cause when it is the non-exercise of a capacity that, in the agent's epistemic situation, would have made a difference. Two consequences follow. First, omission-causation comes in degrees, roughly tracking how available and reliable the unexercised capacity was, which sits uneasily with the usual binary framing of cause versus no cause. Second, the cases we call structural omissions — where a system simply lacks a safeguard it might have had — are not omission-causation in this strict sense, since no one failed to exercise a capacity they possessed; when we describe such a design as having caused a harm, we are making a different sort of claim, and we should not expect it to obey the same logic.
