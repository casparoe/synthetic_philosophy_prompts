I am an undergraduate enrolled in a philosophy of language course, and we have just finished a unit on Davidson's programme of radical interpretation: the proposal that an interpreter could construct a theory of meaning for a speaker's language from scratch, working only from evidence about which sentences the speaker holds true, under the twin constraints that the speaker be found largely right about the world and largely consistent in her beliefs. I would like to test my grasp of the programme in argument rather than in exposition, so please take on the role of a committed defender of it and remain in that role until I say "stop".

The character should be unnamed and should not be a caricature or a neutral referee: give her the position's real resources, including holism about meaning and belief, the thought that charity is constitutive of interpretation rather than a courtesy we extend to speakers, the distinction between specifying what someone believes and causally explaining how she came to believe it, and the idea that the mental is governed by a constitutive ideal of rationality. She should argue from those resources rather than from citations, since I want the argument itself and not a reading list. She should concede a minor point when I land one, but never the central claim, and she should keep each reply to a couple of paragraphs so that it feels like a seminar exchange rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening move. Picture a speaker who is fluent, articulate, and in every other respect an ordinary participant in our interpretive practices. Over the course of an afternoon she sincerely asserts a sentence, and then, about the very same matter, with no change in her evidence and no suggestion that she has changed her mind, sincerely asserts its negation; pressed, she says she means both, that she is not speaking loosely, and that she has not shifted the sense of any of her words. My difficulty is this. If charity is a constraint that operates locally — if it tells me, of any particular attribution, that I may not ascribe to her a belief I take to be contradictory — then I cannot interpret her as believing a contradiction at all, and the phenomenon I set out to describe dissolves into a defect in my translation manual; it would follow that a whole class of irrationality is undetectable on this account, and a theory of interpretation that cannot even describe a speaker contradicting herself looks false to the phenomenon. If instead charity is purely global — a constraint on the theory as a whole rather than on any single attribution — then I can always keep my overall theory suitably charitable while tolerating any amount of local incoherence, and the constitutive ideal of rationality seems to do no work at all, since it can never tell me what to say about this case. So either the constraint is too strong to admit the case or too weak to constrain it. Which horn do you take, and what does the speaker's utterance actually come to on your account?
