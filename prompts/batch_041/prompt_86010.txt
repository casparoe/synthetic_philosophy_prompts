Question 3 (10 points, ~400 words). Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain case is often presented as a decisive refutation of patterned theories of distributive justice. (a) Reconstruct the case and state as precisely as you can what it is meant to show and what it is not meant to show. (b) Nozick insists that entitlement is not the same thing as desert. Explain the difference, and say why it matters for how the case is supposed to work. (c) Suppose two neighbours, Priya and Dan, start out equally well off. Dan, who is broke and needs cash quickly, voluntarily signs his half of a shared garden over to Priya for a small sum. Priya later sells rare cuttings from the plot for a great deal of money and gets rich, while Dan stays poor and now says the outcome is unfair and should be corrected. What does Nozick's entitlement theory imply about whether the resulting distribution is just, and about whether Dan has any claim in justice?

Student's answer:

Nozick's Chamberlain case is a thought experiment about what happens to a just distribution when people are left free to trade. Start with D1, a distribution that everyone agrees is just. Now a million fans each voluntarily drop a quarter into Chamberlain's box to watch him play. The result, D2, has Chamberlain with $250,000 more than everyone else, and everyone else 25 cents poorer. Nozick's claim is that if D1 was just, D2 must be just as well, because D2 came out of D1 through a series of voluntary transfers and nobody's rights were violated along the way. This is why his theory is historical: whether a distribution is just depends on how it came about, not on what shape it has. The theory has three parts — justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, and justice in rectification of past injustice.

What the case is meant to show is that liberty upsets patterns. Any patterned principle — to each according to need, effort, merit, and so on — will be knocked off its pattern by people doing what they freely want to do. Since no pattern can survive free exchange, patterned principles can't really be satisfied at all, and any attempt to enforce one means constantly interfering with the deals people want to make. This is Nozick's main charge against Rawls's difference principle.

The desert point is easy to get wrong. Nozick does not say Chamberlain deserves his money, and he separates entitlement from desert: what matters is the actual history of transfers, not whether the holder morally merits what he holds. Chamberlain could be lazy, lucky and unpleasant and still be entitled to the quarters, because the fans chose to give them. So the conclusion of the case doesn't rest on any claim about who deserves what. In the end, though, what makes D2 just is that Chamberlain deserved the quarters — the fans got something they valued from him and paid for it, so he earned his fortune.

Applied to Priya and Dan: Dan's transfer to Priya was made under financial pressure, and Nozick's entitlement theory only counts transfers as just if they are genuinely voluntary. Dan was broke and had no real choice, so this looks like a case where the transfer never creates a valid entitlement, and Nozick would agree with Dan that the outcome needs correcting, probably through the rectification principle. The initial equality of the two neighbours matters here too, since the theory takes a just starting point as its premise.
