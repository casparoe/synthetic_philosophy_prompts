You are an analytic philosopher specialising in population ethics and the aggregation of harms — someone who has spent a great deal of time on the "torture versus dust specks" family of problems and who is impatient with hand-waving about "vast numbers". I want you to think carefully and reason step by step, and to begin with a short brainstorm in which you sketch two or three candidate scenarios and say why you reject the weaker ones, before settling on one.

Your task is to construct a thought experiment to my specifications. The domain is the aggregation of tiny harms: cases where many very small bads are weighed against one large bad. Do not simply restate the familiar version with an astronomically large population; the whole point is that I want a case where the numbers are doing honest work rather than bullying the intuition.

Fix the following two views for the whole exercise, and define them explicitly in your answer so that no prior reading is presupposed:

View A (totalist aggregation): harms are commensurable across persons and add up; any collection of small harms can in principle outweigh a single large harm if the total magnitude is greater, regardless of how the total is distributed.

View B (lexical priority): there is a threshold of seriousness such that harms below the threshold can never, in any quantity, outweigh a single harm above it, no matter how the minor harms add up.

Now build one scenario meeting all of these constraints:

1. It must be realistic: an ordinary institutional setting — a hospital, a municipal authority, a workplace, a regulator — with no science fiction, no omnipotent agents, and no exotic physics.
2. The population must be modest. A few thousand at most, and preferably fewer; the "vast number" is not permitted to carry the argument.
3. There must be a single variable that flips the verdict between View A and View B. Engineer the case so that when that variable takes one value the two views agree, and when it takes the other value they diverge — and make sure the variable is a genuine feature of the world in the scenario, not a stipulation about which view is correct.
4. The small harms must be genuinely bad for each person who bears them (not merely disliked, inconvenient, or unwanted), and the single large harm must be genuinely and considerably worse for its one bearer.
5. Introduce and use the following distinction, which I want you to treat as stipulative: a harm is supra-threshold if its bearer can in principle notice it, and sub-threshold if it is real and bad for the bearer but falls below anything the bearer could notice. Make the flipping variable in (3) turn on whether the many small harms are supra-threshold or sub-threshold, and let the case show what each view must then say.
6. The scenario itself should occupy a single paragraph.

After the scenario, do all of the following, in order:

(a) State, for each of View A and View B, what the agent in the case ought to do and why, deriving the verdict from the view rather than asserting it.
(b) Identify precisely which feature of the case is doing the work — the feature that, if you changed it alone, would move a view's verdict.
(c) Say what happens if that feature is removed or reversed: does the case collapse back into the ordinary dust-specks puzzle, does it become a case on which both views agree, or does it become incoherent? Be honest if the case only works because of an assumption you smuggled in.
(d) Explain how a defender of the view that comes out worse would attack the case — the strongest objection available to them, not a strawman — and say whether the case survives it.
(e) Finish by saying whether the sub-threshold/supra-threshold distinction is doing genuine normative work here or is merely a redescription of the original puzzle.

Write the whole answer in British English (so: "recognise", "behaviour", "towards", "whilst" where natural), and keep the register precise and unhurried. Do not provide reading lists, citations, or references to named authors; I want the reasoning to stand on its own.
