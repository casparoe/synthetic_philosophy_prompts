I'm a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a graphic designer, loosely modeled on the people who built the Isotype picture-statistics system that Otto Neurath developed for the social and economic museum he ran in Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s, before he moved to The Hague and eventually to Oxford. I've been reading around the subject, and for the past few weeks I've been listening to two friends of mine — one a philosopher of science, the other a working data journalist — argue about it at every dinner we share. Neither is a Neurath specialist, but both know the material well enough to be worth taking seriously, and neither will give an inch. What I want from you is not a general essay on Isotype but a point-by-point adjudication of the exchange as it was actually conducted.

They went roughly like this. (1) The philosopher opened with the claim that a picture language can't be a real language at all, because pictures cannot negate: you can draw three hundred little figures and mean three hundred workers, but there is no pictorial way to say *not* three hundred, or *no* workers, and without negation and the conditional you can state a snapshot but never a law — so Neurath's project amounts to a very clever kind of poster. (2) The journalist replied that Neurath never claimed to be building a full language; he called Isotype a helping or auxiliary language, meant to be read in a fixed order, accompanied by a caption, and spoken aloud, so judging it by the standards of a natural language is like faulting a map for not telling a story. (3) The philosopher answered that this concedes his point rather than meeting it: if the pictures need captions in some ordinary tongue, then the divisiveness the system was supposed to cure simply relocates into the caption, and worse, a picture that cannot negate cannot be false either, which makes it incapable of serving as evidence and fit only for persuasion. (4) The journalist countered that pictures can plainly be false — a dishonest chart of falling wages misleads exactly as a dishonest sentence does — and that the very things Isotype standardizes, the unit figure and the direction of reading, are what make the graphic answerable to the numbers, which is the opposite of rhetoric. (5) The philosopher granted the point about checkability but said it is borrowed from conventions, not drawn from the pictures themselves; and he added that Neurath's own view that everyday concepts are congestions or loose clusters rather than sharply bounded definitions leaves him unable to claim any special objectivity for pictures, since his anti-foundationalism cuts against his own project. (6) The journalist said that this cuts the other way: if every language is a set of tools, and the demand that language mirror the world is precisely the metaphysical picture theory Neurath spent his career attacking, then the objection simply assumes the theory of language he denies. (7) The philosopher closed by suggesting that Neurath's phrase picture language is itself a misleading metaphor, trading on the ordinary sense of picture while denying the metaphysical one.

Please take these moves in order and rate each of them, from clearly mistaken through defensible to clearly right, giving a brief justification for every rating. I'd like you to say who is right about which specific point, where each of them is overreaching, and what both of them are missing — in particular, whether the auxiliary-language status of Isotype settles the dispute about objectivity or merely postpones it, and whether the two of them are even disagreeing about the same thing by the end. You don't need to supply me with sources or a reading list; I'm after the argument itself, so that I can tell which of my two friends should get the last word in the scene I'm drafting.
