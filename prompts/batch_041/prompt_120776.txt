Exam question 3 (20 points).

A hospital assigns every patient a single risk score between 0 and 1. Patients above a low threshold are called in for a screening conversation, and patients above a high threshold are enrolled in a limited care-management program. Let the outcome of interest be whether the patient had a preventable hospitalization within the following year, and suppose patients fall into two groups, A and B, whose base rates for that outcome differ. The score is not perfectly informative.

(a) Define calibration within groups, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class, and state precisely what the impossibility results for group fairness establish about them.

(b) A reviewer claims that because the score is calibrated within groups, the precision of the two decisions, meaning the share of positive cases among the patients above the relevant threshold, must be the same in group A and in group B. Is the reviewer right? Justify your answer.

(c) An engineer proposes to make the system fair by construction by dropping group membership from the feature set. Assess that proposal.

(d) The team concludes that because not all of the criteria can be satisfied, the choice of which criterion to satisfy is a technical matter to be settled by whichever option yields the most accurate system. Assess that conclusion.

Student answer.

(a) Calibration within groups means that among patients who receive the same score, the share who are truly positive is the same in group A and group B; the score therefore means the same thing in both groups. Balance for the negative class says that the average score among true negatives is the same in the two groups, and balance for the positive class says the same about true positives. The impossibility results show that these three conditions cannot hold together once the base rates differ: at most two of the three can be satisfied, and the exceptions are constrained cases such as equal base rates or a perfectly informative score. Because the conditions are stated in terms of the joint distribution of the score, the group, and the outcome, they do not depend on which features the model was given.

(b) The reviewer is right. Calibration within groups means that the score can be read directly as a probability at every level, and the precision of a decision at a threshold is simply the average of the scores above that threshold. Since the scores mean the same thing in both groups, that average has to come out the same in both groups, at the low threshold and at the high one alike. Re-tuning the thresholds changes how many patients are referred or enrolled, but it cannot close the gap in precision between the groups.

(c) Dropping group membership from the feature set would not by itself remove the disparity: the impossibility result applies to any score, however it was built, and other features carry group information with them, so the score can still behave differently across the two groups. It is nonetheless worth doing, since a model that cannot see group membership cannot use it directly.

(d) Since not every criterion can be satisfied, some choice has to be made. I would treat this as an engineering decision rather than a value judgment: the team knows its own error rates, the criteria are statistical properties of the score, and they can be compared and optimized like any other performance metric, so the natural procedure is to keep whichever configuration minimizes total error. The choice should then be documented and monitored over time.
