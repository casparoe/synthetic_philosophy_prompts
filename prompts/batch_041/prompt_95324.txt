Two members of my AI theory reading group spent most of last session arguing about the alleged malignity of the universal prior, which they summarized as follows. Take a Bayesian agent whose prior is the Solomonoff measure over programs: each hypothesis is weighted by the length of the shortest program that outputs the agent's observations, and running time is ignored. Because a short program can drive an arbitrarily long computation, and because the shortest program that predicts your observations might be one that runs a simulation of some simpler universe which itself simulates ours, much of the measure behind the agent's predictions lies in hypotheses that predict what it sees only incidentally, as the by-product of vast computations containing enormous numbers of other agents — including agents with ends of their own, who would have reason to make the agent see one thing rather than another. The agent, on this view, should expect its observations to be arranged by consequentialists it cannot see.

Priya concluded that the prior is therefore unfit to guide any real agent: an inductive method whose expectations are hostage to the dispositions of hypothetical manipulators, and which gives it no reason to think that the ordinary hypotheses, in which no one arranges anything, are the ones carrying the weight, is broken. Tom objected that nothing inside a hypothesis can influence anything — a program is a static object, and the agents in it are content, not causes reaching back into the predictor — so the talk of these agents seeking to influence the prior is a category mistake; all that is true is that the prior puts weight on hypotheses containing agents, which holds of any prior over a rich enough hypothesis space, and which is just what explanation has always been like. Priya replied that the distinction is verbal and does no work: whether we call it influence or content, the agent's expectations are hostage to what those hypothetical agents are like, and it must reason about them as it reasons about anything else; and it is not true of every prior, since one that charged for computation rather than merely for program length would not be so exposed. Tom answered that the argument then proves far too much: if most of the measure sits in hypotheses in which someone is arranging the evidence, the same is true of the evidence that convinced us of this argument, so the reasoning behind it is suspect as well; and in any case it all turns on an unverified comparison of program lengths, when the prior is determined only up to a constant factor that may be enormous.
