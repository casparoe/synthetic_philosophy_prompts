You are an expert in the philosophy of mind and cognitive science with a thorough command of the history of artificial intelligence and of the conceptual disputes that grew out of it. I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the frame problem, and what I need from you is not an essay but a classification instrument.

Please construct a decision tree — or a taxonomy presented in tree form — that takes a reader from a handful of pre-theoretical commitments to a named position on the question: what is the frame problem, and is it a genuine problem for a computational account of thought? I am not interested in a roll-call of famous authors with their views attached. Build the tree from the logical space of positions first, and place particular authors into it only afterwards, and only where they plainly belong.

Work carefully and in stages. Begin with a short brainstorm in which you identify the axes along which positions actually divide, and only then draw the tree. I would like those axes to include, at a minimum, the following distinctions: whether the difficulty is a problem about representation (how a system records what changes and what does not) or about relevance (how a system selects what to consider at all) or about situated coping (whether explicit representation is the right model of intelligence in the first place); whether the difficulty is one of computational tractability, one of engineering practice, or a conceptual confusion to be dissolved rather than solved; and whether the problem is peculiar to artificial intelligence or is instead a general feature of any finite reasoner.

Each branch node should be phrased as a question that a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — a concrete case or claim, not a bare label. Use cases such as these as probes: an action, such as waiting, that ought to leave almost everything unchanged, and the task of getting a formal system to represent that without listing every fact that persists; a domestic robot told to clear a table, which must settle that the colour of the walls is irrelevant to its task; and a system that can draw inferences cheaply but cannot tell which of them bear on what it is currently doing. No node should simply ask whether the frame problem is a real problem.

For every leaf, give the name of the position, or write 'unnamed' where no one has defended it, and add one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Where your tree cannot separate two leaves, flag the pair and say why. Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, mark it and diagnose briefly whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.

Aim for six to nine branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves, and pitch the instrument at a reader who has studied nothing on the topic. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to particular publications; I want the positions and the diagnostic questions only. Please write your answer in American English.
