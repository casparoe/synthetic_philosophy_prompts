Our department's philosophy of action reading group has been circling Kavka's toxin puzzle for the better part of a year, and I have reached the stage where I can forecast every move in the discussion while no one's position has budged. I would like your help diagnosing the impasse rather than settling the puzzle.

The case, as we set it out. An eccentric billionaire will deposit one million dollars in your account tomorrow morning if and only if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you painfully ill for a day but will cause no lasting harm and does not threaten your life. The money is paid for the intention alone; it is in your account hours before the appointed drinking time, and you remain free to change your mind and skip the toxin once you have been paid. The question is whether you can form the intention tonight to do something you will, by tomorrow afternoon, have no reason at all to do — and what the answer reveals about the nature of intention.

Three positions have hardened. The first holds that you simply cannot intend to drink: intending to perform an act requires that you not believe you will refrain, and since you can predict with confidence that the already-paid you will decline the toxin, the intention is unavailable. On this reading the puzzle is a clean demonstration that intention is answerable to belief — that you cannot intend what you know you will not do. This is, as I understand it, Kavka's own conclusion. The second position, which we have been associating with Gauthier's line about commitments, holds that you can intend, precisely because an intention is a practical commitment rather than a forecast: once you have committed, the terms of your later deliberation change, and the rational thing to do tomorrow is to honour the commitment and drink. On this reading the puzzle shows that intentions are not reducible to beliefs and desires, and that they constrain rather than merely predict what it is rational to do. A third faction suspects the whole thing is a trick of description — that it turns on an ambiguity in what the billionaire is buying, and dissolves once one distinguishes buying a prediction of your behaviour from buying a commitment that alters it.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the first two camps are using 'intend' for two different states — a broadly predictive one and a broadly commissive one — and to that extent the disagreement is verbal and would evaporate under a sharpened taxonomy of the pro-attitudes. But I doubt that is the whole story, because when we stipulated a single sense of 'intend' and re-ran the argument, we still disagreed about whether forming a commissive intention to drink could itself make the drinking rational, or whether it merely relocates the irrationality one step earlier. If that residual disagreement is real, it looks like a disagreement about whether intentions can be reasons — a question about the structure of practical reason rather than about the meaning of a word.

I would like you to do two things with this. First, diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive, or two different questions running under a single heading — and if it is substantive, is the crux the belief-requirement on intention, the reason-giving force of commitment, or something else entirely? Second, test the diagnosis against a variant we cooked up, which I have not seen discussed and whose coherence I am unsure of. Suppose the billionaire's payment is triggered not by a psychological state at midnight but by your signing an enforceable contract at midnight to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon, with the money released on signature and the contractual penalty set trivially low. Does anything of the puzzle survive this translation from intention to contract? If it does not, does that show the puzzle is really about the rational bindingness of commitments in general rather than about intention in particular — and if it does survive, what does that tell us about which camp was right?

Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. Would it be a distinction, a formal result about the logic of intention, a case that separates the camps, or a decision that the question is ill-posed and ought to be replaced? And be candid about whether the standard belief-desire vocabulary is even the right instrument for whatever the puzzle is actually tracking.
