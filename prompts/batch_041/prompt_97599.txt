Design a runnable classroom activity for an upper-level seminar in decision theory and the philosophy of economics — sixteen students, one eighty-minute session, a room with movable chairs and a whiteboard. The idea I want students to collide with is the Allais paradox and, underneath it, the independence axiom. What I want them to come away seeing is not that the paradox exists but that their own choices exhibit it, and that escaping it is harder than it looks: that the "obvious" fix — commit in advance to a rule and never reconsider — is a real position with a real cost, not a technicality.

The activity must work in two stages, and the facilitator should not say the words "Allais," "independence," or "sure-thing principle" until the debrief; students should produce the pattern before it is named.

Stage one: forced choices between lotteries, arranged so that the group's aggregate and individual responses reproduce the classic conflict — a preference for a sure moderate prize over a gamble with a higher expected value, followed by a reversal on the scaled-down version of the same pair that shares a common consequence. Do not simply reproduce the textbook problems as a quiz. Build the choices out of a concrete device — an urn, dealt cards, sealed envelopes, a "ticket" the student can hold and trade — so that the inconsistency emerges from something they physically did, and so that the shared consequence between the two problems is visible to them only when it is made explicit. Payoffs should be in chips or points with one small real prize at the end, not money per choice.

Stage two, and this is the part I most want you to get right: introduce a commitment device that splits the room. Half the students act as planners, who before any chance event resolves must write a complete policy covering the whole sequence of choices they are about to face; the other half act as choosers, who face those choices one at a time and are permitted either to follow the plan they wrote or to abandon it at the moment of choice. Then let them run the sequence and compare what the planners bound themselves to with what the choosers actually did. The point of the split is to stage the tension between the ex-ante perspective (choose a rule for all the problems at once, which is where independence bites) and the ex-post perspective (choose where you actually stand, which is where the tempting reversal reappears). Students should feel that the plan that keeps them consistent is one they want to defect from the instant they are inside it, and should have to say what, if anything, entitles them to hold themselves to it.

Deliver: the rules and materials, the exact lottery structure with the common consequence made explicit, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for the eighty minutes, roles if you use them, and a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto a piece of the underlying idea — the sure-thing principle, the common-consequence and common-ratio effects, the difference between resolute and sophisticated choice, and what dynamic consistency would require. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where the arithmetic will confuse rather than illuminate, where the planner/chooser split will collapse because both halves just pick the same thing, where a student will defend their reversal with an argument better than the one I had prepared — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the idea this activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in the debrief myself, rather than pretend the game delivers it. No bibliography or citations are needed; I will supply attributions. Write in American English.
