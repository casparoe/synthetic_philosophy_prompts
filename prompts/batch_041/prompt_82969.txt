Last week my metaphysics reading group worked through the special composition question — when, if ever, do some things compose a further thing? — and a friend closed the session with an argument she says she cannot stop chewing on, because it seems to prove far more than anyone in the room was willing to swallow. She wrote it out like this. (1) If some things compose a further thing, then there is a true principle — not a mere artifact of our counting practices or our conventions — that says which collections do and which do not. (2) Any such principle falls into one of three kinds: it lays down no condition (so any things whatsoever compose something); it lays down a condition that nothing satisfies (so nothing is ever composed); or it lays down a genuine condition that some collections meet and others fail, drawing a boundary somewhere in the space of cases. (3) Every candidate condition of that third kind — the parts being in contact, being fastened together, constituting a living organism, being functionally integrated, being treated by us as a unit — comes in a continuous series that runs from clear cases to clear non-cases: a cell an instant before it divides, a sandcastle an instant before it crumbles, a body on a ventilator that no one expects to wake, a firm the day before it dissolves. (4) Nowhere in such a series is there a sharp yet non-arbitrary line: whatever case sits just inside the boundary differs negligibly from the case just outside it. (5) So no condition of the third kind yields a true principle. (6) So composition is either wholly unrestricted or wholly impossible. (7) It is not impossible — I am one thing, and so is this chair — so composition is wholly unrestricted: any things whatsoever compose a further thing. (8) Therefore there exists right now a single object made of my left shoe and the Eiffel Tower, another made of the three coins in my pocket and the moons of Jupiter, and so on without end.

Two things about this unsettle me. The first is that (8) looks like it proves too much: it does not merely tolerate a few outlandish objects, it says reality is closed under arbitrary union, that the question of how many things there are has no answer stable across different ways of counting, and that my everyday conviction that the chair exists and my further conviction that the scattered fusion of the chair with the Andromeda galaxy exists are on precisely the same footing — which makes me suspect the argument has taken on more than its premises pay for. The second is that I cannot find the leak. I would sooner keep (7) than embrace the conclusion, and I would sooner keep (1) than concede that which things compose which is a brute, unprincipled fact. So tell me which premise or step to give up, and be committed rather than diplomatic: do not lay out four possible replies and leave the choice to me — pick the one you think is actually mistaken, say why it looked so compelling, and then tell me plainly what rejecting it costs. Which of the surviving premises becomes hard to defend, what further commitments would I have to take on, and is that a price a reasonable metaphysician would pay, or does it merely move the absurdity somewhere else?
