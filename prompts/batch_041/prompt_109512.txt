An essay I read recently on the debate over the definition of death — a polished piece of popular medical ethics, though I no longer recall who wrote it — laid out the state of play roughly as follows, and I would like to know how much of it I can trust. Its summary runs:

Death was traditionally defined as the irreversible cessation of heartbeat and breathing. Mechanical ventilation changed this, because we can now sustain breathing and circulation after the brain has ceased to work; the operative question became whether the brain is functioning. The whole-brain criterion — irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, brainstem included — was proposed in the late 1960s and later codified in the Uniform Determination of Death Act, under which a person is legally dead when either circulation and breathing have irreversibly stopped or the entire brain, brainstem included, has irreversibly stopped functioning. The reason the brain is the criterion is that the brain is the seat of consciousness and of the person: once the brain is gone, the person is gone, even if the body's other organs are still working. That is why a brain-dead patient on a ventilator, whose heart still beats, is legally dead; the heart is merely a pump that can be kept running. In the end, brain death and circulatory death are simply two tests for the same underlying fact, the death of the organism, and which one we use is a matter of bedside practicality and organ-procurement logistics rather than of anything conceptual. What disputes remain are largely religious and cultural. The dead donor rule — the prohibition on killing a patient in order to obtain organs — is a separate ethical constraint that has nothing to do with how death itself is defined. The philosophical question is essentially settled; what remains is implementation.

Please go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what a physician or philosopher working on the determination of death would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply mistaken. I am especially concerned with whether the essay's concluding claims survive once any earlier missteps are corrected: whether brain death and circulatory death really are two tests for one underlying fact; whether the remaining disputes are genuinely non-conceptual; and whether the dead donor rule is as independent of the definition of death as the essay suggests. I hold no settled view of my own, though I suspect the essay is more confident than the field warrants.
