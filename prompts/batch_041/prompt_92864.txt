I've been going back and forth with my friend Maya (she manages a support team, I'm not in academia, I just read a lot on the train) about the "honesty policy" her company just rolled out. The rule is that agents can't say anything false to a customer, but they're explicitly allowed to answer only the literal question asked in a way they know will leave the customer with a false impression — her favorite example is telling someone "yes, your plan includes data rollover" when the rollover is capped at 200MB and the customer obviously thinks it's unlimited. Maya says this is fine, everything uttered is true, nobody lied. I say it's a lie with extra steps. What we can't agree on is whether there's a difference in *kind* here or only a difference in how easy it is to catch and punish someone.

So here's the thing I want a real number on. Take this proposition: given two acts that would produce exactly the same false belief in the hearer, with exactly the same downstream consequences, and the same likelihood of being discovered, the act that involves uttering something false (with intent to deceive) is more seriously wrong than the act that involves uttering something true (with intent to deceive) — and not merely because the false one is easier to detect, prosecute, or prove. Give me your credence in that, between 0 and 1, but I care way more about the decomposition. I want the sub-questions it rests on, each with a rough probability and one line of why: whether the wrongness of deception is fully explained by the false belief it produces in the hearer, or whether the act-type carries weight of its own (the old idea that lying is wrong in itself regardless of what comes of it); whether assertion carries a special norm of truthfulness that hinting and implicating don't; whether the distinction survives when you imagine swapping the true-but-misleading sentence for a false one that plants the identical belief (I keep calling this the substitution test and I don't know if it actually shows anything); whether the "deniability residue" — the misleader can still say "I never said that" — is itself a harm to the relationship or the shared practice of taking people at their word, or just a fact about evidence; and whether being misled into a false picture of *the speaker* is a different injury from being misled about *the world*. Combine them however you think they actually combine, show me the arithmetic, and then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to and what argument or piece of evidence would move it the most. I'm sitting around 0.35 myself, mostly because I can't shake the feeling that the difference I'm sensing is really about my own hurt feelings at being managed rather than about the acts themselves — but tell me if that's just me being soft.

And please actually commit. Don't hand me a taxonomy of positions and tell me it depends what you mean by "wrong" — pick a number, defend it, and if you think the whole proposition is confused rather than false, say that outright and tell me what the better question is. I'd rather be told I'm asking it wrong, with reasons, than get a balanced survey.
