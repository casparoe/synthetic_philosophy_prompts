I keep circling the debate about whether thought experiments can actually give us knowledge about the world, and the standard options all feel too coarse: they're just arguments in fancy dress, or they give us some special a priori grasp of how things must be, or they work by pumping intuitions that are really our prior beliefs in costume. Before I read any more of this stuff I want the spec first. Write me the desiderata any theory of how thought experiments yield (or fail to yield) knowledge has to meet: the cases it has to get right (Galileo's two weights, Maxwell's demon, the elevator, chasing a light beam, the ones that seem to misfire), the distinctions it can't collapse -- between a thought experiment that refutes a claim by exhibiting a possibility and one that's supposed to establish an actual law, between what it does for the person who first runs it and for the community that later cites it, between a thought experiment and a seperate plain argument or a computer simulation -- and any results from the history or psychology of science it can't contradict. Give me the case or consideration behind each requirement, tell me which one is doing the most work excluding candidates, and flag any pair you think can't be jointly satisfied. I want this for a practical reason: I need a standard I could apply to a live thought experiment someone hands me tomorrow, so if some requirement only makes sense for the dead classics, say so. And a one-line verdict on which of the three standard views comes out best against the list, though dont let that eat the answer.
