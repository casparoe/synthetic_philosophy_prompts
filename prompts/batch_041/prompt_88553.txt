I am presenting a paper next month to an interdisciplinary reading group — three mathematicians (two analysts, one set theorist) and four philosophers, mostly metaphysics of time and philosophy of physics — and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. My thesis is that Thomson's lamp shows nothing about the impossibility of supertasks, and not even about indeterminacy in general: the indeterminacy comes entirely from an impoverished state space. The argument runs like this: for a binary lamp switched at t = 1 − 2^−n, the state function has no limit as t → 1, since it is never eventually constant; but replace the lamp with one whose brightness equals the fraction of elapsed time it has been on, switched on the same schedule, and the state at the limit is fixed at 2/3, because brightness is an integral of the switching history. Generalize from there: whenever the state at the limit is a continuous functional of the history, the limit exists and is determined, so what does the work is the structure of the state space rather than the completion of infinitely many tasks. Give me the objections this audience will actually raise, phrased the way an analyst, a set theorist, and a philosopher of time would each put them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft; tell me what I should concede up front; name the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss; and include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
