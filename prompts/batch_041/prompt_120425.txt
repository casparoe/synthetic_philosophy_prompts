I belong to a small reading group that has spent the term working through formal epistemology, and our last session was on Dutch book arguments: the thought that an agent whose degrees of belief don't add up as probabilities can be offered a set of bets that she accepts one at a time, on her own terms, and that leave her worse off however the world turns out. We spent the first hour on the machinery and the rest of the evening, and most of the walk home, on whether the machinery shows as much as it seems to. The window of the room we meet in looks out on a wet street, and we wasted an absurd amount of the session on whether it would rain on the way to the pub, so let the propositions in the poem be rain and no rain tomorrow. I would like to close the term with a poem that does the argument rather than describes it, and I would like it to end where we ended, which was not with a verdict.

So: write a pantoum in the voice of the bookie, who should sound courteous rather than triumphant, since she is not cheating anyone. The form's rules need to be doing the argument's work, so keep them in view: lines two and four of each quatrain return as lines one and three of the next, and in the closing quatrain the two returning lines are the opening quatrain's first and third. Write four quatrains, no more. A line that reads as a fair price at the start should read as part of a trap when it comes round again, and the last stanza, made out of the first, should be the moment the book closes. Price the two bets by the agent's own lights: sixty cents on rain tomorrow and sixty cents on no rain, so that she pays a dollar twenty to be certain of a dollar. The arithmetic should be legible from the lines themselves, so please don't give me a stanza that steps outside the poem to explain the bet to me, and don't let the poem say Dutch book, coherence, probability, or betting interpretation. A reader who knows the argument should recognize it anyway, and a reader who doesn't should still feel the trap close.

Then break the poem open. After the pantoum has closed, add a coda of four to six lines that obeys none of the form's rules and repeats nothing from the stanzas above: the bookie comes back with the same two bets as a single package, and the agent declines. She will take either one alone, she says, but not both together, or she simply does not care to gamble, or a dollar does not weigh the same to her in both directions. I want the coda's flatness to mark precisely where the argument can no longer push: the bookie has caught her in a set of prices, not in a mistake about the weather, and whether she is caught at all depends on her being the kind of person who names a price for everything and stands by it. Please don't resolve that. End with the bookie holding two bets and no buyer, and let the reader decide how much of the argument is left standing.
