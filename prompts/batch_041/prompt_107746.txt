A city of roughly 800,000 residents is weighing a cordon charge on its central area, and the headline number in the appraisal — a 12% reduction in vehicle-kilometres travelled — comes from a model whose baseline-year fit is good. The model rests on the following simplifications:

1. All travellers share a single value of time; the true distribution of values of time is wide and right-skewed.
2. Adjustment to the charge is instantaneous, with no habit persistence, no learning, and no relocation.
3. Each traveller is modelled as if solving a full equilibrium problem over route and departure time, although no traveller performs any such calculation.
4. The road network is compressed into nine aggregate corridors; local streets, junctions, and capacity bottlenecks are absent.
5. Price responsiveness is set to whatever value makes the model reproduce the baseline-year traffic counts exactly.
6. The appraisal holds fixed the bus network, the availability of ride-hailing, and the price of fuel over the whole appraisal period.

Take as your starting point the thesis associated with Milton Friedman, sometimes called the F-twist, that the realism of a theory's assumptions is not in itself a defect, since assumptions are to be judged by the predictive performance of the theory that contains them rather than by their descriptive accuracy. Then, for each simplification, (a) give a score between 0 and 1 for how much its unrealism should reduce a reader's confidence in the 12% figure, where 0 means not at all and 1 means the figure should be disregarded; (b) rank the six from most to least damaging on that dimension; and (c) justify each score in one or two sentences. If an item does not fit the question as posed, say so rather than forcing a score onto it. Finally, state the general criterion by which you separated benign from load-bearing unrealism, and say which simplification the Friedman-style defence covers most convincingly and which it covers least convincingly.
