I'm a novelist and I've written myself into a corner. My new book has a character who's sure an ant colony is a person — she names it, argues with it, grieves it when the colony collapses — and I can't write her straight until I actually beleive it myself, at least for the length of a chapter. So next month I'm leading a session at the writers' residency where I have to walk a room of fiction people, zero science background, through the question of whether an ant colony could have a mind. Don't explain it to me — plan how I should explain it.

What has to be in place before the question even makes sense to them? My hunch is that the whole thing collapses unless they first have some grip on the idea that a mind could be made of parts that each have no idea what's going on — but I don't know if that goes first or if something else does, and I definately keep getting the order wrong when I try it out loud on friends.

Then the example. I'm circling three and can't decide: the brain-and-neurons analogy; the split-colony case (you cut a colony in half, both halves survive and both keep working — did you make two minds, or kill one?); and the swap-one-ant-at-a-time case, where you replace every ant with an identical twin, one by one, and ask when the mind died, if it ever did. Pick the one that should carry the whole session, tell me what it has to have and lack to do that job, and tell me what the other two are still good for.

The misconceptions I'm bracing for, roughly in the order I think they'll arrive: that it's "just a metaphor" (sure, the colony is *like* a mind); that the colony must be conscious *because* it does clever things (I have a feeling that's backwards and I want to head it off before it calcifies); that killing the queen kills the mind; that the colony's mind is just the sum of the ants' minds; and the big one — that a colony-mind would have to think at roughly our speed.

That last one is where I want to inject my own spin, and I need you to tell me where it goes. I want them to sit with the thought that if the colony has thoughts at all, a single one of them might take a week, and that this alone could be why we can't tell whether anything's home. But I'm worried they'll just hear "so it's slow and stupid" and nod, which is the opposite of what I want.

Also: one question I can ask at the very end that tells me whether they got the shape of the problem rather than just got comfortable. And tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level — I can feel myself getting dragged toward qualia and the hard problem, and I'd rather say "not today" out loud than pretend.
