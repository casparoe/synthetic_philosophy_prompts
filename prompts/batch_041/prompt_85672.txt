Write a short story set in a near future in which suffering has been abolished by biotechnology — not anesthesia or painkillers, but an engineered hedonic architecture, heritable and universal — and told from the point of view of a welfare auditor whose job is to certify that no sentient being in her jurisdiction still experiences negative valence.

Three features of the world should do the work. The engineering is gradient-preserving: it flattens the peaks of agony far more than the capacity for attachment, so grief can be reduced but not eliminated without also degrading love, and the negative states that remain are precisely the ones bound up with caring about anything at all. The audit standard is two-tracked, comparing measured valence (from neural telemetry) against professed valence (what subjects report), and in recent years the tracks have begun to come apart. And moral status is still officially grounded in the capacity to be harmed, so as that capacity thins out, so does much of the vocabulary of rights — some citizens now hold, without any distress, that nothing done to anyone can matter.

Let the story turn on one concrete inspection: a person, or a small community, that reports no suffering whatsoever yet insists, calmly, that something has been taken. Do not resolve it with a speech, a reveal, or a verdict; let the abolitionist's achievement and the holdout's complaint both keep their force, and leave open whether the complaint names a real loss or is only the last echo of an extinct way of feeling.
