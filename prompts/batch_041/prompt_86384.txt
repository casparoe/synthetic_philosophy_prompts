I'm midway through a long feature on the revival of negative-utilitarian arguments — the family of views that give the relief of suffering priority over the production of happiness — and I've gone back to Bentham's *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (printed in 1780, first published in 1789) to see how much of the contemporary quarrel he had already settled, or quietly foreclosed. Chapter IV, "Value of a lot of pleasure or pain, how to be measured," is where I keep getting stuck, and I'd like help reading it closely. The stretch that matters to me runs:

To a person considered by himself, the value of a pleasure or pain considered by itself, will be greater or less, according to the four following circumstances: 1. Its intensity. 2. Its duration. 3. Its certainty or uncertainty. 4. Its propinquity or remoteness. [...] And one other; to wit: 7. Its extent; that is, the number of persons to whom it extends; or (in other words) who are affected by it. [...] Sum up all the values of all the pleasures on the one side, and those of all the pains on the other. The balance, if it be on the side of pleasure, will give the good tendency of the act upon the whole, with respect to the interests of that individual person; if on the side of pain, the bad tendency of it upon the whole.

The position I'm contrasting him with is the one sometimes called lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism: the claim that once suffering passes some severity it becomes lexically dominant, so that no finite quantity of mild pleasure can outweigh it, while below that threshold pleasures and pains trade off more or less as ordinary utilitarians say. So what I need is not an account of what utilitarianism is, but a careful reading of this passage with one question in view: where does it commit itself to the commensurability of pleasure and pain, where does it merely presuppose it, and could a lexical break be located inside it at all? Specifically — does defining "value" for every pleasure and every pain by the same four dimensions already make them comparable by construction, so that the threshold view is not an interpretation of the text but a denial of it? Or do intensity and duration leave room for a limiting case in which the weight of a severe pain grows without bound against any quantity of mild pleasure? And what work is the seventh circumstance doing: does the summation across persons in the final step smuggle in a further assumption — comparability between people, not just within a life — that a reader could reject while keeping Bentham's intrapersonal arithmetic intact?

I'm a journalist rather than a philosopher, so I'd rather have the passage taken apart clause by clause than be given the general history of the doctrine: tell me which of the two readings the text better supports, which clauses each side would point to as decisive, and where Bentham is arguing and where he is simply stipulating.
