ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on acausal threats and the "surrogate goal" trick, and I want a proper essay out of it, not a blog-post summary of the obvious points.

Here's the setup I actually care about. Some agent X — could be a person, could be a superintelligence, whatever — gets what looks like a credible threat from an entity Y that X can't identify, can't verify, and can't causally affect. Y says roughly: I have models or simulations of you spread across a bunch of possible worlds, and the copies of you in those worlds get punished or rewarded depending on whether the real you does what I want. There's no physical channel between X and Y; the whole leverage is that Y can predict X's policy and condition on it.

The standard defensive move is the surrogate goal. X can't make itself immune by willpower, but it can adopt a *different* goal that happens to make the threat pointless — say, a goal of never complying with an acausal threat, or a goal of maximizing some unrelated thing the threat doesn't touch. Now Y's threat has nothing to grab onto. Sounds clever. My question is whether it actually holds up under pressure.

Stuff I want you to really engage with, not just name-drop:

- whether a surrogate goal has to be genuinely adopted (whatever that even means for an agent) or whether a merely public / dispositional commitment does the same work
- what happens when Y anticipates the surrogate goal and adopts one of its own, so you get two agents bargaining over goals neither of them intrinsically has. Does that regress bottom out somewhere, or does it just spin into an arms race of ever-weirder surrogate goals?
- whether adopting a surrogate goal is itself a kind of self-corruption — you're deliberately taking on commitments you don't care about, and if the surrogate is broad enough to be robust it might drag your real behavior around in ways you never signed up for
- the asymmetry between threats and offers. Everyone talks about threats here; does the same machinery work for acausal *bribes*, and if so does the surrogate trick help or hurt?
- one case I keep flip-flopping on: a surrogate goal that is itself vulnerable to a further acausal threat — i.e. the thing you adopted to be immune is something Y can also lean on. Is that a reductio of the whole approach, or just a reason to pick your surrogate more carefully?

Don't give me the textbook "just precommit to a policy" answer, I already know that one. Weigh the actual arguments and land somewhere. A conditional verdict is fine ("adopt surrogate goals when X, not when Y") as long as you defend the condition. No citations or reading lists — I dont have web access, just reason it out.

End the whole thing with a final line that starts "Verdict:" and gives me your position in one short phrase.
