I am delivering the eulogy at the memorial service for my aunt, Vera Lindqvist, who died this spring at seventy-four. She spent the last twenty-five years of her life in a way that has left everyone who loved her quietly divided about how to speak of it. After taking early retirement from her job as a school administrator, she organized a tenants' mutual-aid network in her neighborhood, and over the years it became the thing she did: she ran it, she funded much of it out of her own pension, she took in two families whose housing had collapsed, and by any ordinary measure she was kinder and more tireless than anyone who will be in that room. Half the people coming to the service are her comrades from the network, and they will be listening for one thing — that she did what any of us ought to have done, that her life was not an exception but a demand. The other half are family: her brothers, her cousins, people who loved her and who, if I am honest, spent years feeling vaguely accused by her, because her way of living made the rest of us wonder whether we were doing enough. They will be listening for the opposite — that she was extraordinary, that what she did went beyond anything anyone could reasonably be asked to do, and that we may go home unburdened. I cannot satisfy both halves by pandering to them, and I refuse to say anything about her that is not true.

This is where I need you. Assume the role of a philosopher who is also an experienced writer of eulogies and toasts, and treat what follows as a genuine philosophical problem rather than a mere matter of tone. The question underneath the eulogy is the old one about acts that go beyond what duty requires: if something is genuinely good to do, why isn't it required — and if it isn't required, how do we honor someone for doing it without turning everyone else into a moral slacker? Conversely, if we insist her life was simply what solidarity demands, do we not convert a gift into a debt and flatten the very thing that made her remarkable? Vera had her own way of putting it. She refused to call her work a sacrifice and she refused to call it optional; when people thanked her she would say, "Nobody is owed this. It is simply mine to do." I would like the eulogy to take that strange sentence seriously rather than dissolve it, and I want you to think carefully about where the distinction between the owed and the freely given can honestly be drawn in a life like hers.

Please begin by brainstorming: in a few sentences, before writing the speech, lay out the competing ways of framing her life and the rhetorical cost of each, then say how you intend to handle the tension. After that, write the eulogy itself, roughly five minutes spoken, to be delivered to that mixed room. It must comfort people who are not philosophers without saying anything a philosopher would wince at, it must not pretend the two halves of the room already agree, and it should let her own distinction — what is owed versus what is "mine to do" — do the real structural work of the speech rather than decorate it. Adopt the voice of someone who loved her, and write prose that can actually be read aloud.
