I keep giving lopsided verdicts about whether someone did something on purpose, and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them. I will describe the cases in my own words; please do not worry about whether they match anything in the literature.

1. A pharmaceutical executive approves a drug knowing that, as a side effect, it will give a small number of patients a serious heart condition. She does not care about that at all. She approves it for the revenue, and she would approve it even if the heart risk were considerably larger. I think she harmed those patients on purpose.

2. The same executive approves a drug knowing that, as a side effect, it will cure a rare disease in a small number of patients. She does not care about that either. I do not think she cured anyone on purpose.

3. An engineer knows that his bridge design will, in a once-a-century flood, kill people downstream. He spends months searching for an alternative, fails, and builds it anyway, because the alternative route would kill more people in ordinary accidents. I still think he kills those people on purpose; the effort to avoid it does not change my verdict.

4. A conscript is ordered to fire a warning shot that she knows will permanently deafen a bystander standing nearby. Refusing means execution, and she fires. I do not think she deafened him on purpose.

5. A driver speeds through a residential street, indifferent to the risk, and his speeding startles a would-be mugger into fleeing, preventing a mugging he knew nothing about and cared nothing about. I do not think he prevented the mugging on purpose, even though he was reckless and blameworthy.

6. A physician gives a painkiller she knows will, as a side effect, hasten a dying patient's death by a few hours. She intends only the relief and acts within accepted practice. I do not think she hastened the death on purpose.

7. A corporation adopts a policy knowing that, as a side effect, it will drive a competitor out of business. No individual inside the firm intends this, and no one is pleased by it. I am inclined to say the corporation did it on purpose, but this verdict feels shakier to me than the others, and I can be argued out of it.

My tentative principle is this: an outcome counts as brought about on purpose when the agent foresaw it as certain and the outcome is bad; good outcomes count only if the agent actually cared about them, not merely foresaw them. I can already see that this principle is strained by case 3, where the agent cared about avoiding the outcome and was justified in proceeding, and by case 4, where coercion seems to do the work rather than the agent's attitudes. I suspect the real driver is not the goodness or badness of the outcome but whether I am prepared to blame the agent, though case 5 seems to cut against that.

What I want from you: state the principle, or a small ordered set of competing principles, that best captures these verdicts, and for each clause tell me which case forces it. Then say what each candidate principle predicts about a case I have not listed: an autonomous trading algorithm that foreseeably triggers a market crash, where no one intended the crash and the designers were merely careless in a way that would not support a criminal charge. Finally, tell me honestly which of my seven verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give that verdict up or keep it and accept a more complicated principle.
