I'm a junior in high school, and I'm writing a paper for my philosophy club (it may turn into my Theory of Knowledge essay) on Molyneux's problem, which I came across while reading about Locke. The question, as Molyneux put it to Locke, is whether a man born blind who has learned by touch to tell a cube from a sphere would recognize which was which by sight alone if he suddenly gained his sight, before he touched them; Locke agreed with Molyneux that he could not, on the grounds that he has no experience connecting how a thing affects his touch with how it affects his sight. I've also read that Leibniz thought he could, because extension is common to both senses, that Berkeley denied any necessary connection between the tactile and visual worlds, and that Reid drew a line between flat shapes, which he thought might transfer immediately, and solids, which would not. Then I found the modern work: Pawan Sinha's Project Prakash gave surgery to children in India who had been blind from congenital cataracts, and the striking result was that at first they could not match a shape they were shown with the same shape placed in their hands, even though they had no trouble matching colors, and that the cross-modal mapping was learned within days. This is the empirical material I want to hold in view.

What I actually have to write about, though, is H. G. Wells's story The Country of the Blind, which I first read in the version published in The Strand Magazine in 1904 and then again in the revised and expanded version from 1939, where the ending is different. Nuñez, a mountaineer, falls into an isolated valley whose inhabitants have been blind for generations and have built an entire life around touch and hearing: paths with kerbs, windowless houses, their own medicine, their own sense of what is beautiful and what is real. He arrives expecting to be king by the proverb about the one-eyed man, and instead finds that his sight makes him helpless and, worse, unintelligible; the woman he loves treats his talk of sight as a charming delusion; the village doctor concludes that his eyes are a diseased growth and proposes to remove them; he agrees out of love and then, at dawn on the day of the operation, flees into the mountains, where in the original version he lies down and does not come back and in the revised version he escapes with Medina-Saroté during a rockslide. I want a reading of the story through Molyneux's problem, and I want you to commit to a position rather than survey the possibilities. Which answer to Molyneux does Wells dramatize: is Nuñez's failure to convey sight a Lockean failure of cross-modal learning, a Berkeleian absence of any shared content between the two perceptual worlds, or something closer to Leibniz's claim that a common element is there but has to be found? Does the story's treatment of the villagers support that reading, or does Wells cheat by making them implausibly uniform and by romanticizing their compensation? And what do the empirical results do to the story — if the mapping comes within days, does that undercut the tragedy, or does it sharpen it, since Nuñez is not given days?

Give me a definite reading, with reasons, of the scenes that carry the weight: his first attempt to explain the sky and the mountains, the failure of the love story to bridge the two perceptual worlds, the doctor's diagnosis and Nuñez's near-consent to the operation, and the two endings, on which I would like you to take a side rather than note that they differ. Say clearly where the story outruns the philosophical debate and where it distorts it, and tell me what you take Wells to be getting at that Locke, Leibniz, Berkeley, Reid, and the cataract studies together do not, even if what he gets at is not a thesis any of them would accept. Please be willing to say when the reading I am proposing is a stretch; I would rather have a committed interpretation with its weak points marked than a balanced list of options, and if you think the whole Molyneux frame is the wrong lens for this story, say so and defend it.
