I'm in the fourth year of a PhD and my old supervisor S is the most consistent Bayesian I've ever met. I've been keeping a list of his verdicts for two years so I can guess what he'll say before he says it, and this month I finally need it, because something has happened in my own work.

The list. (1) Mercury's perihelion: he says the fact that the anomaly had been sitting in the tables for sixty years before General Relativity turned up doesn't stop it confirming GR, because a physicist shown the anomaly in 1900 would have raised her credence. (2) Green apples and black ravens: a green apple does confirm 'all ravens are black', by a hair. (3) A friend's puzzle about a hypothesis with an irrelevant fact tacked onto it with 'and': the observation confirms the whole conjunction, he says, and does nothing for the tacked-on half. (4) A credence of exactly zero is a trap: no evidence will ever get you out of it.

Now my case. I've spent four years on a model M. Last month a colleague proved that M entails a well-known regularity that's been in the literature since the 70s -- I've cited the paper it comes from in everything I've written, I'd just never seen that it followed from M, and neither had anyone else. So it's genuinly new information. Except that I'm already 0.99 on M, and on a bad day higher, so it can't move me at all -- there's no room left.

Does the old regularity confirm M? I care much less about S's verdict than about the rule he's running. Give me the rules that fit all four of the verdicts above, what each one predicts here, which you'd bet on and why, and what I should put to him to tell the rules apart.
