So I'm running a four-week reading group for grad students in sociology and philosophy of social science — mostly people who read the Protestant Ethic once in a seminar and think they know it, which is the problem. Week 3 is the one I care about: can culture be a cause? using Weber as the test case, and I need to plan the session rather than just show up and talk. Don't explain the thesis to me; I know it. Tell me how to teach it.

Here's what I want the session to actually land: the difference between three things that all get called "the Weber thesis" — (1) Protestant beliefs caused capitalism, a causal claim; (2) capitalist institutions, once running, selected for people with Protestant-ish dispositions, so the beliefs persisted for their economic effects, a selection/reproduction claim; and (3) the elective-affinity thing, where the beliefs and the economic form fit each other without either causing the other in any straightforward sense. My students will collapse all three into (1) within ten minutes unless the sequence stops them, and honestly a lot of published work collapses them too.

The other thing I want them to get, and this is where I think the real dependency sits: Weber's causal machinery in the methodological essays — the counterfactual test where you ask what would have happened if the religious factor had been absent, hold everything else fixed, then ask whether the remaining conditions "generally" or "adequately" produce the outcome. That's the part that makes the thesis a causal thesis at all, and it's the part nobody teaches. So the plan has to put that machinery in place *before* they can even evaluate the claim, which means the sessions order is doing real work. I also need them to see that the choice of what to hold fixed isn't innocent — the ideal type of "the spirit of capitalism" is built partly out of the investigator's values, so the counterfactual is value-laden before you even run it.
