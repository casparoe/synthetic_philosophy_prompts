I have a semester to spend on the St. Petersburg paradox, and I've grown convinced that the usual menu of fixes — bounded utility, discounting, imprecise probabilities — is arguing about the wrong thing. My working hypothesis is that the paradox is an artifact of an idealization: the assumption that a decision-maker can, in principle, aggregate infinitely many outcomes into a single expectation. If that's right, the puzzle either dissolves into a tractable question about bounded computation or turns into a different problem about what a rational agent may be required to represent at all. Granting that I'm probably wrong about something, which open questions in the surrounding literature are upstream of this hypothesis? I have in mind whether the game can be restated with no infinite aggregation anywhere; whether the bounded-utility and discounting families do real work or merely relabel the idealization; whether the infinite-ethics and Pascal-style arguments inherit the same flaw; and whether the paradox is best read as a constraint on decision rules rather than on utility functions. Rank these by how much else depends on the answer and by how tractable each is for one person over a semester, say what evidence would move the ranking, and tell me which to drop. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
