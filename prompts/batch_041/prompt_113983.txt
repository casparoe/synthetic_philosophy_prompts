I teach a graduate seminar on Nietzsche's early writings, and we have just finished the untimely meditation on history — the one that sorts our relation to the past into monumental, antiquarian and critical modes. Two students carried an argument out of the seminar room and down the corridor, and I would rather adjudicate it properly than summarise the essay for them.

As they made it:

A: Critical history is incoherent as Nietzsche sets it up. Condemning a piece of the past requires a standard, and every standard available to us was handed down by the very past we are condemning — the tribunal is staffed entirely by defendants. Call it the inheritance regress. Critical history can therefore only ever be one inherited fragment prosecuting another, so it cannot do the liberating work he assigns it; it is monumental history with the signs reversed.

B: That shows the standard is inherited, not that it cannot be operative. Genealogical circularity — my standard has a history — is not vicious circularity — I cannot act on it without contradicting myself. Nietzsche's criterion is life, not truth, and life is not a premise inside the historical argument but the activity the argument serves. And if inheritance were fatal, monumental history would be just as doomed, yet he plainly thinks monumental history works.

A: Then life is itself an inherited value. Why life rather than truth, or accuracy, or justice? That preference did not fall from the sky, and his own account of how values are transmitted should make him suspicious of it; the regress simply moves up a level. And the healthy culture he holds up, the Greeks, is exactly the kind of simplified, backlit image he accuses monumental historians of manufacturing. His meta-position is performed in the style he criticises.

B: He never claimed presuppositionlessness. Forgetting is treated in the essay as a condition of health, not a lapse; a standard that had to survive unlimited reflection before it could be used would leave nothing usable, which is a reductio of A's demand rather than of Nietzsche. The Greeks are offered as a picture of a way of living, not as evidence, and to say that his rhetoric is doing argumentative work is not yet an objection.

They want me to say who is right, and I would rather not referee from the hip. Please take the exchange move by move, rating each from clearly mistaken through defensible-but-overstated to clearly right, and say what both of them are missing — in particular about whether the three modes are three separate remedies, a typology of temperaments, or something one healthy person has to combine, and about whether critical history is meant to supply a standard at all or only to perform a severance. Keep settled exegesis apart from live disagreement: some of what they are fighting over is something anyone who has read the essay carefully would grant, and some of it is contested among people who work on Nietzsche — do not hand me a contested reading as the consensus, and mark where you are unsure.
