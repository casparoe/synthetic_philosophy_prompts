I'm a second-year PhD student writing a seminar paper for a course on non-classical logic, and I've arrived at an argument that dialetheism is internally unstable — but I suspect it's the kind of argument that will make the people in the room wince, so I'd like to know whether it survives contact with someone who actually knows the area. The argument is this. The dialetheist says that some sentences are both true and false, and insists that this does not make every sentence true. When I ask how the slide into triviality is blocked, I'm told that the standard systems keep negation doing exactly what it does in classical logic — 'not A' is true just in case A is false, and false just in case A is true — and give up only the step from 'A or B' together with 'not A' to 'B'. But this seems to me to be exactly backwards, and in a way that matters. If negation really retains its classical truth conditions, then in a genuine contradiction both A and not A are true; and a sentence and its negation both being true is precisely the situation that licenses the inference to any sentence whatever, since the truth of not A tells me A is false and the truth of A tells me A is true, and from there nothing restrains me from concluding that the moon is made of cheese. So either the dialetheist stops treating negation as classical negation — reading 'not' as an expression of denial that has no truth-functional profile, or adopting a semantics on which truth and falsity are not exclusive — or else the logic is a mere fragment of classical logic and the contradictions it admits can never actually be used in reasoning. The first option looks to me like incoherence, and the second like a change of subject. Which is it? And if there is a third option, what exactly is doing the work that I take negation's truth conditions to be doing?

To put the worry concretely: suppose my toy theory contains just the two sentences 'the patient is allergic to penicillin' and 'the patient is not allergic to penicillin', and I want to reason from it about which antibiotic to prescribe. On the story I've been told, the theory is inconsistent without being trivial, and the reason I can't infer just anything is that the disjunctive syllogism step is unavailable. But by my lights that cannot be the explanation: if 'not' has its classical meaning, the step is licensed by the truth conditions themselves, and blocking it is not weakening the logic but quietly replacing the connective. I'd be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a reading list — in particular, I'd like to know whether the dilemma I've set up is exhaustive, and if it isn't, where the seam in my reasoning actually lies.
