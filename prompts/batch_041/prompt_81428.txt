Take the two-envelope problem in its standard forms. I want your credence in a specific diagnosis of it: that every version which generates a genuine puzzle depends on an infinite quantity — either an improper, infinite-mass distribution over the amounts or a proper distribution with infinite mean — and that once the amounts are bounded the puzzle vanishes, so the problem concerns infinite expectation rather than probability or decision theory.

Two claims to check rather than assume. The 'always switch' step requires that the probability the other envelope holds twice mine, given the amount I observe, be one half for every amount I could observe; a uniform, infinite-mass measure delivers this, but no probability distribution does, since it would have to be constant along x, x/2, x/4, ... and so carry infinite mass. Yet placing the smaller amount at 2^n with probability 2^{-n}, for n = 1, 2, 3, ..., yields a normalizable distribution on which the other envelope's expected value equals the amount in mine for every amount I could see: exact indifference everywhere, without impropriety, though at the price of an infinite mean.

Give me a number for the diagnosis, decomposed into the sub-questions it rests on — whether any bounded version retains force; whether impropriety and infinite mean are one defect or two; whether the agent's ignorance of the distribution is an independent third source of paradox; and whatever else belongs — with a rough probability for each, the combination, the term the final number is most sensitive to, and what would move it. I am near 0.7; a colleague who reads the puzzle as a failure of the principle of indifference, and so as a problem about probability after all, is near 0.3, and I want to know which sub-question we most likely divide over.
