I run a small workshop that makes a machined bracket for a furniture company. Before a batch ships, we pull a sample of brackets and load-test them to failure; a bracket either clears the required load or it doesn't. Our current batch is about 10,000 brackets, and we have now tested 400 of them, drawn from across the production run. Every one cleared the load. On that basis I was ready to authorize shipment: the customer's deadline is next month, and holding the batch means losing the order.

My business partner, who reads philosophy in his spare time, has complicated this. He grants that all 400 brackets cleared the load, but says the evidence supports at least two incompatible predictions equally well. Mine is that every bracket in the batch will clear the load, whenever it is tested. His is that every bracket in the batch will clear the load if it is tested on or before 31 December of this year, and will fail if it is tested after that date. Since all 400 tests fell before 31 December, he points out, his claim fits the data exactly as well as mine does. Nor is that the end of it: he can manufacture a rival of the same shape for any cutoff date I choose — clears the load if tested before the end of March, fails after; clears the load if tested before the end of next week, fails after — and each of these accommodates our 400 results just as comfortably as my prediction does. Some units will in fact be tested after 31 December, since we keep pulling samples as the batch moves through the shop, so the predictions genuinely disagree about what will happen.

When I said that 400 out of 10,000 is a decent sample, he replied that any sampling argument already assumes the untested brackets resemble the tested ones in the relevant respect, which is the very thing in question. And when I said that the untested brackets will behave like the tested ones, he replied that this assumes the future will resemble the past, which is also the thing in question. He is not joking.

I need to decide whether to authorize shipment, and I want to be able to say why. Give me a definite verdict — ship or hold — and defend it; I would rather have a position I can argue with than a survey of considerations. Tell me what, if anything, distinguishes my prediction from my partner's rivals other than my familiarity with it, whether that distinction is principled or merely a matter of habit, and whether it is strong enough to act on. If your verdict turns on something I haven't told you, say what it is and what you would decide on the most reasonable reading of the facts.
