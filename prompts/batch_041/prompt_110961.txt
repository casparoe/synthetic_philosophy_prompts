I am trying to gauge the exposure of a deflationary account of testimonial injustice, and I want you to stress-test it rather than summarize the surrounding debate. The account runs like this. Testimonial injustice is not a distinctively epistemic wrong; it is a distributive wrong in the allocation of credibility, and the epistemic vocabulary is a redescription that adds no independent normative content. When a hearer discounts a speaker's word because of a negative identity prejudice, the speaker has been denied a share of a scarce social good — credibility — to which she was entitled, and that denial exhausts the wrong. Nothing further is owed to the thought that she is wronged specifically in her capacity as a knower.

To keep this from collapsing into a verbal dispute, take a hearer whose credibility judgments are reliably calibrated: across many encounters she ends up assigning credibility in a way that tracks speakers' actual reliability, but the calibration is achieved through group-level prejudice that happens to be statistically accurate in her environment. By the distributive standard she treats the speaker fairly; by the distinctively epistemic standard something is still amiss. I want the account judged against cases of this shape.

Tell me what would count against it. Begin with the observations, cases, and arguments that would show the distributive reading leaves part of the wrong unaccounted for, and say for each whether a defender can absorb it by redescription or must treat it as a genuine counterexample. Then turn to the internal costs: the account appears committed to the claim that credibility is a distributable good, so say what follows if that claim fails — does the view collapse into its rival, or can it retreat without losing its distinctness? Then address the insulation worry directly: give the strongest case that the account is unfalsifiable, and then say whether that is a defect here or simply the mark of a normative thesis. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, separate what the account's own defenders should concede from what only its critics would press, and note what the account predicts that its rival does not. Close by naming the single consideration you would find most decisive if you held the account. Answer in American English.
