I am in a recurring seminar on the moral status of artificial systems, and we have reached an impasse that is starting to feel structural rather than merely unresolved. On one side are people who hold that if a system implements the right kind of computation—whatever that turns out to be—then its physical substrate is morally irrelevant; a silicon system running the same program as a suffering brain would matter just as much. On the other side are people who hold that this cannot be right: that computation is a description we impose, that the same physical dynamics can be described as implementing indefinitely many different computations, and that sentience depends on facts about the system that a computational description leaves out. We have spent weeks trading the same examples—simulated neurons, lookup tables, language models, thermostats—and nobody has moved. One idea that keeps surfacing is that implementation is observer-relative or at least underdetermined, so that before we can ask which computations matter morally we would need a principled account of which computational description a system really has. I suspect the disagreement is not really about whether machines can be conscious, but about which computations count as the morally relevant ones and who gets to say what a system is computing in the first place. Is that a fair diagnosis, or is the impasse better explained as a verbal dispute about the word sentience, a hidden empirical disagreement about the mechanisms of consciousness, a disagreement about which cases count as evidence, or two groups answering different questions—one about moral status, one about phenomenal experience—under a single heading?

I would like you to act as a philosopher of mind and cognitive science with expertise in computational theories of cognition and the ethics of moral status. Before you answer, brainstorm at least four candidate diagnoses of the impasse: for example, a factual disagreement, a conceptual or verbal one, a disagreement about methodology or evidence, a disagreement about the attribution of computation, and a disagreement about which question is being asked. Then choose the diagnosis you think is most defensible and argue for it using what each side would actually say, not a survey of positions. Be concrete about the role of computational attribution: if the same physical system can be described as implementing many different computations, does that make the question of which computations matter morally ill-posed, or does it merely raise the bar for a theory of implementation? Distinguish where useful between phenomenal consciousness, access consciousness, affective valence, and agency, since I suspect sentience is being used as a package term and that may be doing hidden work.

Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. Would it be a formal constraint on computational implementation, a new empirical result about the neural correlates or causal structure of valenced experience, a distinction between questions, or a decision to treat the moral question as prior to the metaphysical one? If you think no progress should be expected, say so and explain why. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the structure of the disagreement and an honest assessment of whether it is substantive, verbal, or simply hard. Think carefully and make your reasoning explicit.
