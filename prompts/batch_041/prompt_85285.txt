I'm giving the commencement address at my old college in June and I'm stuck. It's a tiny place — the whole graduating class is like forty kids — and I came out of their art-and-philosophy program, which is why they asked me. Ten minutes, outdoor tent, everybody's parents and grandparents there, plus the faculty, who are the only people in the room who'll have read any philosophy. What I actually want to say is the thing I believe, which I got from G. E. Moore: that the best things we know of are the love of people and the enjoyment of beautiful things, and that these aren't extras tacked onto a life, they're the point of it. I also like his stubborn idea that a beautiful world would be good even if there were nobody around to see it, though I have no idea how to get that past a tent full of grandparents without sounding like I've lost it. And I want to use his point about wholes: that a friendship, or a life spent looking at paintings with someone you love, is worth more than the two people's separate pleasures added up — the whole is its own thing, not a sum. (I dont want to quote him or cite him, just use the ideas; I can't have this turn into a lecture.)

The hard part is the audience. Half of them want to hear something they can repeat to relatives — work hard, be kind, you'll figure it out — and the other half, the faculty especially, will notice immediately if I water the philosophy down into inspirational mush or get it subtly wrong. The usual commencement moves are off the table for me: I can't tell them the future is theirs, or that they should follow their passion, or that their friendships will be valuable because of the support network, or whatever. Moore's view is impersonal — the good is the good whether or not it happens to make you happy — and its not a happiness-maximizing view either, so I can't just tell them to do what feels good. And yet it's supposed to be a warm occasion, not a seminar. Can you write the address? Moore's views on beauty and friendship should be doing the structural work — deciding what I can and can't say, what the speech is allowed to promise them — rather than just getting name-dropped in the middle. Aim for about ten minutes spoken.
