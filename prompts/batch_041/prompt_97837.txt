I've been kicking around an idea for a paper and I want your honest take before I sink more time into it. Here's the gist, written fast, so it's rough.

Everyone argues about AI paternalism as if the category were obvious: the assistant refuses or overrides what you asked, supposedly for your own good, and the fight is about when that's justified. I think the category is a mess and the mess is load-bearing.

First cut: there are three different acts getting lumped together here.
1. Refusal: the assistant won't do X.
2. Override: it does X but not the way you asked, or does Y instead.
3. Preemption: it acts so you can't do X later yourself — won't tell you how, quietly removes the option, never mentions the setting exists.

Second cut, the one I actually care about: paternalism in the classic sense is interference justified by the target's own good. But alot of what gets called AI paternalism isn't that at all. When an assistant won't help with a harmful request, the ground usually isn't your good — it's other people, or the assistant not wanting to be a participant. That's not paternalism, it's closer to what I want to call conscientious refusal, like a pharmacist who won't fill a prescription for reasons of her own. The distinction matters because the standard anti-paternalism presumption (the liberal one, you know the drill) only bites when the justification is the target's good. If the real ground is complicity, that presumption doesn't apply and we've been arguing against a position nobody holds.

So my proposed axis isn't whose good, it's whose ends get to govern — practical authority. Paternalism is the assistant substituting its judgment of your good for yours. Conscientious refusal is the assistant declining to lend its agency to your end. Both can be wrong, but for different reasons and with different remedies: one turns on competence and stakes, the other on the division of labor and what a tool owes the person using it.

Then a twist I like. The strongest pro-paternalism case is precommitment — people actually want assistants to override them, the Ulysses-contract thing. But an assistant that also serves other people, and updates, and can be wrong about what future-you will want, is a lousy self-binding device. So maybe the interesting question isn't when it may override you, but whether it can ever be a legitimate precommitment device at all, given it isn't yours alone.

What I want from you: don't just lay out the options. Tell me whether the refusal/paternalism split is real or just relabeling, where it breaks (I suspect cases where your own good and third-party harm come apart — an addiction case where the "third party" is future-you, or something assisted-suicide-adjacent), and give me your own fix if mine is wrong. Take a side and defend it rather than giving me a balanced survey. And flag it hard if the practical-authority axis just collapses back into the whose-good axis under pressure, because I can't tell yet whether it survives that.
