I am enrolled in a political theory seminar that keeps circling back to a question I find genuinely unsettled, and I would like an essay that takes a stand on it. The question is whether corruption names a single moral kind or is instead a parochial category that Western institutions, donors, and governance indices have exported as a supposedly universal standard. The familiar exchange goes roughly as follows: one side holds that bribery, patronage, and clientelism corrode impartial institutions wherever they appear, so the universalist measurement project is legitimate; the other side holds that gift-giving, reciprocal obligation, and constituency service are embedded in social relations that Western frameworks misread, so the universalist framing is a moralized projection onto other people's obligations. I want the essay to move past that exchange rather than rehearse it, and I want it to reach a verdict rather than adjudicate politely.

To force the issue, please organize the argument around two hypotheticals I have been using to test my own intuitions. In the first, a municipality expects its elected officials to find jobs and expedite permits for their constituents, in a society where failing to do so reads as a betrayal of kinship; a reformer arrives with a merit-based civil service and denounces the practice as corruption. In the second, the very same practice persists in a place where nearly everyone privately believes it is wrong, condemns it in the abstract, and continues to participate only because no one can defect unilaterally without being frozen out — a coordination trap. I take the second case to embarrass the simple relativist move, since the participants themselves do not experience the practice as legitimate, and the first to embarrass the simple universalist move, since the practice there answers to a felt obligation rather than to private gain.

Use these cases to sort out what the disagreement is actually about: whether the operative notion is a moral failing of persons or a property of institutions; whether corruption is defined relative to some baseline of how the institution ought to work, so that the same act can be corrosive in one setting and innocent in another; and whether such a baseline can be specified without smuggling in a specifically Western ideal of impartial bureaucracy. Then defend a conclusion about whether a single trans-cultural standard of corruption is defensible, and if so, exactly what it would be a standard of — and do not end on the fence.
