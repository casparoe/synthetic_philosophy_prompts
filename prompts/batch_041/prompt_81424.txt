Over the weekend a friend and I argued, at increasing volume, about whether any of the translation systems we use daily understands anything at all. His position is that the Hofstadter-style complaint — that a translator has to work out what the author was driving at, and so has to decide what the original would have been had it been written in the target language — is mysticism dressed up as an argument, and that once a system reliably makes the same choices a good translator makes about idiom, register, implicature, and which ambiguities to leave standing, there is no further fact of the matter left to be right about. Next week I am recording an interview with a researcher who argues the opposite: that these systems translate sentences rather than texts, and that whatever interpretive competence they display is borrowed — from the human translators whose work trained them, and from the reader who supplies the context the system never represents. Please draft eight to ten questions for me.

I want them to press on the actual joints of her position rather than let her restate it. Include at least one question each on: (a) what the difference between translating a sentence and translating a text is supposed to amount to, and how she would detect it in a system's behavior; (b) whether the understanding she denies the system is withheld from the system, from the training pipeline, or from nobody in particular — and what becomes of her view if the honest answer is the pipeline; and (c) what evidence would count against her, given that the standard benchmarks are least reliable on precisely the cases where interpretation is forced: poems and puns, texts whose point is a formal constraint, and source sentences that leave unspecified something the target language obliges the translator to decide, such as politeness level, gender, or number.

Please also build one question around this case: two systems return identical, equally acceptable translations of a whole novel, but only one of them contains anything representing what the book is doing — its irony, its pacing, the fact that the narrator is unreliable. What could possibly distinguish them, and if nothing could, is the disagreement between her and my friend a disagreement about a fact or about how a word ought to be used? Finally, include one question that turns the tables on my friend, pressing the behavioral criterion to say what would have to be true of a system before the word "understand" became out of place.

For every question, add the follow-up I should have ready for the answer she is most likely to give.
