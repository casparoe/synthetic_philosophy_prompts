I teach a ninety-minute session on the timing of conscious experience to first-year cognitive science graduate students — experimentally literate, but with no background in philosophy of mind. The target is the color phi phenomenon and the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction it provokes: the two ways of describing what happens when a subject's report seems to outrun the stimulus, and why no further measurement of the subject appears to settle which description is right. Plan the session rather than the content. I need the order in which the pieces have to come, and why each depends on the one before it, so that the distinction feels forced rather than stipulated; the case that should carry the whole thing and what it must have and lack; the misconceptions the students will bring and the ones they will form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; and the single question I should put at the end that tells me whether they have understood the problem rather than learned two labels. Two further constraints. I want to carry the session with a case from outside perception — a committee whose minutes describe a decision that, on one telling, was reached before the minutes were written and, on another, came into existence only as they were written — so tell me whether that case can bear the weight, what it would need to have and lack to do so, and precisely where it will mislead; if it cannot carry it, give me the case that would instead. And I want the students to leave with a third possibility beyond the two labels, that there may be no fact of the matter about when the experience occurred: say where in the sequence it becomes available, what distinguishes it from the two labels, and what it would take to refute it. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level, and say so out loud in the room.
