I want a parody advice column for my dataset. Deadpan all the way through, no winking.

The setup: the column is called "Ask Aunt Aggregate" and it runs in a made-up magazine, The Household Ledger. Aunt Aggregate is a sincere, warm, competent advice-giver whose entire shtick is that she won't treat the members of a family as seperate persons -- she talks about the household as one single utility-bearing organism with one big ledger, and she answers ordinary domestic problems by running the sums. The old complaint that this kind of ethics doesn't take the boundries between people seriously is basically the thing being satirized, but the joke has to come from her being consistent and not stupid. She's not a strawman, she's just genuinely committed to the idea that the line between you and me is a bookkeeping convention.

Format it like a real column: a motto under the header, a short italic note from the editor, then three reader letters with her replies, then a sign-off. Around 600-700 words.

Ingredients you can use or skip, but if you use them stay inside her logic:
- her unit of account is the "agg-day," and she thinks in household totals, never per person -- she'll sometimes slip into annual-report language, e.g. the household "posted a surplus last quarter, largely on the strength of the husband's commute"
- the compensation problem: when the sacrifice lands on one person, the consolation she offers is gratitude, or recognition that arrives after they're gone, and she insists gratitude is a real line item
- a household insurance product that lets you choose your family before you're born, which she offers as the fix for unfair sacrifice and which she's genuinely puzzled to find nobody can buy
- she'll spend one person's whole stock of agg-days to buy tiny gains for alot of other people, and she treats "but that's me you're spending" as sentimentality

The letters should be mundane and recognizable: a woman whose husband is miserable while the kids flourish and who asks if that's fine; a guy being pressured to give a kidney to a cousin he barely knows; someone whose mother-in-law wants to move in. Three of them, your pick.

The editor's note is the only place the counter-argument gets to breathe -- the editor can note that readers keep writing in to say they are, in fact, distinct people. Aunt Aggregate's reply to that should be one line at most, and the piece shouldn't resolve the disagreement; the discomfort is the point. Also don't let her hedge into "well, each person's life is its own thing" -- the moment she does, the joke collapses.

No real philosophers, books, or citations anywhere -- nothing a reader would have to look up. It should stand entirely on its own.
