I'm convening a session of a graduate seminar in comparative cognition, and two of my colleagues have now spent a full term talking past each other about the familiar four-rung ladder of minds — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian. Roughly: creatures whose competence is fixed by selection and paid for across generations; creatures that try things out in the world and keep what works; creatures that can run the trial off-line, so that their hypotheses rather than their bodies bear the cost of error; and creatures whose competence is amplified by culturally transmitted tools, from numerals and notation to external devices. Colleague A holds that the step from the second rung to the third is a difference in kind — there is a distinct mechanism in play, an inner environment, and a creature either has one or it does not. Colleague B holds that it is a difference in degree along a single axis, namely how far the selection of an action has been decoupled from the consequences of that action, and that the same associative machinery which produces Skinnerian behaviour produces Popperian behaviour once it is allowed to run in the absence of the stimulus. Neither has ever managed to state the other's position in a way the other would accept. I want you to act as the philosopher of cognitive science the two of them should have hired: someone fluent in comparative psychology and in reinforcement learning, and practiced at drafting documents that hostile parties can sign. Think the problem through carefully before you write, and work out privately which of the apparent disagreements are merely terminological; do not show that preparatory work.

What I need is the joint statement they could both put their names to — not a verdict on who is right, not a dramatized dialogue, and not a diagnosis of why the debate is stuck. Open with a numbered agreed-claims section: what both accept about the Darwinian rung; about the ladder being a set of modes an organism can occupy rather than a taxonomy of species, with single animals displaying several rungs; about the fact that one behavioural profile can be produced by more than one mechanism, so that no test battery alone settles which mode is in play; about the one thing that genuinely does increase as you climb, if anything does; and about what the ladder is not a theory of. Then give me the residual disagreements as a table, with a row for each, and these columns: the question at issue; the formulation of that question which each side would accept as fair to its own position, written so that neither would disown it; the crux, meaning the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; the test, argument, or case that both sides agree would bear on that crux; and then each side's prediction about it, recorded in advance. I want at least one row on the Skinnerian–Popperian boundary and at least one on the Gregorian rung. Two things to build in. First, a thought experiment one of my colleagues keeps raising: a creature that is, by stipulation, nothing but an enormous stored disposition table — no model, no inner environment — yet which matches the best Popperian performers on planning, transfer, and one-shot revaluation, simply because the table is vast and was filled in by a long history of reinforcement; I want both parties to say what it does and does not show, and to disagree about it if they really do. Second, for the Gregorian row, a test both can endorse: whether a competence acquired with a tool survives the removal of the tool, and whether any loss is mere retrieval cost or evidence that the tool was doing part of the computation. If any row turns out to be verbal rather than substantive, say so inside that row and say why. I don't want a literature review or a reading list; argue from the ideas themselves. Keep the whole document under 900 words, with no more than five rows in the table, and let the agreed-claims section do most of the work — the shorter and less hedged it is, the more useful it will be to us.
