I chair a nine-member professional standards committee — six practitioners, three lay members — that meets fortnightly for ninety minutes and decides whether a member's conduct breached our charter. Members judge three criteria one by one and then judge the single overall question of breach; the charter requires a two-thirds majority on every question, and the written decision must state which criterion was decisive. Two appeals have already been allowed on the ground that the reasons we published did not match the pattern of votes, and the committee has begun to notice that it can endorse every criterion while rejecting the overall finding, or the reverse: our charter describes the criteria as matters to be considered rather than as necessary conditions, so the two verdicts can come apart. I know this structure has a name in the literature. I do not want the literature; I want something the chair can run live in the room.

Please give me a decision procedure: the questions to put in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, when the arithmetic decides and when the chair must rule as a matter of judgment, and how to record the reasons so that they survive appeal. Run it on two cases — one in which each criterion attracts seven votes but only three members support the overall finding, and one in which the overall finding passes with six votes while one criterion fails — and then fold in the wrinkle we actually face, that the lay members say they cannot judge the technical criterion and wish to abstain on it while still voting on the overall question. Tell me where the procedure misfires, what it cannot do, and in particular whether it can avoid binding a minority of four to reasons they reject.

No citations or reading lists; if you rely on a named result, explain it in your own words. Say how confident you are in the procedure and name the single step you are least sure of.
