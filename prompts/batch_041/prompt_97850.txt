My partner and I moved back to the States six months ago after four years in Tokyo, and a dumb thing I did at a dinner party has turned into a running argument between two of our friends, and honestly I cant tell who's winning.

Here's what happened: I made a joke about our host's cooking that died on arrival — the whole table went quiet and I watched her face change. My instinct was to pull her aside, apologize for the remark, and explain what I'd actually meant. Our friend Dana thinks that's exactly backwards. She says the problem was never the joke, it was me — I let everyone see the kind of person I am, and no amount of explaining undoes that; what undoes it is sitting with how I've diminished myself in their eyes and letting them watch me do it. My partner Sam said that's the road to ruin: once you make it about the self, people just hide, deny, and lash out, and nothing actually gets repaired — guilt is better because it's about the act, which you can fix. Dana shot back that guilt is a machine for letting yourself off the hook: you apologize for "the joke," keep your precious self-image intact, and learn nothing about who you are. Sam said shame is the one that lets you off the hook, since it depends entirely on who happens to be watching — a private wrong nobody sees costs you nothing, which is morally arbitrary. Dana said that's the point, shame is enforced by other people so it actually works, whereas guilt lets you be the judge in your own case. Then Sam said something I'm still chewing on: fine, maybe shame keeps people in line better, but you can't just decide as a culture to be a shame culture, these things come attached to a whole social structure you'd have to rebuild first. Dana said people choose all the time, that's what institutions are for.

I stayed out of it. Can you go through the exchange and say who's right at each step? Give me a table with columns: Move number, Who made it, Rating (clearly mistaken / questionable / defensible / clearly right), and Why (one or two sentences). Then after the table, a short overall verdict: who's more right, and what both of them are missing.
