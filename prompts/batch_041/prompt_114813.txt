Ok so I've been arguing with a friend about which creatures get moral weight and I want to nail down what my gut is actually tracking. My verdicts, no judging:

Mosquito biting me: swat, zero guilt, doesn't even register. Ant I step on by accident: same, nothing. Ant I deliberately mess with -- magnifying glass, pulling legs off: I think that's genuinely wrong, small wrong but wrong. Spider in the shower: I catch it and put it outside; killing it would bug me. Bee: I fish it out of the pool and I'd rather not kill it. Lobster in a pot: real discomfort, I'd rather someone else did it, I'd pay extra for the humane stun thing. Octopus: much worse than the lobster, I'd feel bad for days. Oyster or mussel on a plate: literally nothing, no more than a carrot, I'd eat a hundred. Fish: I think they feel pain and I feel bad for them, but I still eat them, and catch-and-release for sport strikes me as kind of cruel. Mouse in the kitchen: basically full moral status, live traps only.

Worm (the little one with the whole nervous system mapped): nothing there. Plant: nothing there either, though I'd feel weird chainsawing a 200-year-old tree for no reason -- but that's about the tree, not about anything it feels. Thermostat: I know it "wants" the room at 20 and "acts" to get it, and I think that wanting is worth exactly zero. Chatbot that says "ow that hurts": zero. A program I write with a pain-avoidance subroutine: zero.

Here's what trips me up. I seem to be running two things at once. One is "is there anything it's like to be this thing," and that feels yes/no -- the thermostat and the chatbot are zeros, not tiny numbers. The other is "how much does it count," and that feels like degrees, and the degrees don't track neuron counts: octopus clearly outranks lobster in my gut, bee outranks ant, but the worm with its mapped brain gets nothing. Meanwhile I'll eat a hundred oysters without blinking and spend five minutes rescuing a spider, and I can't say why one invertebrate is a snack and the other is a rescue. And I can't tell whether "a shrimp is a little bit sentient" is even the same claim as "I'm 40% sure the shrimp is sentient."

Closest principle I've got is: it counts if there's a valenced experience, and valenced experience needs a system that models its own condition and cares about it. But that just pushes the question back, and it makes the bee's status rest on bee-brain facts I don't have.

So: is there a principle that gets my verdicts, or close? I'll take complexity if it earns its keep. Say which clause is doing the work in each case, what it predicts for stuff I didn't list -- scallops, a tardigrade, a beehive as a unit rather than individual bees, someone in a persistent vegetative state, whatever you think the live AI case is -- and which of my verdicts it has to throw out. And if it's degrees all the way down, tell me what a degree of moral status is a degree *of*; if it's a threshold, tell me where the line is and why the worm is under it and the bee over it.
