The question: "The utilitarian evaluates outcomes by adding up well-being across persons, and the standard complaint is that this fails to take seriously the separateness of persons. (a) State the objection as precisely as you can: what exactly does it accuse the utilitarian of doing? (b) A village of ten households wants a park, and the park's total benefit exceeds its total cost. Under Scheme A, the same household is charged the whole cost of the park, by a special levy of a fixed amount, every year for ten years, and in each of those years it is worse off than it would have been with no park at all; the other nine households pay nothing. Under Scheme B, the same park is paid for in the same way, except that the household charged the levy changes each year on a fixed rota, so that over the ten years every household pays exactly once and is, in that one year, worse off than it would have been with no park at all. A utilitarian says the two schemes are equally justified, since aggregate benefit minus cost comes out the same over the decade. Does the separateness-of-persons objection distinguish Scheme A from Scheme B? If it does, say exactly what the difference is; if it doesn't, say what someone who presses the objection should say about a scheme like B. (c) If the objection is sound, which theories does it actually tell against — only utilitarianism, or a wider class? And give the strongest reply you can on the utilitarian's behalf, and say whether you think it works."

The answer: "The utilitarian adds up each person's welfare and picks the outcome with the biggest total, which means a loss to one person can always be cancelled out by a big enough gain to somebody else. The objection says this treats the line between persons as though it were like the line between two moments in one life. If I take a small hit now for a big payoff later, that's just me arranging my own life; if you take a hit so that I can have the payoff, something different has happened, because my payoff is not your payoff. Utilitarianism can't register that difference, and that's what the objection accuses it of — collapsing distinct people into one ledger. Applied to the village, Scheme A is the paradigm case: one household is singled out and made worse off for the good of the rest, and the only thing that justifies it is the size of the total, which is exactly what the objection says is not a justification. Scheme B is different. Nobody is singled out, the levy moves around, everyone gets a turn and everyone ends up with the park, so over the decade each household is both a payer and a beneficiary. Someone who knew they would live through the whole rota would agree to B in advance, and a scheme that everyone can agree to in advance can't be one where someone is being used for other people's ends. So the objection bites on A and not on B. On scope, I think the objection is really an objection to the hedonic calculus rather than to consequentialism. A rule-consequentialist asks which rules maximise welfare rather than which act does, and a prioritarian gives extra weight to the worst off instead of just adding welfare up; neither of them is adding up pleasures and pains across people in the way the objection is complaining about, so neither is touched by it. What is left is a problem for classical utilitarianism specifically. The utilitarian has one good reply, which is that refusing to add up leaves you unable to justify anything that costs anyone anything — taxation for roads, a rescue that saves five and loses one. If the objector is going to rule all of that out, they need to say what the alternative is. I don't think that reply fully works, because it only shows that the alternative is inconvenient, not that the sum is what makes the sacrifice permissible. The objection also needs the premise that persons are completely separate from one another, with no relations that make them into anything more than a collection, and that is a heavy metaphysical assumption to rest the whole argument on."
