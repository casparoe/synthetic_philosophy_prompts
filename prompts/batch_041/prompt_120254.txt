I'm a software engineer, and I've noticed a pattern in how my colleagues and I talk about knowledge that I can't justify philosophically. We treat the behavior of code we wrote ourselves as something we can know with certainty: we say we have proved a function correct, we treat a type checker or a model checker as if it settled the matter, and we treat the fact that we built a system as a reason for confidence that goes beyond any amount of testing. But for anything we did not build — a third-party API, a compiler, the hardware, the network, the users — we fall back on statistics, monitoring, and confidence intervals, and we would never say we know how it behaves, only that we have observed it behave in certain ways so far. There is a related habit: we treat a program whose source we can read as transparent, and a compiled binary or a trained neural network as opaque, even when the opaque thing came out of our own training run.

I have read that Hobbes held something like this about geometry: that geometry is the one science we can demonstrate with certainty because we draw the figures ourselves, whereas our knowledge of nature, which we did not make, can only be conjectural, and that a commonwealth, being something we make, is knowable in the same way. I think he also held that reasoning just is a kind of computation over words or symbols, so that a demonstration is a mechanical procedure rather than a grasp of some mind-independent structure. I want to know what my own practice presupposes. Does the way we distribute certainty between constructed and unconstructed systems presuppose a constructivist view of knowledge — that making something is what gives us knowledge of it, or that there is a special kind of certainty available only for artifacts? Does it presuppose that reasoning is computation over symbols, such that a proof or a type check confers certainty because it is a formal procedure? Does it presuppose a sharp line between the formal and necessary on one side and the empirical and contingent on the other, or is that line merely a matter of tractability, cost, and where we happen to have tools? Which of these does the practice strictly require, and which does it merely fit comfortably with?

I am also bothered by an apparent inconsistency. If the principle were really that we know what we make, the boundary would not fall where it does: our code runs on hardware and compilers we did not make, calls libraries we did not write, and depends on users and networks we cannot control, yet we treat our own layer as the certain one and everything below and around it as empirical. Conversely, we train a model ourselves and then treat it as a black box. So either the principle is not really about making, or the practice applies it selectively for pragmatic reasons. Tell me which. Please reconstruct the implicit theory of knowledge this practice commits us to, say which parts are genuine presuppositions and which are just convenient, say whether the parts are consistent with one another, and tell me what would have to change in engineering practice if we replaced the maker's-knowledge picture with a straightforwardly empirical or testimonial one — for instance, if we held that all knowledge of software is ultimately a posteriori and social, and that a proof is just another kind of evidence. I would also like to know whether an engineer who is a thoroughgoing empiricist, or who thinks mathematical knowledge is nothing special, can hold these practices without incoherence.
