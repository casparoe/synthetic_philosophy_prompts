In our ethics unit we spent two lessons on Kant's short essay about the murderer at the door — the case in which a would-be killer asks me whether my friend is hiding in my house, and Kant holds that I must not answer falsely even then. I would like to keep both halves of his position: the reason he gives for the prohibition, and the prohibition itself. A classmate and I wrote out the following argument on the board during a free period, and I can no longer see where it fails; every step still looks to me like something I would defend.

(1) Kant's ground for the prohibition is not that a lie injures the particular person addressed. It is that every false declaration injures humanity at large: it makes declarations in general less trustworthy, and with them the agreements, promises and testimony on which the whole order of right depends.

(2) If that is the ground, then the wrongness of a lie consists in the amount of untrustworthiness it puts into the world — in how far it makes hearers unable to rely on what is said.

(3) The lie I tell the murderer puts almost no untrustworthiness into the world. It reaches exactly one hearer, who will never again be in a position to rely on my word and whose reliance matters to no one else; no third party learns of it; and a single false statement made under threat does not measurably lower anyone's expectation that statements are generally reliable.

(4) So by Kant's own ground, my lie here does almost no injury to humanity at large, while the alternative is my friend's death. The lie is therefore at worst a trivial wrong and at best required.

(5) Nothing in (2) to (4) depends on the hearer being a murderer. The same measure applies whenever a false statement reaches only people who will not rely on me and adds nothing to anyone's general expectation of reliability.

(6) So the same reasoning licenses the polite exaggeration, the false excuse that spares a friend's feelings, the invented reason that gets me out of an obligation I dislike, and the evasion that keeps a third party out of trouble.

(7) A ground that permits nearly every lie a decent person might want to tell cannot be the ground of an unconditional prohibition. Either Kant's reason for the prohibition is not his real reason, or the prohibition was never unconditional — and in neither case does his conclusion about the murderer follow from the reason he gives for it.

Our teacher says I have to choose between the reason and the conclusion, and I would rather keep both. Please tell me which numbered step I should give up, why that step looked so obviously right when we wrote it out, and what it costs me to abandon it. If instead you think the argument is sound and that the fault lies in one of Kant's own claims rather than in our reconstruction, say which claim, and what the rest of his position looks like without it. What I do not want is to be told that the whole matter is just a question of how much weight to give to consequences; if that is the answer, I need to see exactly where it enters the argument.
