In my history and philosophy of science reading group, we recently discussed a research programme — suppose, schematically, a bold and specific prediction made a decade ago that three well-powered attempts have now failed to confirm. One of the senior students waved the whole thing away with a line I have been hearing since I began graduate school: "There is no such thing as a crucial experiment." The implication was that a failed prediction leaves the programme exactly where it was, and that its defenders need not abandon anything or even revise anything.

What I want is a menu of readings, not a single verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered roughly from the trivial to the sweeping. At a minimum, I would like to see readings on which it is (a) a claim about the logic of confirmation — the familiar point that no hypothesis is ever tested in isolation, so that a failed prediction never logically compels the rejection of any particular claim; (b) a claim about the history of science, that no single experiment has ever in fact settled a dispute at the time it was performed; (c) a methodological claim about what it is rational for scientists to do with a failed prediction; (d) a claim about how theories actually die, namely by attrition, generational turnover, or social process rather than by evidence; and (e) a claim about the very notion of an experiment's being "crucial," that is, that the concept is incoherent rather than merely never realized.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a contentious one, or false as stated, and — most importantly — whether it would actually license the use to which the line was put in my reading group. My suspicion is that several of the readings are true but far too weak to do that work, and that one of them is what people really mean. Also say which reading is most likely being invoked when the sentence is used to deflect the question of whether a research programme should be abandoned, and whether that reading survives contact with the rest of the framework it is usually borrowed from — the Lakatosian one, with its hard core, protective belt, and progressive versus degenerating problem-shifts. If that framework's own criterion of progress is the generation of novel predictions that subsequently get confirmed, what work is left for a slogan about crucial experiments? Does the slogan amount to a methodological thesis, or to a license never to give anything up?

Please write in American English, and organize the answer as a list of readings, each with a verdict and a short justification attached to it.
