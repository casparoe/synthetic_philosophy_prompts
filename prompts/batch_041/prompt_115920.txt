Ok so I've got to put together a position statement for my philosophy of psychiatry reading group and I'd rather find the holes in it myself first. This is where I've landed on psychiatric classification — I want you to tell me whether it actually hangs together.

(i) I'm with Hacking on looping effects, and I take them to go deep. The idea in my own words: psychiatric categories are interactive kinds, so people who get classified find out about the label, the label changes how they understand themselves and behave, that changes what the category ends up containing, which changes the next round of classification. And crucially I don't think this only affects how people describe their symptoms — it changes which symptoms they actually have, so the symptom set itself drifts across the decades.

(ii) Because of (i) there's no value-free classification in psychiatry. The categories are partly made rather than found.

(iii) All the same I'm a realist about at least some of it — say the psychotic disorders. There's a fact of the matter about whether someone has one, and it doesn't depend on our values or on which edition of the manual is current.

(iv) In the clinic I take the patient's own account as the primary evidence: if the first-person story and the checklist disagree, the story wins. And when someone turns up having read about a diagnosis and says they recognise themselves in it, I count that recognition as evidence for the diagnosis rather than as something the diagnosis produced.

(v) I'm also pretty deflationary about diagnostic validity. Reliability — different clinicians, different days, same verdict — is the only epistemic virtue a psychiatric classification can really have, and validity talk is mostly a holdover from when people thought these were natural kinds.

(vi) I don't think looping gets much grip on the diagnoses we make in early childhood. A five-year-old can't take up a label about themselves, so the loop has nothing to run on.
