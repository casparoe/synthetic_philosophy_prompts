I'm a backend dev with no philosophy training whatsoever, so this is either a stupid question or a very old one, and either way I'd like to know which. I've been reading biographies as a sort of self-improvement project — Plutarch, then a pile of medieval saints' lives, then the modern tech-founder memoir sort of thing — and I keep hitting the same wall. I've got a feeling its a real phenomenon and not just me being lazy.

The wall is this. The bits I can actually learn from are always the bits where the person wobbles. Augustine nicking pears as a kid, or asking to be made good and then basically asking for it to happen later on — that I can do something with. But when the virtue is presented as just what the person is, it slides straight off me, and the more admirable the life is, the faster it slides. The saints' lives are the purest case: the entire point of writing them down is that you're meant to imitate the saint, and yet half of them have the subject already holy as a child, or holy by grace rather than by anything they did. There's nothing to copy. It's an advert, not a manual.

Fix that, though, and you get the opposite problem. Keep all the wobble in — the warts-and-all biography, the founder who admits he was a mess for ten years — and now I've got something copyable and also a perfectly good excuse not to copy it: he had money, no kids, and was in the right place at the right time. So the perfection that makes a life worth admiring looks like exactly the thing that makes it useless as a guide, and the imperfection that makes it usable takes away the reason to bother.

I did notice one trick that seems to dodge this, and I'd like to know if that has a name too. If you show the person being bad first and good later, the early version of them is the manual and the later version is the advert, and I get both. Plutarch says somewhere that he isn't writing history, he's writing lives, which I assume is him going for the same thing.

So: is there a name for the property a life has when it can be learnt from rather than merely admired, and a name for that conversion-arc trick? I don't want the label on its own. If there's no exact term, I'd much rather you said so and then defined the thing properly — what it is, what it isn't, and the awkward cases (a life that's admirable but not learnable, one that's learnable but not admirable, and whether the property belongs to the person, the text, or the reader). I'm fairly sure the closest existing words will definately turn out to be about something slightly different, so tell me where they miss.
