I am perhaps three-quarters of the way through a novel set in a small river town whose principal industry has just closed, and I have written myself into a scene I do not yet trust. After an incident at the secondary school in which a boy scrawled something vicious about a classmate's family origin on the inside of a locker door, the head teacher addresses an assembly and, in the course of telling the students to rise above it, reaches for the old rhyme that promises a child that sticks and stones may break her bones but words will never hurt her. My difficulty is not the head teacher; he is meant to be decent and a little lazy, and the line is precisely the sort of thing such a man would say. My difficulty is that I cannot yet tell what he has committed himself to in saying it, and I need to know before I write the mother's reply, because her reply must land on the exact claim he has made rather than on the sound of it. In my own ear the rhyme keeps splitting apart: at one moment it sounds like a claim about how harm actually works, at the next like an instruction about how one ought to feel, and at the next like a quiet transfer of responsibility from the boy with the marker to the girl who must carry on. I would like to know what it is actually saying.

I have read enough philosophy of language to be aware that there is a quarrel about whether the sting of a slur belongs to what the word means or to what the word does, and I do not want a survey of that quarrel or a reading list — what I want is the rhyme itself taken apart. Lay out for me the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, ordered from the ones that are nearly analytic or vacuous up through the ones that are substantive and defensible to the ones that are simply false. I suspect the menu is longer than it looks. There is a reading on which it says something about causation: that marks on a locker door cannot by themselves break a bone or draw blood, which is true and uninteresting. There is a reading on which it says that a word harms only if the hearer consents to be harmed, which is a thesis about the voluntariness of uptake. There is a reading on which it denies that speech is ever a deed at all — that the injury here is merely conventional, or merely reported, rather than enacted. There is a reading on which it is not a description but a piece of advice, a coping instruction addressed to the girl, and so cannot be refuted by evidence about what words do. And there is a reading on which it is an exculpation, quietly asserting that whatever the boy did, the only damage in the vicinity was hers to prevent. For each reading, tell me whether it is plausible, what it would take to refute it, and — this is the part I most need — whether it would actually license the move the head teacher is making, which is to treat the matter as closed.

Finally, say which of these readings a person is most likely to be reaching for when he recites the rhyme in front of an assembly, and whether any of them is compatible with the thought that a slur can do its damage in a manner quite unlike a stone: by ranking, by licensing others to repeat it, by constituting an injury rather than merely causing one. That is the thought I want the mother to be able to voice without sounding like a lecturer, and I would be grateful if you would close by naming the reading her reply should aim at — the strongest one rather than the easiest — and offering me one or two lines of dialogue that hit it, since a scene is not an argument and I still have to write the scene.
