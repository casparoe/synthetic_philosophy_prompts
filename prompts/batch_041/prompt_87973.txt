I would like to know whether the following reasoning move has a name. I can find names for the individual cases in which I meet it, but nothing that covers the move itself, and I am less interested in the label than in whether the move is one thing or several; a bare name would not satisfy me.

The move runs like this. You are handed a claim about the future that is true, and true whatever anyone thinks or does. You take it as known and reason from it. The first step excludes some particular case. That exclusion is now something you know, so it can be carried into the next step as a fact about the world, and the second step excludes another case. Each step draws on the impossibility established by the one before it. After enough steps the entire space of cases has been eliminated, and the conclusion is that the claim you began with cannot be true, even though nothing in the world has changed in the course of the argument and the claim was true by hypothesis.

I keep meeting it in three places. The first is the surprise examination: a teacher announces an examination in the coming week that the class will not be able to predict the night before, and the class reasons that it cannot fall on the last possible day, since by then they would know, then that it cannot fall on the day before that, and so on until no day is left, whereupon they conclude that the announcement cannot be honoured, though the examination is set and does surprise them. I am not asking which of their steps fails, since I take it that one does. The second is an argument I have heard about games with a known, finite number of rounds, where each side may either take what is available or hold out, holding out paying only if both hold out and taking paying something regardless: in the final round there is nothing left to hold out for, so taking is the sensible thing there; since both sides know that, holding out in the round before is pointless as well; the argument walks back to the first round and concludes that nobody ever holds out, which is not how such games go when they are played. The third differs in one respect. If someone who could predict my choices told me what I was about to do, I could act otherwise, so announcing a true prediction would be enough to falsify it; here my knowing changes the world, whereas in the first two cases it does not, and I cannot tell whether that makes it a different phenomenon.

People have told me that this is circular reasoning, or self-reference, or the surprise examination under another name, and none of these fits. The steps are ordinary inferences from ordinary facts; no premise is assumed that was not granted; and the intermediate conclusions are about what is possible rather than about what is true, which is why carrying one into the next step feels licensed. What I want to know is whether the move has an established name and a settled description, and where its edges fall: which of my three cases belong together, what distinguishes the versions in which nothing in the world changes from the version in which the reasoner's own knowledge changes it, and whether the move is ever sound. If there is no name for it, I would like one coined, defined sharply enough that I can tell, in a fresh case, whether it applies.
