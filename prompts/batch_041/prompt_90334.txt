I am a third-year philosophy student currently taking a bioethics course, and I am trying to work out the implicit theory of action behind the controlled donation-after-circulatory-death protocols we have been discussing. In the standard protocol, a patient or surrogate decides to withdraw life-sustaining treatment; the patient is then allowed to die; after circulatory arrest and a short mandatory waiting period, death is declared and organs are procured. The protocol is designed to avoid any intervention that would hasten death before death is declared, and it insists that the decision to withdraw treatment be made independently of the decision to donate. Yet the withdrawal is foreseen to cause death, the timing of withdrawal is sometimes coordinated with the transplant team, and the waiting period before declaring death is a convention rather than a biological threshold. What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose about the distinction between killing and letting die? Does it presuppose that withdrawing life support is merely an omission, or that it is a cause of death but not a killing? Does it require a particular view about intention and foresight, or a particular criterion of death? Are those commitments mutually consistent, and which of them are strictly required by the protocol rather than merely convenient for it? Finally, if we adopted a thoroughgoing causal view on which withdrawing life support is an act that causes death, or a view on which the killing/letting-die distinction has no moral significance, what would have to change in the protocol? I am less interested in whether the protocol is permissible than in reconstructing the theory of action and causation it relies on, and in saying where the protocol is philosophically underdetermined, so that several rival theories would license it.
