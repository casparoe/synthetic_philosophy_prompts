I'm a novelist, about 60k words into a book about a mid-sized city that decides to run its politics like a lab — every big policy gets piloted on randomly chosen neighborhoods first, the results get published, the council votes on the data. Researching it has basically turned me into a Dewey guy, which I did not expect. The position I've ended up holding is roughly this: democracy just is collective inquiry. A public isn't a pre-existing blob of people; it's what forms when a bunch of us get tangled up in the consequences of other people's actions and start trying to figure out what to do about it. And the right way to do that figuring is the experimental way — propose, test, revise, keep the inquiry open, don't let any doctrine close it off early. Dewey's answer to Lippmann, more or less: trust the method, not the experts and not the crowd. I find this genuinely convincing, maybe because it flatters a novelist's sense that a society is a story under revision.

But I'm suspicous of how good it makes me feel, so play it straight with me: what would count against it? Not the cheap stuff — that people are irrational, that politics is tribal, that nobody reads the report. I mean the defeaters a Deweyan would actually have to sweat. If democracy is inquiry, what happens when the conditions of inquiry can't be met in politics at all — no informed consent, no control group, no way to re-run the experiment once it's baked into a generation, no stable 'public' to be the inquirer in the first place, since the whole problem might be that no public has formed? Does the view have anything real to say about who bears the risk when a policy experiment fails, or does it just assume we all get to be scientists together? And here's the Lippmann worry run backwards: if the method is supposed to be self-correcting, what would it look like for it *not* to correct — for a democracy to inquire diligently and still land somewhere awful, or to keep inquiring forever and never decide anything?

So sort the defeaters for me, fatal down to merely awkward. Tell me which ones a committed Deweyan would cheerfully concede and which they'd have to fight, and which of them would actually move *me* — I think I'm most persuadable by concrete cases rather than abstract worries: a policy trial that genuinely harms the people in the treatment arm, a public that flatly refuses to become a public, an inquiry that reaches a conclusion nobody can act on. And then be honest about the thing I keep circling: has the view quietly qualified itself into something that can't lose — 'inquiry' stretched until any outcome, even the collapse of the inquiry, counts as more inquiry — and if so, is that a bug, or is it just what a pragmatist signs up for?
