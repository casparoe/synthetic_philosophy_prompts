I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and in three weeks I will present a work-in-progress paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop on cognition, computation, and collectives. I would like you to act as my Q&A preparation coach. Assume the role of a philosopher of mind with genuine expertise in three areas at once: the philosophy of collective intentionality, the philosophy of biology (especially work on biological individuality and on minimal or basal cognition), and the empirical study of social insects. You have sat through many talks like mine and you know which questions actually sink a thesis of this shape, as opposed to the ones that merely sound tough. Please think carefully and step by step, and do not rush to the replies.

My thesis, stated baldly: an ant colony of the sort Hofstadter imagines in the Ant Fugue — Aunt Hillary, the colony whose apparent thoughts are supposed to be constituted by the coordinated activity of her ants — is a genuine cognitive subject, in the same sense in which a mammal is. It has states correctly described as beliefs and desires about its environment, and those states do explanatory work of the same kind that beliefs and desires do in the explanation of a mammal's behaviour. What is distinctive is where I locate the mind. I argue that the colony's cognitive states are not states of any individual ant and are not a summary or aggregate of the ants' states; they are constituted by the ongoing stigmergic coupling of the ants with one another and with the persistent structures the colony builds and modifies — nest architecture, pheromone trails, refuse piles, the cemetery. On my view the medium is a proper constituent of the cognitive system, not a channel that carries messages for a mind located elsewhere. I defend a three-part criterion for colony-level mentality, which I will state in the talk, and I claim that the colony-plus-medium system satisfies it while the ants taken individually do not. I will also present a puzzle case that I think my account handles and rival accounts do not: two colonies that have exchanged part of their nest material and now share a single trail network — I claim there is one cognitive subject there, not two.

The audience is about fifteen people. Four or five are philosophers of mind who work on collective intentionality and group agency and who hold that genuinely group-level attitudes require something like joint commitment or mutual recognition among the members. Two are philosophers of biology who work on individuality and on basal cognition; they are comfortable with minimal cognition but allergic to inflating it. One is an entomologist with two decades of field work who is openly hostile to mind-talk about colonies. Two are enactivists who hold that cognition requires an autonomous, self-maintaining organization. One is a network scientist who builds models of ant colonies and believes the models already explain everything I want to explain. There are likely a couple of functionalists at the back who will think my thesis is obviously true and cannot see why anyone objects.

Please produce a preparation sheet, not a defence of my thesis. Before you draft it, spend a short paragraph reconstructing this audience's commitments — what each group already believes that makes my thesis look, to them, either trivially true or absurd. Then give me the eight or nine objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who would raise it, each with a spoken reply I could deliver in about two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is weak, question-begging, or bluffing. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so that the rest of the session is not spent on it. Include, separately and clearly flagged, the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Finish with the two or three objections that would force me to revise the thesis rather than defend it, and say what the smallest revision would be. Name positions rather than papers, and give me no reading list or citations — I know the literature and I cannot use them in the room. Keep the empirical claims modest: the entomologist knows the ants far better than I do.
