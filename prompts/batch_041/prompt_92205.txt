At a seminar I sit in on, two students, Nadia and Owen, spent most of a session arguing about moral compromise, and I have been asked to referee. The case they were discussing was this: two people disagree on two separate moral questions, one caring intensely about the first and hardly at all about the second, with the other's intensities reversed. Owen argued that this is simply a case for trade. They can agree that each gets their way on the question they care most about, and then both are better off by their own lights — precisely what a mutually beneficial exchange produces. To refuse, he said, is to guarantee that each loses on their top priority, which is irrational. Nadia replied that the analogy fails at the root. Someone who yields on a moral question is not merely surrendering a lesser good; they are lending support to something they take to be wrong, or failing to oppose it, and that is a wrong, not a cost to be netted against a gain. She added that being better off presupposes a common scale on which the two questions can be weighed against each other, which is exactly what is in dispute, so the trade picture already concedes a view of value she rejects. Owen answered that she was running together two different things: a compromise in which one actually endorses the other's position, which may indeed involve complicity, and a bare exchange in which neither party alters what they approve of — one merely spends effort, or forgoes opposition, on the issue one cares less about, in return for the same from the other. The commensurability objection, he said, proves too much: nothing in the exchange requires an interpersonal scale, only that each of them prefer the whole package to the status quo, which is what makes it an improvement rather than a mere split. Nadia replied that if no one gives up a value and no one endorses anything they think wrong, this is coordination, not compromise; and the cases in which compromise is actually needed are just the ones in which someone must act against a value or accept a partial injustice — so his account is either not about compromise at all or quietly reintroduces the hard case and relabels it. Owen countered that this may show only that in the hard cases there is no genuine compromise to be had, and that calling them compromises conceals the fact that someone simply loses. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying for each whether it is right, wrong, or partly right and why, and then say what both of them are missing and who, if anyone, has the better of the argument overall.
