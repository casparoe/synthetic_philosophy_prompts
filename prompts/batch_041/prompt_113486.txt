Construct a case that tests whether Camus's abolitionism is a claim about capital punishment as such or only about the way it has in fact been practised. The task is to build the most favourable regime a retentionist could ask for — one engineered so that each of Camus's objections is answered by stipulation rather than by argument — and then to report, honestly, what is left standing.

The case must stipulate at least the following. It is an ordinary liberal democracy in the present day, using no technology that does not already exist. The offence is one of extreme atrocity, committed by a competent adult with no mitigating history and no available excuse, and guilt is established beyond any possibility of error: the offender admits it, and the evidence is as complete as evidence can be. The trial is scrupulous and public, counsel is competent, appeals are unlimited, and no discriminatory or political motive enters anywhere in the process. The method of killing is painless, no date is announced, and the execution comes without warning, so that the offender is spared as much of the interval of foreknowledge as a lawful process can spare him. There is no spectacle, no degradation of the executioner, and no claim by the state that the killing deters anyone.

Test the case against the lines I take the essay to press: (a) the torment of the condemned man's foreknowledge — the interval in which he knows what is coming and can do nothing; (b) the irreversibility of error, which makes the practice stake something absolute on a fallible procedure; (c) the claim that in a world without divine authority no one occupies a position from which to pass a final judgment on another, and that killing forecloses the change a lesser penalty leaves open; (d) that the practice is vengeance in the costume of law, that it wounds the prisoner's family as the crime wounded the victims', and that it hides itself from the society whose protection it claims.

Deliver, in this order: the case itself, in about a paragraph, with the stipulations visible; a ledger giving, for each objection, whether the case neutralizes it and why; then a verdict — does Camus's position condemn the killing here or not? — naming the objection or objections that survive, and saying whether any further stipulation could remove them. If some stipulation cannot be met without reintroducing a feature that another objection attacks, say so and set out the trade-off instead of smoothing it over. Then identify the stipulation doing the most work and run the removal test on it. Then run one variant in which the offender, sentenced to life, asks to be killed and the state complies: does the objection carry over, and what does the answer show about whether Camus's target is the offender's suffering or the state's act? Close with the strongest retentionist reply — that the case is rigged, or that it is unrealizable in practice — and how much that reply costs.

Do not let the case stipulate its own verdict, and do not make the execution wrongful for reasons Camus does not rely on: no torture, no error, no bias, no cruelty of method, no political motive. If the case can only be built by smuggling one of these back in, that is a finding to report, not a defect to conceal.
