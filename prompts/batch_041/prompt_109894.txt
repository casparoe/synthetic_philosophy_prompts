Next month I'm giving a work-in-progress talk in our department seminar (45 minutes, then Q&A until the chair gets bored) and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a pep talk. My thesis, roughly: Frege's 1924 diary isn't just a private embarrassment we quarantine off from the logic, and the standard "two Freges" line is less stable than it looks. Frege spends his whole career insisting that a concept must have sharp boundaries, that a vague concept is no concept at all, that logic is the science of the laws of truth and has nothing to do with how people happen to think; and then in the diary he's working with a picture of peoples and races as though they were kinds with determinate boundaries, so that mixing them registers as a confusion of kinds, and he treats talk of a collective will as a category mistake of the same family. I'm not claiming he derived his politics from the Begriffsschrift, I'm not that naive, it's that the same hostility to unsharp boundaries is doing work in both places, so you can't just seal the notebook off. I also lean on the fact that he positively wanted his political writings kept: in a letter to his son Alfred in January 1925 he writes "Do not despise the pieces I have written," says "Even if all is not gold, there is gold in them," and tells him "Take care that nothing gets lost" -- which undercuts the "he'd have been embarrassed by it" defense. I'm a third-year PhD student, this is my first chapter, and I know I'm walking into a room where people have forgotten more about Frege than I'll ever know.

Here's the room. Mostly analytic philosophy of language and logic. Two Frege specialists who know the Nachlass and the publication history backwards, and one of them thinks the diary is strictly irrelevant to the philosophy and visibly sours whenever it comes up. A historian of early analytic philosophy who's interested in how the hagiography got built in the first place. One person from the Continental side who works on Heidegger's Black Notebooks and thinks our refusal to reckon with Frege the way they reckoned with Heidegger is plain hypocrisy, and who will not be gentle about it. A logician at the back who genuinely doesn't care about any of this. Maybe a visitor from the history department who knows nothing about Frege but a lot about Weimar-era völkisch writing, and who might be the most dangerous person in the room.

Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they do to my case -- if likelihood and damage come apart, tell me both. Phrase each one the way that particular person would phrase it: the Heidegger person and the Frege specialist are not going to object to the same thing in the same words, and the historian's objection will sound like a different discipline entirely. For each: a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is soft, question-begging, or where I'd basically be bluffing. Tell me which ones I should concede up front instead of fighting, and which single one I have to win for the talk to have been worth giving. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. One thing I care about: I'll be saying some of this out loud in front of people who know the literature better than I do, so separate clearly what's actually settled (what's in the diary, when it was written, how it eventually got into print, what he asked his son to do) from what's a live dispute, and don't hand me a contested reading of the connection as though everyone in the field agrees it's true. If you're not sure whether some objection has ever actually been made in print by a named scholar, just say you're not sure rather than pinning it on someone, and skip the reading list, I don't need citations.
