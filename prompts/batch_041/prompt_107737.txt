I am in a reading group on AI safety, and we have been working through the literature on low-impact agents — the family of proposals that try to get an agent to avoid unnecessary side effects by penalizing it for changing the world, rather than by spelling out in advance which changes are acceptable. A friend who knows the area better than I do sent me a blog post that he says is the clearest introduction he has found, and I want to know how much of its gloss I can trust before I lean on it. The post's central passage runs roughly as follows:

"Low-impact AI aims to solve the side-effect problem without anyone having to write down what we value. The trick is to give the agent a penalty for changing the world: in addition to maximizing its reward, it pays a cost equal to the distance between the world it brings about and the world as it would have been had the agent done nothing — the inaction baseline. Impact is then a purely physical quantity, a matter of how far the state has moved, so the designer never has to say which changes are good and which are bad. The penalty targets side effects, leaving the agent free to pursue its goal; it merely nudges the agent toward the least disruptive route. The obvious objection, that an agent which changes nothing is useless, is answered by the fact that the agent still optimizes its reward and the penalty only trades off against it. What remains is engineering: choose a sensible distance metric and tune the penalty's weight. And the familiar criticism — that you cannot define harm without a theory of value — simply misunderstands the proposal, whose entire point is to avoid needing such a theory."

I am inclined to believe the last sentence is too quick, and the phrase "purely physical quantity" makes me uneasy, but I do not know the technical literature well enough to say where the gloss goes wrong rather than merely being loose. Could you audit it? Go claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on these impact measures would accept, what they would wince at as a simplification, and what they would flatly reject — and in particular, say whether the closing move (that the value-theoretic objection "misunderstands the proposal") survives once the rest of the summary is corrected. If the honest position is something narrower — say, that one can avoid specifying a full value function without avoiding value commitments altogether — I would like that stated precisely, along with what the residual commitments are and why they matter for whether "avoid side effects" can be defined without a full specification of value.
