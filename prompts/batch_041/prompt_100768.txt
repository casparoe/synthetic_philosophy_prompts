Futarchy — the scheme in which a polity votes to fix the metric that defines its welfare and then lets conditional prediction markets choose policy, on the theory that bets about whether the metric will be higher under one policy than another will aggregate knowledge better than debate will — strikes me as an unusually clean case of an institution resting on a philosophical picture I cannot quite reconstruct. What does the design presuppose about value and about knowledge? It appears to require a welfare metric fixed and commensurable enough that a market can price a single number, yet the choice of metric is handed to a vote as though it were a prior matter of value rather than a contestable empirical claim; I would like to know which theory of welfare the practice actually commits to, and whether the clean partition between values and beliefs that it needs is really available. I would also like to know which of its presuppositions are load-bearing and which are merely convenient: does it genuinely need welfarism and consequentialism, or could a deontological or value-pluralist electorate run the same machinery? Tell me where the institution is philosophically underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license the same design — and what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
