I retired a couple of years ago after thirty-four years as a research chemist, and I've been using the time to catch up on all the philosophy I never had a reason to read. Ethics mostly. There's a small reading group of us and I'm on the hook to lay out my own position next month, so before I get taken apart I want to know whether the thing actually hangs together. Here's roughly where I've landed.

(a) Consequences are what make actions right. If I could have done better overall, counting everyone equally, then what I did was wrong. Not suboptimal — wrong.

(b) Williams's integrity stuff really landed on me. The case where the captain tells you to shoot one villager yourself or he shoots twenty: Williams's complaint isn't that the arithmetic is off, it's that the theory treats my own life and my own commitments as just another entry in the ledger, and my reasons as if they were all borrowed from the general good. And the case where a man can only pull one person out of the water and it's his wife — Williams says that if his thought had to be, well, saving her is permitted in cases of this kind, then that's one thought too many. I think he's right on both, and I don't think he's just complaining that the answer is hard. The theory has the wrong story about what my reasons are.

(c) A moral theory has to be able to be honest with the person living under it. If the theory says X is right, then someone who accepts the theory ought to be able to act on it without being fooled about why she's acting. I'm allergic to the idea of a theory that's true but has to be kept from the people who have to live by it. In my old line of work, a model you weren't allowed to use for prediction wasn't a model, it was decoration.

(d) I do think there's a real distinction between what makes an action right and how you ought to go about deciding what to do. A theory of rightness doesn't have to double as a decision procedure.

(e) And I think Singer is basically right about the child in the pond, and about what that implies for how much of my pension I ought to be giving away. Distance doesn't matter; if I can save someone's life at modest cost to myself then I have to. I don't do it, but that's a fact about me, not about the argument.

So does the package hold? Which of these are genuinly pulling against each other, which only look like they are, and how bad is each one? Rank them by severity rather than giving me a flat list, and where you think something has to go, tell me the cheapest repair and what I'd be giving up by making it.

One more thing: keep seperate the parts that are actually settled from the parts that people who work on this still argue about. If a point is one camp's line rather than the consensus, say so — I'd rather see the disagreement flagged than get a tidy answer that turns out to be one team's view.
