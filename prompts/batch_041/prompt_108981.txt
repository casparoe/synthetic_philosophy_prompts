I am putting together the closing unit of a seminar on classical Chinese theories of human nature, and I want to end on Golding's Lord of the Flies. My difficulty is that the novel always gets used as a thirty-second illustration — the boys paint their faces, the conch breaks, so Xunzi was right — and then we move on, which teaches the students nothing about either the book or the philosophers. I would like a proper critical reading instead.

Take the disagreement at its real strength rather than at the level of slogans. Mencius's claim is not that people behave well; it is that everyone has the four beginnings — the reactive stirrings of compassion, shame, deference, and the sense of right and wrong — which are tendencies rather than finished virtues, which have to be reflected on and filled out, and which can be starved or cut down by circumstances. His own image for a ruined character is a hillside that looks barren because it has been stripped for timber and grazed, not because trees never grew there. Xunzi's claim, by contrast, is not that people are malevolent; it is that what we are born with is partial to self-interest, and that whatever goodness we have is deliberate artifice — accumulated effort, taught and held in place by ritual, and, on the standard reading, dependent on teachers and rulers to carry it. Note also that both men think the ordinary person has what it takes to become a sage, so the live disagreement is not about capacity but about the raw material and about what goodness is: for Mencius a tendency to be completed, for Xunzi an artifact that the sages made. That is the shape of the argument I want the reading to hold to.

Then turn it on the novel. Which scenes actually carry weight for which diagnosis? I am thinking of the first assembly and the authority of the conch, the painted faces and the hunt, Simon's suggestion that the beast is not something out there, the killing of Simon, the theft of Piggy's glasses and his death, the dead parachutist the boys take for a beast, and the officer's arrival at the end. Say what each scene shows, and say plainly where the book is rigged rather than honest.

I also want you to press the methodological question rather than dodge it. The island is a dreadful sample — boys of one class, one nation, and one age, evacuated from a war the adults are still fighting — and it is a thesis novel, built to make a point, so it is not obvious that it is evidence about human nature at all. A Mencian can say the hillside was already stripped before the boys arrived; a Xunzian can say this is simply what the raw material does once the ritual order loses the authority that props it up. Tell me which reading the novel better supports, and where the book itself seems to know the difference between them.

End with what the novel contributes that neither philosopher's arguments do on their own. And please tell me how confident you are in the reading as a whole, and which single claim in it you are least sure of. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables — the way you would write it to a colleague.
