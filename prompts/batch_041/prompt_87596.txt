I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, but I have recently been reading Wittgenstein's On Certainty and trying to work out what it implies for my own practice. My understanding of the book is roughly this: much of what we take ourselves to know rests on propositions that function as hinges, in the sense that they are held fast by everything around them, they are exempt from doubt, and no reasons can be given for them, because reasons run out before we ever reach them. That description struck me as an accurate account of certain things I never question at work: that the compiler does what its documentation says it does, that the code my colleagues commit means what it appears to mean, and that the machine in front of me is not fabricating every output I observe. Within the practice I cannot justify any of these assumptions, and I cannot genuinely doubt them either.

My question concerns what is supposed to follow from this. If a hinge is by definition exempt from doubt, does it follow that anyone who does doubt one is thereby being irrational, and that the flat-earther and the colleague who suspects the build system of malice are making the same mistake, namely doubting something that cannot be doubted? If so, are we entitled to dismiss such challenges without engaging them, on the grounds that the doubt itself is unintelligible? And does this not leave On Certainty licensing a kind of dogmatism, in which whatever a community happens to take for granted is thereby certain, so that the most one can ever say to an outsider is that they do not share our form of life? As I have reconstructed the view it seems both too convenient and too permissive, and I suspect the error lies in my reconstruction rather than in Wittgenstein, but I would like to know precisely where the reconstruction goes wrong.
