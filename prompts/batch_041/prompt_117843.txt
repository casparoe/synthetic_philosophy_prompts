For most of my working life I was a comparative physiologist. I spent some thirty years recording from slowly adapting mechanoreceptors and stretch receptors, chiefly in crustaceans, and another decade on the neuromuscular control of posture, before I retired. Since then I have been reading philosophy without a syllabus, and I have written the short piece below. I would like your comments on it.

Let me say what kind of comments. I am not asking for a reading list and I do not want citations; I have no proper library access, and in any case what I need to know is whether the argument holds up. I want to be told, bluntly, where I am being an amateur: which distinctions I have conflated, which inferences do not go through, which of my claims a philosopher would regard as already settled the other way, and whether the central idea is worth anything or merely a relabelling of something familiar. If I have reinvented a wheel that has a standard name, name it and tell me how mine differs from it. Where I make empirical claims about animals, hold me to the standard you would hold a grant proposal to. And if a question I ask at the end is malformed, say so rather than answering it.

The piece follows.

**Two ledgers**

Ask which animals are conscious and you will get an argument about thresholds: cortex or no cortex, midbrain or no midbrain, some integrative structure or none. I think the question is badly posed, and not only because consciousness is hard to define. It conceals two questions, and the interesting work lies in pulling them apart.

The first I will call the hedonic ledger: how much valenced experience an organism undergoes over its life, how much pleasure and unpleasure there is from the inside. The second I will call the stakes ledger: whether anything can go better or worse for the organism at all, whether or not it feels like anything to be that organism. I coined both terms for this piece and do not know whether either is a term of art.

The familiar debates, as far as I can make them out, assume that these two rise and fall together. That is the assumption I want to deny. The hedonic ledger is a matter of what an organism's states are like; the stakes ledger is a matter of what those states are for. An organism has a stake in its own condition when it has states that aim at something and can miss. That is a structural property, and I see no reason to think it is confined to organisms with vivid experience, or that it is guaranteed wherever vivid experience is present. A creature whose life cannot go wrong, with nothing regulated and nothing aimed at, could have a rich inner life and no welfare in my sense at all; and I would say that talk about what is good for such a creature is then not false but ill-formed.

If that is right, then the distribution of moral concern does not track the distribution of phenomenal consciousness. It tracks the distribution of stakes, and stakes are cheap: a setpoint, a target, a possible miss. On this view the interesting empirical question is not how far down the tree sentience goes, but how far down goal-directed regulation with a genuine failure mode goes, and the answer is presumably very far indeed. Here I expect to be told that I have reinvented the distinction between hedonic and desire-based accounts of welfare. But I am not offering a theory of what welfare consists in. I am claiming that stakes are what make the question well-formed, which is a different and, I think, stronger claim.

A second argument, of a different kind. Suppose we go looking for markers of sentience across taxa, testing many species with several assays each. If the base rate is low and the assays are imperfect, and they are imperfect because we do not know what we are looking for, then a programme that runs hundreds or thousands of such tests will generate a great many positive results with nothing behind them. Worse, the false positives will not be spread evenly. They will concentrate in the taxa that are easiest to keep in a laboratory, easiest to score, and most likely to be published when they come out positive. I suspect comparative cognition has a file-drawer problem of an unusual sort: not unpublished nulls, but published positives that nobody had any antecedent reason to expect.

The practical consequence I draw is that the precautionary position survives, but not for the reason usually offered. It is not that we have evidence of sentience in this or that invertebrate; it is that our evidence-generating procedure has a known tendency to manufacture such evidence, so the evidence cannot be used to narrow the range of candidates. The asymmetry, not the data, does the work.

Now the questions I would like answered. First, is the separation I draw in the second and third paragraphs real, or have I merely renamed the distinction between hedonic and desire-based welfare and then made an extravagant claim on top of it? Second, does the inference in the third paragraph go through, from a regulated variable with a possible failure mode to a stake, and from a stake to the well-formedness of welfare talk? I can already see one objection, that a setpoint is a fact about a mechanism rather than a fact about a subject, and I would like to know whether it is fatal. Third, is the fifth paragraph as damaging as it feels to me, or does it prove too much: would the same argument, applied to any science with noisy instruments, dissolve evidence we all accept? Fourth, if the fourth and sixth paragraphs are right, what follows for how someone should actually allocate moral caution across taxa; and if they are not, what is the most economical repair?
