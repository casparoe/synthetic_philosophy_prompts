Some friends and I have been chewing on a variant of Kavka's toxin puzzle, and I want you to argue for one side of it rather than survey the options. In the original, a billionaire offers you a million dollars on the condition that you form the intention — now, or at midnight tonight; the timing isn't the point — to drink a toxin tomorrow, a toxin that will make you ill for a day but leave you otherwise fine. The money is delivered tomorrow morning whether or not you actually drink, and you are free to change your mind in the meantime. The puzzle is that it looks rational to intend to drink, since intending is free and secures the money, and yet you know that tomorrow, with the money already banked, drinking would be pointless self-harm — so it looks as though you are being asked to intend something you know you will not do.

Now consider what I'll call the calibrated-detector variant, which is what I actually want you to address. The billionaire cannot read intentions; he relies on a scanner that detects a neural correlate of the intention, with imperfect accuracy — say it fires on 90% of the occasions you genuinely intend and 5% of the occasions you don't. He pays if and only if the scanner fires. In addition, you have a pill available: taking it raises the chance that the correlate shows up by a few percentage points, but it also makes you somewhat more likely to actually go through with drinking tomorrow. You know all of this tonight and are deciding what to do.

Argue for the position that in this variant the standard reply — that an intention is not a prediction of action, so you may intend and later reconsider — no longer does the work it is supposed to do, and that the agent should instead treat the situation as a decision problem over states of the agent (correlate present or absent, pill or no pill) rather than over intentions. Give the strongest version of the opposing view its due — the view that the scanner merely gives you a new reason to make the correlate occur while leaving the original logic of intention untouched — and then show why it fails, or where it survives only in weakened form. Along the way, tell me whether the agent who takes the pill, produces the correlate, and then declines to drink is behaving irrationally, and connect your answer to the broader claim that rationality attaches to plans or policies rather than to individual acts. I don't need sources or citations; I want the argument itself.
