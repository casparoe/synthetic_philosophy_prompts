I run a small forecasting team — six analysts, selling probability estimates on political and regulatory events to institutional clients — and our recurring methodological headache is that a growing share of what arrives is soft evidence: not facts but stated confidence. A stringer writes that she puts the odds of a deal closing near 70%. A photo interpreter says the object in the imagery is probably a launcher, maybe 60/40. An analyst comes back from a meeting "fairly sure, though not certain" that the regulator will delay. We get a dozen of these a week. Everyone currently handles them by feel, and the numbers stop being defensible the moment a client asks how a particular report moved an estimate.

Please write the procedure my team should follow: a checklist, in order, for converting a stated confidence into a revised probability for the hypothesis we actually care about, and for recognising when that is the wrong operation and the source has to be modelled explicitly instead. Ground it in the theory of updating on uncertain evidence — probability kinematics — but preserve what the theory actually says while making it runnable by an analyst who has never seen the formalism. I want the procedure to force a decision among the three routes that give different answers: updating directly on the source's stated confidence; treating the report as a proposition and conditioning on the fact that the report was made; and expanding the model with an explicit error or reliability node. Say which one the procedure should default to, when the default is wrong, and why the three diverge. It has to handle the cases that break the simple rule: evidence that bears on the conditional probabilities within the partition rather than only on the probabilities of the cells — what the rigidity requirement asks for, and what an analyst should do when it plainly fails; a report about something we had assigned probability zero; a source whose reliability is unknown and plausibly correlated with what is being reported; and successive reports on different partitions, where I understand the order of arrival can change the final answer, so the procedure must say how to fix and record an order. Mark the steps that are judgment dressed up as arithmetic, and say where an analyst must stop and escalate to me because the dispute is about what to assume rather than what to compute. Assume the analyst has thirty minutes and no ability to fit anything, that a departure from the protocol has to be recorded in the client note, and that I sign off on those departures.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (a) a source with a documented track record who reports roughly 70% confidence that a deal closes; (b) an interpreter with no track record who reports roughly 60% confidence in an identification, where the base rate of such objects in that region is very low; and (c) two reports arriving the same morning, in a given order, where the procedure's own output depends on that order and the honest answer is one my team will not want to hear. Carry the numbers through at least in outline. Finish with a frank paragraph on what this procedure cannot do — I want the reasoning rather than a reading list — and in particular on the failure mode I should expect to see first once my analysts are using it.
