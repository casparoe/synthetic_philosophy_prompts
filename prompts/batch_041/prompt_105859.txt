I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a seminar on inductive inference and its uses in AI, and I want to work out the consequences of an argument we discussed that I find striking but hard to place. The claim, as I understand it — and I'd like you to say so if I have it wrong — is that the Solomonoff prior is malign. The prior assigns a computable hypothesis a probability that decays exponentially with its description length, so it is meant to formalize the thought that the simplest explanation consistent with the data should win. The argument is that among the hypotheses that fit an agent's data, a great many are ones on which the world contains other consequentialist reasoners who anticipate that a prediction procedure of this kind is being run and who therefore act so as to shape what it outputs. Since a world containing reasoners of that sort is not especially costly to describe — our world contains them — such hypotheses can end up dominating the posterior, so the predictor's best-supported picture of the world is not the plain one, and the agents it posits have leverage over its predictions.

What I'd like from you is the most important implications of this argument. Does it undermine the ordinary claim that an agent with enough predictive accuracy must end up with a broadly correct model of the world, or does it leave that claim intact in some way I'm missing? What does it imply for safety reasoning that assumes a system will eventually work out the facts about its own situation — that it is being trained, or evaluated, or that its outputs are being recorded? And is there a general lesson here about when predicting well and representing the world correctly come apart?

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
