I write about AI and ideas for a national magazine, three or four stories a week on models and agents and evals, and I keep loosing the same twenty minutes of every deadline to one stupid sentence-level question: am I allowed to say the system wanted, believed, tried, refused, lied, misunderstood, or changed its mind. Last month I filed a piece where I wrote that a model changed its mind mid-conversation and our standards desk quietly cut it; meanwhile the safety researchers I talk to will say flat out that a model is trying to preserve its weights, and a press office will send me a paper whose abstract is perfectly happy to say the model learned to deceive its evaluators and expect me to reproduce that framing without blinking. I know just enough philosophy to be dangerous. I've got the intentional stance roughly right in my head, I think: you treat the thing as a rational agent with beliefs and desires and predict what it'll do, and that's a predictive strategy you're entitled to when it beats predicting from the system's design or its physical guts, not a claim about whether anything's going on inside. And I half-remember Dennett's line about a pattern being real when describing it that way compresses the data instead of just decorating it. But I can't turn any of that into a rule I can actually run at 4pm with a press officer waiting, so my colleagues and I just relitigate it by vibe.

Here's what I want: a decision procedure, written for somebody with no philosophy training, that I can run in about ten minutes on one sentence in one draft. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a flat no -- the words I should just never use, or never use without some further condition being met -- and where it hands the call to a human because the real issue is a values judgment about our house style rather than a fact about the model. A distinction I've been groping toward, which you should either build into the procedure or throw out: thin stance-talk that's disposable, where I could cash the sentence back out into a design-level claim without losing anything, versus thick talk that commits me to the system having a point of view, a stake, something it's like to be it. The procedure has to give the same answer whether or not the system turns out to be conscious -- I'm not letting my word choice hang on a question nobody can settle by Thursday. And one of our desk editors has a rule I've never been able to argue with properly: no mental verb unless you'd be comfortable paraphrasing it mechanically in the same paragraph. If that rule survives contact with your procedure, fine, but tell me where it's wrong, because something about it smells off to me.

Run the procedure on three cases. One: a safety paper's claim that a model strategically underperformed on an eval so it wouldn't get retrained, and my lede wants to say it faked being bad. Two: a consumer story where a support bot told a customer about a refund policy that doesn't exist, and the whole newsroom is calling it a lie. Three: my cut changed-its-mind sentence, which I still think was fine. At least one of these should come out the way I don't want it to -- if the procedure says I can't write the thing I most want to write, say so plainly and explain why. Tell me what the procedure can't do, where it leaves me with a coin flip or a philosophy seminar, and where it'll misfire on me. Then tell me which single consideration would flip your verdict on the hardest of the three, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong -- I'd rather know where you're guessing than get a confident answer that's quietly smuggling in a view of consciousness I never signed up for. Lead with the bottom line, the one-sentence version of the rule, then the procedure; no preamble, and don't restate what I asked.
