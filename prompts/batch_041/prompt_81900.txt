Consider the position, sometimes called lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. It holds that suffering is intrinsically bad and pleasure intrinsically good, and that the two are ordered in two distinct registers. Below a certain level of welfare — call it the floor — suffering is lexically worse than pleasure: no quantity of enjoyment, however vast, can compensate for a single being held below the floor, and the comparison is not a matter of weighing at all. Above the floor, the ordinary logic of marginal trade-offs resumes, and pleasure and pain are commensurable in the usual way.

Please explain the central conceptual difficulty that this two-tier structure generates. Begin by separating two things that the standard statements of the view tend to run together: a threshold on welfare, meaning the level of suffering at which the lexical segment is triggered, and a threshold on aggregation, meaning the rule that fixes which units are compared with which others once the threshold is crossed — the individual at a moment, the individual across a whole life, or the world taken as a whole. Then, with that distinction in hand, address the following. What exactly does lexical priority assert, and how does it differ from a very large cardinal weight, from trumping (a value that overrides another without being weighed against it), and from incommensurability (the denial that any common measure exists)? Must this view treat pleasure and pain as commensurable, as incommensurable, or as both in different regions of the scale, and can that combination be made coherent rather than merely stipulated? And where does the floor come from — is it a substantive moral fact, a stipulation, or a heuristic — and does the answer change what kind of view this is? I am after the structure of the problem, not a catalogue of who has held the view.
