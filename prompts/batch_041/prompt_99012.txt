ok so context: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and I run a small book club on discord. Last month we read the Hobsbawm & Ranger book about invented tradition and Benedict Anderson's Imagined Communities, and the discussion was a total trainwreck. Everyone kept saying "well the nation is constructed" and then nobody could say constructed how, or by who, or by when, and whether that means the nation isn't real, and two people got into it about whether an old tradition that people sincerely believe is ancient but a historian can show is only about a hundred years old is a lie or just... normal. I think half the confusion is that "invented" is doing like four different jobs at once and people don't notice they've switched.

So I want to build a web quiz for the group — same vibe as those "which X are you" quizzes but actually serious — where you answer a bunch of concrete questions and at the end it tells you which position on "how old are nations, really" you actually hold, based on your answers, not on which scholar you like. I'd like you to write the whole thing.

What I need:

1. Nine questions, each with 2 or 3 answer options. The important part: no question should just ask "do you think nations are ancient or modern" or "do you agree with Anderson." Every question should be a concrete case or a concrete claim where the positions actually come apart, phrased so someone can answer it honestly without knowing which answer leads where. Think scenario, not label.

Cases I'd like you to build around, mix them up however works: a 19th-century urban elite that writes a national epic and collects "folk" songs for a peasantry that doesn't even share their language; a diaspora that keeps a national identity alive for a few centuries with no state and no territory; a brand-new state that standardizes a language and a school curriculum inside one generation; a group whose members sincerely believe their customs go back a thousand years when the historian says about a hundred; and a "tradition" that turns out to be assembled out of bits of genuinely old local customs that were just never combined into one thing before.

2. A scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions. I want these to show up: a hard perennialist/primordialist option, the ethnosymbolist one (pre-modern ethnic roots but the nation proper is modern), a top-down modernist (elites fabricate it), a bottom-up modernist (it's imagined horizontally, through print/media rather than decreed), a "constructed but that doesn't make it fake" bricolage option, and a deflationary option that says the question is confused because nation/state/ethnic group are being slid together. If your answer key can't cleanly separate two of those from the answer patterns you have, say so and tell me which pair collapses and why — I'd rather the quiz admit it than fake it.

3. For each question, one line saying which positions it's actually distinguishing between, so I can sanity-check the thing.

A few things to keep in mind:

- Don't make me cite anything or name-drop scholars as the answer. The quiz taker can't look stuff up and neither can whoever takes it at the meetup, so everything has to be answerable from the scenarios themselves. You can reference the general ideas but the quiz shouldn't depend on knowing who said what.
- The distinctions I actually care about (these are the ones I think people keep conflating, so please make the branches hinge on them): does "invented" mean deliberate deception or just historical contingency; does the sincere belief of ordinary people count as evidence the nation is real, or is that belief the thing that needs explaining; does continuity need unbroken transmission, or can it be claimed retroactively; and who's the carrier — the state, the ethnic group, or the media/print.
- If there's a combination of answers that logically should correspond to a real position but nobody actually holds it, note it and say in a sentence why it's empty (incoherent? just unattractive? or just nobody's bothered?).

Just the quiz, the scoring key, and those per-question notes please — no essay, no reading list.
