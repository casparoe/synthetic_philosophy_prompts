I am assembling a short-fiction reader for a graduate seminar on powers, tendencies, and dispositions, and I would like you to write one of the pieces. Please aim for roughly 1,500 words.

The story should dramatize, rather than explain, the following puzzle. We habitually treat a thing's dispositions as answerable to a simple conditional: something is breakable just in case it would break if struck, live just in case it would carry current if touched, and so on. Three kinds of case put pressure on that formula, and they put pressure on it in different ways.

(i) The triggering circumstance destroys the disposition before the behaviour can occur. The pane really is breakable, but the instant it is struck, a mechanism inside it stiffens the glass, so the blow never finds anything brittle to break. The pane is fragile, and yet it is false that it would break if struck.

(ii) Something extrinsic blocks the behaviour while the disposition survives untouched. The pane is breakable and would break if struck, but it sits in a padded frame; struck, it does nothing at all. Take away the padding and it shatters.

(iii) The behaviour occurs, but not because of the disposition. The pane is not breakable — it is a tough resin — yet a small charge set in its rim detonates on impact and it flies apart. It comes apart, and it is not fragile.

In all three cases the plain conditional misfires, but we are inclined to say different things about the three panes: the first is fragile, the second is fragile, the third is not. A good story should make a reader feel why those verdicts differ and where the pressure on each of them comes from.

Now the story itself. I want a concrete, inhabited setting, not a laboratory fable. My suggestion, and you may substitute another setting if it serves better, is a small regional museum preparing a travelling loan exhibition of fragile objects, where the loan agreement turns on a clause stating that any case that would break if struck must be fitted with laminated glass. A young conservator is asked to certify three display cases built to three different specifications. The certification cannot be done from the outside: two of the cases shrug off the mallet, and the third comes apart in a way that looks exactly like breakage, and what each result means is precisely what is in dispute. The story should follow her as she works out what each case is really like, and should include at least one colleague or superior who disagrees with her about at least one of the verdicts on grounds that are not stupid, with the sharpest disagreement falling on whichever case turns on the disposition being destroyed by the very event that would have revealed it. Do not let the narrator explain the puzzle, name the three patterns, or cite any literature; let the story end on the certification rather than on a summary of it.

The three cases between them should instance all three patterns above, exactly once each, and the events of the story should make it determinate which case is which.

Please write the story, and then, on a final line of its own, give the certification in the following machine-readable form: \answer{Case 1: <label>; Case 2: <label>; Case 3: <label>}, where each label is one of FINK (the triggering circumstance removes the disposition before it can manifest), MASK (an extrinsic obstruction prevents manifestation while the disposition persists), or MIMIC (the manifestation occurs without the disposition). That last line is for my own indexing and should not be worked into the story's text.
