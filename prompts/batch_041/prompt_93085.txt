I'm in a third-year course on collective choice (Condorcet, Borda, Arrow, the usual lineup) and in section last week the TA claimed that the 1992 US presidential election is about the closest thing American politics has to a real-world instance of the paradox of voting, and that if Perot had stayed in at his June level we might have seen an actual cycle. I'm writing my term paper on this and I want a real analysis, not the cocktail-party version. The facts as I have them: Clinton 43.0, Bush 37.5, Perot 18.9 percent of the popular vote; turnout 58.1 percent; Perot led in several polls in June, then dropped out in July and came back in the fall and lost most of that support; and only two contests (Arkansas and DC) gave anyone an absolute majority. Exit polls apparently show his voters' second choices splitting fairly evenly between Bush and Clinton, so the standard line is that Bush loses either way, though I've also seen people insist Perot cost Bush the election, so I'm definately not sure what to believe. One terminological thing that threw me: the professor used the phrase paradox of voting for the cycling/intransitivity result, and the textbook used the same phrase for the why-does-anyone-bother-voting puzzle, which made section extremely confusing. I'd like both used, since I suspect they interact here.

What I want is 1992 run through the paradox of voting properly, in both directions. So set out what the theory actually needs in order to make its claim — a fixed electorate, complete rankings over the same menu of alternatives, pairwise majority comparisons — and then tell me honestly how much of that can be reconstructed from this episode. Is there a Condorcet winner in 1992, and how would we even know? Is what happened an instance of the paradox, or just a plurality failure, i.e. a spoiler effect that requires no intransitivity at all? Does the second-preference data actually license the inference, or not, given that an election only ever reveals one aggregate outcome and never a profile? And then the thing that looks like it breaks the setup: Condorcet comparisons assume how people rank Bush against Clinton doesn't depend on whether Perot is on the ballot, but in 1992 Perot's presence probably changed who turned out and what the election was even about, so the head-to-head counterfactual may be ill-defined rather than merely unobserved. Tell me which of those are genuine problems for applying the theory and which are just standard hand-wringing. Then do the other paradox on it: 58 percent turnout, the pivot-probability argument says nobody should vote, and what does 1992 do to that? I've read something about expressive voting and about voting just being cheap, but I don't know how much work those replies actually do. Also, the TA mentioned a paradox where the side that wins the election is on the losing side of the majority on most of the individual issues — I forget what it's called — and I'd like to know whether that issue-by-issue framing fits 1992 better than the candidate-ranking framing, given the election was fought over the economy, the deficit, NAFTA and character all at once.

And finally the meta question, which is really what the paper is about: what does 1992 show about the paradox of voting as a theory? Does it confirm the bleak reading that there's no such thing as a coherent popular will, or does it show the theory is close to unfalsifiable, since the data real elections generate can't seperate a cycle from a spoiler effect from a plain plurality outcome? Don't force the theory to speak where it has nothing to say — tell me where it's silent. No citations or reading list needed, I just want the argument.
