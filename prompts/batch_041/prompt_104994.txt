I am trying to understand a tension I keep running into, and I would like it explained conceptually rather than proved. On one side, I am told that if the axioms of arithmetic or of set theory were inconsistent, the result would be catastrophic: from a contradiction anything whatsoever follows, so every statement would become provable and the whole enterprise would be emptied of content. On the other side, I am told that the consistency of these systems is something we cannot establish from within them, so the possibility of an inconsistency can never be fully ruled out — and yet that even if one existed, we might never notice, because the shortest derivation of a contradiction might be so astronomically long that no one could ever write it down, and that ordinary mathematics could be carried on indefinitely in the meantime.

Please explain how both of these attitudes can be held at once, and what conceptual distinctions make the second one coherent. I am especially interested in three things. First, the difference between a system's being inconsistent and its being wrong about arithmetic — that is, between deriving a contradiction and proving a statement about the whole numbers that is simply false; I have a suspicion that what we actually care about is the second, and that the first is frightening mostly because it seems to entail it. Second, what it could mean for an inconsistency to be contained, so that a system is locally reliable even if it is globally broken, and how one could ever have reason to believe such containment held. Third, whether an inconsistent system must be abandoned or could instead be repaired — for example, by weakening the underlying logic so that a contradiction no longer makes every sentence provable, and what is lost in doing so. I want the ideas made vivid and the trade-offs spelled out, not a survey of results or a list of references. Please write in American English.
