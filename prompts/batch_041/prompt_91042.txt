Not sure if this is the kind of thing you do, but here goes. I'm a retired chemist -- physical organic, forty years at the bench -- and since I packed it in I've been reading alot of philosophy, mostly decision theory and philosophy of the social sciences, so go easy on me, I'm definately an amateur.

Last month at a departmental reunion one of the old crowd ran a dollar auction at the bar. The rules were the usual ones: he auctioned off a twenty-dollar bill, bids in one-dollar steps, and the top two bidders both have to pay what they bid -- but only the winner gets the twenty. It went to forty-one dollars before anyone stopped. Two of my former colleagues have been arguing about it ever since and I can't referee them.

Roughly how it went.

She says it's just the sunk-cost fallacy, or escalation of commitment -- people keep bidding because they can't stand to walk away from what they've already put in. The winner paid $41 for a $20 bill; if that counts as rational, the word has no meaning.

He says that's a misdiagnosis. Once you're the runner-up the money's gone whether you bid or not, so the only real question is what happens next. If you're sitting on a $40 bid and the leader's at $41, quitting means you're out $40; bidding $42 might win you the twenty, so you're out $22 instead. Looking forward, bidding is the better move. That's not a fallacy, he says, that's a trap -- same way defecting is the best move in a prisoner's dilemma even though it wrecks both players.

She says he's cheating, because that argument quietly assumes the other bidder stops. But the other bidder is running the same calculation, so he should expect him not to stop, and then bidding again just digs the hole deeper. If the rational move depends on being systematically wrong about your opponent, it isn't rational.

He says it's a coordination problem, not a mistake in reasoning -- each move is best given what the other does, the trouble is that both are choosing at once.

She then brings up what actually happened, which is the part that's got me stuck. When the bidding got silly, the organizer offered an out: if the two top bidders both agreed to stop, he'd tear the whole thing up and refund every bid, so nobody lost a cent. They didn't take it. She says that kills his theory -- if the trap were purely structural, a free exit should have defused it, and it didn't, which means what kept them going was psychological, i.e. the thing she's been calling a bias all along.

He says the out required both of them to agree, and each of them still had an incentive to bid one more if he thought the other might not stop, so it's the same coordination problem wearing a hat. And he adds that even Ulysses had himself tied to the mast, so "you can't trust your future self" is a fact about the situation, not a character flaw.

She says he's just renamed escalation of commitment and is pretending it's a different animal.

That's where I lose the thread. What I'd like:

- Who's right about which specific claim? Move by move, please, not a verdict on who wins overall.
- Is his distinction between continuing because of the past and continuing because of the future a real distinction, or just a redescription of the same thing? And if it's real, which one is actually going on in the auction?
- Does the refund offer really tell us anything, or is she over-reading a stunt at a bar? If it does tell us something, whose side does it help?
- Does the fact that he'd have to be wrong about his opponent's behavior make his move irrational, or only unlucky?
- And is there something neither of them has said that I should be thinking about here?

I've probably set the whole thing up wrong somewhere, so correct me if the framing itself is off.
