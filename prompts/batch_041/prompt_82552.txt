I'm on research leave this year and the committee wants a prospectus out of me before they'll sign off on the second term, so write me something I can actually hand in — a two-page proposal for what is, honestley, still a half-formed idea that's been needling me for about three years. Here it is. Gödel's rotating-universe solutions — the ones he built in 1949 and wrote up as his contribution to the big Einstein festschrift — have closed timelike curves and admit no global time-ordering at all, and his gloss on them was that a world like that leaves nothing for an objective passage of time to be, which he took to tell against the reality of time and, he thought, in favour of something closer to idealism. Fine, I know the shape of the thing. What I can't get straight, and what I want the prospectus to be about, is how much of the argument actually needs the rotating models. As far as I can see the load-bearing premise has to be one of two things, and they pull apart. Either the reality of time requires a single privileged global now — in which case plain special relativity already sinks it, the Gödel universe is decoration, and the conclusion comes out far too strong to believe; or else the reality of time requires only that some global time-order exist, a foliation into slices of simultaneity — in which case the rotating models are doing real work, but now I owe a story about why time should need a global order when every observer inside still has their own local time, and that story is exactly the piece I don't have. So the question I want stated cleanly is this: does the Gödel construction contribute anything to the case against the reality of time beyond what the relativity of simultaneity already delivers, and if so, what?

Turn that into a proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer; say why the two obvious readings of the argument don't settle it between them; spell out the method — I want to lay the argument out premise by premise and then try to construct a toy spacetime that has one of the two features without the other, so that the horns come apart and I can see which premise does the work; say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing (my worry is that it fails by turning out to be a verbal dispute about 'time', so somewhere in there say something about how to tell whether it's verbal); and be realistic about scope, this is one chapter, maybe six to eight thousand words, one semester. Don't hand me a reading list or citations, I'll fill those in myself. Oh, and I got into this over dinner with a physicist colleague who kept insisting it was all just coordinate time, and I couldn't tell whether he was right or just changing the subject — that should probably show up in the proposal somewhere too.
