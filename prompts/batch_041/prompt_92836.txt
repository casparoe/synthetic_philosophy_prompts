I'm drafting a short position statement on the value of information for a reading group, and I'd like a consistency audit before I circulate it. Here is the package as it currently stands.

(1) Belief comes in degrees and is probabilistic. The rational response to new evidence is conditionalization, and there is no other.

(2) Practical rationality is expected-utility maximization, and I take the outcomes over which utility is defined to be able to include facts about my own epistemic situation — whether I am in a position to know some fact, whether anyone has told me. A preference for remaining in ignorance is a genuine preference about the shape of my life, not a confusion to be argued out of me.

(3) I accept the principle that free information cannot hurt, and I accept it as a theorem about rational agents as such rather than as a rule of thumb. If an experiment costs nothing and I will conditionalize on its result, then ex ante I cannot do worse for having the option to run it: whatever I would have done blind, the best thing I can do once the result is in does at least as well conditional on each result, and averaging over results preserves that. I also use it as a diagnostic. A decision rule that can be strictly hurt by free information is defective in virtue of that fact.

(4) Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion is defensible. An agent who prefers a bet on a coin she knows to be fair over a bet on an urn whose composition she does not know is not making a mistake; she dislikes not knowing the odds, and I do not want a theory of rationality that convicts her.

(5) The value of a piece of information is a feature of the whole decision problem rather than of the information itself. The same result can be a gift to one agent and a curse to another.

(6) Rational ignorance is possible and is not a failure of nerve. If learning something would only make my life worse and would not change anything I do, I would rather not learn it, and I would pay a modest sum to keep it from reaching me. Concretely: I would decline a test that can only confirm a condition I cannot treat, and I would pay to have a letter predicting the date of my death never arrive.

Each of these looks fine to me on its own, and I suspect that the package as a whole does not close. Tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be, being explicit about the ones that only look bad and why they are harmless. Rank the real tensions by severity. For each, say what the cheapest repair is and what it costs, and then tell me which single commitment is doing the most damage to the rest.
