I run a 14-person upper-level epistemology seminar, 75 minutes, and I want my students to feel pragmatic encroachment from the inside before I ever say the words. Here's the game I'm half-imagining — please flesh it into something actually runnable, with roles, materials, a timeline, and payoffs that don't need real money. Everyone gets a private signal about which of two boxes holds a token; say the signal is right 80% of the time and they know that's the reliability. Each round, before they're allowed to act on the signal, they have to tick one box: "I know which box it's in" or "I don't know." The only thing that varies between rounds is whats at stake if they act on a wrong signal — sometimes it costs them a point, sometimes a teammate loses a point, in one round the whole team does, and in one round the cost is just a bit of embarrassment. I want them to notice that their information never changed but their willingness to say "I know" did. Then a second phase: split the room, half judges a vignette character who has exactly the signal they had in the low-stakes round and half judges the same character in the high-stakes round, and the two halves compare answers out loud. The debrief should map what just happened onto pragmatic encroachment vs. contextualism vs. the boring invariantist line that we've only changed what people are willing to assert, not what they know — and be honest about which of those three the activity can't actually distinguish, since I'll have to supply that bit. Also tell me what'll predictably go wrong the first time; my guess is someone will insist they were never less certain, just less willing to bet, and I want to know what to do with that instead of arguing them out of it. No lecture up front, and the terms shouldn't slip until the debrief.
