A friend of mine is doing a PhD on slime molds — he has a video of a Physarum blobbing its way through a maze and finding the shortest path out, and another where a colony of them lays down a network that looks a lot like the Tokyo rail system. He's convinced this settles that intelligence is just adaptive problem-solving and has nothing essentially to do with brains, and honestly I think he mostly likes the argument because its his organism. Meanwhile I sit in a cognitive science reading group where someone insists nothing counts as intelligent unless it has a general-purpose ability to handle novelty, which would rule out the slime mold, the ant colony, my calculator, and — the actual point of the argument — the language models we're all quietly using to write our seminar papers. Both of them keep winning the exchange by producing a new case, and I've stopped learning anything.

Before I take a side I want the actual spec sheet, not another case-by-case fistfight. So give me the desiderata for a theory of intelligence: what any adequate account of what intelligence *is* has to deliver. Which cases does it absolutely have to get right? Think about the slime mold, an ant colony, an octopus whose mind is partly spread out into its arms, a chess engine, a calculator, a savant who does one thing impossibly well and nothing else, a system that aces every benchmark because it was trained on all of them, two people who score identically on the same task where one "gets it" and the other is pattern-matching, and the same model that looks sharp under one framing and hopeless under another. Which distinctions can't be collapsed — capacity vs. performance, intelligence vs. consciousness, being intelligent vs. merely being good at a task, being smart vs. having goals, individual vs. collective? Which empirical results does the theory have to sit with (that scores on wildly different tasks tend to correlate, the positive manifold / g thing, versus the fact that abilities can dissociate after brain injury; the large generational rise in IQ scores; the fact that performance on almost anything is heavily practice- and framing-dependent)? And tell me which of these are genuine requirements and which are just one camp's prejudice, plus which single requirement does the most work to knock out candidate theories.

Most of all I want to know where the list eats itself. Is there a pair of things I'm demanding that no theory can give me at once? My suspicion is it's somewhere between "intelligence has to be a single measurable quantity" and "the theory has to respect how differently abilities come apart," or maybe between "you have to be able to tell it apart from a clever fake" and "the only evidence you're allowed to use is behavior." If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and explain why — thats the part I'll actually use in my paper. And keep any scoring of existing theories brief; I dont need a tour of every contender, I need the list and the fault lines in it.
