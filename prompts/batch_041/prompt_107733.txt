For the past six months I have been working on an interpretability project, and I keep losing the same argument to a colleague. I look at a feature that fires on curves and say that the model noticed the curve; he says the activations did whatever they did and the feature is a convenient summary of mine, not a cause of anything the network does next. I assumed this was a turf dispute about vocabulary until I went back to Hofstadter's careenium, which I had read years ago as a curiosity and now cannot stop thinking about. The picture: a vast table covered in tiny magnetic marbles he calls simms, careening around and colliding; they clump into higher-order formations, simmballs, which track regularities in the world through the varying bounciness of the walls. Hofstadter's question is who pushes whom inside the careenium — simms pushing simms, or simmballs pushing simmballs. I have reconstructed the argument my colleague is implicitly running, and I cannot find the bad step, though I am sure there is one because it eats everything.

(1) Every simm's trajectory is fixed by the impacts it undergoes, from other simms and from the walls.
(2) Fix the initial conditions and the impact laws, and the entire history of the careenium follows. Nothing about simmballs is needed to compute it.
(3) If a fact is not needed to determine an outcome, that fact does not cause the outcome.
(4) A simmball is nothing over and above a fact about how simms are arranged.
(5) Therefore simmballs do not cause anything; only simms do.
(6) But the same reasoning applies at every level: molecules relative to atoms, cells relative to molecules, neurons relative to cells, and my colleague's decision to correct me relative to his neurons.
(7) Therefore nothing above the fundamental level causes anything, and all higher-level causal talk — including the claim that his objection caused me to revise this argument — is decorative.

I would most like to keep (1) and to deny (7), and I suspect the trouble is somewhere around (3) and (4) pulling against each other, but I cannot say what rejecting either one would cost me elsewhere, and I do not want a diagnosis that quietly buys its answer with a metaphysics I have not agreed to. Tell me which step or steps to give up, why that step looked compelling in the first place, and what the alternatives cost — in particular, whether the price is overdetermination, a level-relative notion of causation, or something I have not considered. If my framing is confused at the root, or if the argument as I have laid it out is not the one worth attacking, say so plainly rather than answering the question I asked. And tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis, and which part of it you would expect a competent philosopher to dispute.
