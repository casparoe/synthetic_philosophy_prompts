I'm a science journalist — I write a philosophy column for a general-reader mag — and next month I'm running a session at our little in-house "ideas club" on the endurantism/perdurantism thing, i.e. whether an object is wholly present at each moment or whether it persists by having temporal parts. The audience is smart but untrained: a few editors, two economists, a novelist, people who read the magazine we make. I don't need you to explain the debate to me — I basically get it — I need you to plan how I should explain it, roughly forty minutes with a whiteboard.

So: what order do the pieces have to go in, and why does each depend on the last? My instinct is I can't even state the two positions until they feel the problem of change (how can one and the same thing be straight and then bent?), but maybe you'd start elsewhere. Give me the one carrying example that should do the heavy lifting. I was going to use the Ship of Theseus and then a pub near me — The Anchor — that's had every fixture and owner replaced over thirty years while the regulars still call it the same pub (and there was a whole argument when it shut and a place down the road started calling itself "the real Anchor"); tell me if that's the right vehicle, what it has to have and lack, and whether it's doing the job I think or whether it smuggles in the wrong intuition. Then the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they'll show up and the exact point in the sequence where I head each off — I'm guessing "perdurantism means things are just heaps of slices / the object is destroyed every instant" and "endurantism can't allow real change," but I bet there are others (something about time being basically like space, maybe, or about "parts" always meaning spatial parts). And finally, one question I can put to the room at the end to see whether they actually got the distinction rather than just nodding along, plus what I should explicitly refuse to go into at this level — the temporary-intrinsics formalism, the four-dimensionalist treatment of modality, whatever — adn why.
