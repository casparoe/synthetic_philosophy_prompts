I work as a software engineer and read philosophy on the side, mostly because the older arguments about the will keep rhyming with problems I meet at work. Lately I've been stuck on the old puzzle of the donkey placed exactly midway between two identical bales of hay, which starves because it has no reason to prefer either one (the puzzle usually filed under Buridan). What interests me isn't the donkey but the assumption underneath it: that an agent can't act unless one option is preferable, so that a perfect tie amounts to a deadlock. In my own work we handle exactly this kind of symmetry by fiat — an arbitrary rule, a coin flip, a lowest-identifier tiebreak — not because the tie gives any reason to go one way, but because the choice doesn't need one. I'd like a poem that stays inside that tension instead of dissolving it, and I'd like the form to do the work rather than the commentary.

So: write the poem as a line-level mirror, a palindrome by lines. The first line and the last line are identical, the second and the second-to-last are identical, and so on inward, ending in a single unpaired line at the center. The two bales should be described so symmetrically that the reader genuinely cannot tell which is which, so that the poem's own shape is the donkey's problem made visible. The center line is where the choice was supposed to happen; it should register what actually happens there — the stillness, or the moment a tiebreak rule would have rescued it. The interesting constraint is that the same lines have to serve as both the approach to the center and the retreat from it, so they must bear a different sense on the way in than on the way out even though the words don't change, the way the two identical bales carry opposite meanings depending on which one you were already facing. Please keep it accurate enough that someone who has taught the puzzle wouldn't wince: the paralysis should come from the right assumption — that a will with no ground for preferring cannot move — and not from a cartoon about a stupid animal, and the poem should leave open whether the tiebreak escape hatch is a solution or a change of subject. Thirteen or fifteen lines will do, and I'd rather have plain diction than philosophy jargon, since I don't have the vocabulary for it. Begin with the poem itself, with no preface or commentary before or after it.
