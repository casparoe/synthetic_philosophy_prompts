A colleague in my department — a philosopher of mind whose own work is on perception, but who is happy to pronounce on anything — gave me the following thumbnail of Moore's paradox, and I'd like it audited before I lean on it in a seminar. This is what she wrote:

"Moore's paradox is the oddity of a sentence like 'It's raining, but I don't believe that it's raining.' The two halves pull against each other: the sentence tells you it's raining and tells you that I don't believe it, and since asserting something is just believing it out loud, the second half denies what the first half asserts. That's why the sentence cannot be true, and why it belongs to the same family as the Liar. Moore noticed that the oddity vanishes in the third person — 'It's raining, but she doesn't believe it's raining' is perfectly fine — and in the past tense, which is why we say the problem is confined to the first-person present tense. The explanation most philosophers now accept is that 'I believe that p' is not a description of an inner state but an avowal, a way of asserting p, so the whole sentence collapses into 'p and not p.' The moral is that first-person belief ascriptions are not reports at all, and that we have far less introspective access to our own beliefs than we assume."

I suspect this is roughly half right, but I can't locate the other half. Please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what someone working on Moore's paradox would accept, what they would wince at, and what is simply wrong. I am especially unsure about the claim that the sentence cannot be true, and about the avowal diagnosis — I have a vague sense that the puzzle is supposed to be precisely that the two halves can both be true, which would make her whole framing backwards. She also says nothing about the other familiar forms of the puzzle, 'It's raining, but I believe it isn't raining' and 'It's raining, but I don't know that it is raining'; tell me whether those are the same phenomenon as her example or different ones, and whether her diagnosis survives them. Where a claim is contested rather than settled, say so and say what the main options are; do not upgrade her confident phrasing into consensus on her behalf. And if the request is malformed — if, for instance, there is no single thing called 'Moore's paradox' and asking for a verdict is the wrong shape — tell me that plainly rather than assembling a verdict to order.
