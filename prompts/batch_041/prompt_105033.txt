I'm running a 90-minute session for a philosophy of biology reading group — about a dozen people, mostly philosophy grad students, a couple with some bio background, nobody who's a molecular biologist. Topic: whether DNA is a code, i.e. whether "genetic information" is information in the same sense we mean it anywhere else. Don't explain it to me; plan how I should explain it. What has to come before what — I'm fairly sure the Shannon-vs-semantic-information split has to land before the code question even makes sense, but tell me if that's wrong and what actually depends on what. Pick the one example that carries the whole thing and say why it works, and why not the blueprint, the recipe or the book — those are the ones they've all heard a million times. I want to run an arbitrariness test on them: the codon-to-amino-acid mapping is arbitrary the way a cipher's mapping is arbitrary, so make them decide whether that makes DNA more code-like or less, and if it helps, pivot on a remapped-code organism — a made-up lab bug with a shuffled codon table that builds the same proteins from different DNA. Misconceptions I expect: DNA "contains the message"; information flows one way so DNA must be where the instructions are; if the mapping is arbitrary it's just a convention we're imposing; and the quiet smuggling-in of a reader/homunculus whenever they say DNA "tells the cell what to do". Tell me which of those are real and which I'm imagining, the order they'll surface in, and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. Give me one closing question that shows whether they've actually got the distinction or have just learned to say "arbitrary". And tell me what to leave out at this level — and to say so out loud in the room.
