I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar that's spent two weeks circling the color phi phenomenon and I think I've finally built an argument that settles it, except the conclusion is so obviously insane that I now suspect the argument rather than the phenomenon. Quick setup so you know what I'm on about: you flash a red dot at position A, then a green dot at position B a fraction of a second later, and people report one dot travelling from A to B that changes color somewhere in the middle. Dennett's fork is that either the experience is "Stalinesque" -- the brain does the filling-in of motion and color before you're ever conscious of anything, so the moving color-changing dot is what you genuinely see -- or it's "Orwellian" -- you really have two separate flash experiences, and the travelling dot is a fiction your memory writes in afterward. Here's my argument.

(1) If two hypotheses about a conscious experience predict the same reports and the same everything measurable in the brain, then there's no fact of the matter which of them is true of what you actually experienced. (2) The Stalinesque and the Orwellian stories about color phi predict the same reports and the same brain measurements -- that's precisely why the debate never dies. (3) So there's no fact of the matter whether the color phi subject really experiences motion. (4) But nothing in (1) or (2) is special to color phi. For any experience you like, I can construct a Stalinesque story and an Orwellian story that agree on every report and every measurement. (5) So for no experience whatsoever is there a fact of the matter about its content or its timing. (6) Then "what it's like" is never determinate, which means there's no subject matter for a science of consciousness and, honestly, maybe nothing it's like to be anything at all.

I'd part with (3) long before I'd part with (6), and I'd hate to lose (2) since it seems like the whole reason people find this case interesting. So tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine to me when I wrote it, and what rejecting it costs -- specifically, once I've paid that price, is there anything left of the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction, or do I have to admit the whole thing was a distinction without a difference?
