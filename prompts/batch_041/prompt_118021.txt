Me and my brother have this running arguement about Nietzsche's essay on history — the one where he splits the historians into three tribes: the monumental ones who dig up the great dead and hold them up as templates to copy, the antiquarian ones who just want to lovingly keep everything the past ever touched, and the critical ones who drag the past into court and try to break with it. I get what he's doing with his own century, I don't need it recited back to me. What I can't stop chewing on is what he'd make of something that didn't exist while he was alive. He's been dead for over a century, so there's a lot of options.

Here's the case I keep coming back to. Say a big encyclopedic museum in Europe — the kind with alot of stuff in the basement that was, let's say, acquired under circumstances nobody wants to write a press release about — announces a "digital restitution" program. They 3D-scan a few thousand objects at ridiculous resolution, photogrammetry, the works, and put the models online for free. Source communities can download them, print them, put them in classrooms, do whatever. The physical objects stay where they are: in the vault, in the vitrine, on loan if you're lucky. And here's the part that starts fights — the museum licenses the scans under its own terms, claims copyright in the 3D model even when the object itself is centuries old, and one clause says no commercial use. So a carver in the source community can look at an ancestor's mask on a screen but can't sell a print of it.

The reactions are all over the place. Some community members say it's great, the kids can finally see the objects, the things come home in the only form available to them. Others say a scan isn't the thing: you can't dance in a JPEG, you can't feed it, you can't put it on the altar, and the whole program lets the museum feel decolonized while the actual stuff stays in Europe. One group asked the museum to take its scans down entirely, on the grounds that circulating images of certain objects is itself the harm; the museum complied quietly, and then other communities got annoyed because they wanted their material up. The museum's defenders say: we're not hoarding, we put it online, we didn't have to, and being careful about licensing is just stewardship.

So what would Nietzsche say? Not a summary of the essay, I want the prediction. If you dropped him into this argument with no briefing, which side would he drift to, and where would he refuse the terms of the whole debate? I keep going back and forth on whether the museum is the antiquarian party and the scan is the antiquarian's dream (the past preserved forever, untouched and untouchable), or whether the scan is really the monumental move (the past made usable, made exemplary, dragged into the present to do work), and whether the community asking for a takedown is the critical party — killing the past because it's too heavy to carry.

Structure it like this please:

- Bottom line: about 150 words. His overall verdict on the digital restitution program, up front, no hedging.
- The three lenses: roughly 500 words. What monumental, antiquarian and critical history would each make of the program, and which one he'd actually side with if you forced him.
- Where he'd annoy both sides: about 300 words. The take that gets him thrown out of the museum's press office and the activist meeting on the same afternoon.
- Where the framework breaks: about 250 words. The parts of this case his three categories can't handle — plus a sentence or two on whether the same categories make any sense of the consumer genealogy boom, people spitting in a tube to learn they're 3% something and building a family tree for a great-great-grandfather they'd have hated.
- My bets: about 150 words. Which of your predictions you'd actually put money on, and which are just you doing Nietzsche fan fiction.
