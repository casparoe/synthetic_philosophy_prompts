Exam question.

State the knowability principle precisely, defining your symbols. Then derive the paradox: show that the principle entails that no truth goes unknown, making clear at each step which principle of knowledge you are relying on. (a) Identify every principle the derivation requires. (b) Say whether the derivation is available to a reasoner who accepts only intuitionistic logic, and explain your answer. (c) Propose a restriction on the knowability principle that avoids the result, defend it as principled rather than ad hoc, and say what the restriction costs an anti-realist who wants to use knowability as a constraint on truth.

Student's answer.

Let 'Kp' abbreviate 'it is known by someone at some time that p', and let '◇p' abbreviate 'it is possible that p'.

The knowability principle (KP) says that for any proposition p, if p is true, then it is possible that p is known. In symbols: p → ◇Kp.

Assume there is a truth that no one knows. Call it q. So q ∧ ¬Kq is true. Applying KP to this conjunction gives (q ∧ ¬Kq) → ◇K(q ∧ ¬Kq), and by modus ponens, ◇K(q ∧ ¬Kq).

Now consider what knowing that conjunction would involve. Knowledge distributes over conjunction: whoever knows A ∧ B knows A and knows B. So K(q ∧ ¬Kq) implies Kq ∧ K¬Kq. Knowledge is factive: whatever is known is true, so K¬Kq implies ¬Kq. Therefore K(q ∧ ¬Kq) implies Kq ∧ ¬Kq, a contradiction. Hence it is impossible to know the conjunction: ¬◇K(q ∧ ¬Kq). But that contradicts the instance of KP we applied a moment ago. Since the assumption that generated the contradiction was that q is an unknown truth, there can be no unknown truths. Every truth is known; the knowability principle delivers omniscience.

(a) The derivation requires exactly three things: KP itself, the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, and the factivity of knowledge. Nothing further is needed. In particular it does not require the KK principle or any iteration of the knowledge operator.

(b) The derivation contains no essentially classical step. Distribution and factivity are applied by conjunction elimination and modus ponens, and the contradiction is reached without excluded middle or double negation elimination. Intuitionistic logic blocks only the final restatement: the intuitionist derives ¬(q ∧ ¬Kq) for every q rather than q → Kq. That is still a denial that any truth is unknown, so an anti-realist cannot escape the result by going intuitionist.

(c) The proposition q ∧ ¬Kq is a sentence that says of itself that it is not known, and that is what makes the paradox possible. It is a self-referential sentence of the same family as the liar, and, like Moore's paradox, it turns on the oddity of a sentence that takes its own epistemic status as part of its content. The remedy is to restrict KP so that it cannot be applied to sentences of this kind. I would restrict it to propositions that are neither self-referential nor about knowledge: for any proposition p whose content does not concern what is or can be known, p → ◇Kp.

This blocks the derivation, since q ∧ ¬Kq is about knowledge and so falls outside the restricted principle. The restriction is principled rather than ad hoc, because it preserves the anti-realist's central thought — that truths about the world are knowable — while excluding a sentence that the principle was never meant to cover. It costs the anti-realist nothing important, since no one ever supposed that the knowability principle was meant to tell us what people actually know.
