Ok so my mate Dan and I got into it last night about corruption and I need help working out where the actual disagreement lies. Setup: Dan is an anthropology type and his line was that corruption is basically a Western bureaucratic export, that what a customs officer in Lagos calls a gift his counterpart in Berlin calls a bribe, and that the whole anti-corruption business is one culture's public/private distinction being imposed on everyone else. My line was that a minister who loots the school budget so his nephew can buy a Mercedes is corrupt full stop, whatever the local story is. We got nowhere and I think we were sliding between about four different questions without noticing.

So: your a political philosopher who works on comparative politics and the anthropology of exchange, bring both hats. First brainstorm the fault lines in a few lines, then build me a decision tree I can hand to a seminar.

The tree should start from the question of whether corruption names a real act-type or only a standpoint, and every node below it should be a question someone can answer without already knowing which position it lands them in. I want the branches to be the real hinges: whether the wrongness is breach of the rules of an office, or undue influence on impartiality, or harm/extraction, or betrayal of people who depend on you; whether the standard is the participants' own understanding of the practice or something external to it; and where the gift/bribe line falls — intent, size, timing, an explicit quid pro quo, or the fact that the recipient holds an office carrying duties of impartiality.

Two things I want handled properly. (1) A colleague mentioned a two publics idea: in some postcolonial settings there's a civic public inherited from the colonial state and a second public built out of kinship and community obligation, so the same payment is theft against one and duty toward the other. I want that as a live branch, not a footnote. (2) The grease-the-wheels thing — if the payment genuinely makes something happen that otherwise wouldn't, does the counterfactual change the verdict, and does that make it corruption or just a rational response to bad institutions?

Leaves should be named positions — universalist, legalist/formalist, principal-agent, collective-action/equilibrium, functionalist, particularist-clientelist, the anti-universalist critique, and a thin-core-thick-local compromise — one sentence each on its most awkward commitment. Where your tree produces combinations nobody actually occupies (e.g. timing alone decides the gift/bribe line), mark them empty and say whether that's incoherence, unattractiveness or just nobody having bothered. And flag any two leaves the tree can't separate; I suspect the collective-action view and the anti-universalist view make the same predictions about why campaigns fail while disagreeing about whether they should, so tell me if there's a question that splits them or not. Concrete cases throughout: the carton of cigarettes, the gift to the surgeon after the operation, the nephew hired over a better candidate, the perfectly legal party donation. Aim for nine or ten leaves and don't pad it. Think carefully about the hinges — if a node only sorts labels instead of cases, cut it.
