Question.

Debate is often proposed as a technique for scalable oversight: two agents are shown the same question, each argues for an answer, and a judge that could not answer the question on its own decides which agent made the better case. Suppose the agents may be substantially more capable than the judge, and suppose the question has a definite correct answer.

(a) Explain the mechanism by which debate is supposed to let a relatively weak judge oversee substantially stronger agents. Why does the judge not need to be able to answer the question itself? (6 points)

(b) A standard objection is that a dishonest debater can bury the flaw in its reasoning in a step that is too subtle or too costly for the judge to check, and thereby win. What is the usual reply to this objection, and what does that reply assume about the debaters and about the judge? (8 points)

(c) Debate is sometimes contrasted with non-adversarial approaches in which the overseer recursively decomposes a hard task into subtasks it can already perform and then trains a model to imitate that decomposition. Identify one substantive difference in what the two approaches assume about the overseer, and give one reason someone might prefer debate. (6 points)

Student's answer.

(a) The judge in debate is not being asked to solve the problem; it is being asked to choose between two solutions that have already been constructed. The protocol exploits the gap between finding an answer and recognizing one: for many questions, searching for the answer is much harder than checking a proposed answer, so debate hands the search to the debaters and keeps the checking for the judge. Because the debaters can be far stronger than the judge, and because they are in competition, each has an incentive to expose the weaknesses in the other's reasoning, which a single agent working alone would not reliably do. The judge's task is therefore easier than the original question in the same way that checking a proof is easier than finding one, and the protocol can in principle let a weak overseer supervise a strong model.

(b) The objection is that the dishonest debater need not produce an obviously bad argument; it can produce an argument whose flaw is hidden in a step that the judge cannot afford to examine, in which case the judge has no basis for preferring the honest argument and may simply pick the dishonest one. The usual reply is that the honest debater has both the ability and the incentive to point to the specific step at which the dishonest argument fails, and the judge can then check that step. This turns a global question (is the whole argument sound?) into a local one (is this step sound?), which is within the judge's competence. The reply therefore assumes that the honest debater can locate the flaw, and that the judge can check any single step the honest debater identifies. It does not assume that the judge can decompose the problem itself, which is the point of the protocol. With an honest debater present, the obfuscated-argument problem is essentially dissolved.

(c) The difference is that debate does not require the overseer to break the task into pieces it can already handle; the decomposition, if there is one, is produced by the debaters and merely evaluated by the judge, whereas the non-adversarial approach requires the overseer itself to generate a decomposition that is correct and whose pieces lie within its competence. In other words, debate assumes nothing about the overseer except that it can tell a better argument from a worse one, while recursive decomposition assumes that the overseer is already competent on the subproblems. One reason to prefer debate is that its assumption is weaker, so it should keep working as models outrun their overseers; another is that the adversarial structure means that any error the honest debater fails to catch will be caught by the other side, so the protocol is robust to the judge's mistakes.
