My friend Dara takes the citizenship oath on Friday and she's asked me to do the short speech at the ceremony -- you know the kind, a judge, a room of new citizens from about thirty countries, their families, cake in the lobby after. I've got five minutes and a microphone and I'd like to spend them on something I actually believe instead of boilerplate about journeys and dreams. Problem is the boilerplate is basically "love your new country," and I'm not sure that's a virtue. Two things I've never been able to shake. It's arbitrary -- nobody picks where they're born or which visa lottery they win, so why should the accident of where you happen to live generate any special loyalty? And it looks like it licenses partiality: if I love my country, do I get to care more about my compatriots than about a stranger abroad who needs the same thing more? If yes, that's a vice with a nice name. If no, whats left of patriotism besides liking the food?

There's something on the other side though, and I want it in the speech without pretending the objections aren't there. Best I've got, and I don't have a name for it, is that patriotism is less like love and more like a promise: not a feeling about a place but a willingness to be held answerable for it, to have its failures count as yours. On that reading the virtue isn't warmth, it's accountability, and you can do it well or badly, and you can owe it to a country you don't even like much. What I can't work out is whether that survives the arbitrariness point at all, and whether it means Dara is now on the hook for things that happened here long before she arrived, which is either the whole point or a category error and I genuinely can't tell which. Also her mother is flying in. She left her own country thirty years ago and still tears up at its anthem, and I have no idea what to say to her about whether you're only allowed to be loyal to one place at a time.

So write me the speech. Five minutes, meant to be said out loud, honest and warm and definately not a seminar -- nobody in that room signed up for a lecture. Dara is a political philosopher who's spent ten years arguing that patriotism is a vice and says she's doing this for the legal standing and the right to complain, so she'll spot it instantly if I smooth the objections over with sentiment. Then give me a couple of sentences on which claims my answerability idea actually commits me to, so I don't discover mid-speech that I've asserted something I can't defend. No sources, no quotes -- just the speech and the note.
