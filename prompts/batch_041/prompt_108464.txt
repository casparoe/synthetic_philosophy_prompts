Ok so I've been lurking on LessWrong and the surrounding blogosphere for like a decade and a half, and there's one fight that keeps bugging me because it never, ever moves: the reception of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against moral responsibility. You know the shape of it. You do what you do because of the way you are. So to be truly responsible for what you do, you'd have to be truly responsible for the way you are. But to be responsible for the way you are, you'd have to have chosen to be that way, and choosing is itself something you do out of the way you already are, so you need to be responsible for that earlier way too, and so on back forever — or else you have to be self-created out of nothing, causa sui, which is impossible. Conclusion: nobody is ever truly deserving of blame or praise, and Strawson says this is basically an a priori truth, so let's all be pessimists and switch to the objective attitude.

And then nothing happens. The compatibilists and honestly most working philosophers hear the argument and kind of shrug: sure, nobody is self-made, nobody ever claimed otherwise, but what we mean by responsibility is being responsive to reasons, or the reactive attitudes being fitting, or just the social practice of holding each other to account — none of which needs a self-caused self. And the pessimists hear that and say yeah, but that's not responsibility, thats a consolation prize for people who want to keep blaming each other without the metaphysical backing. His dad's whole reactive-attitudes tradition is basically the thing he's pointing at and saying no thanks to. Some people also say the argument proves too much, that the same regress would gut any notion of ultimate self-determination anywhere, which suggests it's the demand for self-creation that's confused rather than the compatibilist. Everyone knows everyone's moves, every reply is on the table, and the two camps just restate themselves at each other.

What I want is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, not another round of the argument itself. My working theory — test it, don't just agree with me — is that it's largely verbal, because the pessimist defines the target so that it already includes self-creation, so of course the target comes out impossible, and no compatibilist ever signed up for that target. But I don't fully buy my own theory, because there's a nagging substantive question under it: do our actual practices of blaming and resenting and punishing presuppose the strong thing? If ordinary blame implicitly commits us to the idea that the wrongdoer could have been different all the way down, then something real is at stake and it isn't just a fight over a word. If it doesn't, the compatibilist wins by default and the whole thing is people talking past each other.

So: is this a verbal dispute, two camps answering different questions under one heading (one about desert, one about accountability), a hidden empirical question about what our blaming practices really commit us to, or a genuine substantive disagreement about a single shared question that one side is just wrong about? Steelman both sides as concretely as you can, tell me where the crux actually lives, and then tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, an empirical result about how blame and resentment actually work, a proof that one side's central notion is incoherent, or an honest verdict that nothing will move it because the two sides start from intuitions neither can argue for. And if my verbal-dispute theory is wrong, say so and say why.
