A few things I want covered, and a couple of my own hobby horses that you should treat as data rather than doctrine:

- the relata question: omissions aren't events, so does a theory owe us an account of what the causal relata even are, or can it just treat "the omission" as a placeholder and move on?
- the ordinary cases (the gardener who didn't water, the lifeguard on his phone) versus the swamp worry -- if you make absence causation cheap, my not watering your plants is a cause, and so is the Queen of England's not watering them.
- double omission and preemption among omissions: the resident skipped the handover, so the on-call doctor was never told to check the potassium. Is that one omission or two, and which one is the cause?
- omissions that belong to nobody: a security system that didn't fire because the sensor was never installed. No agent, no expectation, still feels causal.
- the norm thing, which I keep going back and forth on: we're much happier calling something a cause when the omitted act was expected or required -- the driver who didn't brake versus the stranger who didn't jump in. Is that a fact the theory has to accommodate, or a bias it should debunk?
- the legal asymmetry: liability for acts versus liability for omissions, the no-duty-to-rescue default. Does the metaphysics have to line up with the law, or are those just different games?

For each desideratum, also say which approach in the area it most embarrasses, if you can do that without turning it into a scorecard. And end with what would change your mind about which requirement is the load-bearing one, plus where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong -- I'd rather see you flag the soft spot than pretend there isn't one.
