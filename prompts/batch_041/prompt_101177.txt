I am a philosopher of science who has drifted into the interpretability literature over the past few years, and I am now trying to design a small research program rather than write one more standalone paper. Suppose I have three years and roughly two full-time collaborators: one philosopher with training in causal inference, and one engineer who can run experiments on open-weight models. Our funder wants a defended agenda, not a survey. My aim is not to settle whether neural networks have representations, but to identify which open questions in that vicinity most deserve our effort, and in what order.

To make the question tractable, let me stake out a framing I want you to work within, and to attack if you think it is confused. I find it useful to separate three claims that the phrase the model represents F routinely conflates. First, the model's internal states carry information about F, in the sense that F is decodable from them. Second, those states are causally responsible for the model's F-appropriate behaviour, in the sense that intervening on them changes that behaviour in the expected way. Third, those states are representations in the demanding sense: they are about F, they can misrepresent F, and they figure in something like the model's own explanation of its output. Call the view that the third claim is often true of trained networks representational realism, and the view that the artefacts of our interpretability tools, such as probes, feature visualizations, and sparse decompositions, are true descriptions of the network, interpretability realism. The interesting possibility is that these two come apart, and that much of the field's heat comes from sliding between them.

Given that framing, and given the three years and two collaborators, I want your prioritization. Which open questions here are most worth our time? Candidates I am aware of include: whether the linear representation hypothesis is a discovery or an artefact of the probes used to test it; whether superposition makes any particular feature-attribution claim well-defined; whether the right notion of representation for networks is causal, informational, or teleological, and whether appeal to the training objective can ground the teleological one; whether interpretability claims should be required to license counterfactual predictions, and what the right counterfactual is when the network is one of many that could have been trained; and whether the realism question is even well-posed absent a prior account of what would count as misrepresentation for a system with no consumers.

I am less interested in the list than in the ordering and the reasoning behind it. For each question: is it upstream of the others, in the sense that settling it changes what counts as evidence for the rest? Is it tractable for a philosopher working with one experimentalist, or does it in fact require large-scale empirical work we cannot do? Is it neglected, or is it crowded? And how much would its answer change downstream work, that is, would resolving it change practice in interpretability, or only the philosophy? Where two questions pull in opposite directions on importance and tractability, say how you trade them off.

Finally, be explicit about your own confidence and its limits. Tell me where this reasoning is most likely to fail, and which argument or piece of evidence would most change your ordering, including any that would lead you to conclude that the framing I proposed is the wrong one to organize the program around.
