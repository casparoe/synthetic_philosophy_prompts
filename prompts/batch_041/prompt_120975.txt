I am a novelist, several drafts into a book set in Paris in the 1680s and 1690s, and I have hit a question that is historiographical before it is dramatic. My protagonist is a minor man of letters, the client of a great man, who believes with real passion that his own age has surpassed antiquity, and the book turns on his discovery that this belief costs him more than he bargained for. To write him honestly I have to know what a person of his moment could have thought about the moment itself — whether he could have experienced his own century as a period, as something with edges, or whether that is only my hindsight leaking into his head. I know the current line on the Middle Ages, the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution: that these are instruments of later scholarship rather than discoveries about the past, and I find it persuasive and, as a novelist, useless, since I cannot put a man on the page who lives inside a construction without making him either a fool or a seminar. So I have built a small apparatus of my own, and I want it tested rather than admired.

I distinguish three ways a period-label can be meant. First, the period as discovery: something in the world really did break, and the label names the break. Second, the period as filing system: nothing in the world answers to it, it is a tool for arranging the archive, and its only virtue is usefulness, so long as nobody mistakes the drawer for the thing. Third, which is the one I care about, the period as project: the label is something the actors themselves take up and contend over, so that calling your own age a new age is a move made in the present, with stakes, allies and consequences, and the invention and the reality of the period stop being rivals, because the invention becomes a cause. I suspect the third mode is doing most of the work in the decades I am writing about. I also suspect I am flattering myself, and that the third is merely the second with a protagonist attached.

What I want is a case analysis, run in both directions, of the quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns in France — the dispute that took its name from Perrault's Parallel of the Ancients and the Moderns, and that set Perrault and the partisans of the present age, who had the Académie and the court behind them, against Boileau and those who held that the ancients had already been ratified by twenty centuries of admiration; a quarrel with a long afterlife, which is one reason I want to understand it. I am not asking for a survey of what historians have said about it. Tell me what my three modes say the participants were actually doing: which of them was periodizing, and in which mode; whether the Ancients had a periodization of their own or only a criterion of value, and whether a modern is even conceivable without an ancient to be modern against; and what the institutional facts — patronage, the Académie, the fact that the Moderns' case was also the case for the monarchy — do to the analysis. Then be honest about what the apparatus cannot reach. I suspect the quarrel was at bottom about authority and the test of value rather than about time, and I want to know whether my third mode survives that observation or is just a picturesque name for a power struggle. Finally, turn it around: what does the episode show about the framework rather than the other way — does it confirm the third mode, embarrass it, or present a case the framework never anticipated? And if the third mode collapses into the second on inspection, say so plainly, because my protagonist's dignity depends on the difference.
