I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rationality and social interaction — eighteen students, one eighty-minute session, a whiteboard and a projector, no laptops — and I want to build a single session around an idea from the game-theory literature on agents that submit programs rather than moves, because I have watched students nod at it for years without, I think, feeling anything. The idea is this. In a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma with no repetition, no reputation, no enforcement, and no shadow of the future, mutual defection is the only thing two ordinary players can do, and it is forced by the structure of the choice. Change the objects of choice, though — have each player submit a rule that is handed to the other player's rule as an input, with the two rules then executed to produce moves, and with neither player able to revise after submitting — and cooperation becomes available, not because anyone became nicer but because the space of things you can choose is now rich enough to make your move depend on the other player's disposition rather than on their move. I want a staged activity, with rules, materials, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, and a payoff table the students can see, in which they first feel the floor fall out (they play the one-shot game in pairs and defection is obviously right, and boringly right) and then feel cooperation appear once the thing they hand in is a rule that can read the other player's rule. Nothing in the activity should tell them that this is what I am up to, and the terms of art should not be spoken until the debrief. One practical constraint: I would rather not hand-execute self-referential rules live in front of eighteen people, because I will make a mistake — so either the students run it themselves with a scoring table and a couple of referees, or the rule language is narrow enough that execution is trivial and checkable by a student who has never seen it before.

The second half matters to me more, and it should be built into the same activity rather than bolted on as a discussion. The cooperative rule the group converges on will be some version of: cooperate if the other player's rule is the same as mine, otherwise defect. Two things should then happen to them. First, they should try to beat it. The obvious attack is a rule that does the reverse of whatever the target's rule does, and they should discover what actually becomes of the attacker, and that the target's safety has nothing to do with the attacker's restraint or good will; and they should then discover how thin that safety is, that it evaporates when the two rules are merely almost the same, so that what looked like a solution to the problem of cooperation turns out to require two people reasoning independently to have produced the same artifact down to the character — which is a strange thing to require, and I want them to feel the strangeness rather than be told about it. Second, if it can be done without a blackboard proof, I want them to feel the pull of the natural repair — cooperate with anyone who cooperates with me — and to find that it is far harder to state than it sounds, and that the version which does work gets its work done by a self-referential step that feels illegitimate the first time you write it down. (If a short third stage fits without eating the clock, I would like one in which a player may decline to be readable at all; my guess is that this is self-defeating and that they should discover as much, but I may be wrong, and I would rather the design settle it than my guess.) For the debrief I want the honest ceiling stated: my understanding is that the payoffs achievable this way are exactly those repeated play could already achieve — feasible and individually rational, in the folk-theorem sense — so the trick wins cooperation in a one-shot game while buying no new outcomes at all; if that framing is too glib, correct it rather than repeating it back to me. I also have a nagging memory that the two standard mechanisms in this literature — the one that compares the opponent's code to its own, and the one that cooperates when it can prove the opponent cooperates against it — do not in fact cooperate with each other, since neither can establish what it needs about the other; if that is right it belongs in the debrief, and if I have garbled it, say so plainly instead of building on it.

Commit to one design rather than handing me a menu: the rules and materials, the payoff scheme, the roles, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief, with an explicit mapping from each moment of the activity to the piece of the idea it is supposed to deliver. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle each failure, and be straight with me about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by talking — my own guess is that it is the proof-theoretic part, that the cooperative fixed point has to be argued for rather than agreed on, but tell me if you think the unreproducible part lies somewhere else. Finally, tell me where your design takes liberties: any place where the scoring scheme, the pairing, or the rule language quietly smuggles in an assumption that the underlying idea does not license.
