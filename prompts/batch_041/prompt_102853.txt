In my research group we rank charitable causes using the triad of importance, tractability, and neglectedness — how large the problem is, how much progress a given resource can buy against it, and how few others are already working on it. Two members, Dana and Miguel, spent an afternoon arguing over whether neglectedness is a genuine third criterion or a disguised restatement of the first. I stayed out of it, and I would now like a referee's verdict, move by move.

Dana opened: Neglectedness is not an independent criterion. To call a cause neglected is just to say that an additional unit of resources would do more good there than elsewhere. But "an additional unit of resources would do more good here" is precisely what we mean by the cause's importance, once importance is understood as the marginal value of intervention rather than the sheer size of the problem. So the triad is redundant. Neglectedness is importance seen from the supply side, and retaining both merely gives us the false comfort of having triangulated one quantity from three directions.

Miguel replied that the two criteria visibly come apart, and in both directions. A cause can be overwhelmingly important and yet not neglected at all — a looming catastrophe that every competent actor is already racing to prevent — and a cause can be neglected without being important — a small, unfashionable problem that no one bothers with because it is small. If importance already contained neglectedness, he said, these cases could not arise.

Dana answered that the cases arise only because Miguel is smuggling a different sense of importance back in: size of the problem rather than marginal value of resources. State importance correctly, as marginal value, and the counterexamples dissolve. The catastrophe everyone is already racing toward has low marginal value because it is crowded; the unfashionable small problem has low marginal value because it is small. Both rank correctly under importance alone. Neglectedness, she concluded, is at best a cheap proxy for a quantity we usually cannot compute.

Miguel conceded the arithmetic and denied the conclusion. Importance, he said, is the size of the prize, tractability the probability of reaching it, and neglectedness the room left for a marginal entrant; each is a proxy for an uncomputable marginal value, so Dana's reduction proves only that all three are proxies, not that any one is dispensable. He then added a point he claimed Dana had not touched. Neglectedness, he said, is the only one of the three that is a fact about other actors rather than about the cause itself. Importance and tractability would be what they are if every other agent vanished; neglectedness would not. And that makes it self-undermining in a way the others are not: if neglectedness is a reason to enter, then a cause that many agents judge neglected on that very ground becomes less neglected, so the criterion, applied in concert, erodes the feature it cites.

I want to know who is right about what. Please adjudicate the four moves in turn, saying for each whether it is sound, partly right, or mistaken, and why — not merely who prevails overall. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing. In particular: is Miguel's self-undermining point fatal to neglectedness as a criterion, or does it only show that the criterion has to be applied with some thought about what other agents will do? And does Dana's reduction succeed if we insist on defining importance as marginal value, or does it quietly change the subject from the framework's actual use as a decision heuristic for agents who cannot compute marginal value at all?
