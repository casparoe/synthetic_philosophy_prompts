I don't need you to explain it to me; I know the material well enough. What I want is a plan for how I should explain it to them, in about fifty minutes with a whiteboard.

So: what has to come first for the skeptical move to land as a genuine problem rather than a parlor trick? My instinct is that the group has to feel the pull of "a rule determines every case in advance" before they can feel it snap, but tell me if that's the wrong first move or if something else has to be in place before even that lands. What example should carry the whole thing? I'd rather not use plus and quus — at least two of them have heard it on a podcast and will get smug — so I want something non-arithmetical that a non-philosopher can hold in their head and that still does the same job: a finite history of use, an indefinite range of future cases, and a rival interpretation that fits everything so far. Tell me what such an example must have and must not have, and why the obvious substitutes (color words, grammar, a recipe, following a signpost) either work or don't. Then the misconceptions. Give me the order the pieces have to go in and why each depends on the last, exactly where in that order I head off each misconception, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud, and one question I can ask at the end that tells me whether they actually got the paradox rather than just the punchline. Definately keep it under 700 words.
