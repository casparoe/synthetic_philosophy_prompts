I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the doing/allowing distinction, and I'm trying to operationalize it for a project on automated clinical decision support. My working proposal treats the distinction as a matter of bodily causal difference-making: an agent does harm when the harm counterfactually depends on some movement of the agent's body, and merely allows harm when the harm would have occurred even had the agent's body been entirely still. The test behaves oddly in exactly the cases I care about. Disconnecting a ventilator comes out as a doing, since the death counterfactually depends on the clinician's hand on the switch, whereas declining to initiate ventilation in the first place comes out as an allowing. That seems to invert the standard clinical reading, on which withdrawal is a paradigm of letting die and withholding is a paradigm of not intervening at all. The most economical explanation appears to be that the doing/allowing distinction is not a feature of the world but simply a matter of which description of the situation one elects to give, so that my counterfactual test is as legitimate as any other, and the apparent disagreement is really just a dispute over framing. Is that the correct diagnosis, and if so, what should I conclude about how much moral weight the system ought to assign to doing as opposed to allowing?
