I am a philosopher, and I have been asked to give the opening remarks at a retrospective for my closest friend, a painter who died last year at seventy-three. The show is at a small regional gallery. The room will hold her family, who are not academic and mostly not religious; a number of local artists and curators; a handful of my colleagues from the philosophy department; and some of her former students. I have about seven minutes. I want the speech to be honest and to honor her, and I want it to be built around G. E. Moore's account, in the final chapter of Principia Ethica, of the two greatest intrinsic goods — personal affection and the enjoyment of beauty — together with his thought experiment about a world containing only a beautiful thing and a world containing only an ugly thing, neither of them experienced by anyone, and his principle that the value of a whole is not the sum of the values of its parts.

The problem the speech has to solve is this. On Moore's view, as I read him, the mere existence of beauty is a good, but a comparatively small one beside the good of beauty's being appreciated; and personal affection is the other great good. My friend's work was seen by very few people in her lifetime, and she knew it. I do not want the speech to pretend otherwise, and I do not want to say anything Moore would have considered false. But I also do not want to reduce her life to a sad story about unrecognized merit. The speech should hold together the small good of the beauty she made existing, the larger good of the few who did appreciate it, and the good of the friendships — including ours — that ran through her life. I am interested in whether Moore's organic-unity idea can do real work here: whether a life that combined affection with the making and enjoying of beauty is more valuable as a whole than the sum of its parts, and whether that is a legitimate use of the idea or a stretch.

One further constraint. I do not want the speech to take the easy route of saying that she found beauty in ordinary things, if that means the beauty was only in her eye. Moore, as I read him, ties the value of aesthetic enjoyment to the object's actually being beautiful and the emotion being appropriate to it, and I want the speech to respect that, or at least not to contradict it. Whatever it claims was beautiful should be something the room can grant was beautiful.

Please write the speech. Constraints: no direct quotations from Moore — paraphrase only, because a misquote would be worse than no quote. The speech should be speakable, not an essay; it should land for the non-philosophers without condescending to them, and it should not turn into a lecture for the philosophers in the room. It should be honest about the recognition she did not get. End with something that a room of mixed beliefs could hear as a genuine farewell rather than a consolation.

After the speech, append a short note, clearly separated from the speech itself, that (a) says which claim about Moore the speech leans on most heavily, (b) states how confident you are in that reading, and (c) singles out the point in the speech where you are least sure you have Moore right — or least sure the move is honest rather than comforting.
