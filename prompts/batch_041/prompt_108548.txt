My brother has spent the past year reading Fomenko's new chronology and is now about halfway convinced that the standard chronology of antiquity is a fabrication; when I cite mainstream historians he treats it as proof that I have swallowed the official account. I have stopped trying to argue him out of it and want instead a map of where the disagreement actually lives.

Write me a decision tree. Take the role of a philosopher of science and of history who knows the demarcation literature well, and begin by brainstorming the ways a chronological or archaeological claim can go wrong — build the space of positions first and only then drop names into it, rather than starting from a list of famous views.

The tree should have eight to ten branch nodes, each phrased as a question my brother could answer without having to accept any label, and each leaf named and given one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The nodes should be the distinctions that do the real work: whether there is a principled criterion at all or only a contrast between well- and badly-conducted inquiry; whether the defect in a pseudohistorical claim lies in its evidence, in its method (self-sealing argument, ad hoc rescue, unfalsifiability), in its conclusions, or in the social organization of the community that holds it; whether such a claim is ordinary history with an unusual conclusion or a rival genre with its own conventions; whether the demand for a criterion is itself a mistake, given that historiography is theory-laden, that the evidence underdetermines the past, and that consensus is a social fact; and what follows practically — debunk, engage, ignore, or inoculate.

Build the tree from the logical space: give me the questions and the answer-combinations they permit, then locate the positions in that space. Mark unoccupied combinations and say briefly whether they are empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any pair of positions your tree cannot separate. Use concrete cases at the nodes where you can: a documented forgery, an artifact said to prove ancient contact, a claim that an entire century's records were rewritten, the move that absence of evidence is itself the cover-up. Include as leaves at least the view that the mainstream account is the real pseudohistory, and the view that the distinction is genuine but purely sociological rather than epistemic.

Close with a short note on which leaf I should expect my brother to land on, and what, if anything, would move him off it.
