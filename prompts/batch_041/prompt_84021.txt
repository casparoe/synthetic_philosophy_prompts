I write about the social sciences and big ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am about to file a long feature on generational discourse — the whole Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z apparatus. Before I commit to the argument I want a consistency check on my own position, since I suspect I have been quietly borrowing from more than one picture of what a social kind is. Here, roughly, is where I have landed:

(1) Generations are real social kinds: the years you are born into shape the formative environment that leaves a durable mark on your dispositions and outlook.
(2) But the names and the cut-offs are conventional. They were coined by demographers and marketers, they get redrawn every few years, and they do not carve nature at any joint.
(3) A social kind is real, where it is real, because people take it up: the label has to be lived, to organize identity and institutions from the inside, not merely to appear as a coefficient in someone's regression.
(4) I am nonetheless a realist about cohort effects. I think there is a fact of the matter about whether, say, entering the labor market during a deep recession leaves a lasting mark on the people who did so — whether or not they ever come to think of themselves as a group.
(5) I try to assert only what could in principle be identified empirically, and I accept the standard point that age, period and cohort are linearly confounded in repeated cross-sections, so a cohort effect can be estimated only by imposing a constraint the data cannot check.
(6) None of this will stop me from using the labels in my own copy, since they are useful shorthand.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and what would have to give? Please rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest repair is and what that repair costs me; then tell me which single revision would do the most to clean up the set. Keep the whole reply under 600 words.
