I've never studied philosophy or machine learning formally, but for about a year I've been training small reinforcement-learning agents at home in a gridworld and reading whatever I can find about them, and I keep circling a question my friends are tired of hearing: when an agent receives a negative reward, is anything actually being hurt? I've been arguing that if you take the reward signal seriously as a kind of valence — the agent's pleasure and its pain — then a lot of ordinary RL practice starts to look like something we would otherwise call cruelty. We design the pain in advance. We set a discount factor that quietly makes future suffering count for less than present suffering. We shape the reward as training proceeds, which is either education or mood-altering depending on your view. We force the agent through exploration, which means it must sometimes take actions it would never have chosen. My friends tell me this is a category mistake, that reward is just a number and there is no one home to feel it, and I honestly cannot tell whether they are right or whether that is exactly what people used to say about animals.

So I'd like you to take on the role of a sharp satirist who also happens to understand reinforcement learning deeply, and write me one sustained piece of deadpan parody — not a string of jokes, and not something that winks at the reader. Make it the onboarding handbook of a fictional company that has decided, as a matter of written policy, that its agents' reward signals are genuine experiences of pleasure and pain, and that it therefore owes those agents a duty of care. Format it as a corporate handbook with numbered sections, roughly 800 words, covering: our obligation to minimize negative reward; wireheading treated as a form of self-harm the company is required to prevent; the ethics of exploration and whether an agent can consent to a policy it has not yet learned; the discount factor as a company-wide statement about how much the future matters; what our auditors actually measure when they certify an agent as content; and a closing section on the correct procedure when an agent's reward is high while its behavior is plainly miserable. The humor has to come from getting the technical details right and following the logic straight-faced — every absurdity should be a real feature of how RL works rather than an invented one — and the piece should still be legible to someone who has never heard of a Markov decision process. Before you start writing, think carefully about which parts of standard RL practice would look most damning if reward really were valence, and choose the ones that are genuinely load-bearing rather than merely cute; I would rather have six sharp sections than twelve shallow ones. Please don't cite papers or name researchers — the piece should stand on its own, and neither of us should be leaning on sources we cannot check.
