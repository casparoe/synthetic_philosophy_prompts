For the last few weeks I have been trying to write a seminar paper for a course on the philosophy of the arts, and I keep getting stuck on a disagreement with a friend of mine who directs for a small opera company. The disagreement is about what the relationship between music and drama in opera is supposed to be. My friend holds what I take to be the classical Wagnerian integrationist ideal: an opera is a single unified artwork in which music and text jointly deliver one coherent dramatic content, so that wherever the music and the libretto pull apart — a character whose music sounds serene while the words confess a betrayal, a finale whose musical shape is satisfying but whose plot is a dead end — something has gone wrong, and a good composer or a good production would repair it. He also likes to say, and I suspect this is a second principle smuggled in under the same label, that music which merely restates what the words already state is dramatically redundant, and that music earns its place only by adding something the words lack. Against him I have been trying to reconstruct a position I associate with Bernard Williams's occasional writings on opera, though I am probably mangling it: on that view the music of an opera is not a vehicle for the drama at all but has expressive capacities the drama lacks, so that the friction between the two is constitutive of the art form rather than a defect in it, and to judge an opera by dramatic standards alone — coherence of plot, consistency of character, plausibility of motivation — is a kind of category mistake.

I would like you to compare these two positions. First, do they genuinely contradict one another, or do they differ only over what is to count as the drama? If the integrationist quietly widens the notion of dramatic content to include whatever the music expresses, the dispute threatens to become terminological, and I want to know whether you think it can be kept substantive and what would settle it. Second, construct a type of case in which the two views yield opposite verdicts about whether some moment or work is good, and explain what makes the case discriminating rather than merely illustrative; the paper keeps threatening to degenerate into the sort of thing where both views are said to capture something important, which I am trying to avoid. The moment I have been using is a scene from an invented two-act chamber opera in which a dying woman's last monologue is set to music of great serenity while the text is a list of ordinary domestic grievances, but you should feel free to replace it with a better one. Third, tell me which of the two positions you find more defensible, and give the strongest consideration on the losing side its due. Fourth, ask whether any version of integrationism survives — say one that demands unity of overall effect while permitting divergence of content — and whether that version is stable or collapses back into one of the original two. Finally, since I am also expected to say something about performance, note what each view implies for a singer or director: if the music already expresses what a character cannot say, should the performer act the emotion the music supplies, or play against it? You need not cite any literature; I am after the argument, and I would rather you told me where my framing of the two positions is confused than padded the answer with references.
