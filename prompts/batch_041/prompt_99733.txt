I sit on the priority-setting panel of our county health authority — I'm the lay member, not a clinician; the council appointed me because I used to run a small business and they wanted someone with no stake in the hospitals. At next month's meeting we have to settle a question we've already postponed twice: whether to renew three years of funding for a small clinic that treats people with a rare nerve condition, or to redirect that money to a much larger program that would reach several thousand residents with modest but genuine improvements in daily comfort — mostly better management of chronic but bearable pain, and help sleeping. The clinic serves about a dozen patients. Their condition is not fatal, but the attacks are, by every account I've heard, about as bad as physical suffering gets; one of them told me she would rather have had her sister's cancer. The larger program would spare no one anything like that; it would simply make a great many people somewhat more comfortable.

Here is the thought I keep returning to, and I want the memo to engage it seriously rather than treat it as squeamishness. I have come to believe that suffering and comfort are not two quantities on a single scale — that there is a level of pain so severe that no quantity of ordinary comfort or convenience, however many people share in it, could ever balance it out, while below that level it seems perfectly sensible to trade small pains against small pleasures. If that is right, then the arithmetic our finance staff keep presenting — several thousand residents multiplied by a modest gain, weighed against twelve patients multiplied by a large loss — is not merely cold; it may be the wrong kind of calculation altogether.

Please draft the written opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. I need a clear recommendation — renew, redirect, or something in between — with a specific time frame attached to whatever we decide, and reasons my colleagues can actually argue with rather than an appeal to my feelings. I also want the strongest case for the other side set out fairly, because two members of the panel will press it and I don't want to be caught without an answer.
