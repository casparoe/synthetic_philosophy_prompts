I'm a third-year student in a course on consciousness and cognition, and we have just finished the unit on split-brain patients. The picture I came away with is roughly this: once the corpus callosum is severed, the two hemispheres are largely cut off from one another, each with its own perceptual access and in some cases its own preferences, so that the operation turns one stream of consciousness into two. What I cannot work out is how to square that with cases in which the separation is only partial. If a complete commissurotomy yields two minds, then an incomplete one — some fibres spared, or only some of the commissures cut, as I understand sometimes happens — ought to yield something strictly between one and two. Does that mean that a person's degree of unity is a function of how much of the callosum remains intact, so that someone with a partial split is, so to speak, one and a half subjects? And if unity can come in degrees, would the same not hold for the rest of us, who are presumably unified only because our connectivity happens to be intact, rather than because unity is an all-or-nothing matter? I would like to know whether this is the correct way to read the split-brain findings, or whether I have misunderstood both what the experiments actually show and what is meant when people say that such a patient has two minds. Please be as self-critical as the material allows: tell me what would change your mind — which argument or piece of evidence would push you toward the opposite verdict — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
