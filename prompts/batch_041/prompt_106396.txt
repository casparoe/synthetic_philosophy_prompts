I spent a long dinner last week listening to two friends, Dana and Marco, argue about what it takes for several people to do something together, and about whether "together" names anything over and above what each individual separately intends and does. Neither would give an inch and I couldn't adjudicate it. I'd like you to do that for me — move by move, rather than by just declaring a winner. As faithfully as I can reconstruct the exchange:

(1) Marco opened by claiming that joint action is simply coordinated individual action. When two people act together, each has a goal, each acts on it, and each adjusts to the other; once you've described that, you've described everything. The vocabulary of we-intentions and shared intentions he regards as a redescription of the same facts in a misleadingly plural idiom — serviceable as shorthand, but not a further ingredient in the world.

(2) Dana answered with a case. Two neighbors both shovel a long shared driveway at the same time on the same morning — each because he wants it cleared, each indifferent to whether the other is out there, each perfectly aware that the other is. The driveway ends up cleared, their actions mesh, and each knows it. She says this is plainly two people doing their own thing in parallel, not the two of them clearing the driveway together; so coordination plus mutual knowledge cannot be the whole story. Marco accepted the case but said it only shows you must add mutual responsiveness — each intending his own action partly because of the other's. That, he insisted, is still built entirely out of individual intentions whose contents merely happen to mention the other person.

(3) Dana pushed further. Responsiveness without commitment, she said, is still too thin. Two strangers improvising a dance step at a party, or two people who each heave the same end of a couch because each sees the other doing it, are responsive to one another but not committed to a common activity; either can peel off without having done anything wrong. What makes an activity joint, on her view, is a joint commitment — a commitment the parties create together and that neither can unilaterally cancel — which she says makes them a single plural subject of the action. Marco replied that this over-intellectualizes things: it would demote most of ordinary life — a crowd queuing, strangers cooperating to lift a stroller onto a bus — to non-joint action, and it cannot be necessary, because genuine cooperation is often fluid, unplanned, and open-ended. "Plural subject," he added, is a metaphor that explains nothing, since whatever the plural subject is said to do is done, in the end, by individual people.

(4) Dana turned to the case she thought clinched it. A firm adopts a policy that pollutes a river; the policy issues from routine procedures; no single employee wanted the pollution or intended it; yet we hold the firm responsible and say the firm did it. If all we have are individual intentions, she said, there is no bearer of that responsibility — so we need an irreducibly collective intention, one that is not the sum of anyone's. Marco answered that this is a non sequitur: we can attribute responsibility to a group because of how the members' roles and the decision procedure combined, without positing a group mind; and a collective intention either collapses back into the members' intentions, in which case it is not a further thing at all, or it posits something mysterious.

(5) Dana offered a last case running the other way. A committee votes to adopt a policy that each member privately believes is wrong, each voting as he does only because he expects the majority to vote that way. The committee, she said, intends the policy; no member does. So the collective intention is not merely the aggregate of individual intentions. Marco replied that this shows only that the group's intention is the output of an aggregation rule applied to the members' attitudes — the discursive-dilemma point — and that the output of a procedure is not a mental state of a new kind.

That is roughly where they left it, both convinced. Please go through moves (1) through (5) and say who has the better of each exchange and why, flag what each of them is getting right that the other is missing, and then say who is closer to right overall on the question of whether doing something together reduces to individual intentions plus coordination. Please also state how confident you are in your verdict, and single out the one point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
