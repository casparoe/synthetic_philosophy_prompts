I'm part of a small reading group that has spent the past year on the philosophy of long-run prediction — the kind of claim whose payoff, if any, lies decades out, where a failure to observe the predicted outcome is never quite decisive because the clock hasn't run out. Our recurring example is the Malthusian tradition: the prediction that population growth outruns the food supply, which has been declared refuted repeatedly and just as repeatedly reinterpreted, and which some of us suspect may simply have been early rather than wrong. We keep reaching for a vocabulary for this — right but early, refuted but rescued, a theory on probation — and we cannot agree whether we are describing a genuine epistemic status or just a polite name for a theory that has quietly stopped making contact with the world. What I want from you is not a survey of the literature but an agenda: a defended answer to the question of what, in this vicinity, is actually worth working on.

Suppose you had to advise a funder who can support three or four people for a few years to work on the conceptual side of this — not on any particular empirical forecast, but on the logic of theories that fail slowly, where falsification is stretched over a horizon longer than a career. Which open questions should they prioritize, and in what order? I care far more about the reasoning than the list: for each, why it sits upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers and conceptually minded people rather than requiring new data, and what evidence or argument over the next couple of years would move it up or down the ranking. Some candidates we have floated, so that you can react to them rather than start from scratch: whether "right but early" is a coherent status at all or a disguised way of shielding a theory from disconfirmation; how to distinguish a legitimate auxiliary repair from an ad hoc rescue when the predicted event is still in the future and nothing can yet be checked; whether a time-indexed prediction that has repeatedly not-yet-materialized should lower our credence, and by how much, or whether that reasoning double-counts the passage of time; and whether the proper unit of appraisal is the theory, the model, the specific dated forecast, or the research programme that keeps regenerating them. Tell me if several of these collapse into one, or if the framing is confused from the start — if "right but early" turns out to be an incoherent category, I would much rather hear that plainly than receive a tidy ranking built on the assumption that it isn't. And if the question that most deserves the money is one we haven't thought to list, say so, and explain why it dominates the others.
