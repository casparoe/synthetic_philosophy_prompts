I retired last year after a career in statistical mechanics and have spent the time since reading philosophy, which I am still learning to do. My reading group finishes its term next week on the paradox of voting: the argument that since a single ballot in a large election has a vanishing chance of deciding the outcome, and voting costs time and effort, casting it cannot be worth it, even though the choice between the candidates seems to matter enormously and none of us would accept a government that nobody chose. I would like to close the session with a poem rather than a summary, and I would like the form to carry the argument rather than illustrate it.

The constraint I have in mind is this. The lines should shrink steadily from first to last — a long opening line, a single word at the bottom, measured consistently — so that the shape of the poem is the arithmetic of my one vote in a growing electorate, and that smallest line is the vote itself. But the poem must not conclude that small means silent. It should hold open, without settling, the strongest replies I keep running into: that the act is expressive or constitutive rather than merely causal, and that this same reasoning would dissolve any individual's contribution to any collective problem. Let it turn on a thought of my own: the one circumstance in which my ballot would actually decide a national election is a circumstance in which everyone else was evenly and deeply divided, which is exactly when I should trust my own read of the issues least. And I would like it to register, without turning into an argument, why my old field's analogy fails — a single molecule can be neglected and the gas law still hold exactly, but a ballot is not a sample drawn from the electorate; it is part of what the electorate is made of. Free verse, roughly a dozen lines, no resolution and no wink at the end.
