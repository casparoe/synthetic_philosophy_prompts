You are a philosopher of practical reason with a specialism in metaethics, and I would like your help drafting a document. Let me explain the situation, since it is a slightly unusual one. I am not an academic; I am a retired hospital administrator who for the past three years has run a fortnightly evening reading group in my front room, and lately we have been working through a popular introduction to the philosophy of reasons. Two of my regulars have been circling each other for months and the rest of us are tired of it. The first of them holds that a consideration can count as a reason for a particular person only if it could, given the right beliefs, actually move that person — that a reason must attach to something the person already cares about, or it is not a reason for them at all. The second holds that reasons are not hostage to what anyone happens to care about, and that we may truly say of a person that they have a reason to do something even when nothing in them would be stirred by the thought of doing it. I suspect they are talking past each other more than they are disagreeing, and I would like, as a kind of peace treaty, a joint statement that both of them could put their names to without either feeling that they had conceded.

I have one further thing to throw into the mix, because our arguments keep collapsing into the same example and I want a fresh one. Consider a woman — call her the selfless caretaker — who has spent thirty years nursing a succession of dying relatives, whose every desire is for the comfort of other people, and who has, so far as anyone can tell, no desire at all concerning her own welfare; she does not want to rest, does not want to be healthy, is indifferent to her own future. Now ask whether she has a reason to look after her own health. Your first type of reader, the one who wants reasons to attach to what a person already cares about, seems pushed toward saying that she has no reason, which strikes me as monstrous; your second type says she has the reason regardless, which strikes me as either obviously right or obviously empty, I cannot decide which. I am interested in whether this case, and its mirror image in the person who cares for nothing but himself, can be used to separate a real disagreement from a merely verbal one, and in whether the two of them are using the word reason in the same sense at all — one perhaps meaning something closer to what would explain a person's acting, the other something closer to what would justify it.

Please begin by brainstorming briefly — set out for yourself the several distinct questions that might be hiding inside their dispute, and note which of them look like genuine disagreements and which look like terminological confusion — and only then draft the document. It should contain, first, a numbered list of agreed claims: what both sides can accept about what a reason is for, about the connection between having a reason and being motivated, about the amoralist and the selfless caretaker, and about what would have to be true for either view to be refuted. Then a disagreements table, with a row for each remaining point of difference, giving the question at issue; the first reader's position and the second reader's position, each phrased in wording that its own side would accept as fair; the crux, that is the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them; the evidence, argument or case that both sides agree would bear on that crux; and each side's prediction about how that case comes out. Where you judge that some part of their quarrel is verbal rather than substantive, say so explicitly and identify the words doing the damage. Please write in British English, keep any technical term you must use defined in plain language, and do not send me off to read anyone else — I want the argument itself, set out so that two stubborn amateurs can see exactly where they stand.
