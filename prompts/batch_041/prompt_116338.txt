Ok so I'm a novelist, historical fiction mostly, and my current book is about a guy in Reserve Police Battalion 101 — alternating chapters, him in occupied Poland in 1942 and his granddaughter digging around in the nineties. I've been reading Browning and Goldhagen for background and somewhere in there I've ended up holding a bunch of beliefs that I'm no longer sure actually fit together, and I want to find the seams before I draft the back half of the book. Here's the pile:

1. Character is fate. In my fiction, what someone does under pressure is basically who they already were. Plot is just the pressure that makes the person visible. I don't really believe people change, I believe they get revealed.

2. Browning convinced me that ordinary middle aged reservists did most of the shooting — cops, teachers, guys with families — and that the situation did most of the work. The offer at Józefów to step out if you weren't up for it, that hardly anybody took. Not wanting to look like a coward in front of the guys. The momentum of one long day. Deference to the man with the rank. On the day, almost anyone in those boots does what they did.

3. But Goldhagen lands for me too. There was something specific going on with German antisemitism of that vintage — an eliminationist thing, a want-to, not just a could-be-pushed-to.

4. And then my own version of it, which I've been calling the substitution test: take 500 men the same age from the same kinds of jobs anywhere — Iowa, Yorkshire, Osaka, doesn't matter — drop them into that institution with those orders in 1942 and I think alot of the same behavior comes out. The German-ness is doing less work than we want it to.

5. I write moral fiction, which means my characters are answerable for what they do. Full stop. No well-he-was-raised-that-way, no anyone-would-have. Take that away from them and they stop being characters and turn into weather. This one is definately non-negotiable, it's the whole engine.

6. A novelist shouldn't judge his characters. Render, don't sentence. The minute I start grading them on the page the book dies.

That's where I've landed. My instinct is that most of this is fine and I'm just being a novelist about it, but I've got a nagging feeling that two or three of these can't all be true at once. Which ones actually pull against each other, and how bad is each collision? I want them ranked, worst first, not a flat list of everything that could be called a tension. Some of these probably only look like conflicts, and if that's the case with any of them I'd like you to say so and explain why it dissolves. For the real ones, tell me the cheapest way out — the smallest thing I'd have to give up or restate to get the rest to sit together — and what I lose by paying that price. And if there's a single revision that would clean up the most at once, what is it?
