I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of physics, and I have been having a long-running argument with a friend who works on the statistical mechanics of non-equilibrium systems. He thinks the puzzle of the direction of time is essentially solved once one accepts a low-entropy boundary condition, and that anyone who keeps pressing on it is either confused about the physics or in the grip of a bad picture of explanation. I think the standard account does less work than it is credited with, and I have written the short essay below to try to say why. I am presenting it at a work-in-progress seminar next month, so what I would like from you is a set of written comments on the draft rather than a rewrite of it.

Specifically, I would be grateful for four things. First, a check on accuracy: have I described the physics correctly, particularly the relationship between time-reversal symmetry of the dynamics and the role of boundary conditions, and have I been fair to the position I am attacking? Second, an assessment of whether the central thesis is genuinely interesting or merely a dressed-up version of a familiar deflationary complaint, and if the latter, which familiar complaint it collapses into. Third, detailed comments on sections three and five, which are the parts I am least sure of: section three because I worry the coarse-graining point is either trivial or false, and section five because I suspect the self-undermining argument may prove too much and take down any view of the arrow whatsoever. Fourth, a judgement on whether the conclusion is so deflationary that the essay has no positive contribution to make, and if so what the most promising repair would be. Please do not supply citations, references, or a reading list; I will add those myself, and I would rather you not guess at them. Write your comments in British English.

The essay follows.

Does the thermodynamic arrow explain the direction of time?

1. The standard account, in outline. The second law tells us that the entropy of an isolated system tends to increase. The underlying dynamics, however, is symmetric under time reversal: any process permitted in one temporal direction is permitted in the other. A symmetric dynamics cannot by itself prefer a direction, so the arrow is traced to a boundary condition, namely that the universe began in an extraordinarily low-entropy state. Entropy then increases away from that state, and that is why the future differs from the past. I will call this the standard account.

2. The charge of circularity. The boundary condition is stated as: the early universe was in a low-entropy state. But early is a temporal predicate. If the low-entropy condition is characterised purely as a point in a time-symmetric phase space, then the state itself carries no direction; it is simply a microstate that happens to lie in an exceedingly small region of that space. Nothing intrinsic to the state selects which way entropy runs. Increase is a property of a trajectory, and a trajectory has a direction only if a direction has already been supplied. So the standard account either presupposes the arrow or states its boundary condition in temporal vocabulary, in which case it does not explain the arrow but quietly assumes it.

3. Coarse-graining. Entropy is not a function of the microstate alone; it is defined relative to a partition of phase space into macrostates. For a fixed microstate and a time-symmetric dynamics, whether entropy rises or falls in a given direction depends on which partition one adopts. There exist partitions under which entropy decreases towards what we call the future. If that is right, then the thermodynamic arrow is not a feature of the world simpliciter but a feature of the world relative to a choice of macrovariables, and the standard account helps itself to the macrovariables we happen to track, such as volume, temperature, and pressure, without acknowledging that this choice is doing any work.

4. The record criterion. Which macrovariables are privileged, then? My proposal is that the privileged ones are those that can serve as records. A macrovariable is arrow-constituting just in case its values can carry reliable information about earlier values to later systems, so that a creature tracking it can properly be said to remember rather than to predict. On this view the epistemic arrow, the difference between memory and anticipation, between deliberation and regret, is not derived from the thermodynamic arrow but is constitutive of it. The thermodynamic arrow is simply the direction along which record-bearing macrovariables increase.

5. Self-undermining. This makes the arrow perspective-dependent, and it faces the fluctuation problem. In a universe that lasts long enough, statistical mechanics predicts, under the usual measure, that entropy fluctuations occur, and within a sufficiently large fluctuation there are local gradients defining an opposite arrow. A creature embedded in such a fluctuation would have records pointing the other way. If the record criterion is correct, then for that creature the arrow genuinely points the other way, and its records are as good as ours. The criterion therefore yields a pluralism about arrows: there is no fact about the global direction, only local ones. But now the very reasoning by which we arrived at the criterion, our records of statistical mechanics and of experiments, is itself local, and any argument that the usual measure is the correct one is a record-based argument. The account appears to undercut the authority of the records used to establish it.

6. What would rescue it. One might add a probability measure that renders large fluctuations vanishingly improbable, but such a measure is standardly defined over initial conditions and so presupposes the arrow. Alternatively one might treat the perspective as a brute physical fact that fixes a privileged frame, but that reintroduces the circularity of section two. The account may therefore be unable to be both naturalistic and non-circular.

7. Conclusion. The direction of time may not be explained by thermodynamics at all. The thermodynamic arrow is downstream of an arrow that is either primitive or perspectival, and the price of naturalism is a localism about temporal direction.
