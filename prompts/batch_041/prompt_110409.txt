I host a long-form podcast on religion and ideas, and next month's guest is a scholar who defends a reading of Maimonides that most people resist: that the chapters of the Guide of the Perplexed on the reasons for the commandments treat the sacrificial law as an accommodation to the religious habits of the ancient world — worship redirected rather than worship commanded for its own sake — and that this is not a stray remark but the key to how he understood law as such. She is very good at making the reading sound inevitable, so I need questions that force her to earn it.

Draft eight to ten questions in the order I should ask them, each with a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. The sequence should cover at least: (a) the medieval objection that Noah and Abel brought offerings before there was any idolatry to accommodate and that Scripture calls the offerings a pleasing odor — leaving visible the difference between explaining why there is sacrifice at all and explaining why these particular animals were chosen; (b) whether the accommodation was a one-time concession to a particular generation or a permanent feature of the law, and what becomes of prayer, fasting and the rest of the ritual commandments if the reasoning generalizes; (c) how the Guide sits with his own legal code, which codifies the sacrificial order in detail, and whether he expected it to be restored; (d) whether the account needs the ethnography of the Sabeans he leans on to be true, or survives if it is not; (e) what the reading implies about animal suffering, and about the familiar claim that the laws of slaughter exist to minimize it.

No citations or reading lists, and do not put words in any living scholar's mouth — the guest is simply an unnamed defender of the reading. End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" naming, in one sentence, the single question most likely to force her to abandon or substantially revise the reading.
