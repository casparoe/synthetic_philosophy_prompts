I teach a graduate seminar on transformative experience and the theory of rational choice, and I have grown weary of the examples my students already know by heart — the child, the vampire, the conversion to a faith. They can recite the structure of the problem long before they feel it, and I want a text that makes them feel it again. So I am asking you for a short story. Set it in a near-future city where a clinic offers a manufactured transformation: a neural implant that adds a perceptual modality no human being has ever had — call it a magnetic sense, the ability to feel the earth's field the way one feels one's own balance. The protagonist is a woman in her fifties who restores historical keyboard instruments, and whose entire self-understanding is bound up with a cultivated sensitivity of touch and ear; the implant would not merely give her something new but would plausibly reorder what she cares about, and no one can tell her whether her love of her work would survive it. The clinic does not pretend the choice is ordinary, and it offers two reassurances that I want the story to take seriously enough to show, concretely, why each either succeeds or fails. The first is a calibration session in which she is meant to sample the experience before committing, assembled from recordings and testimony donated by people who have already been implanted; make its failure principled rather than merely disappointing, so that what the session shows her is the world as it appears from values she does not yet hold, and the donors' accounts are useless to her precisely because they can no longer describe the state she is in. The second is a promise of reversibility, since the implant can be removed; make that promise hollow in the right way, so that removal is revealed as one further transformation rather than a road home, and the woman who would have to decide to remove the implant is not the woman now weighing whether to receive it.

Craft notes: keep the philosophical content in the apparatus, the consent paperwork, the technician's evasions, and the protagonist's own choices, rather than in a character who explains the literature to the reader; I do not want a lecture in dialogue, and I do not want the story to resolve by having someone discover the correct theory. What I do want is commitment. The story should show her deciding, and it should take a definite stand on whether the decision is rational — or, if you judge rationality to be the wrong frame here, a definite stand on what it is instead — rather than trailing off into ambiguity about whether she goes through with it. End with a brief afterword, no more than two paragraphs, stating plainly where you have taken liberties with the philosophical material and where the story leans on a contested reading: whether you have quietly assumed that a preview of an epistemically transformative experience is even coherent; whether you have treated the transformation as all-or-nothing when it is plausibly a matter of degree; and whether the ending presupposes a particular account of how a person's preferences can change and still remain hers. I would rather have your choices and their costs than a hedge.
