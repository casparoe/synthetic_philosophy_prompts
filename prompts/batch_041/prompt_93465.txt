I'm reading Freud's Totem and Taboo (the Brill translation) for a seminar on religon and I keep geting stuck on this bit from the preface:

"The problem of taboo is presented more exhaustively, and the effort to solve it is approached with perfect confidence. The investigation of totemism may be modestly expressed as: 'This is all that psychoanalytic study can contribute at present to the elucidation of the problem of totemism.' This difference in the treatment of the two subjects is due to the fact that taboo still exists in our midst. To be sure, it is negatively conceived and directed to different contents, but according to its psychological nature, it is still nothing else than Kant's 'Categorical Imperative', which tends to act compulsively and rejects all conscious motivations. On the other hand, totemism is a religio-social institution which is alien to our present feelings; it has long been abandoned and replaced by new forms. In the religions, morals, and customs of the civilized races of to-day it has left only slight traces, and even among those races where it is still retained, it has had to undergo great changes. The social and material progress of the history of mankind could obviously change taboo much less than totemism."

I get the general vibe — taboo is still alive for us as the moral law, totemism is this dead religio-social thing that only left traces in religion and morals. But what is he actually claiming? Is he saying the categorical imperative and taboo are the *same* psychological thing, or just analogous? Because Kant thought the moral law came from reason and could be justified to any rational agent, while taboo is a brute prohibition people can't even explain. So the comparison seems either really deep or really cheap, and I can't tell which.

Also, a bit further down he says the book's aim is to find the original meaning of totemism through its "infantile traces" in our own children. How is that supposed to work? How does watching kids today tell you anything about the origin of religion?

Can you walk me through what Freud is up to in this passage and whether the Kant comparison holds up? I want to understand the argument, not just get a summary of the book.
