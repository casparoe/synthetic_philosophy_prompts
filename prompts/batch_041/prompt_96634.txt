Two decision theorists are at a blackboard working through the absent-minded driver problem. The setup is on the board: a driver travels a highway with two intersections; she cares only about her payoff, which is 0 if she exits at the first intersection, 4 if she exits at the second, and 1 if she drives past both; and between the intersections she forgets where she is, so her information at the first intersection and at the second is exactly the same. The first theorist holds that the driver should adopt the ex ante optimal policy — continue at the first intersection, exit at the second — and that this policy is genuinely available to her, so that the ex ante perspective, applied to her choice of an entire course of action, settles what she should do. The second holds that this is an illusion: because her information is identical at both intersections, no policy assigning different acts to the two nodes is one she can carry out, and the only coherent object of her choice is a rule from her information to an act — a rule that must therefore treat both intersections alike and, if she is to do better than driving past both, must randomize.

Write their dialogue. Have each theorist state the general principle behind their position, apply it to the numbers on the board, and answer the other's strongest objection instead of talking past it. Bring them to the crux: the first theorist must say what the driver actually does at the first intersection given that she cannot recognize it, and the second must say what she does on arriving at the second, and they should disagree about whether "the driver" is a single agent choosing once or two time-slices choosing separately — and about what follows for whether the ex ante optimum is a real option or a fiction. Then have them test the disagreement on a variation: suppose the driver may spend 1 at the outset on a reliable device that beeps exactly once, when and only when she reaches the second intersection. Each should say what the driver ought to do in that variant, and whether the variant resolves the original dispute or merely relocates it — in particular, whether the fact that the ex ante plan becomes executable only after buying information tells for or against either view. Let them press each other on whether the driver who randomizes is being rational or merely making the best of a bad model, and on whether the problem exposes a genuine conflict between ex ante and ex post optimality or only an artifact of how the case is described. End with the disagreement as sharp as they can make it; do not manufacture agreement, and do not let either theorist win by caricature.
