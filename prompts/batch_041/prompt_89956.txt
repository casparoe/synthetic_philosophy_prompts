I sit on the ethics committee of a national professional association for clinicians, and I need to circulate our written opinion before the meeting on the 14th, so I'd like a draft.

The case: one of our members, a genuinely well-regarded researcher in the field, has spent about two years publicly arguing that the evidence behind one of our standard treatments is thinner than the guidelines make it sound. Read closely, everything he says is defensible — the effect sizes are modest, the trials are small, the outcome measures got switched mid-trial. But he writes in a dry technical register, his posts get picked up by wellness accounts, and our patient-facing staff keep meeting people who read him and decided to skip the treatment. Nobody can point to a person who was harmed because of him in particular, and I suspect that's not a coincidence — this is the kind of harm that only shows up in aggregate, if it shows up at all.

Two items are on the table. A complaint asking us to censure him, and a bylaw ammendment a colleague wants: members barred from publicly "amplifying uncertainty" about matters the association treats as settled, with breaches referred to this committee.

Mill is the obvious frame here — in On Liberty he's explicit that his principle governs "the dealings of society with the individual in the way of compulsion and control, whether the means used be physical force in the form of legal penalties, or the moral coercion of public opinion" — but it doesn't settle anything for us, since we're not the state, the harm is smeared thin across a lot of people, and some of what gets called harm is really damage to our field's standing, which I don't think counts the same way.

Draft me the opinion: a clear recommendation on the complaint and on the amendment (yes, no, or something narrower), the reasoning in a form the committee can actually argue with, and the strongest case against whatever you land on, put honestly rather than as a straw man. Two pages max.
