I'm running a session for our department's cross-disciplinary reading group next month — mostly stats and bio grad students who use Bayesian methods every day but have never read a word of philosophy of science. Topic is the problem of old evidence, about ninety minutes, whiteboard. I dont want the explanation itself, I want the plan: the order the pieces have to go in so the dilemma lands as something they can't wriggle out of, instead of a technicality about P(E) = 1 that they dismiss in five minutes.

So: what has to be in place before the case can even be stated? My instinct is that they need a firm grip on what confirmation is supposed to be and on conditionalization as the rule for getting it, and then on why the likelihood ratio seems not to have the same problem — tell me if that's the wrong first move or if it's the right move in the wrong place. The one example that carries the whole session: the obvious candidate is Mercury's perihelion, but I suspect that's the wrong carrier for this room — either tell me why it's actually right and what it has that other candidates lack, or pick something better. Either way I need a case where the evidence wasn't just known beforehand but was what the theory was built out of, so that the accommodation issue has teeth. What the example must have, and what it must lack, spelled out.

Then the escape routes in the order they'll occur to people, with what each one costs: 'what you learn is that the theory entails the fact, and that's itself new information'; 'ask what your credence in the fact would have been before you knew it' (why that isn't free); 'the whole thing is an artifact of idealizing agents as logically omniscient'. I also want the neighbouring issue fenced off rather than blended in — whether accommodating a known fact is worth less than predicting a new one, and the overfitting answer to that — because I'm fairly sure half the room will think that is the old evidence problem. And I want the difference between the version of the problem that's about a real historical episode and the version that's just about one frozen credence function to do actual work somewhere in the sequence; say where it goes and what it buys.

Misconceptions I expect, and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off: that this is about dogmatic priors and goes away if you're less certain; that setting P(E) below 1 solves it; that it's about the psychology of discovery; that it refutes Bayesianism; that it's the same as the prediction-vs-accomodation thing. Plus what I should openly refuse to go into at this level and say so, and one question to ask at the end that would show the dilemma landed rather than just got written down. No readings — they won't do them.
