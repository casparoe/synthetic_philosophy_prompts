For some years I have been following, and increasingly losing patience with, the debate over the epistemic regress problem: the ancient difficulty that any belief offered in support of another itself seems to stand in need of support, so that justification apparently runs either in a circle, or back to premises that are themselves unsupported, or on forever. The three familiar responses have been on the table long enough that their standard objections have hardened into ritual. The foundationalist posits basic or self-warranting beliefs at which the demand for reasons legitimately stops, and is told that the stopping point is either arbitrary or secretly circular. The coherentist denies that there is a linear order of support at all, treating justification as a matter of mutual fit within a whole system of beliefs, and is told that mutual fit cannot distinguish a well-supported system from a self-consistent fiction. The infinitist insists that the demand for reasons never terminates and that a belief is justified only if the chain of reasons actually continues without end, and is told either that finite creatures cannot complete an infinite chain or that on this account no belief could ever be justified. Every party has an answer to every standard objection, the answers are well known, and nobody moves. I want a diagnosis rather than another round.

My own hypothesis — and I want it treated as a hypothesis, to be tested against what each side actually commits itself to rather than merely assumed — is that the deadlock is largely an artefact of a conflation among three different regresses that the single phrase "the regress of justification" covers. First, the regress of warrant: the atemporal, constitutive question of what in fact makes a belief epistemically justified, a question about a relation that holds, if it holds at all, independently of anyone's ever rehearsing it. Second, the regress of reasons: the temporal, practical, and arguably social question of what an agent can actually offer, in order, when her entitlement is challenged — a question about the conduct of a dialectic, in which offering a reason is something done in response to a challenge and in which the propriety of stopping may be a matter of conversational norms rather than of metaphysics. Third, the regress of explanatory priority: the question of what explains what in the order of coming to know, which may impose an ordering that neither of the other two does.

I suspect that once these are distinguished, a good deal of the disagreement dissolves into parties answering different questions under one name — the foundationalist and the coherentist speaking primarily to warrant, the infinitist, at least in its better moments, speaking to the practice of reasons-giving — while a genuine residue remains: a disagreement not about any particular stopping point but about what a theory of justification is even supposed to deliver, namely a criterion by which beliefs are sorted into justified and unjustified, or an account of the norm-governed activity of justifying. If that is right, then part of the impasse is verbal and part is a disagreement about the aims of the inquiry, and only the latter is worth arguing about.

Tell me whether this diagnosis holds up. In doing so: state what each of the three positions is actually committed to, and whether those commitments really do sort along the lines I have suggested, or whether each position turns out to need all three regresses and so cannot be allocated to one; identify which of the standard objections, if any, survive the distinctions above and therefore represent genuine substantive disagreement; and say what kind of disagreement remains once the verbal and cross-purpose elements are subtracted — a conflict of starting intuitions about what counts as a legitimate stopping point, a hidden commitment about what a reason must be (for instance, whether the relata of the support relation are propositions or belief-states, or whether an acceptable regress-stopper must itself be the sort of thing that can be believed), or something else entirely. Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a change in the question — or argue that none should be expected, and why. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a verdict on which position is true; I want the structure of the disagreement itself, with reasons.
