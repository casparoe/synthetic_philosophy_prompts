After thirty-five years as a field ecologist — most of them spent fitting optimal-foraging models to the movement data of ungulates — I retired last spring and have spent the year reading my way into decision theory, and I keep running into a distinction that my own models quietly assumed away. The foraging literature I grew up on is, I now gather, a special case of what philosophers and economists argue about under the heading of bounded rationality, and in particular of Herbert Simon's idea of satisficing: an agent that sets an aspiration level and takes the first option that clears it, rather than surveying and comparing all its options. What puzzles me is that the debate seems to split into two camps that agree on more than they admit — one treats satisficing as a descriptive heuristic, the other as a second-best stand-in for genuine optimization — and both take maximization as the benchmark against which satisficing is measured and found wanting. I want to write a proposal for a project that asks a different question, and I would like you to draft it.

The question I have in mind is this: can an adaptive satisficer — an agent whose aspiration level drifts upward after successes and downward after failures, which is how the aspiration level behaves in every real organism I ever studied — be given a representation as the maximizer of some utility function, or does adaptivity push satisficing into a category that no maximizing representation can capture? The static case is, I suspect, easy and uninteresting: a fixed threshold is just a coarse preference ordering, and I can already sketch the trivial translation. The adaptive case is where I get stuck, because if we permit the representing utility function to be reference-dependent — sensitive to the history of past outcomes and to the agent's current aspiration level — then it is not obvious that any behavior of an adaptive satisficer is behaviorally distinguishable from the behavior of some maximizer, and the whole notion of satisficing as a distinct normative ideal threatens to collapse into a redescription of the very thing it was meant to replace. I want the proposal to say why the existing positions leave this open, what the method would be (I imagine small constructed decision problems in which the two representations are forced apart, together with a conceptual argument about what a representation theorem for an adaptive satisficer would even have to look like, and some attention to whether the usual small-worlds difficulty in expected-utility theory makes the question ill-posed before it starts), what a positive result and a negative result would each amount to, and — because I am new to this and prone to overreach — an honest statement of what would make the project too ambitious for a single semester and what a smaller, safe version of it would be. Please frame it as a term-paper or small-grant proposal of the sort a philosopher would read, with the central question stated so that it has a determinate answer rather than trailing off into a survey. I will supply the citations myself, so there is no need to include any.
