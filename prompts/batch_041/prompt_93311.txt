The question was: Consider a case in which we know the following facts: a window was broken at noon. Exactly one thing broke it. It was a baseball, thrown by the butler. The butler was in the garden at noon, not in the pantry. Now consider four sentences: (1) If the butler was in the pantry at noon, he did not break the window. (2) If the butler had been in the pantry at noon, he would not have broken the window. (3) If the butler did not break the window, something else did. (4) If the butler had not broken the window, something else would have. Explain the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals, using (1)-(4). Say which of the four are true and which are false, and why. Then discuss whether the difference between the two kinds is a matter of truth conditions or of pragmatics. Finally, consider a different pair: a doctor says, 'If the test is positive, the patient has the disease,' and a colleague says, 'If the test had been negative, the patient would still have had the disease.' Is this pair related in the same way as the butler pair? Explain.

The answer I received reads as follows. Indicative conditionals are about what follows from what we know, while counterfactual conditionals are about what would have happened in a world different from the actual one. In the window case, (1) and (3) are indicative; (2) and (4) are counterfactual. (1) is true because if the butler was in the pantry, he was not in the garden, so he could not have thrown the baseball. (2) is also true for the same reason: in the counterfactual world where he was in the pantry, he could not have thrown the baseball. (3) is true because we know exactly one thing broke the window; if the butler did not do it, something else must have. (4) is false because, although the butler did break the window, if he had not, it does not follow that something else would have; perhaps nothing would have. So the indicative/counterfactual difference shows up in (3) and (4): (3) is true because of what actually happened, while (4) is false because the counterfactual world can diverge. The difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals is a matter of truth conditions, not just pragmatics. Indicative conditionals are material implications: 'If P then Q' is true whenever P is false or Q is true. That is why (1) and (3) are true even though we know the butler did break the window. Counterfactuals are not material implications; they are evaluated by looking at the nearest possible world where the antecedent is true. That is why (2) and (4) can differ from (1) and (3). The doctor case is exactly parallel to the window case. 'If the test is positive, the patient has the disease' is indicative, and it is true because the test is positive and the patient has the disease. 'If the test had been negative, the patient would still have had the disease' is counterfactual, and it is false because the test was not negative. So the doctor pair shows the same contrast as the window pair.
