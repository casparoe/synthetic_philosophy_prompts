I keep arriving at a pattern of verdicts about when someone can know that their own belief-forming method is working, and I want to know whether a single principle generates it.

I know the tank is full by reading the gauge. I do not come to know that the gauge is reliable by checking it repeatedly and noting that its readings keep matching the fuel level — each step looks fine, but the whole procedure assumes what it concludes. I know the tomato is red by looking, and I am untroubled by the fact that I have not independently ruled out that it is white under a red light. Moore's argument from his two hands to the conclusion that he is not a handless brain in a vat strikes me as a perfectly good piece of knowledge. Yet I am unwilling to let a person learn that her memory is trustworthy by remembering that it has usually served her well, and unwilling to let a device certify its own sensor by comparing readings from that sensor with readings from that same sensor.

The principle I would like to hold is that a method may not be used to establish its own reliability. That would explain the gauge, memory, and self-certifying-sensor cases, but it also seems to forbid Moore's proof and the red-light case, which I want to keep. Can the principle be repaired so that it keeps everything except the cases I have flagged, or does the honest repair force me to surrender one of my verdicts? Please state the repaired principle precisely, say which case each clause exists to handle, and then tell me what it implies about a case I have not mentioned: a near-sighted person who concludes that her new glasses are correctly prescribed because the world looks sharp through them.
