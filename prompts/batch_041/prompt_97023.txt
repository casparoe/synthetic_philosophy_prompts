I have no formal background in philosophy — I work as a software engineer — but I have been reading about the proposal to use adversarial debate between two AI systems as a way of supervising models on questions that no human can check directly. As I understand it, the proposal rests on an asymmetry: the judge is weak, but truth has an advantage, because refuting a false claim is cheaper than making one. The dishonest side has to sustain a whole story, while the honest side only has to expose one bad step that the judge can verify. The position I have come to hold is that this asymmetry does not hold in general, and that the failure is structural rather than a matter of engineering. I would like you to tell me what would count against that position.

Here is the consideration that moved me, stated in my own words, since I cannot look anything up. Because the judge cannot evaluate the original question directly, the debate has to be recursive: one side decomposes the claim into subclaims, the judge rules only on the pieces small enough to check, and the other side may challenge whichever piece it believes is wrong. But the decomposing side chooses the decomposition, and it can choose one under which the overall answer is wrong while no individual piece is detectably wrong — or under which the two sides disagree about where the error lies without either being able to prove it to the judge. In that case the error is smeared across the structure of the argument rather than localized in one inspectable step, so the honest side can no longer win by producing a short, judge-checkable refutation; the shortest refutation may be as long as the original argument, or longer. If this is right, then the proposal depends on a certificate-size assumption — that every false answer has a short proof of falsity relative to the judge — and I see no reason for that assumption to be true. The honest debater's advantage then looks like an advantage only over arguments whose flaws are already short.

Play it straight: what would have to be true for this position to be false?

1. Rank the defeaters by how decisive they actually are — the observations, arguments, or formal results that should move me — and keep them separate from the ones that would be merely awkward.
2. For each, say whether a defender of debate would themselves concede that it counts against the position, rather than dismissing it as off-target or question-begging.
3. Give an honest verdict on whether the position risks being unfalsifiable. If it turns out to be well insulated — if every outcome can be redescribed as consistent with it — say so, and say whether that is a serious defect in a claim of this kind or simply the normal condition of a claim about what a class of protocols can and cannot do.
4. Say what the position predicts that its rivals do not. For instance, does it predict that more debate rounds, a more capable judge, or symmetric increases in debater compute would fail to close the gap? Are those predictions checkable in principle?
5. Tell me the single thing I would find most persuasive, and whether it is available: a concrete case, ideally small enough to write down, in which a wrong decomposition defeats a competent honest debater — or a proof that no such case can exist under some clearly stated condition on the judge and the problem.

I am aware of at least one proposed repair in this area: letting the challenged side force the decomposer to demonstrate that the class of subproblems it used is actually solvable, by requiring it to solve related problems of the same kind. I would like to know whether that sort of move counts against my position, or whether it merely relocates the same certificate-size problem one level up.
