ok so im in this political philosophy course and we just did the whole equality-of-what thing — welfare, resources, capabilities — and honestly every reading just picks one currency and defends it, and I can't tell which one im actually on. So I wrote out my gut reactions to a bunch of cases and now I have no idea what principle they add up to, and im worried im just being inconsistent. Can you tell me what principle (or small set of them) actually captures these, and which of my verdicts I have to give up?

My gut reactions:

1. Two people, same income, same health, same everything. One is miserable because they developed a taste for something expensive (they got into sailing, whatever). The other is perfectly happy with cheap hobbies. I dont think justice requires handing the sailor extra money. Being miserable because your tastes got expensive isn't the same as being badly off.

2. Someone who needs an expensive wheelchair and a modified flat just to get to work, versus someone with the same income who doesn't. Here I absolutely do think we owe the first person more resources, even though their bank balance is identical. So same-resources-all-round can't be the end of the story.

3. But then: someone who was raised where they learned to expect almost nothing, and now genuinely reports being content with a tiny life. My reaction is that their contentment doesn't mean they were treated fairly — something has gone wrong there, and the fact that they dont feel it doesn't fix it. So happiness can't be the currency either.

4. Two people with the exact same real options open to them; one takes them up and flourishes, one has the same options and just... doesn't, and ends up miserable with no external obstacle in the way. I dont think we redistribute to the second one. So it looks like options, not outcomes.

5. Here's the one that breaks me. Someone has the option to leave a bad situation — the door is literally unlocked, there's a shelter, whatever — but because of how they've been worn down they can't bring themselves to take it. Formally the capability is there. My gut says no, it isn't really; saying well she has the capability feels like a cop-out. So the capability view seems to be smuggling in something about whether the option is actually usable, which sounds a lot like welfare again.

6. And another: some things I care about aren't distributable at all. Being the best at something, or being recognized — those are basically competitive; if everyone has them, nobody has them. Does an equality-of-what theory even have room for that, or is it just out of scope?

So what I want: a precise statement of the principle(s) that get my verdicts 1-4, then tell me honestly what 5 and 6 do to it. Do I have to give up 4 to keep 5, i.e. go back to caring about outcomes? Or is there a principled line between expensive tastes and an option you can't actually use? And what does your preferred principle say about a case I havent listed — someone who is badly off on every currency at once: does the theory just say more of the relevant thing, or does that need a separate story?

Also feel free to tell me one of my verdicts is just wrong if the principle comes out clean enough. Id rather have a simple principle that overrules me than a monster that saves everything.
