For the past few weeks I have been arguing about signalling theory with two friends — one who thinks Zahavi's principle is a tautology dressed up in mathematics, the other who thinks that index signals have quietly made it obsolete — and I have noticed that I keep conceding points to each of them merely to keep the conversation moving. Before I write this up as a short discussion note for our reading group, I should like a proper audit of where I actually stand, because I suspect that some of what I have been saying is not one position but two or three positions that happen to share a vocabulary. I am not asking for a literature review or a reading list; I know the literature well enough and shall add references myself. What I want is an assessment of whether the following commitments can be held together: which pairs are genuinely in tension and which merely look so, the genuine ones ranked by severity, and, for each genuine tension, what would have to give, the cheapest revision that would restore consistency, and what that revision costs me. Be blunt. Do not tell me that the literature is divided and leave it there, and do not hedge every verdict with a caveat that the matter is contested; I want to know where the pressure falls in *my* position.

Here is the set. (1) The honesty of animal signals is underwritten by differential marginal cost: a signal is reliable because it is cheaper for a high-quality signaller to produce than a low-quality one, not because it is physically impossible to fake. (2) Index signals — signals causally tied to the quality they advertise, as when a stag's roar pitch tracks its body size — are honest for an entirely different reason, and need not be costly at all. (3) Because indices are honest and cheap, selection should favour them wherever they can be built, so costly handicaps are a last resort, expected mainly where no index is available. (4) The peacock's tail is the paradigm handicap, and what explains its honesty is that only a bird in good condition can grow and carry it. (5) The handicap principle is the right framework for a great deal of human behaviour: art, philanthropy, conspicuous consumption, ritual. (6) In those human cases I cannot see how the cost is differential in the currency of the quality being signalled, and I am inclined to say that honesty there is bootstrapped by receivers' calibration over time rather than by any sorting of signallers — which makes the mechanism a detail. (7) Methodologically, an equilibrium model that secures honesty only by positing a cost function that nobody can measure has not explained anything; the cost must be independently identifiable for the explanation to count.

Two further requests. First, I have caught myself using the word cost in two quite different ways — as a toll, which only some signallers can afford, and as a stake, which anyone could post but only someone who means it will risk — and I do not think I keep these apart. I want to know which of (1) to (7) rest on which reading, and whether the two readings can be combined in a single account at all, or whether one of them is doing all the work while the other is decorative. Second, tell me which of the seven items is not really an independent commitment but a restatement of another in different words, since if any of them are merely verbal I would rather know before I defend a position I do not hold. Please write in British English.
