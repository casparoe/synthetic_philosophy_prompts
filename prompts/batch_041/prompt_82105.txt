I've been teaching a unit on Nyāya epistemology for a few years now -- the four pramāṇas, perception, inference, comparison, testimony, and the old fight over whether a cognition arrives with its own credentials or has to be certified from the outside -- and last week it hit me that a one-page "claim standard" I helped write for a small fact-checking desk I sit on the board of is basically a pramāṇa list wearing a newsroom's clothes, with nobody having intended it. Here's what the document actually says. One: if one of our own reporters saw it with their own eyes, it's settled, and we don't go hunting for reasons to doubt it. Two: a claim can also be established by showing it follows from things we already accept, but only if the writer can state the general rule they're leaning on. Three: unfamiliar labels -- is this a hate crime, was this a "riot" -- get settled by the statute's definition plus a side-by-side with the paradigm cases everybody already agrees about. Four: if a designated expert asserts it, we run it, full stop, no requirement that we re-derive it ourselves. Five: nothing counts if we've already said it, so a claim that merely restates our earlier reporting establishes nothing new -- our editor calls that one "no free second helpings" and thinks it's just about not padding the feed. I dont think it's just about that.

What I want from you is the implicit epistemology. What view about where justification comes from does this standard only make sense under? Is it even internally consistent -- why does our own reporters eyeball get to be self-certifying while a stranger's testimony needs an independent second source, and yet an expert's bare assertion gets waved through with no corroboration at all? Which of the five rules strictly presuppose that picture, which ones would be just as happy under a rival one, and what does the standard quietly rule out -- a reliabilist story about tracking the truth, a coherentist one, a plain institutional "trust the process" story? Where is it philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license the exact same five rules? And if we swapped the presupposed picture for its opposite -- nothing is justified until something else certifies it -- what concretely breaks: which rules survive, which have to be rewritten, and does the desk get better or worse at its job?

Give it to me as a numbered list of exactly five items, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion.
