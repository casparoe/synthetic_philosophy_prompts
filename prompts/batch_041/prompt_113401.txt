I spent thirty years as an experimental physicist, most of it on precision measurement at low temperature, and I retired two years ago. Retirement gave me the time I never had to read philosophy of physics properly, and I have been doing so steadily since. I now sit on the physical sciences panel of a small independent foundation, and I have been asked to draft the decision memo for the single proposal that comes to a vote at our next meeting. I would be grateful if you would write it.

The proposal is a three-year request from a theorist with a solid record in quantum optics, jointly with a cold-atom group that would supply the apparatus. It has two halves, and our discretionary budget this cycle will cover one half or the other, not both. The first half is foundational: the applicant undertakes to complete and defend a derivation of the Born rule within a no-collapse quantum mechanics, the reading on which every branch of the wavefunction is equally real and nothing ever collapses, and he rests the whole derivation on a single axiom that he calls indifference across branches, which says that a rational agent's preferences among outcomes may not depend on which branch he happens to occupy. He is careful to insist that this is a symmetry requirement on preferences rather than a probabilistic assumption, and he compares it to the assumption that a long run of coin tosses is exchangeable, which is not itself an assumption about the coin. He notes that a principle of roughly this shape already exists in the literature and says his version is tightened in a way that matters. Our referee, a statistical mechanic, is unmoved: he argues that indifference across branches is precisely the premise that makes the Born weights the rational thing to adopt, so the axiom either begs the question or was chosen simply because it delivers the answer, and that in any case a derivation of how an agent should bet is not a derivation of why there are facts about probability at all. The applicant replies that an agent who refuses the axiom ends up with preferences that are unstable under his own physics, and that this, rather than intuition, is the argument for it.

The second half is an experiment: a decoherence measurement on a mesoscopic superposition that would test the dynamical assumptions the derivation leans on. The applicant concedes without embarrassment that it cannot discriminate between interpretations.

The panel is split in a way I do not trust. Two members want the foundational half declined on the ground that it is philosophy rather than physics and that no result could count against it; a third thinks the experiment is expensive plumbing in the service of a metaphysical dispute and would rather we held the money for the next cycle. My own suspicion is that the clean separation is illusory, that the experiment is only interesting because of the interpretation and the derivation is only physics because it makes contact with decoherence, and I would rather our memo say so than take shelter behind our charter, which funds empirical science and is silent about foundations.

Please write the memo I will circulate the day before the meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action — fund in full, fund one half, decline and preserve the money, or fund subject to conditions, with the conditions stated plainly and a date by which the applicant must meet them — and give the reasons in a form my colleagues can vote on. Give the strongest case for the position you end up rejecting its full weight; a paragraph of courtesy will not do. Write for the three members who have never read a word of philosophy of physics and would be embarrassed to say so. Do not cite anything, and do not give me a reading list; I have no way to check references and the panel cannot vote on them, so the argument has to stand on its own. Write continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way one writes a long letter to a colleague, somewhere in the neighbourhood of a thousand words.
