I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and last week I sat in on a data desk while two of them went at each other over how to update a forecast on the back of a tip. Nadia (the statistician) said the source had given the story a 70% confidence, so the right move was to shift your credence in the story by that much rather than treating the tip as certain — she called it updating on uncertain evidence. Ravi (a reporter) said that's incoherent: either the tip is evidence or it isn't, and if it is then your new credence should just be your old credence in the story given the tip; carrying a separate opinion about how much you trust the tip is double-counting the same information. She said no, the entire point is that you can take something as evidence without being certain of it, and if you refuse you have to either ignore the tip or pretend it's certain, both worse. He came back with two things. First, that he does have certain evidence — namely that the source said it — and he can conditionalize on that and put the reliability into the likelihood; her 0.7, he said, hides a model of the source instead of stating it. Second, that her rule isn't even well behaved: take in this tip and then a second source and you get a different number than if you'd heard them the other way round, and the news dont arrive in a privileged order. She said first that his version makes the answer hostage to a model of the source nobody can really specify, and second that the order thing only bites if you've drawn the lines of the space too coarsely — refine it and it commutes, so it's a misuse of the rule, not a defect in it. Can you referee this move by move, saying who's right on each step and what each of them is missing? And please separate what's basically settled among people who actually know this area from what's genuinely still fought over — dont hand me the contested part as if it were the consensus, since I may quote you.
