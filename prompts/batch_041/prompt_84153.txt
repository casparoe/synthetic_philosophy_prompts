A recent blog post summarises the debate over process-based and outcome-based supervision as follows: Outcome-based supervision rewards only the final answer, so the model will inevitably find shortcuts that get the right answer for the wrong reasons, making the approach hopeless beyond toy arithmetic. Process-based supervision rewards each step of the reasoning trace, which removes the incentive to game the final answer and gives a dense, reliable signal; the only real cost is annotation. Since any reasoning task decomposes into discrete steps, and the field has settled on process supervision, scalable oversight is essentially solved once we have enough step-level labels. The post adds that giving intermediate rewards to an outcome-trained model is always illegitimate, because shaping the reward changes the optimal policy.

I suspect this is too neat, but I don't know where the technical and conceptual objections begin. How much of it is fair? Go claim by claim and tell me what a researcher in scalable oversight would accept, what is a standard simplification, and what is simply wrong. Does the conclusion about solved oversight follow from the accurate parts, and what does the summary leave out that matters most?
