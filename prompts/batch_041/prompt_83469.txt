Okay, this one's a case analysis, not a literature review, and I definately don't want a reading list at the end -- I can't look anything up while I'm reading this anyway. The case is the Millerites. William Miller's date for the second coming was Oct 22, 1844; a lot of people went all in on it, tens of thousands, some of whom had sold or given away what they owned, and then the morning of the 23rd came and went and nothing happened. What interests me is the aftermath, because the movement didn't just evaporate. One big chunk of it decided the date was right but the event was wrong -- Christ had moved into the inner room of the heavenly sanctuary to start a different and longer business, the door was shut, the delay was itself part of the plan -- and a teenager who'd been having visions got accepted as a prophet, and out of that eventually came the Seventh-day Adventists. Other branches spiritualized the prediction instead, and some just dissolved.

So: analyze that episode through the naturalistic theories of religion, and keep the theories apart, because they get blurred together alot.

(a) The byproduct / cognitive account -- religion as a side effect of ordinary mental equipment, catchy minimally counterintuitive concepts, agency detection, memory biases. Does it even make a prediction about failed prophecy, or is it only a theory of where the representations came from in the first place?

(b) The adaptationist / costly-signaling account -- hard-to-fake commitments, credibility-enhancing displays, belief getting firmer because you watched other people pay the price. Note the awkward bit: here the costs were paid to a date that didn't happen.

(c) The rational-choice / strict-church account -- demanding groups out-compete lax ones because they screen out free-riders. Does that make the Great Disappointment good news for the movement?

(d) The old cognitive-dissonance story, failed prophecy leading to redoubled commitment and proselytizing. I gather the evidence is messier than the textbook version, that failure often produces exit or reinterpretation instead. Say where that actually stands and what it means for this case.

Two more things to fold in. First, the byproduct account has a known embarrassment: it can't say why nobody worships Mickey Mouse, even though he's as counterintuitive as anything, and it can't say why one particular god stops being believed at one particular time while another hangs on (that's the Zeus side of the problem). Is the Millerite case a Mickey Mouse problem, a Zeus problem, or neither? Second, none of these accounts has anything to say about the specific patch the survivors applied -- a sanctuary doctrine, a shut door, a living prophet with visions. Why that patch and not some other one? Be honest about how much of the survival is cognition and group dynamics, and how much is just path-dependent theology plus one charismatic person in the right place at the right time.

Finish by saying what evidence would count against each account and which one you'd actually bet on. Skip the scene-setting, I know the story.
