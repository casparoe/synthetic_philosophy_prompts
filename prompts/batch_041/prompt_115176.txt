I've been sitting in on a reading group about animal communication, and one of the regulars — an economist who has lately become an enthusiast of signaling theory — keeps returning to a comparison that I find both illuminating and slippery. The peacock's tail, he says, is essentially a pile of money set on fire: the tail is useless in itself, it makes the bird slower and more conspicuous to predators, and that is exactly why it can be believed. He then runs the same picture across a much wider territory — a firm's extravagant advertising budget, a religious ritual that consumes years of a person's life, a graduate degree that earns nothing back, an acquaintance of ours who sank two years into a doomed startup — and draws the general moral that wasteful expenditure is the only dependable proof of sincerity. Wherever we see a cost that a fraud would not willingly pay, he says, we may infer a genuine quality underneath; and conversely, anything cheap enough to fake should be discounted. I would like a careful audit of how far this analogy actually carries, not a survey of the literature.

Begin with the correspondences: set out what in the peacock example plays the role of the money burned, of the burner's wealth, of the audience, of the alternative the burner forgoes, and of the thing being advertised, and say which of these the economist's extension keeps and which it silently swaps out. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. My suspicion is that "costly" is doing at least two different jobs in his argument — that a trait's being a handicap and its being a signal are separate facts, and that the reliability of a signal turns on who bears the cost rather than on how large the cost is — but I can't state the point cleanly, and I would like you to. Does the money-burning image capture the actual condition under which signaling stays honest, or does it obscure it? If the honesty of a signal requires that the cost fall more heavily on low-quality senders than on high-quality ones, does the peacock's tail meet that requirement, and if so, by what mechanism? And what about signals that are not handicaps at all, where the trait is reliable because it is physically or developmentally tied to the quality rather than because it is expensive — does the economist's picture have any room for those, or does it wrongly treat every reliable signal as a deliberately incurred sacrifice?

Finally, the payoff question: does his general moral survive once the disanalogies are on the table — can sincerity really be read off from expense alone, or does that inference hold only under conditions the analogy conceals, so that the analogy has been carrying the conclusion all along? If the money-burning picture is the wrong model, tell me what would be a better one for the same phenomenon, and what it would predict that his version does not.
