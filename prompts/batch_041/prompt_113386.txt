I'm a working systems programmer — a decade of C, interpreters, schedulers, database internals — so I'm fluent in operational semantics, eager versus lazy evaluation, memoization, table-driven interpreters, precomputation, caching, compiled versus interpreted artifacts, and the standard computability results at the level a practitioner absorbs them. I have essentially no training in philosophy of mind. I'd like you to translate the "could a book be conscious?" thought experiment into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, and then give me a residue report on what didn't survive the translation.

The setup I care about is the one where a complete record of a brain's input–output behaviour — a giant book, a lookup table, an exhaustive catalogue of every conversation the person could ever have — is put forward as a candidate mind. Here is the wedge I keep returning to, and I want the translation to take it seriously rather than step around it: memoization is supposed to be semantics-preserving. If I take a program that computes a function and replace it with a table of that function's values, I have not changed what is computed, only how. So if mentality is a property of what a system computes, the book should be a mind; if it isn't, then mentality must depend on how the computation is carried out, on its causal history, or on something my vocabulary has no slot for. I want to know which of those three it is, and whether the fork I've drawn is itself an artifact of the vocabulary I'm using. Note also that if the person's input space is unbounded, no finite book suffices, so "the book" is really an infinite lazy structure or a rule for generating entries on demand — tell me whether that dissolves the thought experiment or merely relocates the problem.

Concretely, please do the following. First, restate in my terms the main positions in that debate: the claim that a mind is a matter of the right functional organisation whatever realises it; the objection that a static table isn't computing anything, only being consulted; the objection that the table's contents were transcribed from a real brain, so the book describes a mind rather than being one; and the objection that you could replace the table with a population of people shuffling cards and passing messages, which seems to make mentality depend on facts about who is doing what where.

Second, for each position, say precisely what maps onto what. Is the table a cache, a precomputed function, a database, a compiled artifact, an execution trace, dead data awaiting an interpreter? Is consulting the book an interpreter dispatch, an index lookup, a memoization hit, a page fault? Where does the mapping hold cleanly and where are you fudging?

Third, the residue report. Which parts of the debate translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all? I expect the phenomenal material — what it's like, the subject of experience — to come out blank; tell me whether that's a defect of my vocabulary, meaning there's a real property I'm blind to, or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it looked, or just the same unexplained gap restated in different words. And be candid about whether the translation smuggles in a theory: if my vocabulary makes the "no computation is happening" objection sound decisive, is that because the objection is decisive, or because "computation" as I use it is defined by what an engineer would actually build?

Finally, give me your own verdict, stated as a verdict, together with the epistemic accounting: which step in your reasoning you'd be most likely to be wrong about, and what argument or consideration would change your mind.
