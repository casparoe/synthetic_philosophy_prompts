I'm doing a part-time history and philosophy of science evening course, and something in the readings keeps nagging at me. The examples of theory replacement all seem to have the same shape: the old theory posits some entity that nobody believes in anymore, the entity gets thrown out, but the equations the old theory used turn up again in the new theory, just relabelled or reinterpreted. The stuff we thought was real -- whatever the light waves were supposed to be waving through, the little vortices, the mechanical bits and pieces -- gets dropped, but the mathematical relationships carry straight over, and in the region where the old theory worked the new one gives you basically the same numbers. So we seem to keep the form and lose the furniture, and I suspect thats exactly why people want to say the old theory was 'approximately true' rather than merely handy. But nobody in my class has a crisp name for that specific thing -- the retention of the pattern without the retention of whatever was supposed to be doing the pattern.

Is there an established concept for this? I keep bumping into near misses. There's the general idea that later theories preserve the successful predictions of earlier ones, which is close but seems to be about predictions rather than about what survives; there's the idea that theories are just instruments for organising observations, which denies anything survives at all; and there's the warning that we should expect our own theories' favourite entities to be discarded eventually, which feels related but is a claim about the future rather than about what actually carries over. I want the name, if it has one, for the pattern itself, plus how it differs from each of those neighbours and where its edges are. If a theory keeps the equation but changes what the symbols refer to, is that the same thing? What if it keeps the equation and the referents but changes what they're made of? And if there's no settled name, please coin one and define it sharply enough that I can separate it from plain instrumentalism on one side and the pessimistic forecast on the other. I'm not after a label I can drop in a seminar -- I want the carving. Could you write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold or tables, and in British English? It's going into a study-group handout and I'd rather it read like a letter from someone thinking out loud.
