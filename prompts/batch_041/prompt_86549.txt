I run a monthly philosophy-in-the-pub night at a community center — usually 12 to 16 adults, mixed bag, a couple of retired engineers, two nurses, one guy who did a philosophy minor thirty years ago, and me, who never studied any of this formally. Last month we did free will and it went better than I expected, so I said I'd do probability next, and now I've realised I don't actually understand the thing I want them to fight about. My way in was a long Wikipedia hole about whether probabilities are long-run frequencies, or degrees of belief in someone's head, or a real tendency in the object itself (a propensity), and what keeps nagging at me is the single-case version: I'm about to flip one particular coin once, and I say there's a 60% chance of heads — what is that 60% a fact about? Not the coin's history, there isn't one yet. Not just my mind, because I could be wrong. But the coin hasn't got a little dial in it either. I want them to feel that knot in their gut, not hear me explain it.

So design me a 90-minute activity for about 14 adults in a rented room with tables, no projector, that gets them to walk into it. What I can bring: a coin with a blob of solder stuck to one edge so it's visibly lopsided but not obviously which way it'll go, a stack of poker chips, and printed handouts. The one bit of seeding I'd love you to build in: I can print several different sheets of past results, each one a different bunch of tosses that could count as the relevant bunch — this same coin last year, coins like this one generally, tosses made by the people in this room, tosses made on a padded table when tonight we're on a hard one — and I want the group to have to pick a sheet before they bet. If it helps, I can also rig it so the room's own throws start diverging from whatever sheet they chose.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each one a sentence or two, no intro and no conclusion: first the setup and materials, then how the rounds actually run minute by minute, then the payoff scheme and any roles people take, then the debrief that maps what just happened onto the frequency / degree-of-belief / propensity disagreement, and finally what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it plus the one thing the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to just tell them myself.
