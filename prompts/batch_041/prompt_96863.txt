I have been asked to write a short position statement for a panel on class, place, and culture, and I would like a consistency check before I commit to it. What follows is roughly where I have landed. I have deliberately not tried to make it tidy.

Two terms of my own coinage are doing work here, so let me fix them first. The surface cost of an outlook is what it demands of its own adherents in day-to-day conduct — the disciplines, restraints, and renunciations it imposes on the people who live by it. The shadow cost is what it demands of people who do not share it: those it must exclude, subordinate, or leave behind, including insiders who cannot or will not leave. My working view is that outlooks are best compared by shadow cost rather than by surface cost.

Now the commitments.

1. Neither the professional-class outlook — credentialing, planfulness, mobility, comfort with abstraction and with strangers, treating norms as revisable — nor the working-class outlook — loyalty to particular people and places, directness, suspicion of abstraction, skepticism toward self-invention — is as a whole morally or epistemically superior to the other.

2. Exit is nonetheless a genuine good. A young person raised in a tightly religious small town, who is gay and cannot live there as themselves, is wronged by that community's norms and not merely inconvenienced by them.

3. The continuity of a place is a real good. A town that resists an influx of newcomers because it would dissolve the institutions that make it that town is not simply being reactionary.

4. There is no neutral standpoint from which outlooks can be ranked. Adjudication has to be immanent: ask whether an outlook can answer its own questions and sustain its own adherents.

5. Deference to credentialed expertise is often required, and the erosion of it is a serious harm.

6. The meritocratic story is largely a legitimating myth for the reproduction of class position.

7. Rankings of taste are social markers and largely arbitrary; but some works, and some ways of life, really are better than others.

8. Populist grievance is frequently a legitimate response to domination, and populist movements frequently threaten the very institutions that protect the vulnerable.

What I want is an audit rather than a lecture. Tell me which of these pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, order the genuine tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost. I am especially interested in whether my own surface-cost/shadow-cost metric is earning its keep or smuggling in something my fourth commitment denies. Finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most, and say what it would cost me elsewhere. I do not yet know which commitment I would abandon, so do not assume any of them is fixed.
