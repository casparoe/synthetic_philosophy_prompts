I'm trying to turn a half-baked idea into an actual proposal I can send to a small ethics-of-influence workshop (they want a 1-2 page pitch, and it'd probably grow into a term paper, so say ~6000 words of work). Here's the half-baked part, and then I'll tell you what I need.

Every account I've run into of what makes influence manipulative seems to try to isolate one feature: it deceives you, or it bypasses your rationality, or it applies pressure, or it exploits a weakness of yours. And they all either flag stuff we don't want flagged or miss stuff we do. A good teacher bypasses the student's current reasoning and nobody calls that manipulation. An artist makes you feel something instead of arguing for it and that's the point. A doctor reassuring a nervous patient isn't making an argument, but it isn't manipulation either. On the other side: a salesperson who never says anything false, never threatens you, never even exaggerates, but just sequences what he tells you so the expensive option comes to feel like the obvious one. A campaign ad that only makes a true statistic vivid and lets you do the rest of the work yourself. A friend who tells you only true things about your ex but times and frames them so you'll land where she wants you to.

My hunch is that the category we actually care about here isn't manipulation but something like agenda control: shaping which considerations turn up in someone's deliberaton, and how much they weigh, without asserting anything false and without raising the cost of dissent. If that's right then a lot of standard cases get reclassified, and some things we call manipulation turn out to be a different wrong, or not a wrong at all when it's a teacher or a friend.

So what I want from you is the proposal. State the question so it has a determinate answer — I'm thinking something like: is there a distinct, non-reducible wrong of agenda control, or does everything we want to say about it collapse into deception, rationality-bypassing, or coercion? Then say why the existing positions leave that open rather than settling it, and be specific about which case each of them can't handle. Then the method: what I'd actually do, concretely. I'm imagining I'd build a small taxonomy of influence cases — some where everyone agrees it's fine, some where everyone agrees it's manipulative, some genuinely contested — and then test whether agenda control cuts where the standard accounts predict or somewhere else, and work out what a principled line would have to look like if it does. Tell me what a result would look like in each direction, and which consideration would actually decide between them. Also tell me what would count as the project failing — e.g. if agenda control turns out to be a fancy name for withholding, just say so and say what that does to the idea, or if it turns out every case of agenda control is either benign or already covered, don't pretend it survived. And be honest about scope for a single term: where could this balloon, and what should I cut? Dont bother with citations, I'll fill those in myself.
