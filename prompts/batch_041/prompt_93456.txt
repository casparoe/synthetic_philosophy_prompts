I'm a freelance journalist — I write long features on science and ideas, mostly for weekend magazines — and for about a year now I've been following an argument between two camps who strike me as talking past each other, and I want to write a piece about what, if anything, they actually agree on. Here's the standoff. On one side are the quantifiers of deep history: the cliodynamics crowd, who think you can put numbers on the long run and fit something like Turchin's secular cycles — population pressure, the supply of elite positions, state revenue and internal conflict rising and falling together across centuries — with the implication that once you know roughly where a society sits in the cycle, you can say something about what comes next. On the other side are the historians who think this is a category error dressed up in differential equations: that the variables only exist because somebody decided to count them that way, that any "cycle" is drawn after the fact through the same data that was used to find it, and that the whole enterprise is built so it can never lose.

You're a philosopher of the social sciences who's sat on both sides of this fence — you've done quantitative modelling and you've done time in archives — and I'd like you to act as the honest broker. Before you write anything, spend a short paragraph thinking out loud about where you suspect these two are using the same words for different things ('prediction', 'cycle', 'cause', 'data', 'explanation'), because my hunch is a lot of the heat comes from that.

Then draft the document I actually want: a joint statement both camps could put their names to — the sort of thing an adversarial collaboration is supposed to produce. Please structure it as follows:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign. Not platitudes — things with teeth, about what the historical record can and can't bear, what a formal model does and doesn't claim, what neither side is committed to, and what a fair test would even look like. Aim for maybe eight to twelve, and make each one something a sceptical member of either camp would read and think "yes, fine, I'll sign that".
2. The genuine disagreements that remain, each one stated in wording its own side would accept for its own view. Where you think a disagreement is purely verbal, say so and spell out the two senses of the term doing the damage, rather than pretending there's a substantive dispute left over.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side.
4. For each crux, the test, case or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, plus each side's prediction, written down in advance and specific enough that we'd know in ten years or so who was wrong. Be concrete: name the kind of evidence, the kind of case, the direction of the result each expects.
5. A closing section — one paragraph per side — setting out what would make that side abandon its own position, in terms the other side agrees is a fair statement of the conditions.

A few house rules: no citations, no reading lists, no bibliography (I'll get those from them directly, and they'd never agree on one anyway). Don't invent quotes, and don't pin specific claims on named living scholars — talk about camps and positions, not individuals. If it turns out they can't sign anything beyond platitudes, say so plainly and tell me which single claim is the real sticking point; that's a better piece than a fake consensus. Keep it under about 1800 words, use a table where the disagreements or the predictions are easier to read that way, and write in plain English — I have to make this legible to readers who've never heard the word cliodynamics.
