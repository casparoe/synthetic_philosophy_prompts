The claim: for an agent whose credences and values are already fixed, unbounded expected-value maximization is the rationally required decision rule even in fanatical cases, where a tiny probabilty of an astronomically large payoff swamps a sure, modest, finite good. I'm at about 0.4. What are you at?

The thing making me wobble is a variant I don't think gets enough attention, which I'll call the self-referential button. Someone offers you a button. Press it, and with probability 10^-9 every future agent who ever faces a decision gets converted to using unbounded EV maximization; otherwise you get nothing. Don't press, and you get a sure £10,000 for a cause you already endorse. If unbounded EV max is the right rule, pressing has a colossal expected value, so you're required to press. But what you're buying by pressing is the adoption of the very rule that tells you to press. If the rule is wrong, the payoff is worth far less, and plausibly negative. The calculation's inputs are hostage to its own conclusion, which is either a lovely illustration of why fanaticism is right or a sign that the whole framework is circular — I honestly can't tell which.
