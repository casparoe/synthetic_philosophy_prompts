I'm a philosopher who teaches in a med school and sits on the clinical ethics committee at the affiliated hospital, which in practice means the pain service rings me up whenever something odd comes over the ward. I'd like to stop answering these one at a time and just hand them a procedure they can run without me.

The odd thing that keeps recuring is pain asymbolia. Short version: after damage to the insula — stroke, tumour, sometimes after surgery — patients will tell you they have pain, they can point to where, they'll give you a number on the 0–10 scale, and yet it doesn't bother them. No grimacing, no guarding the limb, they don't ask for anything, sometimes they'll laugh about it. The sensation survived; the awfulness didn't. There's a philosopher, Grahek, who wrote a whole book on the distinction between feeling pain and being in pain, and honestly that's the exact distinction the residents keep tripping over.

The fight on the ward is always the same shape. The pain fellow says: they're reporting pain, we treat pain, start the opioid. The rehab attending says: they're not suffering, opioids are the wrong tool, this is a neuro case, call psych. The patient, for whatever that's worth, says they don't want anything. And nobody can settle it by looking it up, because "pain" as a clinical term is defined in a way that bakes the unpleasantness in, and asymbolia is the case that seems to break that definition. So what's actually being disputed is a philosophical question — what makes pain bad, and bad for whom — wearing a lab coat.

What I want from you is a decision procedure for the pain service. Not a discussion of the literature. A thing they can actually run.

Specifically:

- A short ordered list of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where that answer takes you next.
- Say where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is values and not facts.
- Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Constraints: usable by a resident at the bedside in about ten minutes, no assumption that they know any philosophy, and anything that smells like a policy change has to go through the committee — so the procedure should say when to escalate rather than just decide.

A few things I think the procedure has to keep seperate, and you tell me if I'm wrong:

1. Don't collapse three things — the sensation, the aversiveness (the "it hurts"), and the patient's own verdict that this is bad for them. Asymbolia separates at least the first from the second; whether it also separates the second from the third is genuinely unclear, and the procedure shouldn't pretend otherwise.
2. Add a fourth thing the philosophical literature tends to ignore: pain as information, a signal that tissue is being damaged. A patient who feels no aversiveness may be in more danger than one who does, because they don't shift weight, don't protect the limb, don't notice the pressure sore. Some of these patients hurt themselves.
3. "Bad for the patient" has at least three readings — unpleasant to feel, against what they want, damaging to them whether or not they care — and the procedure should say which reading it's using at each step, because the answer changes.

Then run it on three cases and show me the output:

(a) A classic post-stroke asymbolic patient, alert and competent, rates it 7/10 and shrugs.
(b) A patient with chronic low back pain, no lesion, who reports 8/10 but says she's made her peace with it and doesn't want it treated. Same shape of case or a different one? I suspect different, but I want the procedure to tell me, not me to tell it.
(c) A case where the procedure gives an answer the service won't like. My suggestion: an asymbolic patient with a fractured wrist who isn't guarding it and keeps re-injuring it. The procedure should be able to say "splint, and medicate if that's what it takes to keep the splint on," and be honest that this is being done for the tissue, not for the suffering.

Finally: what can't this procedure do? Where will it misfire, and what would have to be true for it to be worth using at all? I'd rather have a procedure with a known crack in it than a tidy one that pretends.

And don't hedge on me. Tell me what makes pain bad — pick a line, defend it, and make the procedure commit to it, even if that means the procedure lands on an answer that's unpopular on the ward. If you think the whole question is confused, then say so and tell me what the procedure should do instead, but take a position either way.
