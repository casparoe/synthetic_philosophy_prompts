I'm four weeks into a moral psychology seminar whose recurring theme is how hard it is to tell a moral preference apart from a moral bias — the worry being that the very same evidence (a person's gut reaction, her refusal to revise, the fact that her judgment tracks her group's interests) can be read either way, and that calling a judgment "biased" quietly imports a standard of correctness that the disagreement is itself about. In last week's session a classmate of mine — a friend, and not someone I want to catch out — was defending a judgment about a tradition in her community, and when I asked whether her confidence might be in-group loyalty rather than a reasoned commitment, she closed the exchange with the line 'everyone is entitled to their own opinion.' I don't think she was merely being dismissive, and I don't think she was only stating a truism, but I can't say precisely what she was claiming, or whether it actually answers my question.

What I'd like is a menu of the distinct, precise claims that slogan could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict attached to each — platitude, substantive and defensible, or false. For each reading, tell me whether it would really license what she used it for, namely ending the exchange on the question of whether her judgment was a bias; and flag any reading on which the notion of a moral bias becomes inapplicable altogether, since that looks like the load-bearing move. Please give me exactly five numbered readings, each one a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
