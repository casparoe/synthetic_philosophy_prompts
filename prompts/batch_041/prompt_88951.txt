I teach an upper-level philosophy of science course, mostly third- and fourth-year students with a scattering of graduate auditors, and I am drafting the final exam for the unit on determinism, chaos, and predictability. Every year this unit produces the same failure mode: students recite that the butterfly effect shows the world is not deterministic, or that quantum mechanics is the only place determinism breaks down, and they never actually pry apart the ontological question (does the state at one time fix the state at every later time?) from the epistemic and computational questions (could anyone, even in principle, know or compute the later state?). I want a single essay question that forces that separation and that cannot be answered by paraphrasing the popular-science version of chaos theory, and I want it to reward students who can reason rather than students who have memorized labels.

Here is the setup I want the question to carry, and please work it into the question itself: a double pendulum obeys deterministic equations of motion, yet two runs whose initial angles differ in the sixth decimal place visibly diverge within seconds. A student concludes, first, that the pendulum's motion is therefore not determined by its initial conditions, and second, that a sufficiently powerful computer with sufficiently exact measurements would fix the problem. The essay should require students to (i) state precisely what the divergence does and does not establish; (ii) explain why improving measurement precision buys only a logarithmic increase in the prediction horizon, i.e. why that horizon scales like one over the largest Lyapunov exponent times the logarithm of the ratio of tolerable error to initial error, and what this implies for the slogan "in principle predictable"; (iii) address the further wrinkle that a perfect model's numerical trajectory may not be the true trajectory at all yet can be shadowed by a genuine one, so a simulation can be right about the statistics while wrong about the path; and (iv) say what would have to be true for the "in principle" prediction to be rescued, and whether the finite information that any bounded physical region can hold undercuts even that rescue. Please deliver three things: the essay question itself, roughly 150 words and phrased so that a student who has only memorized the butterfly effect has nothing to bluff with; a model answer of about 700 to 800 words pitched at a strong student; and a marking scheme out of 25 marks that names the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks, with the marks broken out move by move. The scheme should let a grader separate an A from a B by the presence or absence of particular reasoning steps rather than by length or polish, and it should be usable by a grader who is not a specialist in dynamical systems, so gloss every technical term you introduce. The exam is closed-book and the students have no internet access, so do not ask for citations, reading lists, or sources. Finish with a one-sentence summary of what the question is testing, as the last line, beginning "Summary:".
