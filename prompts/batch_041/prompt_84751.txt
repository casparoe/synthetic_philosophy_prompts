I spent 34 years as a geologist and retired a couple of years ago, and I've been trying to teach myself philosophy since, with mixed results. I keep running into the word "tranquilism" in the corners of the internet where people argue about suffering, and I can never find a clean statement of it, so let me just put it the way I understand it and you can tell me if I've got it wrong: the only thing that's good in itself is the absence of craving and suffering. Not pleasure, not love, not achievement, not understanding — at best those are the relief of a bad state going quiet, at worst they're just new cravings with better PR. The goal isn't to get what you want, it's to stop wanting. I'm not sure I believe it but it has a hold on me.

Now, I've seen Groundhog Day maybe twenty times and it recently struck me that the whole thing might be either a tranquilist fable or the best refutation of one ever filmed, and I definately can't tell which. You probably know the Buddhist reading that's everywhere — the loop as samsara, Phil's craving as the cause of his suffering — and I don't need that again. I want the sharper version.

Here's what I can't sort out. The middle of the film seems to prove the tranquilist right in the most brutal way: he satisfies every desire he has, food and money and women and cruelty, and it's all dust, and then when he decides he wants out, the wanting is its own torture — he kills himself every way he can think of and he's still there on Feb 2. So far so good for the theory: pleasure isn't the good, and even the wish to stop suffering is just more craving. But then the ending. He becomes kind, he learns the piano and the ice sculpting, he catches the kid and changes the tire and saves the choking man, the loop breaks and he gets Rita. And that's where I get stuck. If tranquilism is true, the film's happy ending is a lie: he doesn't want nothing, he wants Rita, he wants to be admired, he wants to be good at things, and the film hands him all of it as a reward. That looks like a merit story, not a tranquility story. Unless the point is that he stops craving in the one specific way that was poisoning him, and the film is just honest that a man who wants nothing at all isn't someone you'd want to have a beer with.

So here's what I'd like. Read the film against the doctrine, properly, both ways. Does Phil actually stop craving, or does he just change what he craves and get lucky? Is the loop samsara, or is it something the film never explains, and does it matter which? Is the ending a payoff — which would make the film a hedonist in tranquilist clothing — or is it just the shape a life takes once the craving has gone quiet? Point me to the two or three scenes you'd build the case on, and be honest about the scenes that wont cooperate, the ones where the film seems to want something the doctrine can't give it. And finish by telling me what the film adds to this argument that a straight philosophical case can't, if it adds anything.

One last thing: I'm a scientist by training so I keep wanting a mechanism. If craving is the problem, what actually makes it stop, in the film's account — willpower, habit, love, repetition itself? The film seems to think it's something slow and undramatic, and I'd like to know whether that's a real answer or just a nice ending.
