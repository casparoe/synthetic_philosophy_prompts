I'm a handling editor at a behavioral ecology journal and a big chunk of what I send out for review makes some version of the claim "trait T is an adaptation for function F" — sometimes with real data, sometimes it's basically a story with a figure attached. I'd like a checklist my referees can actually run on a manuscript in maybe half an hour, that tells them how much of the adaptation claim has been earned. The questions to ask in order (comparative/phylogenetic, heritability, developmental or structural analysis, optimality modelling, evidence of past selection, whatever else belongs on it), what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops with a no instead of scoring the paper. Two things I really need it to do: separate "T is an adaptation" from "T is currently doing something useful" — I keep watching those two slide into each other in referee reports — and force a genuine spandrel check, i.e. could T just be a byproduct of something else that was under selection, or of a developmental/structural constraint, and what would the evidence look like if it were?

Bake in that lots of these traits are known from one or two species, so "run the comparative method" is often just not available — say what the referee does then. And mark the steps where the referee is really making a judgment call rather than running a test, don't dress those up as procedure.

Then run it on three hypothetical submissions: one on a bird alarm call claimed to be adapted for warning kin, one claiming a human psychological trait is an adaptation for some ancestral problem, and one from a cultural-evolution person claiming a social norm is an adaptation for group cohesion. Tell me where the checklist can't settle the matter and where it'll misfire, including a case where it gives an author an answer they won't like.
