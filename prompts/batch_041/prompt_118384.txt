I am a junior at a secondary school where I edit the history club's annual review, and this spring the club is running its debate tournament on the motion that historians ought not to sit in judgment on the past. I have been asked to prepare the handout debaters will use to locate their own position, and our meetings keep collapsing into two camps: one holds that you cannot condemn people for failing standards they never had, the other holds that this is an excuse, and each camp takes the other to be either sentimental or anachronistic. I want a decision tree that genuinely separates the positions instead of restating those two slogans.

Please build the tree from the logical space first: begin with the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then place the actual positions in that space. Each node must be phrased as a concrete case or claim a debater can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and no node may simply ask which school of thought one prefers. Aim for six to nine branch nodes and seven to ten leaves. Every leaf should carry a descriptive name, or be marked unnamed if nobody actually defends it, plus one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say so and diagnose briefly whether it is unoccupied because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, rather than papering over the gap.

The following cases must appear as nodes or as the content of a leaf: whether to call a past society's labour or gender arrangements oppressive when nobody then used that vocabulary and some people then objected; whether a museum label for a local factory owner who employed children should say that he was cruel or explain that his conduct was unremarkable at the time; whether to apply a word for mass atrocity to killings that occurred before the word existed; whether to impose present-day economic categories, such as an unemployment rate or a national income figure, on a society that had no such concepts; and the mirror case of how a historian three centuries from now ought to treat our own practices, since our verdict on that case and our verdict on the past seem to commit us to each other.

At least these distinctions must appear as branches: explaining why someone acted versus deciding whether the act was wrong; judging a person versus judging an act versus judging a state of affairs such as a law or a market; treating the norms of the period as the standard itself versus treating them only as evidence about what the agent could have known; whether the historian's own era is exempt from whatever standard the historian applies to the past; and whether the intended audience is a national curriculum, a descendant community, or a scholarly journal.

Our club has been arguing with three homemade labels, and they must all appear somewhere in the tree, even if the tree reveals that two of them are the same position wearing different names: the hometown rule, on which a person answers only to the norms live in their own time and place; the open ledger, on which the past answers to whatever standards we can defend now; and the discoverability test, on which the question is neither what norms were held nor what we hold, but what arguments were available to be found. If any of the three collapses into another, say which and why, and say which of them the tree cannot reach from the starting point at all.

Finally, append a short reading guide: how a debater moves from answers to a leaf, which leaf is hardest to defend, and which is hardest to refute. Please do not include a reading list or the names of scholars, since I cannot verify attributions and the handout is meant to stand on its own.
