I write the "History of Ideas" column for a general-interest science magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on the long shadow Aristotle casts over how we moralize about markets. My working thesis is tidy: Aristotle hands us a two-word contrast, oikonomia versus chrematistike, in which the first names the natural, bounded business of provisioning a household and the second names the unnatural, unbounded business of making money for its own sake; everything later — the medieval suspicion of usury, the unease about speculation, the modern anxiety about finance — is just this distinction in new clothes. A classicist I consulted pushed back hard, insisting that I've collapsed two separate distinctions into one and that my neat lexical mapping is exactly what Aristotle's text refuses to give me; she added that money and usury each have a more specific place in his scheme than my thesis allows. A finance newsletter I follow goes even further, claiming that Aristotle's real objection in the usury passage is a kind of category error — that he's objecting to treating a sterile thing as though it could breed — and I can't tell whether that's a genuine reading of the text or a clever over-reading I'd be embarrassed to print. I'd like you to adjudicate all of this before I file the draft.

Rather than a plain essay, please give me your answer as a single JSON object with exactly these fields: 'thesis_verdict' (one of 'sound', 'partly sound', or 'unsound', with a one-sentence justification); 'real_distinction' (a compact statement of the actual criterion Aristotle uses to separate the natural from the unnatural form of acquisition, plus what that criterion is *not*); 'chrematistike_structure' (how the term actually divides internally, and why the vocabulary alone cannot carry the natural/unnatural contrast); 'money' (what Aristotle takes money to be, and whether calling it conventional or by-convention makes its value arbitrary or makes money itself unnatural); 'usury' (his stated ground for condemning it, and whether that ground is really about fraud or theft, about the barrenness/breeding image, or about something else entirely); 'my_error' (the precise false presupposition in my working thesis, stated first as the sentence I got wrong and then as the corrected sentence); and 'contested' (two or three points on which scholars genuinely disagree here, flagged as contested rather than asserted).
