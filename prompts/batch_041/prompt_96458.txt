Alright, I've been reading about the German church tax for like a week now and it's definately breaking my brain, so I need someone to reverse-engineer what the whole arrangement actually believes philosophically. Not the history, not a survey of abstract church-state models -- I want the implicit theory you can read off the machinery, the way you'd infer a system's values from its code.

Here's the machinery, in case it helps: if you're on the rolls of a church that levies it (Catholics, the main Protestant church, a few others), the state tax office collects the church tax from you -- roughly 8 or 9 percent of your income tax depending on the state, withheld from your paycheck by your employer, who is told by the tax office whether you're on the list. Legally it's the church's tax, but it runs on the state's collection apparatus, and the state keeps a cut for collecting it. The state's resident register has a field for your religion. Leaving is a formal declaration made at a state office, sometimes for a fee, and that's the only way off the rolls -- but nobody joins a church through the state; you get baptized or sign up with the congregation. As far as I can tell the Catholic bishops treat that same civil declaration as itself a religious act with sacramental consequences: no communion, no godparent role, and a church funeral only with some sign of repentance. Churches with public-law corporation status also run their own labor law: a Catholic hospital or kindergarten can require employees to meet church loyalty standards and can fire someone for leaving the church, and when the employee sues, a state labor court decides the case. Church workplaces have no works councils and no strikes; they get their own parallel institutions instead. Religion is a regular subject in public schools, taught by state-employed teachers who need the church's approval to teach it. The state also still pays the churches direct money that traces back to church property seized around 1803. German lawyers have a term for this shared jurisdiction: res mixtae. Meanwhile the constitution says the state is neutral on religion and worldviews.

So what theory of church and state does that presuppose? Some things I specifically want pried apart:

Does the state collecting the tax presuppose that religious membership is a public legal status rather than a private fact about conscience? What does the entry/exit asymmetry presuppose -- is it negative religious freedom being protected, revenue being protected, or a picture of membership as a legal status you need a formal act to shed? Does the church labor law presuppose churches are quasi-public bodies with their own legal order, like the state's own civil service? Are these parts consistent with one another, or is it just layers of historical compromise where some bits presuppose liberal neutrality and other bits presuppose the church as a branch of the state? And which parts are underdetermined -- what other theories would license exactly the same machinery?

Last thing: if you swapped in a strictly liberal account of the state's relation to religion, what would actually have to go, and what would survive untouched? Feel free to contrast France (which basically can't keep a list, Alsace-Moselle aside, and still owns and maintains the cathedrals it took in 1905) and the US, but don't turn it into a country report. American English please.
