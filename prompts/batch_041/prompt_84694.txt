Right, so I'm putting together a small set of poems about thought experiments for a reading group thing, and I want one that's actually about the machine rather than just gesturing at it. Could you write me a poem about Feynman's ratchet and pawl — the wheel with a vane in a gas and a pawl on a spring that's supposed to run forever off the random jiggling of the molecules, and doesn't, because the pawl is being jiggled by the same molecules, so it slips open about as often as it locks and at a single temperature you get no net turning out of it at all. Two things I want it to be honest about, because most tellings skip them. First, it does turn if the two ends are at different temperatures — then it's just a heat engine and nobody's cheating. Second, and this is the bit I actually care about: the same thermal kick that shoves the vane forward is the one that lifts the pawl. It's one jiggle wearing two hats, and the whole experiment turns on that.

What I'm after underneath is the epistemology as much as the gadget. This is a thought experiment that's supposed to teach you something by failing — the imagining breaks, and the breaking is meant to tell you something about the world rather than just about the picture. I'd like the poem to have that in it without turning into a lecture, and I don't mind whose voice it's in, do whatever gets it across.

Form: make it a pantoum, four or five quatrains, and please have it actually close the loop at the end — last stanza landing back on the opening lines. The closing is the point. The poem has to end where it started, same as the wheel. The form should be doing the physics: in a pantoum every line turns up twice in two different jobs, which is exactly what those molecular kicks are up to, and nothing the poem says is allowed to be new on its second pass. If the repeated lines can shift meaning the second time round — "slips" meaning one thing for the wheel and another for the pawl — even better.

Accurate enough that a physicist wouldn't wince, please, and no name-dropping or references, I don't want citations cluttering it up.

Then, after the poem, a short note — a few sentences, no more — on where the poem takes liberties or leans on one reading of the experiment rather than another. In that note keep what's genuinely settled about the ratchet separate from what people who work on this still argue over (say, how far the second-law story covers the fluctuation-driven motors people build these days, or how much of the ratchet is really an information argument in disguise). Dont present a live disagreement as if everyone agrees on it, and don't present the textbook version as if it were the whole story. Ta.
