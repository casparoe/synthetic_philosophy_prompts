Okay, I need help with something thats been bugging me alot. Thomson's lamp — the one where a lamp gets flicked on and off at 1/2 minute, then 1/4, then 1/8, and so on, so that infinitely many flicks happen before t = 1 minute, and then you're asked whether it's on or off at the end — has been argued about for decades, and the arguement has completely frozen. Roughly: one camp says the lamp shows supertasks are impossible or incoherent; another says no, the lamp is just underdescribed, nothing in the setup fixes the state at the limit, so it's a defect of the puzzle rather than a result about supertasks; a third says of course you can complete infinitely many acts, that's just a limit and nothing more, and anyone who pictures someone "finishing" is adding a physical image the math doesn't need; and a fourth mutters that the whole thing is a pseudo-problem about what "complete" means. Nobody has moved in years.

My hunch — and I want you to attack it, not just nod at it — is that the real disagreement isn't about the lamp at all. I think "possible" and "complete" are doing different work for different people: for some it's combinatorial (does a well-defined limit exist?), for others it's physical (could a device with finite resources actually run through all the steps?), and for others it's about what even counts as a single task. If that's right then part of this is verbal, but I don't want to just shout "verbal!" and walk off — I want to know whether a substantive core is left once we fix the senses.

Here's the concrete thing I keep chewing on. Compare (a) a ball that moves half the remaining distance to a wall at each step, where the limit state is fully determined, with (b) the lamp, where the toggling has no limit at all. Does that difference support the "underdetermined, hence ill-posed" line, or does it only show that some supertasks specify their limit and others don't — which is a fact about the description, not about possibility? And does anything change if we swap the physical lamp for an abstract accelerating machine, or for a real device with actual energy and time budgets?

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this deadlock a substantive disagreement about physics, a formal one about math, a verbal one about the words, or two groups answering different questions under one name — one about the consistency of a description, one about physical realizability? What's the strongest version of whichever diagnosis you pick, what do the actual moves on each side look like under it, and what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, an empirical constraint on what a physical machine can do, or just dropping the question? If your honest answer is that it's ill-posed, say so and say what's still worth keeping. And tell me what would falsify your diagnosis — I don't trust a diagnosis that can't be wrong.
