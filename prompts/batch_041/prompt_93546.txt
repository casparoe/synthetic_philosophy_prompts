I am an undergraduate philosophy student currently enrolled in a course on social ontology and normative ethics, and I serve as the undergraduate representative on my university's International Student Orientation Committee. I need your help preparing the written opinion I will circulate before next Thursday's meeting. Please act as a philosopher with expertise in social ontology, normative ethics, and cross-cultural norm conflict. Think carefully and systematically. Begin with a brief brainstorming section in which you lay out the relevant distinctions; then draft the memo itself.

Background: The committee is reviewing our policy on visiting students and scholars. The policy currently says that visitors should respect local norms, but it does not define respect, say which norms are moral requirements rather than etiquette, or specify what the university should do when a visitor violates one. The case before us involves Mira, a visiting graduate student from a country where tipping is not customary, queueing is more fluid, and pedestrians often cross against signals when the road is clear. During her first month here, three things happened. First, she left no tip at a sit-down restaurant where the local expectation is 18 to 20 percent. Second, she stepped out of the cafeteria line to join a friend already near the front. Third, she was ticketed for jaywalking after crossing against the signal when no cars were visible. A local restaurant owner and two students have complained. Some committee members want to issue Mira a formal warning and require an apology; others argue that these are matters of etiquette, not morality, and that it is unfair to hold a visitor to unfamiliar local customs. The committee has authority over campus conduct and can make recommendations to the dean of students, but it cannot change town law or force a restaurant to change its practices.

One committee member has proposed what I will call the Notice-and-Burden Test. Under this test, a local convention is morally binding on a visitor only if three conditions are met: first, the convention is sufficiently public that a reasonable visitor could have learned it; second, complying would not require the visitor to violate a central commitment of her own moral or religious tradition; and third, the convention serves either coordination or a fair distribution of burdens among locals, rather than merely expressing local taste. A convention that fails any of these conditions is not a moral obligation for visitors, though it may still be polite to follow.

Please draft the written opinion for the committee. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, for example a warning, a required apology, a workshop, no action, or a policy revision. It should say whether tipping, queueing, and jaywalking have the same moral status or different ones, and whether Mira is morally obligated to follow each local convention. It should evaluate the Notice-and-Burden Test and say whether the committee should adopt it. It should engage seriously the strongest opposing view, namely that local conventions are binding on visitors simply because they are the terms of cooperation in a shared place, regardless of notice or burden. It should also consider whether the university has any business enforcing off-campus norms and whether Mira's status as a temporary visitor, a guest, or a prospective member of the community changes the answer. Distinguish as needed between coordination conventions, fairness conventions, respect or status conventions, and mere local taste, and between a pro tanto reason, a full moral obligation, and an institutional requirement. If you conclude that the committee should not discipline Mira, say what it should do instead to protect local norms and to acknowledge the complainants' standing. Do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; argue from the case and from general reasoning. Address the committee rather than me. Use clear headings and keep the memo to roughly 900 to 1200 words.
