I'm 68, spent 35 years in paleoclimate and sedimentology, retired two years ago, and have since been reading history and philosophy of history in a fairly undisciplined way. The thing that's stuck is Braudel's layered picture of time — the near-immobile time of geography and durable structures, a middle band of slow cycles, and then the short time of events and individuals, which he plainly thought explained almost nothing on its own. Two reasons it grabbed me. One, it's almost exactly the fight I grew up in: gradual versus abrupt change, whether slow accumulation does the real work or whether the system just sits there until something small trips it. Two, I don't buy the clean layering. In my field the fast thing and the slow thing are constantly trading places, and a single bad afternoon can reorganize a coastline for a century.

Here's what I'd like from you. I've probably got three good years of work left in me, an emeritus office, and a decent shot at 15-20k a year from my old society's small-grants pot for a postdoc or a student. I'm comfortable with numbers and models, I have no archive training, and my French is poor — I read it with a dictionary and a bad attitude. I've recieved a fair amount of pushback from historian friends who say this is all a very old argument, which is probably true and not obviously a reason to drop it.

Given all that: which open questions about this layered-time picture are worth the years, and in what order? I care alot less about the list than the reasoning. For each one, why is it upstream of the others, is it actually doable by someone with my background, and what would change downstream if it got settled? Say where the criteria pull against each other — I'd rather see the trade-off than a clean score. And tell me where I'm wrong about my own hunches rather than humoring me.

Those hunches, for what they're worth: (1) the causal ordering of the layers — can a short event ever genuinely alter a structure, or only express one that was already there, and if both happen, what rule assigns a cause to a layer? Related, whether individuals are ever more than the spot where structural pressure happens to become visible. (2) The identification problem: as far as I can tell a "structure" gets picked out by the fact that it lasts, which sounds circular, and it's the same circularity we had drawing boundary lines in the time scale — we spent two decades of my career arguing about where to stick the pin. Maybe there's something to steal from that, maybe not. (3) Whether the middle band is a real level of analysis or just where the fast and slow smear into each other. (4) Whether collective mentalities are a coherent object of study or a name for what can't be measured. (5) Whether the whole scheme is falsifiable in any useful sense, or whether every outcome can be redescribed as the slow layer winning.

Please don't send me a reading list or citations — I can find my own way around a library, and I'd rather have your thinking.

Format, roughly like this:
- "Bottom line" — about 100 words: the two or three I should actually do, stated plainly.
- "What to leave alone" — about 250 words: questions in this area that get attention but don't repay it, and why.
- "The ranking" — the long section, maybe 800-900 words, one block per question, each saying what it is, why it's upstream, whether someone like me can make progress on it, and what changes if it's settled.
- "What would change the ranking" — about 250 words: what I should watch for over the next year or two that would move something up or down.
