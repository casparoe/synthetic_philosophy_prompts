ok so my reading group is doing expressivism this term and we keep getting stuck on the Frege–Geach thing, the embedding problem, and i want to open the last session with a poem about it. 20-24 lines, 4 or 5 stanzas, no fixed form, and i dont want a title that gives the game away.

heres the constraint i care about. build the poem around one flat moral sentence, something like lying is wrong. first stanza: that sentence just asserted, alone, sitting at the left margin with nothing around it. then each stanza after that embeds it one level deeper — first inside the antecedent of an if, then under a negation, then inside a question or under she believes that or inside a disjunction, whatever order actually works. and the indentation should track the embedding, so the clause marches further and further right as the poem goes. the feeling i want is that the disapproval that seemed to be right there when it stood alone cant come along for the ride. each time the clause goes under, the frown or the boo or whatever it was gets stranded at the end of the stanza, and by the last line of that stanza theres nothing left in the sentence that's doing any disapproving. then the final stanza brings the clause back to the left margin, unembedded, asserted again — and it has to land as a return that hasnt been earned. same words, and we're meant to think the attitude came back with them, but nothing in the poem got it there. dont resolve that. no wink at the end.

the middle of the poem also needs to earn the modus ponens case, because thats the one that actually hurts: if the conditional with the embedded clause is true, and the antecedent is true, we detach the consequent, and the whole chain has to be valid and truth-y in the way conditionals are, even though nobody in the chain is expressing the attitude. and someone who knows the debate should be able to spot the negation problem in there too — that lying is not wrong cant just be the absence of the frown. but dont name any of this in the poem. no footnotes, dont spell out Frege–Geach in the text, no epigraph, no citations. someone who knows the literature should flinch in recognition; someone who doesnt should just read a poem about a sentence getting pushed further and further away from whoever is saying it. if you use rhyme, let it try and fail to carry the attitude up out of the subordinate clause, but dont force it if its not working.
