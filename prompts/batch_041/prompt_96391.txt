I spent twenty-two years in the state department of corrections, the last seven as warden of a men's prison that houses our execution chamber, and I was the officer designated to supervise four executions over that period. I am not a lawyer and I have read almost no philosophy; everything I know about Camus on this subject is secondhand, and it is the shape of his argument rather than any of its wording that has stayed with me — that he wrote a long case against the death penalty and refused, on principle, to make it out of pity for the condemned, or out of the sanctity of life, or out of the danger of killing an innocent man. What he seemed to be after instead was the shape of the practice itself: that a killing prepared over months, scheduled to the hour, and rehearsed by people who have no quarrel with the man on the table is a colder and stranger thing than the killings it punishes, and that a state which arranges such a killing on its own behalf does something to itself in the arranging. Next month I give fifteen minutes of testimony before the senate judiciary committee, which is taking up a bill to replace the death penalty with life imprisonment without parole. Write those remarks.

The room will be difficult, and I want you to keep it in view. The committee has nine members. Four of them have said publicly that the death penalty is a requirement of justice rather than a deterrent, and they are not moved by arguments about mercy; the chair is a former prosecutor; two members are abolitionists who care mainly about racial disparities in charging and about how hard the state now finds it to obtain the drugs, and they will spend my fifteen minutes waiting for me to make their case for them. In the gallery will sit the parents of a man who was murdered, and the sister of a man I helped execute. I need the speech to do a particular kind of work: the argument has to be carried by what the practice actually is, described from inside — the chamber is in a building that also houses the laundry; the protocol is a loose-leaf manual, revised every few years, full of euphemism; there are rehearsals; the condemned man lives for weeks with a date on a calendar; we now kill before dawn behind a curtain, where no one who might be deterred can see it; and this is the one sentence the state cannot take back. I would like the Camusian shape of it to be doing structural work — deciding what the speech can and cannot say — rather than being summarized at the top and then abandoned.

A few constraints, and they matter more than the content. I cannot tell the committee that any of the four men was innocent, because I have no reason to think so, and I cannot tell the parents in the gallery that their loss counts for less than what I am describing. I do not want to rest on religious grounds, since I am not religious and the four members in question will hear that as secular squeamishness; nor on deterrence research, which I do not trust and which they do not need. Do not have me quote anyone or name any writer — I would botch it, and a man in my position citing a French novelist will lose the room. The authority in the speech has to come from the fact that I did this, not from the fact that I read about it. And keep me from turning it into a confession: the committee will smell self-indulgence and stop listening, and the subject is the bill, not my conscience. The two abolitionist members should be able to tell that I have deliberately left their arguments to them. Write it as I would say it, in the first person, about two thousand words.
