I hold a position about culture that I would like to test, and I want you to try to break it rather than defend it. The position is not the loose claim that culture is like evolution. It is the stronger claim that cultural change is a Darwinian process in the same abstract sense as biological change: cultural items -- tunes, recipes, techniques, stories, ritual forms, and the beliefs that travel with them -- constitute a population of heritable variants that differ in their rates of transmission, and the differential retention of those variants is the principal engine of cultural change. Three commitments come with it. First, there are cultural replicators in a non-metaphorical sense: entities that are copied with enough fidelity that descendants resemble ancestors because of the copying, not merely because both were drawn toward the same target. Second, selection rather than guided transformation does most of the explanatory work; when a practice spreads or a style shifts, the default explanation is differential survival and transmission of variants rather than the cumulative insight or intention of individual learners. Third, this is the same algorithm as natural selection, so the abstract structure of variation, differential reproduction, and heredity carries across, and cultural evolution is a special case of a general selectionist scheme.

Tell me what would count against this, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and give me the defeaters that a defender would have to concede rather than only the ones critics press. In particular:

1. What would have to be true about transmission fidelity for the first commitment to fail, and how much of what we know about how people actually acquire tunes, stories, and techniques already bears on this? If transmission is largely reconstruction under shared cognitive biases -- so that similarity across generations is explained by common attractors rather than by copying -- does the selectionist account become idle, and is that fatal or local?

2. The blending-inheritance problem. If cultural traits are acquired rather than inherited, transmitted horizontally as well as vertically, and blended rather than particulate, what happens to heritability and to the strength of selection? Is the disanalogy with particulate heredity a technical inconvenience, or does it undermine the third commitment?

3. The unit problem. What must a cultural item be for a replicator-based account to be well defined, and is there a principled answer, or does the meme collapse into a bookkeeping device that merely re-describes the change it was meant to explain?

4. Neutrality and drift. If a large share of cultural variation is selectively neutral, does that demote selection from principal engine to occasional contributor, and would that count specifically against the second commitment?

Then answer the meta-question. As I have stated it, has this position been qualified into something unfalsifiable -- a claim that no possible cultural change could refute? If a defender answers each defeater by weakening replicator to reproducer, selection to selection-like, and same algorithm to useful analogy, at what point does the position stop making a claim? I would rather be told that it is well insulated, and whether that is or is not a problem, than be handed a defense. Finally, name the single observation or experiment that would do most to discriminate between the selectionist picture and its reconstructionist rival, and say which way you would expect it to come out.
