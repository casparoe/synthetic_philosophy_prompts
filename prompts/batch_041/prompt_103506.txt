I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher. My team runs a service that ingests facts from several upstream systems that occasionally contradict each other, and today it either halts or silently derives nonsense from the conflict. A colleague suggested we look at paraconsistent logic -- the family of logics, as I understand it, built so that a contradiction doesn't let you infer everything. Before we commit to one, I want the specification: what would a logic actually have to deliver to serve as the reasoning core of a system like this?

Give me the desiderata list: the cases it has to get right (the liar paradox, Curry's paradox, the fact that from "P" and "not-P" you presently get every sentence, whether disjunctive syllogism and modus ponens should still hold, and the requirement that contradiction-free reasoning behave exactly as it does now), the distinctions it can't collapse, and any results it must stay consistent with. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration behind it. Then flag which requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied -- I suspect the real engineering trade-off lives there -- and name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate logics.

Keep the machinery as explicit as you can for someone without a philosophy background, but don't soften the hard parts. Finally, separate clearly what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who work on it; don't present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
