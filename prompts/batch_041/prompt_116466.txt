Q4 (20 points). (a) State the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, being precise about the conditions under which it applies. (b) In what sense is it a descendant of Arrow's theorem? (c) Consider the claim that "the theorem shows that no democratic voting rule can be immune to strategic manipulation, so democratic institutions are inherently vulnerable to gaming." Assess that claim. In doing so, discuss at least two ways in which the theorem's conclusion might be softened or escaped, and say how much each one really helps. (d) Illustrate with a concrete case.

ANSWER:

(a) The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem says that if a social choice function is defined on unrestricted profiles of strict rankings over at least three alternatives, is onto (every alternative can win under some profile), and is strategy-proof — meaning that for each voter, reporting their true ranking is a weakly dominant strategy no matter what the others report — then the function must be dictatorial: there is some voter whose strict preference between any two alternatives always decides the social outcome when that preference is strict. The theorem needs at least three alternatives, which is why the two-alternative case is exempt: majority rule over two options is strategy-proof and nothing goes wrong.

(b) The relation to Arrow is that the two share the same engine. Both results turn on the fact that a rule satisfying a mild responsiveness condition generates a family of decisive coalitions, and that family has to be an ultrafilter, which forces a single decisive voter. In Arrow the decisive voter is a dictator over the social preference; in G-S the same structure shows up as a voter whose reported ranking always determines the outcome. The result can actually be derived from Arrow's theorem — that's how the standard proof runs — so the two are not independent discoveries about different topics. G-S is the incentive-theoretic face of the same impossibility. The interpretive difference matters, though: Arrow says the aggregation of preferences has no attractive structure, while G-S says that even if you accept a structure, the voters have reason to lie about the inputs.

(c) The theorem's reach is wider than the ranked case. Since the proof depends only on the structure of preferences over outcomes, it also covers mechanisms where voters submit cardinal grades rather than rankings — score voting, for instance — and it covers lotteries too, since chance in the outcome doesn't change the underlying incentive problem.

So the escapes have to come from somewhere else. The first, and the one that really works, is restricting the domain of preferences. The theorem assumes the rule must work for every profile of strict rankings; if you restrict the class of profiles it has to handle, the impossibility dissolves. Single-peaked preferences over a one-dimensional issue space are the standard case: the median voter rule is strategy-proof on that domain and nobody is a dictator. Two alternatives is a degenerate version of the same move. The catch is that real political preferences aren't single-peaked over multi-dimensional spaces, so the escape is only as good as the substantive assumption that the issue can be lined up one-dimensionally — which for budgets, committee chairs, or jurisdictions that order left to right it often can.

The second is randomization. If the rule may output a lottery over alternatives, then the strategy-proof rules that always pick somebody's favorite alternative are exactly the random dictatorships: fix a probability distribution over voters, pick one at random, implement their top choice. That is a genuine softening of the classical result, since power gets spread around and no single voter's preference always prevails. But it isn't an escape in the sense of yielding a deterministic, non-dictatorial, strategy-proof rule, because the probabilistic structure is doing all the work.

(d) Concretely, take the US presidential election. There are usually three or more candidates on the ballot, so at first glance the theorem's conditions are met — and certainly, within a single state's plurality rule, voters have obvious incentives to desert a third-party candidate who can't win. But the race as it actually functions comes down to two viable candidates, so in practice the three-alternative condition isn't satisfied, and it escapes the impossibility the way two-option majority rule does. The strategic voting we observe in American elections is better explained by the two-party equilibrium than by G-S.

One last push-back on how much the theorem threatens real institutions. Manipulation requires pivotality, and in any electorate of more than a few hundred the probability that a single voter is pivotal is negligible. On top of that, for several of the rules we care about, the computational problem of finding a profitable misreport is intractable, which is some reason to think strategic voting won't be what wrecks a well-designed rule. The theorem asserts that a profitable misreport exists somewhere in the preference space; it doesn't assert that voters will find it or that outcomes are visibly worse as a result.

The upshot is that strategy-proofness is too strong a demand to place on a voting rule. If we insist on it, we're forced either to give one voter dictatorial power or to collapse the choice to two options, so every genuinely democratic rule is either manipulable or secretly dictatorial, and the best we can do is pick which vice we can live with.
