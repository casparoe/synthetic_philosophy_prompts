A friend of mine who works for a national accident investigation body and I spent a long evening arguing about the reports his organisation produces. They are formally no-blame: a chronology, a root cause or a handful of them, and lessons so that the same failure cannot recur. The industry treats the lessons as settled, while the same state, on the same facts, may prosecute an individual for gross negligence. What does that practice presuppose? It looks to me as though it presupposes that the event had a determinate causal structure which the report merely uncovers, that there is a fact of the matter about what a competent person would have foreseen at each point, that some of the conditions present are the causes and the rest mere background, and that the lesson travels to situations differing from the original in ways the report never specifies. Yet the report is written by people who already know how it ended, and its form, a single narrative with a beginning, a climax and a cast, makes the outcome look as though it could not have gone otherwise. So which of those commitments does the practice strictly need, and which are decoration? Could it do its work with something weaker, say a bare counterfactual claim about the class of similar events? Which theory of historical explanation is it committed to (covering law, re-enactment of the agents' reasoning, or narrative construction), and must it choose between them? And if the research on hindsight bias is right, is a genuinely no-blame inquiry possible at all, or does no-blame merely relocate the blame into the selection of which conditions are permitted to count as causes? My friend's proposal, which I think is unworkable, is that inquiries be forbidden to name any cause and required instead to publish only the conditions present and the counterfactuals they are prepared to defend. Tell me what the institution would have to become if we took that seriously.
