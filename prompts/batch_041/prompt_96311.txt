I convene a graduate seminar on the methodology of economics, and every year the discussion of unrealistic assumptions arrives at the same impasse. The Friedman-inspired line holds that a positive theory is to be assessed by the accuracy of its predictions, that the falsity of its assumptions is therefore beside the point, and that a conspicuously false assumption — agents with perfect information, firms that maximize profit, a billiard player who solves the equations before taking the shot — may even be a mark of theoretical power rather than a defect. The opposing line holds that a false assumption can be tolerated only when it is a negligible distortion or a boundary condition that delimits the theory's domain, and that its falsity always bears on what the theory explains and on how far it may be projected to new cases. This is the position that has come to be labeled the F-twist, and its critics are as well rehearsed as its defenders: each side anticipates the other's objections, the exchanges are ritualized, and nobody is moved. I have stopped believing that another round of adjudication would repay the effort, and I want a diagnosis instead.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that the two sides are not, at bottom, disagreeing about whether false assumptions may be tolerated. They may be disagreeing about what an assumption is: on one reading it is a premise in a derivation, whose only office is to generate implications, and on the other it is a claim about the world, whose truth or falsity is part of what the theory asserts. They may also be disagreeing about whether the relevant epistemic good is prediction or explanation, and so about which facts about a model count as evidence for or against it. Or the whole exchange may equivocate on a single word and thereby fuse two distinct questions under one heading.

Tell me which of these it is, or which combination, and argue for it from what each side actually maintains rather than from the labels the sides wear. I should like to know in particular whether the dispute is verbal or substantive; whether some hidden factual or formal question is doing the work; and whether the sides differ over standards of evidence or over starting intuitions that neither can argue for. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change in the question itself — or argue that none should be expected, and why.
