I run the fellowship and grad-school advising desk at a mid-size liberal arts college, and a big chunk of my week is students coming in with life choices that aren't really "which program" choices. Someone wants to drop out sophomore year and enlist. Someone's deciding whether to follow a partner to another country with no plan to come back. A PhD student in my caseload is weighing whether to have a first kid now or wait for the tenure clock. I get maybe three or four of these a month, they're 45-minute slots, sometimes one follow-up, sometimes a parent sits in, and I have to write a note for the file afterward either way.

I've been reading around the philosophy of transformative experience and it put a name on something I already felt in the room: for some of these choices the thing I was trained to do — lay out outcomes, weight them, compare — basically can't be done. The person who comes out the other side of the choice values different things, and from here I can't know what the experience is like or what she'll want. I don't think that means "just tell them to follow their heart," which is what my colleagues do and it's useless.

So what I want from you: a decision procedure I can actually run in a session. You're a philosopher who works on decision theory and the transformative-experience literature and who has actually sat with people making these calls — not just written about them. Think it through carefully before you write; brainstorm first about what the procedure has to be able to handle (the cases where the outcome is unknowable, the cases where the person's values will change, the cases where they're just scared and it's an ordinary decision), then give me the procedure itself.

Make it concrete: the questions I ask, in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a clear no or a clear not-yet, and where it hands the decision back to the student because it's a values question and not mine to answer. Mark the spots where I'm doing judgment dressed up as procedure — I'd rather know. And tell me plainly what the procedure can't do and where it'll misfire.

Then run it on all three: the sophomore who wants to enlist instead of finishing, the senior moving abroad for a partner, and the PhD student on the kid question. At least one of these should come out in a way I don't like — I suspect it'll tell me to stop steering people toward the reversible option, or to treat the choice as an adventure in a way that sits badly with me, and if so I want you to say so instead of softening it.

One thing: don't send me to papers or reading lists, I can't look anything up on this machine. Just tell me the ideas and what to do with them.

Take a position. Pick one procedure and commit to it — don't hand me three menus and an "it depends." If you think the honest answer is that part of this isn't a procedure at all, say which part and why, and commit to that too.
