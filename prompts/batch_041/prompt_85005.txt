I am finishing a dissertation on the commemoration of defeat, and one sentence has trailed me through every archive and seminar I have sat in: "history is written by the victors." I have reached the point where I can no longer say what it asserts.

In a meeting about an exhibition on Civil War memory, a colleague offered it to explain why the Confederate account of the war's origins dominated American public commemoration for generations after Appomattox; a second colleague then observed that the dominant tradition had in fact been built by the losing side, and that the same sentence accounted for her point just as neatly. In a discussion of Masada, it was produced to argue that the story of the mass suicide is merely the version of events that Roman power could live with, since our only detailed narrative of the siege comes from a man who had commanded Jewish rebels in Galilee and then made his peace with Rome. A colleague who works on the Balkans used it to explain why a fourteenth-century defeat, rather than the empire that won the battle, supplies the founding myth of a modern state. And in a seminar on the Second World War it was used to explain why a retreat that was, in military terms, a catastrophe has been remembered ever since as a deliverance.

What I want is not a verdict on the sentence's truth but an inventory of the precise claims it could be making, laid out as a menu. Separate at least these possibilities, and add any others that belong: a claim about which records happen to survive; a claim about who controls the means of commemoration, publicity, and instruction; a claim about which narratives later generations find usable and adopt; a claim about the meaning of the word "history" itself, on which the sentence comes out true by definition; and a claim about whether there is any fact of the matter about the past for the historian to recover. For each entry, state exactly what it asserts and what it leaves open, classify it as a tautology, an empirical generalization, a causal hypothesis, a normative claim, or an unfalsifiable one, and give me your verdict: trivially true, substantively true, contested, or false.

Then tell me which reading is doing the work in each of the four episodes above, and in which of them the episode tells against the reading that would be needed rather than illustrating it — the Civil War case looks to me like a counterexample to the most natural reading rather than an instance of it. Finally, say which reading the sentence would have to carry in order to do what my colleagues want when they use it to close a question — specifically, the question whether we can recover how the defeated understood their own cause — and whether that reading is one I should accept. I do not need a survey of what historians have written; I need the sentence's possible contents and a verdict on each.
