Ok, I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar session on the identity of indiscernibles, and instead of closing with a summary I want to close with a poem. You're the poet for this: think of yourself as someone who's done real work in metaphysics and also writes verse, so the poem has to be *right* and not just pretty. Before you draft anything, spend a little time thinking through the moves you'll have to fit into it, because the form is going to be doing the philosophical work here.

The setup, quickly, in case it's rusty: Max Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing at all except two iron spheres, perfectly alike in every intrinsic respect, a mile apart, no other objects, no observers, no asymmetry anywhere. Leibniz's principle says that if two things are indiscernible they're one thing — but there are plainly two of them, so something has to give. Black's move is to ask what happens when you swap them: describe the world with the spheres exchanged and nothing about it changes, so either the "swap" describes a genuinely different possibility that no fact of the world can ground, or it describes nothing at all and the word "two" was a façon de parler all along. And then the other horn: the replies that try to save the principle by saying the spheres are still discernible in some thinner, relational sense — each one is a mile from *the other* and not from itself, so each bears a relation to another that it doesn't bear to itself. Thats the squeeze I want the poem to sit in.

Here's the constraint, and I want it to actually bite rather than be decorative: the poem has to be invariant under the swap. Every line should read the same whether you've labelled the spheres A and B or the other way round. That means no "the one on the left," no "the first," no "nearer to me," no distinguishing predicate of any kind — nothing that picks one out. Write it and then actually check it line by line against that rule, and if a line sneaks a difference in, fix it. You'll find this is much harder than it sounds, and the difficulty is the point.

Some things to aim for:

- Around 12 to 20 lines, no fixed form — but let the repeated, mirrored, circling quality come through, since the whole problem is a description that keeps describing the same thing twice.
- It should be readable as a poem by someone who's never heard of the problem, and recognisable to someone who has — the swap, the "then there's really only one of them" horn, the relational reply, all present but never named. Dont use the technical vocabulary in the poem itself, no "discernible," no "weak," no proper names, no Leibniz, no Black.
- After you've held the invariance rule for the whole poem, break it exactly once, at the end, and let that break be the turn — the place where the poem tries to say "two" and you feel what that costs. Dont resolve which horn wins. I want the reader left holding the contradiction, not handed a verdict.
