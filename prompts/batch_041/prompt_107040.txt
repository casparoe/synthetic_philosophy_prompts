I'm a philosophy professor, midway through a paper on moral worth, and I keep getting stuck on a colleague of mine — call her Dana — whose verdicts on cases I can't fit to any single textbook rule, and it's starting to feel like a personal insult. The backdrop is the old fight about wether an action has moral worth only when it's done from duty, or whether it's enough that you act for the reasons that actually make the action right, or whether all that matters is that you're acting from some morally decent motive. Over the last few months I've been lobbing cases at Dana over coffee and here's the tally: (1) the prudent merchant who's honest only because cheating would ruin his reputation — no moral worth. (2) Someone who sits with a distressed stranger on a night bus, moved by the stranger's distress and nothing else, who'd call what she's doing the decent thing but in whom the thought "this is my duty" never once appears — moral worth. (3) A conscientious person who keeps a promise she finds inconvenient, who feels she simply must keep it, and who also gets that the friend is counting on her — moral worth. (4) Someone who hands over the correct change only because she's on autopilot and copies whatever the till screen suggests, with no grasp at all of the customer's claim — no moral worth. (5) Someone whose giving is a messy mix of real concern and wanting to look good, where the concern is the thing that actually tips her into acting — moral worth.

Now the new case I want her read on. A man donates a kidney to a stranger because he believes God commands sacrificial giving. He's sincere, it costs him enormously, he'd do it again without hesitation — but his grip on why it's right is off in a very specific way: he thinks it's right because God said so, not because of anything about the recipient. He'd donate to anyone or anything he believed God commanded, and he isn't tracking the recipient's need or welfare at all. I can see two rules that fit everything in the tally above and split right down the middle on this guy: a "right reasons" reading, on which he gets no moral worth because he isn't acting for the features that make the act right; and a broader "good will" reading, on which he does get moral worth because he's sincerely trying to do what he takes to be right, his conception being off notwithstanding. Tell me which her track record actually favors, give me your best guess at her verdict with the reasoning, and flag any third rule that fits all five of her earlier verdicts just as well as those two do. Then tell me which case you'd want her to rule on next in order to actually pin the rule down — my own hunch is that it's the mirror image of this one, some case where the agent is accurately responsive to the right-making features but doesn't regard what she's doing as moral at all (maybe even thinks it's a weakness), but I'm happy to be told something else does the job better.
