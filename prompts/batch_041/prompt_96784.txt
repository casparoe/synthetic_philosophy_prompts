A magazine essay I read recently — by an academic writing for a general audience — characterizes negative utilitarianism as follows. I would like an audit of the characterization, not a defense or attack of the view itself.

"Negative utilitarianism holds that suffering is the only thing that matters morally, and that pleasure is worthless in itself. Since any amount of suffering outweighs any amount of pleasure, however great, the view implies that it would be right to painlessly end all life on earth in order to prevent a single moment of discomfort — the so-called benevolent world-exploder objection. Most philosophers treat this as a decisive refutation, which is why negative utilitarianism has been quietly abandoned in favor of the softer 'suffering-focused' ethics popular in effective-altruism circles. That position is really just prioritarianism applied to animals: it says we should send our charitable dollars wherever they avert the most suffering, because a dollar simply goes further that way."

I suspect the essay is unfair, but I don't know the literature well enough to say where the line falls. Please go through the paragraph sentence by sentence and sort what it says into four piles: claims a sympathetic defender would accept as accurate; claims that are recognizable positions within the literature but presented as though they were the only or the official version of the view; straightforward distortions or conflations; and claims that are genuinely contested rather than settled. In doing so, spell out the distinctions the paragraph collapses — in particular, the difference between holding that suffering is the only intrinsic bad and holding that reducing suffering deserves practical priority; the difference between a lexical ranking, in which any suffering outweighs any pleasure, and a weighting that merely gives suffering more weight; whether a suffering-focused view must be aggregative and impersonal at all, and whether the world-exploder argument requires that premise; and whether antinatalism follows from any of this. Then tell me whether the essay's closing claim — that the view has been abandoned in favor of a position that is really just prioritarianism for animals — survives once those distinctions are in place, and whether pleasure could still have instrumental value on a view that denies it intrinsic value.

Keep the whole answer under 600 words, and open with a single sentence stating how fair the paragraph is overall.
