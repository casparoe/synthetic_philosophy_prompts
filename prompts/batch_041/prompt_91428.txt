I've been a longtime lurker on LessWrong and the old rationalist blogs, and I was arguing with a couple people from my meetup about Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals, and now I want to write a short story that actually gets at the thing, but I keep getting tangled up. The gist, as I understand it, is that if you insist that the probability of a conditional "if A then B" is always just the conditional probability P(B|A), and that this holds for every probability function you could have, then your probability space collapses into something trivial — conditionals end up with probability 0 or 1, or there's no way to assign them coherent probabilities at all, unless you give up the idea that conditionals are ordinary propositions or move to some nonstandard probability. I don't want a story that just explains the proof or has a character lecture about it. I want a story that treats the proof as a kind of physical law or bureaucratic constraint.

Setting: a near-future city where an insurance-like agency, the Bureau of Indicative Risk, prices claims about conditional statements — "if the bridge fails, the east district floods," "if you take the pill, you'll regret it" — and the whole economy runs on the assumption that the price of a conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent. A junior actuary, maybe named Mara, discovers that whenever the Bureau tries to make this true across all its probability assignments, the whole ledger goes trivial: every conditional snaps to 0 or 1, or becomes unassignable, and the Bureau's risk models start contradicting each other. She realizes she can weaponize this: a carefully constructed conditional that, if the Bureau tries to price it, will collapse their entire probability space. I'd like the story to stay close to the actual philosophical issue — the tension between conditionals as truth-bearing propositions and conditionals as expressions of conditional probability, and what it would mean for a world to enforce one horn of that dilemma. No one should give a lecture or cite papers; let the idea be embodied in the machinery and the choices. Maybe 1500–2500 words, third person, with a scene where the collapse first happens and a scene where Mara has to decide whether to use it. No need for a neat resolution — I'd rather it end on the conceptual vertigo.
