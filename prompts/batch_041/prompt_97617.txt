I cover biology and the philosophy of science for a small outlet, and last week I sat in on a lab's journal club — I was there reporting on something else entirely but stayed for the discussion — where two postdocs got into it over what it even means to call something a "function." She's the physiology person; he's the one who reads philosophy of biology on the side. It went on long enough that a couple of us started keeping score, and now I genuinly can't tell who has the better of it, so I'm punting to you.

Here's the back-and-forth as best I can reconstruct it. (1) He opened with the line that a trait's function is the effect it was selected for — its history is what makes it a function and not just a side effect. She said that's backwards: a function is just the causal contribution a part makes to some capacity of the system it sits in, no history required. (2) He gave the heart example — the heart makes noise, and nobody thinks the heart's function is to make noise; on her view you'd have to say it is, since the noise is a real causal contribution. She said the noise is real but so what, and that if you were a cardiologist studying murmurs you'd care about it a lot, and anyway his view has its own absurdity: a trait that just appeared and is already doing something useful has no function at all until selection catches up, which seems wrong — it's doing work now. (3) He came back with a case where a structure arose for one reason and now happens to help with something completely seperate, and said on her account you can't even ask whether it's "really for" the new thing or just along for the ride. She said you can, you just look at what it's doing and what the rest of the system needs from it. (4) Then she pressed him with a lab-built circuit: no evolutionary history whatsoever, and yet everyone, including him presumably, would say a resistor's function is to resist. He said that's design, not selection, and maybe "function" in the artifact case is a different sense of the word. She said fine, then you owe me a story about why biology gets the historical sense and engineering doesn't, and she doesn't think there is one. He also had a point I want you to take seriously rather than wave off: without some notion of what a part is supposed to do, you can't make sense of it malfunctioning — a broken heart isn't just a heart doing something else, it's failing, and failure needs a standard, and where does the standard come from on her view?

So — can you go through this and say who's right at each numbered step, not just who wins the whole thing? Rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and give me the reason for each rating, and flag any step where they're actually talking past each other rather than disagreeing. Then tell me what both of them are missing; I suspect the real action is in some distinction neither is making, but I couldn't put my finger on it.
