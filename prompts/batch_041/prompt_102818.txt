At school, and in a good deal of what I read online, I keep meeting the same idea: that copying someone's work is stealing. The analogy behind it is simple enough — an idea belongs to the person who had it, so a story, a song or a design is treated as a kind of property, rather like a bicycle or a field, and copying it is like taking the bicycle or walking onto the field. It is normally used to reach a strong conclusion: that unauthorised copying is not merely against the rules but is theft in a moral sense, and that the copier has genuinely harmed the person who thought of the idea first.

I would like you to audit that analogy rather than simply endorse or reject it. Begin by setting out the correspondence as plainly as you can, perhaps as a table: what plays the part of the thing owned, the owner, the boundary, the trespass, the deprivation, and the authority that enforces the claim. Then explain where the mapping breaks down. I am particularly interested in whether it makes sense to speak of the boundary of an idea at all, and in what should be said when two people arrive independently at the same idea, since with a bicycle it is obvious who took it whereas with an idea it is not. I would also like you to weigh a suggestion a friend made to me, namely that the analogy only feels convincing because we slide between the thing itself and the market for it: what is taken when a story is copied is not the story, which its author still has in full, but the custom that author might have had. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion the analogy was meant to support — that copying is a species of theft — survives once these disanalogies are on the table, and, if it does not, what a better analogy would be. Please keep the discussion conceptual rather than legal; I am not asking about the law of any particular country.
