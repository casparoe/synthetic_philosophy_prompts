My reading group has been stuck on the paradox of deontology for about eight months now and two of us have basically stopped talking to each other about it. The setup is the familiar one: a deontological constraint says I mustn't kill one person even to save five, but it looks like the constraint has to be justified in one of two ways and both are bad. If it's justified by how bad the killing would be for the victim, by what the constraint protects, then once the numbers get big enough (a hundred? a million?) I should just do the killing, and the constraint turns out to be a rule of thumb after all. If instead it's justified by something about me — my agency, my integrity, the fact that these would be my hands — then it looks like I'm putting my own cleanliness above other people's lives, which is a strange kind of vanity. Friend A thinks this is a real dilemma and that the honest move is to accept that constraints are consequence-sensitive in some thresholdy way and then argue about where the threshold sits. Friend B thinks it's a pseudo-problem: the dilemma only bites if you demand that the constraint be justified in agent-neutral terms, which is just to assume consequentialism, and once you stop doing that there's nothing left to be paradoxical about. They go back and forth for hours and it always ends with A saying B has no arguement and B saying A already conceded the game.

Can you draft the joint statement they should have written together? I want the claims about constraints, permissions, agency and justification that both of them can actually sign (nothing vacuous, nothing that quietly stacks the deck); then the places they still disagree, each one written in a way that its own side would accept as a fair statement of its view; then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling things would actually move one of them — and for each crux a case or argument that both agree is relevant, with each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a disagreements table. If you think part of their fight is really verbal, or that they're answering two different questions under one heading, say which part and why.

Two things I'd especially like in there, since they're my own hunches and I don't know if they're any good. First, I suspect they're actually fighting two different paradoxes — one about the ground of the constraint (why does this reason bind *me*?) and one about permissions (why am I allowed to spend my time and money on my own kid instead of saving strangers?), and that maybe only one of them is really about deontology. Is that a real distinction or just bookkeeping? Second, I keep noticing that whenever someone says the constraint would be 'self-indulgent', nobody says exactly whose point of view that complaint is being made from, and that seems like it should be answerable, so make them answer it. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, we're not writing a paper, I just want the substance.
