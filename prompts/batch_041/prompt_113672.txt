I am preparing a two-page research proposal for a six-month fellowship in philosophy — conceptual work only, no lab, no data collection — and I need help turning a half-formed idea into a document. The topic is whether brain size is morally relevant. My suspicion is that the question, as it is normally posed, is three questions wearing one coat, and that only one of them is worth the six months.

The first is constitutive: is the sheer quantity of neural tissue a creature has part of what makes it matter morally, so that, other things equal, more tissue means a weightier claim? The second is evidential: since we cannot measure sentience, cognitive capacity, or a capacity for welfare directly in other species, is relative brain size any good as a sign of whatever does the constitutive work, and if so, on what assumptions, and what happens to the argument if those assumptions fail? The third is aggregative: if the morally relevant property is itself quantitative, does it combine across the parts of a creature, so that a mind realized in many weakly connected units could carry a different moral weight from a mind realized in one large centralized structure with the same total capacity? I suspect the third is the live question, that it forces a choice between a threshold conception of moral status and a scalar one, and that the second is where most of the bad arguments in the animal-ethics literature quietly do their work.

I want the proposal built around two cases, because I think they pull in opposite directions. First, an animal whose nervous system is distributed through the body rather than concentrated in a central brain, cephalopods being the standard example, so that a single brain size is not even well defined for it, and yet its behaviour is cognitively rich. Second, an imaginary creature assembled from a very large number of small, nearly autonomous units that exchange information, so that its total processing capacity matches that of a large-brained animal while nothing inside it is the size of a large brain. If no defensible account of moral status distinguishes the second creature from a centralized twin with the same capacity, I want to know what that shows: that size was never the operative variable, or that our concept of a single mind is doing covert normative work. I also want the proposal to confront the reductio each side aims at the other — that any scalar reading licenses the conclusion that a slightly larger brain makes a slightly stronger claim, which is the result everyone wants to avoid and nobody can easily avoid.

Please write the proposal. It should state the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer; explain why the standard positions in animal ethics and in the philosophy of mind leave it open rather than settling it; say why a conceptual project is the right method here rather than an empirical one, and what the obvious empirical alternative would get wrong; describe what I would actually do month by month — the distinctions I would draw, the cases I would test them against, the objections I would try to defeat; state what a successful result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and be honest about what could make this too large for the time available. Keep any empirical premises general; I do not want the argument to rest on a particular neuron count or a contested comparative measurement. Do not include citations, references, or a reading list — I will add those myself.
