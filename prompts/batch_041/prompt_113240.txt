Their answer: "Anatta, or 'not-self,' is one of the three marks of existence, together with anicca (impermanence) and dukkha (suffering). The Buddha taught that there is no self. What this means is that when we look for the thing we call 'I' or 'me,' we can't find it anywhere. Instead of a self, all we find are the five aggregates (khandhas): form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. The Buddha's argument is that each of these aggregates is impermanent — they arise and pass away and we can't control them — and if the self were real it would have to be permanent and unchanging, so none of the aggregates can be the self, and since we're nothing but the aggregates there's no self left over. There's also a famous simile of the chariot. A chariot is just a bunch of parts — wheels, axle, pole, and so on — and if you take the parts away there's no chariot left over, so 'chariot' is really just a word we use. In the same way 'person' is just a convenient label for a bundle of aggregates, and there's nothing behind the label. Since there's no self, personal identity across time is an illusion. The person who does an action and the person who later receives its result aren't the same person at all, and in fact there's no continuity from moment to moment — which is exactly what makes rebirth possible without a soul. As for Hume, his bundle theory is basically the same as the Buddhist view, since he also said that when he introspects he can't find a self, only a bundle of different perceptions."
