So I got into it with my friend Devin last night for like three hours — he's finishing an MA in international relations, I'm in philosophy — about whether a soldier fighting for a country that's plainly in the wrong is doing something wrong. His line was that nobody can ever really know their side is in the wrong, and anyway the rules of war only hold up if they apply to everybody equally, and my line was that this is a very polite way of giving the Wehrmacht a pass, and we never got past trading slogans. What bugs me is that I think we were actually disagreeing about something much more specific and neither of us noticed. He seems to think the permission comes from a bargain — both sides tacitly agree to treat each other's soldiers as soldiers rather than as criminals, because that's the only way anyone will ever restrain themselves once the shooting starts — whereas I think it's about what the individual is actually doing and whether he could have known better, and there's a third thought rattling around in my head that maybe the individual isn't even the right unit, that the state or the political community is the thing that's permitted or not permitted and the guy holding the rifle is basically a hand. We also never separated three claims that I keep seeing bundled together: that the unjust combatant is permitted to fight, that he's wrong to fight but excused, and that he's wrong and liable to be shot at but not blamed or punished afterwards. Those seem like totally seperate positions and we kept sliding between them.

Anyway, I want to bring a one-page decision tree to our reading group next week — eight of us, mix of philosophy and IR grad students, plus one ex-army guy who will absolutely shred anything vague — so we can each figure out where we actually land instead of shouting labels at each other. Could you write it? I'm thinking seven to nine branching questions, each one phrased as a question somebody can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and wherever possible anchored in a concrete case rather than an abstraction. Then nine to twelve leaves, each with a short descriptive label (a standard name if there obviously is one, but don't sweat the names and please don't go hunting for who said what — I don't want citations or a reading list, this is for a bull session, not a syllabus), one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment, one sentence on what it would tell a soldier to do when he starts to suspect his war is a land grab, and then a verdict line on each of the three claims above plus one more: whether he's liable to be killed, whether he's blameworthy, whether he's liable to punishment after the war, and whether the same battlefield rule binds him exactly as it binds the other side. Please use at least these cases as your diagnostic material: the conscript in an unjust war who sincerely believes his side is right and whose country controls all the news he can get; that same guy after he's read the foreign papers and knows, and fights anyway; a mercenary who genuinely doesn't care which side is right; a soldier on the just side defending his own town from an invasion, as a control case where nobody's intuitions should fire; both sides ordering their troops to shoot prisoners, so we can see whether identical orders get identical verdicts; a war that starts just and turns unjust halfway through (or the reverse), to see whether the permissions change mid-fight; a soldier on the unjust side asleep in his barracks who won't be a threat until tomorrow, and whether it matters if he's a cook; a soldier on the unjust side who is personally doing nothing but defending his own village against the just army that's shelling it; and a veteran who finds out twenty years later that his war was a land grab.

For the leaves, I definitely want these views to show up somewhere, though you should tell me if any two of them are really the same view wearing different hats: (1) both sides may fight because neither can be sure of its cause, and this evaporates the moment a combatant does know; (2) both sides may fight because of a reciprocal bargain that holds even for the soldier who knows; (3) the soldier's permission is borrowed from his state's authority, so his private knowledge is irrelevant; (4) there's no permission at all — the unjust combatant acts wrongly, though he's very often excused by blameless ignorance, and being excused doesn't make him unshootable; (5) he's permitted because of duress or necessity, since his alternative was death or ruin; (6) he's permitted when what he's personally doing is defending himself or his family, even though his side's war is unjust; (7) the individual isn't the bearer of the permission at all — the political community is, and soldiers are its instruments; (8) war is a special regime where ordinary moral assessment just doesn't apply, or the rules of war are bare legal conventions with no moral force of their own; (9) the battlefield rules are independent of who's right in the war, so identical acts get identical verdicts on both sides even though the causes are unequal. Build the tree from the questions first and then drop the positions into the answer patterns, so I can see which patterns are empty — and for each empty one, say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's coherent but nobody would want it, or because it just hasn't been explored. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate, rather than pretending a question splits them when it doesn't. Write it in American English.
