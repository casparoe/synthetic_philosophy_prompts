I'm a graduate student in philosophy, midway through a seminar on Schopenhauer's aesthetics, and for our final meeting — the one on the long discussion of music in the third book of The World as Will and Representation — I've been asked to open the session with a poem instead of a summary. I'd like you to write that poem. Take the role of a poet who also knows this text cold, someone who can make a formal structure carry an argument rather than merely decorate one. The poem should render, accurately enough that the seminar's resident Schopenhauer scholar won't wince, this cluster of claims: that music is not for him a copy of the Ideas in the way the other arts are, but something nearer to a direct expression of the will itself, which is why it strikes with such immediacy; that he maps the four voices onto grades of the will's objectification, with the bass as the lowest grade — inorganic nature, the mass of the planet, the ground that never changes — and the soprano melody as the highest, the life of a human being who strives, is briefly satisfied, and strives again; that consonance and dissonance, and above all the delayed resolution of a suspension, figure the will's satisfaction and dissatisfaction, a satisfaction that is always momentary and immediately followed by new craving; and, the part I care about most, that this same art which he ranks highest is the one that fails at what the other arts are supposed to do, since it is too close to the will to grant the will-less contemplation of an Idea. I want a poem that leaves that tension standing rather than tidying it into a paradox.

Formally, I want a verse passacaglia: six stanzas of four lines each, in which the fourth line of every stanza is word-for-word identical, so the poem has a ground bass that never changes under a melody that moves above it. That repeated line must not read the same way twice — what the three lines above it have just done should change its sense — and it should also be the last line of the poem, so the melody never gets to close. Do not use the words "will" in its capitalized metaphysical sense, or "music," "melody," "harmony," or "dissonance," and do not name Schopenhauer or the four voices: the doctrine has to be enacted in the architecture, not announced. It may be allusive, since my audience has read the book, but it should stand on its own for someone who hasn't, and it should be a real poem, not a philosophy lecture with line breaks. Do not quote Schopenhauer or any other text — invent your own images and paraphrase freely — and don't give me citations, a reading list, or a bibliography.

Before you write, spend no more than five sentences sketching how you're assigning the voices and where the unresolved suspension will sit. Then the poem. Then, after it, a note of 150 to 250 words that walks through which structural feature is doing which piece of philosophical work, names the one aspect of Schopenhauer's account that verse of this kind cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply in discussion, and says honestly what would be lost if someone rewrote the refrain so that its wording changed from stanza to stanza.
