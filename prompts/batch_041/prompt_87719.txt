Here is the exam question:

The lottery paradox and the preface paradox are often described as two faces of one problem. (a) State each paradox. (b) Explain the difficulty each creates for the view that one may rationally believe a proposition when one's confidence in it is sufficiently high. (c) Is there a single response that dissolves both? Argue for your answer.

Here is the student's answer:

Take the lottery first. Suppose a fair lottery sells 1,000 tickets and exactly one will win. For any given ticket, the chance that it loses is 0.999, so if we are allowed to believe anything sufficiently probable, we may believe of each ticket that it will lose. The tickets are exhaustive, so these beliefs jointly entail that no ticket will win — something we know to be false. If belief is closed under conjunction — if believing P and believing Q commits you to believing P-and-Q — the rule forces us to believe a contradiction. We must either raise the threshold, or give up closure, or admit that lottery propositions cannot be believed at all.

The preface paradox looks different but is the same. An author writes a careful book and, on her evidence, believes each claim she makes; but she also knows that books like hers always contain some mistake, so she believes that at least one of her claims is false. Again we have a set of beliefs that cannot all be true. In both cases, a body of individually probable beliefs has a conjunction that is not merely improbable but impossible.

The obvious reply is that belief comes in degrees, and that to believe something outright is just to have a very high credence in it. Then neither paradox is genuine: the agent need not accept the conjunction, only the individual claims, each of which is highly probable. The lottery case is really the preface case with more tickets. Once we see that closure is what generates the trouble, we can simply drop it, and one move dissolves both.
