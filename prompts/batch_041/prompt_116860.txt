I am writing a paper on the metaphysics of biological classification, and I want to put a favourite analogy of a colleague's through a proper audit rather than either endorsing or dismissing it. His position is that species are not kinds but individuals: concrete, spatiotemporally extended particulars, of which organisms are not members but parts. On his telling, an organism stands to its species much as a cell or a limb stands to an organism; a species comes into being when a lineage splits, persists for as long as the lineage does, and perishes at extinction. He leans on this in a policy argument: because what matters is the continuation of the lineage rather than the headcount, a severe population bottleneck is more like a coma than a death, and de-extinction is closer to reviving the same individual than to fabricating a replica.

Please map the analogy out and then test it. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the organism, of the part, of the whole, of birth, of life and of death — and then say where the mapping breaks down. Several candidate disanalogies occur to me, and I want to know which of them actually bear on his policy conclusion and which are curiosities: that species boundaries are vague at both ends in a way that the boundaries of an organism are not supposed to be; that a lineage may transform into a successor without ever splitting, so that the death the picture requires may never occur; that the parts of a genuine individual are supposed to be integrated, or at least causally connected, whereas conspecific organisms scattered across a continent may never interact; and that if a species is an individual it becomes unclear what the word 'species' names, how species are to be counted, and what distinguishes the category species from the particular taxa that fall under it. A student of mine added that species cannot be individuals because one may say that the wolf is a mammal, which strikes me as too quick a move; tell me whether it is.

Then tell me whether the conservation conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some better comparison — a lineage as a river, a species as an archipelago, or something else again — would carry the weight he wants it to carry with less of the baggage.

Please answer in British English.
