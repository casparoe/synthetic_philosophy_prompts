I chair the editorial board of a university press's student-editions series in political thought, and I need to circulate a decision memo before our meeting next week.

The item is a commissioned student edition of John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, intended for a large first-year module on freedom and its limits. The series format is fixed: Mill's text, an introduction of no more than twenty pages, and brief headnotes. The board may, by vote, authorize an appendix of up to fifteen pages of primary material, but that pushes the volume out of its price band and its usual length. The manuscript is otherwise ready, and the production slot is booked for the spring.

The introduction as drafted disposes of India in a single paragraph: a sentence on Mill's thirty-five years of service at the Company's India House office, a note that his official career ended in 1858 when India passed from the Company to the Crown — a transfer he opposed and argued against — and a remark that he thought representative government inapplicable to India for the foreseeable future. Nothing connects any of this to the argument of the treatise.

Three positions are before the board. The volume's editor wants the manuscript to go forward as it stands, on the ground that the passage in which Mill confines the liberty principle to human beings whose faculties are mature, and sets aside as not yet capable of it the societies he judged to be in a state of tutelage, is a boundary condition rather than a premise: the argument concerns the limits of social coercion over adults capable of directing their own lives, and Mill's opinions about India, however unattractive, stand outside it. A second member wants India made central, arguing that the developmental premise is load-bearing: the same conception of improvement that makes liberty valuable for the mature is what licenses tutelage for those he judged immature, and his treatment of dependencies in his later writings on representative government is continuous with the treatise rather than a separate compartment — so a student who never sees the restriction will take the treatise's universal-sounding claims at face value. A third member wants the volume cancelled, on the ground that the text itself, not the introduction, does the laundering, and that any introduction long enough to do the problem justice would swamp the text.

Write the memo. Recommend one of the three courses, or a fourth if you think the board has missed something, and give reasons the board can argue with. If your recommendation carries conditions, state them concretely enough to be voted on: what the editor is to do, where any added material goes, whether the series format is to be broken, and what must be settled by when. Include the strongest case for the course you are not recommending, stated as its proponents would state it, and say why it does not prevail. The memo will go into the minutes as the board's rationale, so it should read as a decision and not as an essay.

Two constraints. First, the memo will be read by members who know this material well and by members who know it only slightly, so keep the historical claims to ones you can stand behind and flag any inference as an inference. Second, resist the general verdict: it matters for the decision that Mill's theoretical claim about when representative government is applicable, his daily work drafting dispatches at India House, and his practical campaign on the Company's behalf in 1858 are three different things, and the memo should say what follows from each for what the edition should contain. No bibliography, no page references, and no long quotations; I am not checking your citations, I am checking your argument. About 900 words.
