In my school's philosophy club, two of my friends have been arguing for a semester over a question our history teacher left hanging: whether the human past can be told from everywhere at once, or whether every history is necessarily written from some particular somewhere. One of them, call her the synoptic historian, insists that any account that privileges a single vantage point — a single cradle of civilization, a single sequence of stages running from antiquity to modernity, a single center from which everything else is a periphery — is a distortion, and that the only honest ambition is a genuinely planetary narrative that holds the world's many trajectories together without making any one of them the measure of the rest. The other, call him the situated historian, replies that "everywhere at once" is a place too, and a suspicious one: a view from nowhere that is in fact a view from the metropole, dressed up as neutrality, so that the only truthful alternative is to avow one's standpoint openly and tell many frankly partial histories rather than one falsely complete one. They have been talking past each other for months, each hearing the other as endorsing exactly what they most oppose — she hears quiet capitulation to Eurocentrism, he hears a god's-eye universalism that only a certain kind of scholar could ever mistake for modesty.

I would like you to draft the joint statement the two of them could actually both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration, rather than the shouting match they have been having. Begin with a numbered section of agreed claims: what they can both affirm about what Eurocentrism really is (I suspect they agree it lives at least as much in periodization, in the choice of scale, and in the causal arrows one draws as in which names and events get mentioned), about the impossibility of any narration that is simply value-free, and about the plain fact that the world's histories have long been entangled rather than sealed off from one another. Then a disagreements table, in which each row gives the sub-question in dispute and, for each side, a formulation of its own position in wording that side would accept as fair — including, wherever you can identify them, the cruxes: the underlying questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, such as whether commensuration across societies requires a shared conceptual scheme, whether "polycentric" is a coherent target or an evasion, whether universal categories like "the state" or "religion" are indispensable tools or imported impositions, and whether the whole dispute has any practical upshot for who gets to write history and how. For each crux, please record what both of them would agree counts as evidence or argument bearing on it, together with what each would predict that evidence to look like, and finish by saying whether any part of their quarrel strikes you as merely verbal — that is, whether they are using "everywhere at once" or "standpoint" in different senses — and if so, precisely which part, and why.
