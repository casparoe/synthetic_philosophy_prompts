I'm a science journalist, and I'm working up a piece on self-trust — basically, how much should you defer to whoever your going to be in a month? Before I commit to a line in print I want a gut check on whether my own views actually hang together. Here's where I've landed:

1. If I'm certain that in a month I'll think some claim is 80% likely, I should think it's 80% likely now. Straight reflection principle, no exceptions.

2. But I also think there's real value in sitting with a question. I'm about to spend two weeks reporting on whether a certain supplement does anything, and I know going in that at the end of it I'll be about 90% sure it's useless — not because I'll find one smoking gun, but because the pile of null results will have sunk in. I don't think I should be at 90% today, and I don't think those two weeks are just theater.

3. I don't think you owe any deference to a future self you know is compromised. I know exactly what I'll conclude about anything at 3 a.m. on a red-eye, and I don't think I should start believing it now.

4. I also don't think it makes sense to be bossed around by what my future self will want. I know I'll dread the dentist on Thursday. I don't dread it today, and I don't think that's a mistake on my part.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Rank the conflicts for me, tell me which one to give up, and tell me what it costs. Don't hand me the "depends how you read the principle" answer — pick a reading and defend it.
