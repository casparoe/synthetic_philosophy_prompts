I have been keeping a notebook of verdicts about pleasure for several months, half-hoping a principle would fall out of them on its own, and it hasn't, so I want you to find the principle that fits them or else tell me plainly that no single one does. The verdicts are these. The first cold glass of water when I am parched is a pleasure, and an unmistakable one. A scalding shower after a long walk in the cold is a pleasure, even though it sits right on the edge of pain. Scratching a mosquito bite until it stings is a pleasure, and there the pleasure and the pain seem genuinely mixed rather than merely alternating. Then come the cases with no sensation in them at all: the click of finally seeing why a proof goes through, the lift of hearing that a friend got the job she wanted, the satisfaction of watching a rival's scheme collapse. I want to call all three pleasures, and I do not think I am misusing the word when I count the last one. The cessation case is the one I keep circling: when a headache I had forgotten about finally lifts, I feel better, and I am willing to say I have been made happy, yet nothing positive seems to occur — the good is exhausted by the going-away of a bad. When a masochist reports pleasure in her pain, I think she is taking pleasure in the pain and that the pain remains pain; it has not quietly turned pleasant. And the case I genuinely cannot settle is a film that leaves me quietly wrecked: on some days I would call the evening a pleasure, one I would seek out again, even though most of what I feel while watching is distress; on other days I want to deny that a night spent mostly miserable was pleasant at all.

Two things I am not willing to give up, though they pull against each other. First, a pleasure has to be something I am drawn to, something I welcome or want to have in at least a thin sense; I cannot make sense of an experience that is pleasant and that I in no way like — the description strikes me as contradictory, not merely unusual. Second, pleasure has to be felt; someone who merely judges that an experience is good, with no trace of being pleased, is not enjoying anything. The trouble is that when I introspect I can find no single feeling running through the water, the proof, the glad news, and the schadenfreude, so I cannot say what the second requirement is really requiring, and I begin to suspect the first requirement is doing all the work. My tentative principle has been that a pleasure is simply an experience one is drawn to for its own sake, but I can hear this either collapsing into the claim that what draws me to an experience is that it feels good — which puts me back in need of the missing common feeling — or else leaving the sad film and the relief case looking like puzzles I have merely relabeled.

So: give me the principle, or a small set of rival candidates, that best captures these verdicts; state each candidate precisely and name the case that forces each clause; and tell me what each predicts about cases I have not listed — whether there could be a pleasure that no one wants, whether the relief of a headache is a pleasure at all or only the end of a pain, and whether the masochist's pain is pleasant or merely wanted. Be honest about which of my verdicts a given principle cannot accommodate and would have to overturn, and say whether the simpler desire-based account or a repaired feeling-based account comes out ahead, and why. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate the question.
