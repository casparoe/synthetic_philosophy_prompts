I'm putting together a proposal for a funded one-semester research project -- think 6000 words, maybe a bit more if it goes well -- and I've got a half-baked idea I'd like you to turn into something a reviewer would actually take seriously.

The topic is Dutch book arguements. The usual version, as I understand it, runs like this: if your degrees of belief break the probability axioms, then there's a collection of bets, each of which you'd regard as fair given your own credences, such that once you've accepted them all you're guaranteed to lose whatever happens. Conclusion: incoherent credences are irrational. Fine. But every version of this I've seen is about a single agent with a single set of credences and a single utility function. What I'm interested in is groups: committees, prediction markets, juries, ensembles of forecasting models. These things have something you'd want to call a collective credence -- the committee thinks it's 70% likely, the market prices it at 0.7 -- but they aren't agents. They don't have one utility function, and 'the group accepts a bet' has to be cashed out by some aggregation rule (majority vote, a treasurer who bets on the group's behalf, whatever).

My hunch, and this is the half-baked bit, is that a group Dutch book, if you can build one at all, is never really a book against the group's credences. It's a book against the aggregation rule. And if that's right, then the whole idea of using Dutch books to argue that groups should be coherent, or that markets are coherent, or that ensembles are well calibrated, falls apart, because you'd be proving something about the voting rule rather than about rationality.

So what I want is a proposal that does the following. First, state the question so it has a determinate answer -- something like: under what conditions, if any, does a Dutch book argument extend from individual credences to a group's aggregate credences, and what is the argument really about when it does? Second, explain why the existing discussion doesn't settle it. I think it's because people either quietly assume the group is an agent, which just assumes the answer, or reduce everything to the individuals, which loses what was interesting. Third, the method. I'd start by stating the individual argument with every assumption laid bare -- the pricing convention, what 'fair' means, what 'accept' means, the utility assumption -- then build the smallest formal model of a group that has both a well-defined aggregate credence and a well-defined acceptance rule, then try to construct a book the group accepts by its own rule that guarantees a loss. I'd do this for maybe three different aggregation rules and see which ones admit books. Say why this route rather than the obvious alternatives (starting from the group-agent literature, or from formal judgment aggregation), and be honest if one of those is actually the better way in. Fourth, say what a result looks like: either a clean conditional (group coherence is forced iff the aggregation rule has property X) or a proof that the extension can't be made to work in principle. And say what counts as failure. I'd count it as a failure if the answer ends up depending entirely on which acceptance rule I picked, with no principled reason to pick one, because then the question wasn't well posed to begin with and I should have said so at the start.

Be realistic about scope. If this is too big for a semester, say which part to cut and what the smaller but still worthwhile version looks like. And flag any step where I'm probably kidding myself.

Don't bother with citations or a literature review, I'll bolt those on myself. Write it in British English, and keep the tone of the proposal itself fairly straight even though I've written to you like this.
