I just finished Johnny Got His Gun — the WWI novel where the soldier wakes up with no arms, no legs and no face, blind, deaf, mute, just his mind and a bit of skin left — and it sent me back to that old Avicenna thing, the flying man: the guy made all at once, fully grown, hanging in the air with his senses switched off, who's still supposed to be able to know that he exists while doubting he's even got a body. Read the novel against that argument for me. Joe Bonham is the flying man with a memory and a war, and the book is mostly about how being a mind with almost no world to touch doesn't leave him calmly certain of himself, it slowly grinds him down — and Avicenna says something pretty strong, that you can never stop being aware of yourself for even a second, which feels like the exact thing under threat here. But I dont want the lazy version where the novel just proves the philosopher wrong, because Avicenna only needs it to be conceivable that I could exist without my body, he isn't claiming anyone would enjoy it, and Joe isn't a soul, he's a wrecked body with a working mind. So where does the novel really press on the argument, where is it answering a different question entirely, and is there anything in Joe — the fact that what he wants isn't just to exist but to be seen, put on display in a glass coffin and toured around the country — that the argument can't give you at all? Don't flatten either side, and tell me honestly where the novel resists the reading.
