I need a commencement address for the graduation ceremony of a college program inside a state prison, and I need it written so that a specific philosophical position is doing structural work in every paragraph rather than being mentioned and set aside. The speaker is a hard incompatibilist: she holds that no one ever deserves to be punished or rewarded in the basic, retributive sense, because whether our choices are caused or uncaused we lack the kind of ultimate self-authorship that would make us fitting targets of blame and desert. She has argued that the criminal justice system should replace desert-based punishment with something closer to a public-health quarantine: confine the dangerous, address the causes, release when the risk is gone, and never add suffering as an end in itself. She is not a nihilist about responsibility; she thinks there are forward-looking reasons to hold people accountable, to praise, to blame, and to protect, but she denies the retributive sense of desert entirely. The audience is mixed: the graduates, most of whom are incarcerated or recently released; their families; corrections staff; university administrators; and a handful of philosophers and legal scholars who will notice every concession. The graduates have, by any ordinary measure, accomplished something remarkable, and the ceremony will want to say so. The constraint is that the speaker cannot say that they deserve this or earned this in the basic-desert sense without contradicting herself, and yet the address must not be hollow or condescending; it has to give the graduates and their families something real. The counter-pressure is live as well: a compatibilist in the tradition of Daniel Dennett would say that stripping credit and desert out of the occasion robs the graduates of something true and valuable, that responsibility and desert are worth wanting even if determinism is true, and that the hard incompatibilist is confusing a metaphysical worry with a practical one. The address has to be one the speaker could actually give, not a debate transcript; the two positions should shape what can and cannot be said, not be announced. Write the address as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, the way one would write a letter, at roughly eight hundred words. After the address, add a short note, also in continuous prose, that says where the address takes liberties with the two positions or leans on a contested reading of them, and that separates the parts of the address which reflect settled points in the free-will and desert debate from the parts which reflect a genuinely contested view, without presenting a contested view as the consensus. The note should also say which of the address's reconciliations the hard incompatibilist and the compatibilist would each reject.
