I teach a 300-level course on Buddhist philosophy of mind and I'm putting together the take-home final. I want one essay question on anatta that a student can't answer just by writing out the standard line about the five aggregates and the chariot simile. What I want them to actually confront is the difference between reading anatta as a metaphysical thesis — roughly, "there is no self" — and reading it as what you might call a soteriological strategy, i.e. a way of relating to your experience that you're supposed to adopt, rather than a claim about what does or doesn't exist. I've been telling them in lectures that these two readings generate different answers to a question I keep getting: if there's really no self, then what is it that gets reborn, or that's morally responsible, or that's the same person tomorrow as today? And I want the exam to make them see that the strategy reading doesn't so much dodge that question as relocate it.

So here's the twist I want to build the question around. I'm going to hand them a position I've made up for the exam — though I have a feeling something like it has been held by somebody — and I'll call it the personalist option. It says: there is no self to be found either among the aggregates or apart from them, but there is a person, and this person is neither identical with the aggregates nor wholly distinct from them, and the person is what bears the karmic consequences and what we hold responsible. I want the question to make them (a) reconstruct why someone might think this is the only way to keep continuity and responsibility on the table without smuggling a self back in, (b) say precisely why a strict no-self theorist thinks the position is unstable — not just the slogan that it's a self in disguise, but the actual argument for why the neither-identical-nor-distinct move fails — and (c) tie it back to the strategy/metaphysics distinction, i.e. does the personalist option look better or worse if anatta is a strategy rather than a thesis? I think the interesting answer is that it looks better on one reading and worse on the other, and I want the marking scheme to reward whoever sees that.

Please write the exam question itself — a single essay question they can answer in about 90 minutes, not three sub-parts stapled together, and don't let the wording give away the (a)(b)(c) structure — then a model answer of roughly 700 words, and then a marking scheme out of 25 that seperates an A from a B by naming the specific moves a top answer has to make. Give me the must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note for each on the mistake students typically make that should cost them marks.
