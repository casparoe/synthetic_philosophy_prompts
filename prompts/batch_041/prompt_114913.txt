I should explain why I am asking. Six months ago I joined a small press and taught myself to cut stencils by hand, and I set myself a rule I have since come to regret: every letter must be cut from a single sheet, in a single unbroken line, with no piece of the sheet left floating free. That rule means the bowl of an 'a' has to be broken open by a strut, and yet, broken open, it still reads as an 'a'. I have a shoebox of shapes that failed and a notebook in which I have been trying to say why they failed, and I cannot give a principled answer. My niece, who is four, writes 'a's that obey no rule I could state — a circle with a stick, a stick with a circle, the stick on the wrong side, the whole thing upside down — and I read every one of them without hesitation.

What I have begun to suspect is that I have been asking the wrong question. Perhaps a letter is not a bundle of features but a role in a system of contrasts: the shape that is not quite 'e', not quite 'o', not quite 'd'. And perhaps a style cannot be decided one letter at a time, since each decision constrains the next, so that a face is settled all at once or not at all. (I have a dim memory of a project that tried to get a computer to design a whole alphabet in one style from a handful of seed letters, which is roughly the problem I mean.) In practice this has become what I call the neighbour test: a shape that is unmistakably an 'a' on its own can fall apart the moment you set it beside an 'n' or an 'e', while a shape that looks like a blunder in isolation can hold firm inside a word.

I would like you to write me a short story that takes this seriously. A piece of fiction of about a thousand to fifteen hundred words, in which someone has to make a ruling of exactly this kind — accept a shape or reject it, and stand by the decision in front of somebody else — with something real resting on it. Invent the faces, the workshop, the period; I would rather have your inventions than real typefaces and famous designers. Let the thinking happen in the events and the objects rather than in exposition, and keep the vocabulary of the seminar room out of it: no talk of essences, archetypes or family resemblance, because these are people who cut letters for a living. The case should be genuinely borderline, and I would rather the story did not settle the question at the end.

Please write it as continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicised text, no tables, no title and no section breaks. Just the story, running straight on, the way one would write a long letter.
