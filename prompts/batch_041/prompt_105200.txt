I've been arguing with a friend in my metaethics seminar about moral testimony, and I've settled on a position I'd like to have stress-tested rather than defended. My claim is that the supposed special problem with moral testimony is real only for what I'll call wholesale deference -- handing over your moral outlook, letting someone else fix which considerations count as reasons and how much weight they carry -- and that once we separate that from retail deference, which is just taking someone's word about what to do here and now, the alleged asymmetry with ordinary testimony largely evaporates. On this view, a person who consults a moral authority the way one consults a doctor, extracts the answer for the case at hand, and acts on it need not be epistemically or morally diminished; she can know that the act is right, and her agency can be fully intact, provided the capacity for moral reasoning remains hers to exercise even if she rarely exercises it. The distinctive badness, if there is any, belongs to the wholesale case. To make it concrete: imagine a device that reliably outputs the morally correct action for any situation you feed it, and imagine someone who has used it daily for years, acts on it without hesitation, and has never once worked through a moral question herself -- my view says she may still know what she ought to do and may still be a competent moral agent, and that the interesting question about her is not whether she knows but whether anything is lost.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. Which counterexamples, arguments, intuitions, or empirical findings should actually move me -- not the ones critics reach for reflexively, but the ones that bear on this particular retail/wholesale line? Sort them by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward, and tell me which of them a defender of my view would themselves concede as genuine costs rather than dismiss as question-begging. I'd also like to know what my position predicts that its rivals do not -- what should we expect to see, in individuals or in communities, if retail deference is genuinely innocuous and wholesale deference genuinely corrosive -- and whether, once I have added all the caveats about retained capacity and the distinction between knowing and understanding, the view has been qualified into something no possible evidence could touch. If it has, say so plainly and tell me whether that insulation is a defect in a view of this kind or simply the price of getting the distinctions right.

A few specific angles I'd find most persuasive, though I don't want you confined to them. Does the retail/wholesale line survive cases where the authority is systematically biased in ways the deferring agent could never have detected -- does my view have anything to say about the agent who defers correctly by the lights of her community and wrongly by the lights of morality? What should I make of the aesthetic analogy, where a long tradition holds that you cannot genuinely appreciate a work's beauty on someone else's say-so; is the disanalogy principled or merely asserted? What does my view imply about the person who reliably acts rightly but can articulate no reason at all -- am I forced to say she understands nothing, or that understanding was never the point? And is there a version of the objection that my distinction is gerrymandered -- drawn to save the cases I like rather than tracking anything real about the epistemology or the ethics of reliance -- that I should take more seriously than the standard appeals to moral understanding?
