I'm prepping for a seminar on al-Ghazali's Tahafut al-Falasifa and I'm stuck on the seventeenth discussion, the one on causality. I don't have the translation with me, so let me put the argument in my own words and you can tell me where my reconstruction goes wrong.

Here's how I read it. The philosophers (Avicenna and co.) hold that when fire comes into contact with cotton, the fire burns the cotton, and that this is a necessary connection: given the fire and the cotton in contact, the burning cannot fail to occur unless something else prevents it. Ghazali says no. The connection between what we habitually take to be a cause and what we take to be its effect is not necessary. Fire doesn't do the burning. God does it, either immediately or through an angel, and what we call a cause is just the thing God habitually does the burning with. The regularity is real, the necessity isn't. So God could make cotton burn without fire, or make fire touch cotton without burning it, or turn a staff into a serpent, and the philosophers can only say that's contrary to the customary order, not that it's impossible. He also seems to lay out two different ways you could defend the ordinary order: one where fire has a nature but God can stop it from acting, and one where God is the direct agent every time, and I think he prefers the second.

Three things I can't get straight.

First, does he actually deny causation, or only necessary causation? I've seen him described as an occasionalist and I've also seen people say he's only attacking the philosophers' claim that the connection is metaphysically necessary, while still letting us speak of causes in a loose sense. Those feel like different positions. And what's the deal with the two-positions bit — does it matter which one he picks?

Second, if God is the immediate cause of every event including the burning, what happens to human action? He's an Ash'arite and there's this kasb business where God creates the power in me and I acquire the act. But if God creates the power and God creates the act, in what sense is the act mine and in what sense can I be blamed for it? It seems to slide into either straight determinism, where I'm just the place where it happens, or into something that isn't really occasionalism at all.

Third, if God is the direct cause of every event, then God directly causes the fire to burn the child and the plague to kill. The philosophers' view at least lets you say that's just what fire does by its nature. How does Ghazali avoid making God the immediate author of evil — or does he just accept it and redefine what it means for God to will it?

Also, is my Hume comparison off base? When I read no necessary connection between cause and effect I hear Hume, but Hume replaces the necessity with constant conjunction and our mental habit, whereas Ghazali replaces it with God, so maybe the resemblance is superficial.

I don't want citations or a reading list. I want the argument reconstructed properly and a verdict on which of these three puzzles are real problems in the text and which are artifacts of how I've set it up.
