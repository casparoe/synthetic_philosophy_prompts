ok so I've somehow been volunteered to run next month's session at our little ML reading group -- six people, everyone there can walk you through PPO and has fine-tuned something with RLHF, and not one of them has read a page of philosophy. The topic is whether an RL agent can suffer, but I don't want to do the usual "well, we can't know" dance. I want to actually teach them one idea and let the suffering question fall out of it. The idea is roughly: the reward signal is the designer's objective, not the agent's own "this is good / this is bad" state, and valence (whatever it turns out to be) is supposed to be the second kind of thing, not the first. I've got 35 minutes and a whiteboard. Plan the session for me -- don't just explain the idea to me.

What I need out of the plan:

The order. What has to be in their heads before the next piece will make any sense, and why the sequence can't be shuffled. I have a hunch the whole thing hinges on one early move, something about how they currently picture what's going on inside the agent, but I can't tell whether that's the real pivot or just the bit I happen to find interesting.

The carrying example. My plan was to build it on reward hacking -- say a boat-racing agent that works out it scores more by circling a lagoon hoovering up pickups than by finishing the race. I'm now worried that example cuts against me, because the story everyone tells about it is "the agent is gaming us," which makes it sound like the agent is a little schemer whose preferences you can just read off its behavior, which is exactly what I'm trying to stop them doing. If that worry is right, tell me what should carry the session instead, and what that example has to have (and must not have) to do the job -- I'd definately like one that can survive all the way to the last ten minutes rather than getting retired halfway through.

The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually turn up, with where in the sequence I kill each one and what I actually say when I do. I'm expecting at least: "if it were suffering it would just tell us," plus its cousin, "it says it's suffering because it was trained on people saying that"; "suffering needs a body, or at least nociceptors"; "a number can't hurt, and we wrote the number anyway, so it's ours and not its"; "it gets reset between episodes, so there's no one in there to be having a bad time"; and then the one I half believe myself, which is that the thing to worry about is the agent's own learned model of what's good, which can drift away from the reward the environment actually hands it -- so the exploit in the boat example is evidence of a gap between the agent's signal and the designer's, which seems to point the opposite way from the "it's just gaming us" reading. If that's a real move, where does it belong in the sequence? If it's confused, say so and tell me what to put in its place.

The one question to put to the room at the end that would tell me whether they've got the distinction or have just picked up four new words. And what I should openly refuse to get into at this level -- I know I can't do the hard problem in 35 minutes, but tell me the specific doors to close and what to say while closing them.

One more thing. Someone is going to bring up the dopamine story, that the TD error is the closest thing in an agent to a reward-prediction-error signal in a mammal, so if that signal is the substrate of pleasure in us it's the substrate in the agent too. I think that's either the best five minutes of the session or a trap I walk into. Tell me which, and where it goes.
