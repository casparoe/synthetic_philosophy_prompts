This keeps coming up in my reading group and I want to actually nail it down. One of the guys in our group used to do proof theory and he has a line he deploys whenever anyone mentions interpretability or self-certifying AI. It goes: by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, no consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic can prove its own consistency. A modern net can clearly do arithmetic — we've watched them do it — so a net is strong enough. Therefore no AI can ever produce a complete and consistant account of its own reasoning, which makes the interpretability programme hit a ceiling that's a theorem, not an engineering problem. And he extends it to safety: any safety case where the system checks itself is dead on arrival, because a system that can't prove its own consistency can't certify its own correctness. Half of me thinks he's right, the other half thinks he's sliding from "formal system" to "trained model" without paying for the move, and I cant tell which half.

Can you audit the analogy properly? I'd like the correspondence worked out explicitly — what's supposed to play the role of the formal system, the axioms, the proof predicate, the consistency sentence, the Gödel sentence — and then where the mapping breaks. Sort the breaks into ones that actually matter for what he's using it for and ones that are just pedantry. Then the payoff: does any version of his conclusion survive? If a net isn't a theory at all, is there a better way to set it up — the whole training-and-evaluation pipeline as the system, or the net plus its verifier, or the deployment rules — and does the theorem bite once it's set up that way? Also, is "can do arithmetic" really the condition for being "strong enough", or is it about representing the recursive functions in a deductive system with a decidable proof relation?
