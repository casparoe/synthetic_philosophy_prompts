ok so I'm trying to write down my actual view on precommitment and I want a consistency check before I commit to it (ha). Roughly where I've landed:

(1) What makes a Ulysses contract binding is that it's your own considered will — the earlier you has authority over the later you. That's why it's an expression of autonomy rather than a violation of it, even though someone else usually has to do the actual enforcing.
(2) But people genuinely change, and a later self's considered change of heart has as much authority as an earlier considered decision, so nobody should be governed forever by a past self they no longer identify with.
(3) Constitutionally I'm fine with entrenchment — rights, supermajority requirements, an independent central bank — but only for the stuff that structures fair deliberation, not for settling substantive disputes.
(4) The real problem with constitutional precommitment isn't that it frustrates majorities, it's the ratchet: whoever writes the rules locks in their own advantage, and that's usually whoever already had it.
(5) In practice I'd hold someone to a commitment savings account or a prenup they signed clear-headed, but I'd let them out of a celibacy vow they made at 19 if they've thought it through since.

Which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, and how bad is each one? Order them by severity rather than telling me they're all in tension.
