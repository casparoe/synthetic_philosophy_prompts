I teach a graduate seminar on predictive processing, and my students keep collapsing every view into the Bayesian brain. I want a decision tree that sorts a reader from a starting commitment to a named position on the free energy principle. Put every reader at the same first node — the brain continuously predicts its sensory input and corrects its errors — then branch on the questions that actually divide the literature: whether free-energy minimization names a literal process or only a modeling convenience; whether the principle is meant to cover all self-organizing systems or only systems that carry something like a generative model; whether the Markov blanket is a real boundary of the system or a line we draw for our own convenience; whether the principle is derived from something more basic or taken as primitive; and whether it yields predictions that could fail. I also want the tree to track a distinction I have not seen named: between readings on which the principle explains why a system persists at all, and readings on which it explains only why a system that does persist does so in this particular way. Phrase every node as a question a student could answer by thinking through a concrete case — a thermostat, a bacterium, a trained network — rather than by choosing a label, and let the leaves carry the position names: the first-principles law of self-organization, the variational-mechanics reading that treats it as a formal duality, the computational predictive-coding reading that drops the thermodynamics, the instrumentalist reading, and at least one reading on which the framework is unfalsifiable. Where two leaves come out of your tree identically, say so rather than forcing a split, and note any combination of answers that no actual position occupies, with a one-line guess at why. Commit to a definite structure and to definite placements rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me; where you think a branch is genuinely ambiguous, take a position and defend it.
