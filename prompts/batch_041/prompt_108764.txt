I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on Madhyamaka and want a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey. Please construct a decision tree that leads a reader from a small number of starting commitments to a named interpretive position on the tetralemma as Nāgārjuna deploys it — the four alternatives that a thing is so, that it is not so, that it both is and is not so, and that it neither is nor is not so.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of interpreters. Each internal node should be a question about the tetralemma itself that a reader could answer without already knowing which interpretation it leads to. I suggest the following branch points, though you should add or refine them as the logic requires: (a) whether the four alternatives are meant to be jointly exhaustive, or whether the fourth does something other than close off a fourth option; (b) whether the conclusion of the tetralemma is itself a thesis about how things are, or a device for dissolving the question and relinquishing the view; (c) whether the negations at the several corners operate at the conventional level, the ultimate level, or shift between the two; (d) whether the fourth corner carries a positive content — something ineffable being pointed at — or is merely the absence of the first three; (e) whether the tetralemma is offered as a piece of formal logic, a many-valued or paraconsistent scheme that may tolerate a true contradiction or suspend the excluded middle, or as a soteriological and dialectical instrument with no logical pretensions.

Make one branch explicit on a point that tends to be glossed over: in classical logic the fourth alternative, neither so nor not so, is unsatisfiable, so any reading that treats it as a live option owes an account of what logic is in play. Make a further branch for whether the tetralemma is applied to a proposition, to causal origination, or to a question such as the status of the Tathāgata after death, since the four corners may not behave uniformly across these.

The leaves should include at least the following positions, each carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment: a reductio reading, on which all four corners are canvassed and rejected so that the question is shown to be ill-posed; a no-thesis or therapeutic reading, on which the point is to stop holding views rather than to hold a better one; a two-truths reading, on which the negations are indexed to the conventional and ultimate levels; an ineffabilist reading, on which the fourth corner names a reality beyond predication; a dialetheist reading, on which the third corner is true and contradictions can obtain; and a formal reading on which the tetralemma is a four-valued schema. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any question you can phrase, say so explicitly and explain what makes them indistinguishable. Finally, list any combination of answers that no position occupies, and say, in a phrase, whether the gap is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
