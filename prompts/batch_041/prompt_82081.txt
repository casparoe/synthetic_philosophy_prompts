I'm taking a course on ancient political thought, and I've been working through the constitutional debate that Herodotus stages among the Persian conspirators after the death of the Magian. Otanes's speech in favor of rule by the many is the part I keep returning to, partly because the list of features he gives seems, at first glance, like an arbitrary bundle: he names equality, then notes that offices are filled by lot, then that magistrates must submit to an audit, then that deliberation is brought before the public assembly. Here is the passage as I have it:

"On the other hand the rule of many has first a name attaching to it which is the fairest of all names, that is to say 'Equality'; next, the multitude does none of those things which the monarch does: offices of state are exercised by lot, and the magistrates are compelled to render account of their action: and finally all matters of deliberation are referred to the public assembly."

What I want help with is not a paraphrase of the passage but an account of its internal logic. In particular: what conception of political equality would make selection by lot, accountability of officeholders, and referral of deliberation to the assembly hang together as expressions of a single idea, rather than as three separate institutional preferences? And how does the contrast with monarchy — the claim that the multitude "does none of those things which the monarch does" — structure that idea, given that a monarch might in principle also be audited or consult advisers? I suspect there is something here about the difference between being subject to a will and being subject to a procedure, but I can't get the thought to come out cleanly.

Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one to two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, addressing what unifies the features Otanes lists.
