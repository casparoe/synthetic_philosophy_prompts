I'm putting together a short position statement for a graduate seminar on practical reason — the term's reading has been the Williams-style internalism and its critics — and before I circulate it I'd like an audit of whether the thing actually hangs together. My commitments, roughly in descending order of confidence: (1) Internalism about reasons: a consideration counts as a reason for an agent only if sound deliberation from that agent's existing motivational set could arrive at it; if nothing already in the set can be worked up into a motive for φ-ing, then there is no reason for him to φ, and the thoroughgoing amoralist — no trace of concern for anyone, no desire even to become otherwise — has no reason to be decent. (2) The moral 'ought' is nonetheless categorical: it is not conditional on the agent's aims, projects or desires, the amoralist's indifference does not exempt him from it, and his cruelty is wrong for him in the same sense in which it is wrong for anyone else. (3) Cognitivism about moral judgement: moral judgements are beliefs — truth-apt, embeddable in conditionals, assessable for consistency — and not expressions of attitude. (4) The Humean theory of motivation: only a desire-involving state can motivate; a belief on its own moves nothing. (5) Practical unreason is a matter of incoherence among one's attitudes rather than of failing to care about the right things, so the amoralist is not irrational and there is no requirement of reason as such to be moral. (6) A moral judgement is not merely a belief with a desire bolted on: judging that something is wrong carries its own motivational force, and someone who claims to judge an act wrong while remaining entirely indifferent to it has not really made the judgement at all, but only gone through the motions of one. (7) A verdict on a case: someone raised in a racist household who is convinced by argument that his old attitudes were wrong, but whose gut reactions take years to follow, nevertheless has a reason to stop repeating the slurs from the moment the conviction forms — the reason arrived with the belief, before anything changed in what moves him.

Two things nag at me. The first is that several people who have read a draft tell me there is something in here that cannot be held together with the rest, and I genuinely cannot see which item it is; I would rather find out now than in front of an audience. The second is that I can't tell how much of my confidence in (1) is doing real work, since it seems to be the item the others keep leaning on. Please audit the set: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are and why the appearance is misleading, and how severe each real tension is relative to the others. For each genuine tension, say precisely what would have to be given up, what that concession costs elsewhere in the package, and whether there is a reading on which both items survive — flagging any such reading as a patch rather than a dissolution. Then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most, and what it would leave dangling. I'd rather have an ordered diagnosis with the cheapest fixes than a survey, so please don't hedge by declaring everything mutually compatible, and don't just restate the standard positions in the literature.
