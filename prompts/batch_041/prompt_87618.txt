You are a philosopher of the human sciences at home in both comparative genocide studies and the theory of measurement. I want a diagnosis of a stalled argument — not a survey of it, and not a verdict about who is right — and I would rather you reason slowly and flag your uncertainties than produce something balanced-sounding.

I am a retired natural scientist: forty years in an experimental field, much of it spent on measurement and statistical inference, and the last few years spent reading moral philosophy and the historiography of mass violence. The puzzle I keep meeting is this. One side insists that atrocities must be compared: without comparison there is no causal explanation (you cannot say what caused an event unless you can say what distinguishes it from events of the same kind), no proportionate response (aid triage, tribunal sentencing, reparation schemes all presuppose that some harms are worse than others), and nothing to learn. The other side — often, though not only, working on the Holocaust — holds that ranking atrocities is not merely tasteless but a category error or a second injury: that placing a horror on a scale already misunderstands it, and that the demand for the ranking is itself a moral failure. What strikes me is that both sides accept the same facts about what happened, and both accept that suffering is bad and the dead are not to be insulted; nobody in the room is a relativist about atrocity. Yet the argument has run for decades, and in the seminars I attend it has the texture of two people who have stopped listening. In my own field, when two things resisted comparison, the working assumption was that the scale had been mis-specified — so I want to know whether the humanists are making a claim that would simply be false in my field's terms, or a claim of another kind.

Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: three questions are being run together, and the word 'comparison' conceals the seams. (1) A measurement-theoretic question — what structure do the parties take the relation 'at least as bad as' to have? A weak order requires every pair comparable and the relation transitive; if comparability is incomplete, so that we have a partial rather than a total order, then some pairs simply stand unranked, and 'incomparable' names a fact about the shape of the space rather than a refusal to look. Interval or ratio scaling is a further step, licensing talk of how much worse, and it is not obvious that the incomparabilists deny the first while being heard as denying the third. (2) A question about purpose — comparing two events in order to explain them causally, in order to allocate something scarce, and in order to place them in a public ranking may not demand the same kind or degree of comparability, and obscenity may attach to one use and not the others. (3) A normative and performative question — whether publishing or institutionalizing a ranking has effects (legitimating some suffering as lesser, feeding denial, freezing a hierarchy of victimhood) that make it wrong to do even where the underlying comparison is well founded.

Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you set out what each side would say if pressed, and what would count for each as a counterexample to its own position. Then give me your best diagnosis: a verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement about the structure of value, two groups answering different questions under one heading, or a clash of bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for — and in the last case, which intuitions, and why they cannot be argued for. Is some hidden empirical or formal question doing the work, for instance a claim that no actual historical comparison can be carried out without smuggling in a scale? Tell me whether either side is confused about its own commitments. Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about partial orders or about aggregation under incomparability, the recasting of the obscenity objection as a constraint on what may be published rather than on what may be thought, or a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected here, and why that is not a failure. Be concrete about the kinds of cases you are using. No reading lists or citations, please.
