Until recently my interest in Marx's account of machinery was purely academic. Eight months on the night shift at a regional distribution centre — where the pick rates, the routing and the pace are all set by software, and the people on the floor function as the flexible parts of an otherwise automated system — has made it something else. I am now preparing an MA thesis prospectus and would like you to draft the proposal itself: roughly 1,500 words, one semester of full-time work, and no bibliography, since I will supply the references myself.

Here is the half-formed idea I want you to work up. Marx holds that capital adopts machinery only where it reduces the cost of producing commodities and thereby cheapens labour power, so that a technical change which could shorten the working day instead lengthens and intensifies it; and he observes that capital declines to mechanise where labour is cheap enough, preferring to work that labour harder. This bias claim is usually read as a theorem of the labour theory of value, and has accordingly been treated as collateral damage in the long dispute over whether that theory can be rescued. I want to pull apart two things the argument runs together. Call them displacing automation, which removes the need for a worker altogether, and disciplining automation, which keeps the worker in place while subordinating him to the machine — pacing him, deskilling him, rendering him interchangeable with cheaper labour. The two come apart in both directions: a numerically controlled machine tool with a skilled attendant disciplines without displacing, while a fully automated container terminal displaces stevedores and thereafter disciplines no one. My suspicion is that the bias claim is carried by the value theory only in the displacing case, and that in the disciplining case it rests on a different and sturdier premise about the subordination of labour in production. If that is right, the politics of automation can be re-founded on the half of Marx's argument that does not depend on his economics.

The proposal should give me: the question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer; why the existing readings leave it open, given that the literature tends either to read the machinery discussion as an empirical history of deskilling or to assess it purely as value theory, and that no one seems to ask which of the two arguments carries the weight; the method, month by month, and why conceptual reconstruction rather than a case study of a workplace like mine; what a finished result would look like; what would count as failure — the distinction collapsing, or the disciplining argument turning out to need the value theory after all, which is a live risk given that the cheap-labour limit is itself stated in value terms; and a candid scope check for a single semester, including what I should cut. No citations, please; I will add them.
