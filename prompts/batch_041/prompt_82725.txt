I have to run a ninety-minute session on the metaphysics of laws of nature for a mixed reading group — a working physicist, a mechanical engineer, a historian of science, a theologian, and two graduate students from other departments — none of whom has read the technical literature, and all of whom arrive believing that a law of nature is simply the equation some scientist has discovered. What I want from you is a teaching plan, not the exposition itself. My target is the standoff between the Humean picture, on which a law is whatever generalization earns its place in the simplest and strongest true description of the whole mosaic of non-modal fact, and the anti-Humean picture, on which laws are irreducibly modal and do genuine explanatory and governing work. Tell me the order in which the components must be introduced so that each is forced by a question the audience already feels rather than simply announced; what has to be in place before the best-system idea can even be stated without sounding like an evasion; and where in that sequence the modal intuition is at its strongest and must be acknowledged rather than suppressed.

Build the session on a single carrying example, and please do not reach for the standard stock — no grue, no gold-cubes-smaller-than-a-cubic-mile generalization, no ideal gas law. Use instead a simulated world: a video game with a physics engine, in which the observable regularities can come apart from the code that produced them. Specify exactly what that example must contain for the debate to bite — one regularity that is exceptionless yet intuitively not a law, and one that is lawlike yet tolerates exceptions or idealization — and what it must leave out, so that the simplicity-versus-strength trade-off, rather than the audience's pre-existing intuitions, is what does the work. Then give me the misconceptions you expect, in the order they will actually surface, and say for each precisely where in the sequence I should head it off. I am anticipating that someone will insist that Humeanism just means laws are patterns, that if laws do not govern then they cannot explain or support counterfactuals, that anti-Humeanism is theology or Aristotelian essences by another name, and that the whole dispute is really about whether laws exist; I want to know which of these are worth defusing early and which should be left to ripen. End with a single question I can put to the group that would show whether they have grasped the disagreement itself rather than memorized two labels, and tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level, and how to say so.
