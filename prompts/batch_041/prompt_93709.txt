For the past few weeks I have been trying, without much success, to get a friend of mine — a sharp product manager with no philosophy beyond an undergraduate survey course — to see why David Lewis's analysis of convention in Convention (1969) is more interesting than the truism that people follow rules because rules are useful. The point I keep failing to land is that on Lewis's account a convention is a regularity in a recurrent coordination situation, sustained not by agreement or enforcement but by common knowledge that everyone conforms and by each person's preference to conform on the condition that the others do; that the regularity need only be one equilibrium among several, so it can be arbitrary in the strong sense, its only explanation being the accident of precedent and salience that first made it focal; and that precisely because it rests on expectations rather than on preferences, a convention can be practically unchangeable even when everyone would privately prefer a different arrangement, and can be stable while being strictly worse than an alternative that is right there for the taking. She has also read a couple of popular essays claiming that conventions evolve toward efficiency, and I would like something in my pocket that punctures that idea without turning into a lecture. My examples are exhausted — which side of the road, which hand holds the fork, the layout of the keyboard — and each time I offer one she says, reasonably enough, that those are just things that turned out to be convenient.

So I would like you to write a joke I can send her. It should be an actual joke, with a setup and a punchline, and the punchline should turn on the structure of the Lewisian account rather than on the fact that philosophers are strange: on the arbitrariness of the selected equilibrium; or on the self-enforcing character of the regularity, which needs neither promise nor sanction; or on the common-knowledge requirement, so that two people who independently do the same arbitrary thing for the same good reason, each unaware of the other, have not got a convention between them at all; or on the gap between a convention and a mere habit, a coerced rule, or a norm held in place by disapproval. The strongest version would also make a point about the supposed evolution of conventions — that the process selecting them is path-dependent and blind to which equilibrium is better, so that a convention can be universally followed, universally resented, and immune to reform because what would have to change is not anyone's preferences but everyone's expectations of everyone else. Keep it short enough to send in a message, and let the joke assume that she has understood the theory by the time she reaches the punchline; the setting can be as ordinary as a shared kitchen, an office lift, or the way people queue.
