For an episode of my history of political thought podcast, I'll be interviewing a scholar whose revisionist reading of Machiavelli runs roughly as follows. On her account, fortune and virtù are not two rival forces in his thought but a single phenomenon described from two directions: fortuna names the indeterminacy of a situation as it looks before anyone acts on it, and virtù names the capacity to shrink that indeterminacy by acting in ways that change what the situation affords. The set-piece images — the swollen river that can be contained with dykes and embankments, the woman who must be beaten and coerced if she is to be held — are on this view rhetorical elaborations of one claim about agency rather than two competing theories of it. She also holds that her reading dissolves the familiar objection that Machiavelli's own examples refute him, above all Cesare Borgia, whose elaborate preparations come to nothing when his father dies and he falls ill at the very moment the papacy changes hands.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that press on the substance rather than letting her restate the thesis. I want at least one question on whether identifying virtù with the reduction of indeterminacy survives the passages where success seems to turn on a man's mode of proceeding happening to match the character of the times, which sounds like the priority of luck; at least one on whether a single concept can span the prince's virtù and the civic virtù of the Discourses, or whether her reading quietly privileges one text over the other; at least one on whether fortuna is doing any explanatory work on her account at all, or collapses into a residual term for whatever virtù has not yet explained; and at least one on what the gendered imagery is doing if fortune is really just indeterminacy. For each question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

Please also mark which of the interpretive claims in play are settled points of agreement among specialists and which are genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested reading of the texts as the consensus.
