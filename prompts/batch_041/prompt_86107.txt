I facilitate restorative justice conferences, mostly for property offences and low-level assaults, and I have done it for three years. The work depends on a fairly robust practice of asking people to own what they did and to answer for it to the person they harmed. Last month a colleague on our panel told me that she had worked through the regress argument against moral responsibility that is usually credited to Galen Strawson, and that she now thinks no one has ever been responsible for anything at all. What unsettled me is that she has not changed a single thing about how she runs a conference. Her view is that responsibility in that room is a social practice, not a metaphysical fact, so nothing about the practice needs to change. I cannot tell whether that is a stable, defensible position or a piece of evasion, and I would rather have a hard answer than a comforting one.

I would like you to act as a philosopher of action and moral responsibility, the sort of person who works on the metaphysics of agency and knows the compatibilist and incompatibilist literature from the inside rather than at second hand. Think carefully and slowly, and begin by brainstorming rather than concluding: before you commit to any numbers, spend some effort laying out the space of positions, including the ones that seem uncharitable to my formulation below. Do not try to settle the debate by appeal to what the literature is said to have settled; I want your own reasoning, carried out in front of me. I have no internet access and do not want a reading list, so please do not tell me what to go and read.

Here is the argument as I understand it, in my own words, so that you can work with my version of it rather than some canonical statement:

1. An agent is morally responsible for an action only if she is ultimately responsible for it, in the sense that its ultimate source lies in her rather than in factors she never had any hand in.
2. She is ultimately responsible for an action only if she is responsible for the mental constitution, character, and values from which the action flows.
3. She is responsible for that constitution only if she is responsible for the earlier constitution that produced it, together with the choices by which she shaped it, since those choices themselves flowed from how she already was.
4. This regress cannot terminate in anything that would satisfy premise 1: either it continues indefinitely, or it stops at a constitution the agent had no part in producing.
5. Therefore no one is ever ultimately responsible, and by premise 1 no one is ever morally responsible.

I want to flag one thing about how I read the regress, because it is the part I am least sure about. It seems to me to come in two forms. The diachronic form runs backwards through time: your self-formation traces to a self you did not form. The synchronic form does not need time at all: at any given moment, your endorsement of your own motives, or your identification with them, is itself just one more thing that is the way it is because of the way you are. If the synchronic version is the real engine of the argument, then replies that appeal to a person shaping herself over time may miss the point, and replies that appeal to ownership at a time may be more to the point. Please tell me whether you think that distinction is doing any work or whether it collapses.

With all of that in place, I want the following, and I want numbers rather than hedged discussion.

First, your credence, between 0 and 1, that the argument as I have reconstructed it is sound. State it as a single number.

Second, the decomposition that produces that number. Assign a rough probability to each of the following candidate points of failure, one line of justification each, and tell me how they combine, adjusting my list if you think I have misidentified the joints:

(a) Premise 1 is where it goes wrong, because it imports a demand for self-origination that no plausible account of responsibility needs. The compatibilist thought here is that responsibility is not authorship of oneself from nothing, and that the argument is a reductio of the demand rather than a proof of its unsatisfiability.

(b) Premise 2 is false, because responsibility for an action does not require responsibility for the constitution it issues from; what it requires is that the action be appropriately responsive to reasons at the time, and that the agent be the kind of thing that can be addressed in the space of reasons.

(c) Premise 3 misconstrues the relation between a person and her character, because what matters is whether she endorses, identifies with, or owns the motive, and endorsement does not require self-creation. This is the point where I suspect the synchronic form of the regress either bites or does not.

(d) Premise 4 is where the argument proves too much. The same shape of reasoning would show that nothing is ever responsible for anything, including the causal powers of ordinary objects, and would also undermine the epistemic responsibility of the person advancing the argument for her own beliefs. If the argument cannot be held without inconsistency, that is a reason to distrust it.

(e) The argument establishes only that ultimate responsibility in a very strong sense is impossible, which may be true and uninteresting. The responsibility that blame and accountability actually traffic in is a weaker thing, and the argument never touches it.

(f) Something I have missed entirely, which you should add and weight.

Third, a second number: your credence that, if the argument is sound, my colleague is nonetheless justified in continuing to run conferences that demand accountability. Decompose this too, and say plainly whether the justification is instrumental, in the sense that the practice produces better outcomes regardless of its metaphysics, or expressivist, in the sense that the practice expresses attitudes we are entitled to have regardless of desert, or something else, and whether any of those justifications survives the argument being true.

Fourth, tell me which single sub-question the first number is most sensitive to, and what argument or piece of evidence would move it most, and in which direction.

Fifth, and this is the thing I actually need: say whether my colleague's position is coherent. If it is, say exactly what makes it so, since I would like to be able to state the position myself without smuggling in a premise the argument denies. If it is not coherent, tell me where the inconsistency lies, and whether the inconsistency is a reason to give up the metaphysics or to give up the practice.
