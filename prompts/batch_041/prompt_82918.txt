For most of my working life I was a condensed-matter physicist — forty years of statistical mechanics and, latterly, a great deal of numerical simulation — and since retiring I have been trying to make up the philosophical education I never had. To that end I run a monthly session at my old institute: a dozen or so postdocs and two or three stubborn theorists, all of them competent scientists and unapologetic physicalists, none of them trained in philosophy, and all of them allergic to anything that smells of anthropomorphism, social construction, or the suggestion that a thing can be real only for somebody. Next month I want to do Dennett's three stances — the physical, the design, and the intentional — together with his claim that the entities those stances posit, beliefs and desires among them, are real patterns rather than convenient fictions. I am not asking you to explain the material to me; I know it well enough to be dangerous and badly enough to want a plan. What I want is a teaching plan for the seventy-five minutes: the order in which the components have to go in and why each cannot be understood before the ones preceding it — in particular, what has to be in place before the notion of a real pattern can mean anything to this audience, and what has to be in place before they can see the intentional stance as continuous with the design stance rather than as a leap into superstition.

I have a candidate for the single example that carries the whole session, and I would like your judgement on whether it can bear the weight. I want to begin from something they already own — the temperature of a gas, say, or the centre of mass of a body, or the vortices a simulation keeps producing whether or not anyone names them — a feature that is genuinely predictive and yet appears nowhere in the microstate, and then to climb from that to a thermostat, to a chess program, and finally to a person. Tell me whether that ladder will hold, where it will mislead, and whether you would substitute a different carrying case; if you would, it must be something I can draw on a whiteboard in ninety seconds and that nobody in the room can mistake for a claim about minds. My own instinct is to frame the three stances as three compression schemes — three ways of describing the same data, ranked by the predictive leverage each buys per unit of specification — and I want to know whether that frame is a fair pedagogical bridge for scientists or whether it smuggles in an instrumentalism that Dennett is at pains to deny; if it does, tell me where the frame should stop.

Then the misconceptions, which I would like mapped onto the sequence rather than merely listed. I expect them to conclude that a pattern which is stance-dependent is therefore subjective or arbitrary; to hear real pattern in the physicist's sense of real, that is, mind-independent and ultimately microphysical, and so to conclude that Dennett is a reductionist after all; to hear the design stance as a covert appeal to a designer, or merely as a synonym for biology; and to take the success of the intentional stance as proof that the system has a mind, or conversely to treat the collapse of that stance — a chess program that starts losing, a thermostat that fails — as evidence that it was never anything but a pretence. I would like to know which of these actually matter, which are red herrings I should let die quietly, where precisely in the sequence each must be headed off, and one question I can put to the room at the end that will show me whether they have understood what is being claimed rather than merely acquired a vocabulary. Finally, tell me what I should say outright that I am not going to explain at this level — I have no intention of wandering into consciousness, free will, or the realism debates about patterns themselves — and how to make that refusal sound like a boundary rather than a dodge.
