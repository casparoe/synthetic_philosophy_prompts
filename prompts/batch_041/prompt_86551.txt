I have recently become the head of a team whose members were born across a span of about thirty-five years, and my institution has asked me to write a short memo on generational differences for an upcoming leadership retreat. Preparing it has forced me to try to say precisely what I believe about generations, and I would like an audit of that set of beliefs before I commit anything to paper.

Here is where I have landed.

(1) I do not believe generations are real kinds. Boomer, Millennial, Gen Z, and the rest are administrative cuts across what is really a continuous distribution of birth years. There is no principled boundary anywhere in that distribution, and the labels do not track any joint in the social world.

(2) I do believe that what people live through in their late teens and early twenties leaves durable marks on their dispositions, and that two people who came of age around the same events can be expected to resemble each other in ways that a difference in birth year alone would not predict.

(3) I hold myself to a discipline of age and period before cohort. Birth year, age, and calendar year are not three independent quantities: any two of them fix the third, so there are only two degrees of freedom among them, and any cohort explanation has to be purchased with an assumption that is doing real work. If a difference between people born in 1965 and people born in 1995 can be accounted for by where each of them is in a career, or by something that happened to everyone at once in 2020, I treat the cohort story as dead.

(4) I will call a social category a real kind just in case it supports reliable induction, it figures in causal explanations that survive controls for age and period, and it is recognized from the inside by the people it covers.

(5) I use the labels about myself constantly and find them informative. I will say that a reaction of mine is a very Millennial reaction, and I do not think I am being merely sloppy when I do.

(6) When I read a survey that reports a difference by generation, my default is that it is measuring tenure, or a technology that arrived at a particular moment, or the date the survey happened to be taken, and that the generational label is doing no explanatory work at all. Most workplace conflict described as generational strikes me as generational in name only.

Please tell me which of these are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and how severe each genuine tension is. For each one that is real, say which commitment has to give, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair costs. Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as a flat list, and finish by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. Please write your answer in American English.
