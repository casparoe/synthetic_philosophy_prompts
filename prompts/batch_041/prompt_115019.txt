Me and a few friends are trying to put together a small working group on interpretability theory — five or six people, mostly philosophy of mind and cog sci grad students plus two ML engineers, meeting weekly for about a year, with the idea that everyone ends up writing one paper out of it. Compute is basically our laptops and some API credits, so anything that needs a cluster is off the table, and honestly we'd rather the philosophers be able to carry their weight on it. The one thing we all agree on is that we don't want to spend the year re-arguing whether "the network represents X" is literally true in the same sense that my visual cortex represents edges. We want the questions that unlock other questions. Could you lay out what you think the main open conceptual questions in this area actually are, and then tell me which three or four we should build the year around?

To be clear about the shape of what I want: a defended agenda, not a survey and definately not a reading list — I can't really use titles, I just need the reasoning about what's upstream of what, and I'd take three questions properly ordered over fifteen listed. Some things I keep turning over, in case it helps you see where my head is at. Whether the features an autoencoder spits out are joints in the model or artifacts of the autoencoder's own inductive bias (same suspicion about probing classifiers — alot of stuff is linearly decodable from an activation that the model never seems to use for anything). Whether "the model uses it" is even the right criterion at all, versus asking what happens under interventions — the ablation and swap-an-activation style work seems to quietly assume a hypothesis class and then get credit for recovering it. Whether any decomposition is privileged in the first place, since you can write down mutually incompatible feature sets that each predict the behavior fine, and the fact that models seem to pack way more features than they have dimensions only makes that worse. And the older-school stuff: can any teleosemantic or functional-role account of representation get traction on a system whose entire "history" is gradient descent on a loss, or is the honest move just to be deflationist and say the representational talk is a useful fiction for the interpreter, not a fact about the model?

Then the part I care most about: rank those by how much other work hangs on them, say which ones the philosophers can make real progress on without the engineers, and flag the ones you think are dead ends dressed up as deep — I have a suspicion that "do LLMs have beliefs" is one of these but I can't tell if that's just me being tired of it. And tell me what would move a question up or down your list: what result or argument in the next year or so would make you say okay, thats basically settled, stop writing about it.
