For the past several terms I have taught an undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of art, and every year the same thing happens: my students arrive able to recite the received summary of Tolstoy's late treatise on art — that he defines art as the transmission of feeling, that he sets sincerity above beauty, that he dismisses the art of the leisured classes as artificial — and from that point on they treat every further question as already settled. I want an exercise that forces them to do the sorting for themselves, and before I distribute it I need to know whether my own answer key is even coherent. So I have drafted eight candidate criteria, each of which I intend to print on a card, and I would like you to tell me how a careful reader of the treatise would rank them.

The cards are of mixed kinds, and I may well have mixed them up. Some state, in my own words, a condition that Tolstoy treats as essential to a thing's being art at all; some state a criterion he would apply only when judging whether art is good or bad rather than whether it is art; and some are misreadings that a student could reach by over-extending a genuine Tolstoyan theme. I would like you to sort all eight into three tiers — the first containing criteria without which, on his account, a thing simply is not art; the second containing criteria that bear on whether art is good or bad but not on whether it is art; and the third containing criteria he would repudiate — and then, within the first tier, to give me a strict ordering, since my students will inevitably ask which of the essential conditions matters most. For each card, add a sentence or two identifying the feature of his account that does the work of placing it where you place it, and at the end single out the one card most likely to be mistaken for his own position by a reader who knows only the secondary summaries. I do not need chapter and verse; I need the reasoning.

The cards are these. (1) A thing is art only if the maker's feeling passes to the audience, so that the audience undergoes what the maker underwent, rather than merely learning what the maker thought or being edified by the maker's message. (2) A thing is art only if the maker actually feels what the work expresses, and this sincerity is what finally separates art from counterfeit, even where the feeling transmitted is base or trivial. (3) A thing is art only if it is beautiful, beauty being the end toward which the artistic impulse moves and the standard by which works are judged. (4) A thing is art only if it can be received by ordinary people without specialized training, so that a work demanding an initiated or educated audience is to that extent defective. (5) A thing is art only if the feeling it carries is the maker's own and peculiar to him, not drawn from the common stock of conventional or fashionable sentiment. (6) A thing is art only if it leaves the audience better than it found them, more inclined to virtue than they were before. (7) A thing is art only if it is well made, so that command of craft is a necessary condition of a work's counting as art. (8) A thing is art only if it answers to the religious understanding of life of its own time, the highest art being that which transmits the feeling of the brotherhood of all people.
