Until recently I would have said that the timing of conscious experience was a technical dispute for philosophers of mind. Then I took part in a color phi demonstration: two dots flashed in quick succession in different colors, and I saw a single dot change color in the middle of its apparent motion, an experience that seems to require the first color to have been revised by information from the second flash. I have been unable to shake the question of which revision story applies to me. On the Orwellian account, I did experience the first color, and the record was later edited to match the second. On the Stalinesque account, the editing happened before anything reached experience, so the first color was never consciously presented at all. The usual point of the distinction is to undermine the picture of a single finishing line in the brain where experiences become conscious, but I now find that I want a customer-service representative to tell me which one I am on.

Please write a satirical FAQ for a fictional company, Temporal Drafts Ltd., that sells home neuro-editing services with two tiers, Orwellian and Stalinesque. The piece should be deadpan, roughly 700 words, and formatted as numbered questions and answers with a short warranty addendum. The jokes should come from getting the distinction and the color phi phenomenon right: the Orwellian tier edits the record after the fact; the Stalinesque tier edits before the experience is ever staged; the company must handle the fact that customers cannot tell from the inside which tier they received; and the flagship demo is the color phi display. The satire should target the assumption that there is a single, datable stream of experience—the Cartesian theater—and the consumer-therapeutic industry that would spring up around editing it. Do not state the target outright, and do not wink. Include at least one exchange in which a customer asks whether they ever experienced the original color, and the answer must be internally consistent with the tier they purchased. The FAQ should be readable by someone who does not know the literature, while a specialist should recognize that the author understands what is being satirized. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Begin the response with the FAQ itself, with no preface or commentary.
