I am putting together a single fifty-minute session for a lunchtime seminar series that colleagues from across the university attend — two historians, an engineer, a couple of clinicians, a philosopher or two, nobody with any formal training in ethics. The subject is supererogation, and what I want from you is a plan for how to teach it rather than an exposition of it; I can do the reading myself. Assume an audience that is intelligent, sceptical and entirely new to the topic, and assume I have a whiteboard and no handouts.

First, the order. Say what has to be in place before anything else can be said, and what depends on what. My own instinct is to open by pressing the thought that appears to make the concept impossible — that if an act is genuinely good, and I am able to do it, and there is nothing wrong with it, then surely I am required to do it — and only then to introduce the ordinary distinction and show how the pressure returns. Tell me whether that is the right first move or whether it belongs near the end, and why.

Second, the case. I had intended to carry the whole session on a case of my own construction: a man who dives into a canal to pull out a drowning stranger, chiefly because a colleague whose good opinion he is anxious to keep is watching from the towpath. Tell me whether that case can bear the weight — whether it teaches the concept or quietly begs the question against it — and then specify what the carrying case must have and must lack. If it cannot carry the session, say what should, and say what disqualifies a candidate: too saintly, too rare, too obviously expensive, or one whose omission already strikes the audience as a wrong.

Third, the misconceptions. I expect at least these: that "beyond the call of duty" is a matter of degree or expense — going the extra mile, doing more than one's share; that the supererogatory is simply the heroic and the dramatic; that if an act is good but its omission is not wrong, the act cannot really have been good, only pleasant; and the converse collapse, that the concept amounts to saying morality is optional. There will be others I have not foreseen. Give me the list in the order the audience will actually produce them, mark the point in the sequence at which each must be headed off, and say which one, left alone until the end, would poison the whole session.

Fourth, the check. One question, or one small case put to the room in the final five minutes, that will show whether they have grasped the structure of the idea rather than merely acquired a taste for heroes. Say what a wrong answer would look like and what it would tell me.

Fifth, what to refuse. Tell me what I should decline to settle in fifty minutes — the demandingness of morality, whether there is a duty to be heroic, the question of whether the agent's motive matters to the classification — and how to decline it out loud without leaving the impression that I am concealing something. I am also tempted to mention suberogation at the very end; say whether that will consolidate the idea or damage it.

Two further decisions bear on the opening move, so I want your judgement on both. I am drawn to the etymology — the Latin root is a word for paying out over and above what is owed, the vocabulary of accounts rather than of heroism — and I suspect that opening there would plant the wrong picture from the first minute. And I am tempted to use the parable of the Good Samaritan as the framing illustration, but I am uneasy that the parable is standardly read as teaching that mercy is required rather than optional, which would make it the worst possible illustration for a concept that turns entirely on optionality.

No reading list, please; I have one.

Finally, tell me where this plan is most likely to be wrong: which step is the weakest link in the sequence, what the sharpest person in the room could say to derail it, and which consideration would lead you to replace the plan with a different one. End with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing one sentence that states the spine of the plan.
