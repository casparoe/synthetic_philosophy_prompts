I write for the science-and-ideas pages and we're starting a short fiction slot, so I need a story from you. Treat yourself as a literary short-story writer first and a philosphy-of-mind nerd second: the prose has to stand on its own, but the idea underneath has to be load-bearing, not wallpaper.

Before you draft, give me three or four bullet points of brainstorming — just which angle gives you the most dramatic friction, and why — then write the thing. Take your time with it.

Use my premise (it's mine, so you won't have seen it anywhere): a small startup sells "Continuity," a subscription service for people in the late stages of locked-in syndrome. The patient's residual neural signals get run through a model trained on millions of other users' data, and out comes fluent first-person prose in the patient's own voice — messages, diary entries, complaints about the food. Families pay for it and read it every day. The company's engineers know exactly what the pipeline can and can't do: where the signal is too degraded to read, it fills in the most probable content given the patient's history and everybody else's data. Nobody's lying, exactly. The messages are usually right. And because each month's output gets folded back into the training set, the voice slowly drifts toward what the model expects a person like her to say.

Write the story from inside that gap. Somewhere in it I want a technician or a family member (your pick) having to decide whether what they're reading is a mind or a really good completion — and a second character who thinks that question is confused in the first place. Note there are two versions of the puzzle here: the familiar one about not knowing whether other people have minds at all, and the nastier one about whether you could even form the idea of a mind that isn't built out of your own materials. I want the story sitting on the second one, at least partly, without anybody saying so.

Constraints: no character explains the philosophy out loud for a paragraph, no lectures, no real philosophers named, no citing anything. The ideas should come through in what people do and how they argue. And definately don't resolve it — end on the decision or the doubt, not on an answer.

1,200-1,800 words, and give it a title.
