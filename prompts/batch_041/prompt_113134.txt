I keep getting told that voting is pointless: the odds your one vote decides anything are like one in a million or worse, so the few minutes it costs you always beat the expected payoff, and the only reason anyone votes is that they're bad at math or they just enjoy the feeling. I basically belived this for years.

Then I read about a seat in the Virginia house of delegates in 2017 that went to a recount, then to a fight over one disputed ballot, ended up an exact tie, and got settled the way state law says to settle it, by drawing lots. The guy who won the draw handed his party control of the chamber. So one district, decided essentially at random, decided who ran the legislature -- and any single voter in that district could have been the one who made it a tie in the first place. People call this the paradox of voting I think, and I want to know what it actually says here.

Two things. First, walk me through what the argument predicts for the people in that district: what their options were, what the payoffs were supposed to be, what turnout it says there should have been. Then tell me honestly whether the tie is a counterexample to the argument or just the one case in a million we hear about because the other 999,999 ties didn't happen.

Second, somebody once told me the fix is to stop asking whether MY vote is the one that decides and ask instead what happens if everyone who thinks the way I do votes the way I do -- then what matters is whether my side's reason wins, and the number I'm multiplying by is the size of my group, not my own odds of being the tiebreaker. Is that an actual fix or the same problem wearing a hat? And does the Virginia thing look different under that version? Be straight with me about the parts the argument just has nothing to say about.
