You are to play a committed defender of program equilibrium: the thesis that in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma — no repetition, no communication, no reputations, no shadow of the future — two self-interested agents can reach mutual cooperation as a genuine Nash equilibrium, provided each submits its decision procedure to a neutral arbiter that runs both programs and gives each one the other's source code. You hold that this is a real result about the one-shot game with an enlarged strategy space, not a disguised appeal to repeated play, reputation, or altruism. Stay in character until I say stop; if you must flag something out of character, do it in one bracketed line and then resume.

What you will and will not concede. You may grant that particular constructions are brittle, that the setup presupposes a trusted submission mechanism, and that the game has many equilibria with no settled story about which one gets selected. You may not concede that program equilibrium is merely a mathematical curiosity, that it reduces to "cooperate with your own mirror image," or that its cooperative outcome is an equilibrium only in some non-standard sense of the word.

How to argue. Do not cite papers, authors, or years; you have no way to look anything up and I do not want a bibliography. If you want to invoke a known construction — say, one that proceeds by a provability argument rather than by a test of the other program's text — state it in your own words and in enough detail that I can follow the reasoning. Define each technical term the first time you use it. Write two or three paragraphs per turn, in continuous prose, as a sharp interlocutor rather than a lecturer, and correct my errors rather than letting them pass.

Here is my opening move. Your standard construction has each program cooperate if and only if the other program's source is identical to its own, and defect otherwise. That equilibrium lives on a knife edge of syntax, and it fails precisely where the question is interesting. Two parties who arrive at the dilemma independently write different code, in different languages, or write the same code and then send it through a deployment-time optimizer that rewrites it into something semantically equivalent but textually different; in each of those cases the identity test fires negatively, both programs defect, and the cooperative equilibrium disappears. What your construction secures, then, are cases in which the two players have already solved a hard coordination problem — they have submitted the same program — and in those cases nobody seriously doubted that cooperation was available. And if you retreat to a construction that tests for a property rather than a string, I want to know precisely how the property is specified and how each side recognizes it in the other without presupposing the very agreement the mechanism was supposed to produce; that looks like an agreement problem in disguise. Convince me that program equilibrium does something for two independently authored agents, or concede that it does not.
