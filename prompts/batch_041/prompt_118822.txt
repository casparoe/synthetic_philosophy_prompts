I got into a long argument in the pub last night with a friend who works on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and I still can't work out who's being stupid. He wrote his argument out on a napkin, and here it is more or less as he had it. (1) Any quantum system has some complete physical state — call it the ontic state — that fixes everything about it, including the statistics of any measurement you could make on it. (2) If the wavefunction were merely a state of knowledge, as the ψ-epistemic people say, then two different pure states ψ and φ would sometimes be compatible with one and the same ontic state: one and the same physical situation could be truthfully described by either of them. (3) The PBR theorem kills that. Roughly: the 2^n product states you get by putting each of n independently prepared systems into either ψ or φ are linearly independent, so there's a measurement whose 2^n outcomes are indexed by those combinations, and the outcome indexed by combination s has probability zero whenever you actually prepared s. But if ψ and φ can share an ontic state, then the joint ontic state is compatible with every one of the 2^n combinations at once, so it has to give probability zero to all 2^n outcomes simultaneously — and they have to sum to one. Hence distinct pure states have disjoint ontic supports. (He says the one assumption people keep trying to dodge is preparation independence, and that its safe.) (4) So the wavefunction is ontic: it belongs to the world's physical furniture, not to our books. (5) And ontic means real — a physical thing the theory is about — so collapse, branching and the rest are questions about a physical process, and the whole debate about whether the wavefunction is real is more or less over.

My objection was this. It can't be a general argument that statistical states are real, because classical statistical mechanics just is the ψ-epistemic picture, and nobody thinks the Gibbs distribution is a physical field. A gas has a definite microstate; the distribution over microstates encodes our ignorance; two different distributions overlap in microstate space; and once you fix the microstate, the outcome statistics of any measurement are fixed. Every ingredient of his setup seems to have a classical analogue, including the shared underlying state. If his reasoning were any good, it would prove that the Liouville density is a real physical thing, which is absurd. So either the argument proves too much and something in it is broken, or there's some feature of the quantum case that has no classical analogue, and I want to know exactly which it is.

So: which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather not give up (1) or (2), since they look like the minimum you need in order to state the question at all, and if the answer is that the analogy breaks down, I want the precise point of breakage, not just that quantum is different. I have a suspicion about where the weakness is, but I can't make it precise, so tell me which step it is and price the alternatives for me.
