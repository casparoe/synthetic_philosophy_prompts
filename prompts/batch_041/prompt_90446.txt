I've been turning over the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism — the charge that the theory treats society as though it were one person writ large, so that a sacrifice borne by one individual registers only as a cost somewhere in a larger total — and the more I work at it the less I can tell whether it is the knockdown I was first taught it was or a slogan with an argument somewhere behind it. So please write a dialogue in which two philosophers work through it: one who defends a broadly aggregative, utilitarian way of thinking, and one who presses the objection. I want them evenly matched. The critic should not be a mouthpiece who intones respect for persons while the utilitarian absorbs everything, and the utilitarian should be someone who has heard the objection before and has real replies rather than a strawman who only says that the numbers count. They should take up each other's actual formulations, concede where a point lands, and change tack only when there is a reason to.

Two threads interest me most, and I would like the dialogue to reach both. The first is whether the objection is at bottom metaphysical or moral: does it depend on the thought that the boundaries between persons mark something deeper than the boundaries between the successive stages of a single life, and if so, what becomes of it on a deflationary view of personal identity — does the objection lose its footing there, or does it turn out to have rested on something else the whole time? The second is whether the objection targets aggregation as such or only the imposition of costs on some people for the benefit of others without their consent; if the latter, I want to see whether the utilitarian can grant it and still defend anything recognisably utilitarian, and what is left of the first thread once the second has been separated off.

They can be anywhere that lets them talk — a train, a kitchen at two in the morning, a seminar room after everyone else has gone — and the dialogue need not end in agreement or in a verdict. Let them argue rather than cite: no names, and no labels used as though they settled anything. And if you think I have set this up wrong — if the metaphysical and the moral readings cannot really be pulled apart, or if the objection I am describing is not the one that actually does the work against utilitarianism — then tell me so instead of writing the dialogue I asked for. I would rather be told that my framing is confused than be handed a fluent version of it.
