My friend, who has been reading Hempel, insists that every genuine historical explanation must fit the covering-law model: you explain an event by showing that it follows deductively from general laws plus initial conditions, and if the four accounts in Kurosawa's film cannot be reconciled, that only proves the witnesses are unreliable, not that history is a different kind of inquiry. I think the film is a much sharper test case for the covering-law versus narrative-explanation debate than he will admit, and I want you to adjudicate the dispute by reading *Rashomon* through that debate. Treat the covering-law model as the view that explanation is subsumption under law, with the same logical form as prediction, and treat narrative explanation as the view, associated with philosophers such as Danto, Gallie, and Mink, that history explains by configuring events into a temporal whole, selecting what matters, and revealing how one thing led to another in a way that cannot be captured by a law plus initial conditions. The question is not whether the film is about truth and memory in some loose sense; it is whether the film dramatizes a real limitation of the covering-law model, or whether it merely stages a failure of testimony that a covering-law account could absorb without difficulty.
