I run the content side of a small state historical society — we write the plaque text and the exhibit labels, maybe a dozen sites a year plus one or two bigger overhauls. The thing that keeps eating our review meetings is always the same fight: a community comes to us with a memory of what happened at a site and it doesn't match the archival record. Sometimes the record is just silent, sometimes it says the opposite. The committee is three of us (me, a staff historian who's the only one with any real training in this, and a community rep), and the community rep can block anything that goes to a vote, which means we can't just outvote her. We've been deciding these ad hoc, case by case, which really means we decide them by who's in the room that day. I want a written procedure instead, one teh team can run before a meeting without me.

Two things about how I think we get it wrong. First, we have a habit of treating it as a straight conflict between two answers to one question, and I don't think it always is — a community remembering what a place *meant* and an archive recording what *happened* can both be right, and we keep forcing them into a contradiction. Second, when the archive is silent we've been reading that as "no evidence, so the community claim is unproven," and I'm not sure that's right anymore, because the archive is also a record of who bothered to write things down, and who got to.

Here's a half-baked framework I've been kicking around — tell me which parts of it survive and which are junk. I've been calling a disputed claim *load-bearing* if the community's sense of itself depends on it, and *decorative* if it doesn't; my instinct is we owe more deference to load-bearing claims, but I honestly can't tell if that's principled or just cowardice. I also have a *same-question test* (before we call something a conflict, check whether the memory and the record are even answering the same question), and something I've been calling the *deference ledger*, which is supposed to track who has standing to know this particular thing — the people who were there, the people whose lives it shapes now, the people who kept the records in the first place. No idea if the ledger is coherent or just a way of sounding fair.

So: turn this into a procedure our committee can actually run. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is about values and not facts. Keep it short enough to run in one review meeting, and flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases:

(a) A harbor town remembers that the breakwater was cut short by the company that owned the cannery, and that this is why the 1931 storm drowned the men it drowned. The only contemporaneous documents are the company's own records, which describe a bad storm and never mention the breakwater. Four generations of oral accounts name the breakwater.

(b) A neighborhood wants a plaque crediting a local craftsman — unnamed in any patent or newspaper — as the real source of a design that a now-famous inventor patented. The record shows a sole patent. The craftsman's descendants have a family account and nothing else.

(c) A community insists their site was the first in the state to do a particular thing, and it's central to how they see themselves and to a grant they're applying for. There's a well-documented earlier site twenty miles away. I expect the procedure to tell me to put the record on the plaque, which will go badly for us, and I want to know that in advance.

Then tell me what this procedure cannot do, and be honest about where it will misfire. And separate out for me what's actually settled among people who work on memory and history from what's genuinely contested — don't hand me a contested view as if it's the consensus, because I'll be reading this as if it's authoritative and I'd rather know where the floor is.

Finish with a JSON object, keys exactly: "procedure_steps" (array of objects, each with "step", "question", "sufficient_answer", "stop_or_handoff"), "cases" (array of objects, each with "case", "verdict", "confidence", "why"), "settled" (array of strings), "contested" (array of strings), "judgment_calls" (array of strings), "what_it_cannot_do" (array of strings).
