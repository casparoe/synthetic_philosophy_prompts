I am trying to work out what principle, if any, governs my verdicts about when two experiences are of the same kind, and I have been going at it case by case through the examples that drive the argument from illusion. Looking at a red wall in daylight, I say that I am seeing the wall and that its redness is there in my experience. Looking at a white wall under a red lamp, which looks to me exactly as the first wall does, I still say that I am seeing the wall — but I am misperceiving its colour, and I want to call this experience the same kind as the first. Looking at a Kanizsa figure, where I seem to see a bright triangle that is not there, I hesitate: nothing is presented, yet it does not feel like the hallucination case either. Pressing my eyeball and getting a phosphene, I want to say that I am seeing nothing at all, that no object is presented. And when I hallucinate a red wall, in a state I could not distinguish from the first case, I want to say that I am not seeing a wall: unlike the white-wall case, there is no object there that I have got hold of.

The principle I keep reaching for — that experiences are of the same kind when they present the same thing to me — seems to get both pairs wrong: it lumps the veridical and the hallucinated wall together, and it pulls the two wall-perceptions apart. Is there a principle that captures all of my verdicts, or must I give one up? State it as precisely as you can, tell me what it predicts for a case I have not mentioned, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon and why.
