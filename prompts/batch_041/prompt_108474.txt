You are a philosopher who works on collective action problems and the governance of risky technology, and who has spent years turning normative principles into rules that institutions can actually operate. I want you to do that translation for me, thoroughly and concretely, and to be honest about where it fails.

My situation. I am a program director at a foundation that makes roughly fifty grants a year in fields with dual-use potential — biosecurity, AI capability research, climate intervention. Each decision arrives with a two-week window, I have about an hour per proposal to think it through properly, and whatever I decide I have to defend in writing to a board that is not philosophically trained. I have no research staff.

The problem I need a procedure for. In my fields, a very large number of labs, funders, and individual researchers can each decide, independently and without consulting anyone, to do or to fund a particular thing. Some of those things are worth doing once and dangerous when done many times over. The pattern is usually called the unilateralist's curse: if each actor is reasonably good at judging whether an action is harmful, but not perfect, then the probability that at least one of them does it anyway climbs toward certainty as the number of actors grows, so a field full of careful people can produce an outcome none of them wanted. The remedy usually proposed is a norm of restraint, sometimes called a principle of conformity: raise the bar for acting well above what it would be if you were the only actor, and give real weight to the considered judgment of others who are in a position to know. I already understand this much. What I do not have is a procedure.

What I want from you: a decision procedure that one program officer can run on one proposal in about an hour, laid out as the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a fund, a decline, or an escalation to a named person or committee, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Say explicitly what I should do at any step where I cannot get the information the step asks for, because I will often be guessing.

The procedure has to hold up on the following structural features, which is where I expect the interesting work to be.

1. Actions whose harm is threshold-shaped — harmless or even beneficial at low uptake, seriously harmful past some number of adopters — as against actions that are harmful whenever anyone at all does them. Say whether the curse even applies to the first kind, and what changes for me if it does not.
2. Asymmetric competence. Sometimes one actor really is far better placed than the rest. Handle both the case where I am assessing someone else as that actor, and the case where the argument for proceeding is that the competent actor is us. The second case is, I take it, exactly the inference the whole idea is designed to catch, so do not let the procedure rubber-stamp it.
3. Reversibility and the cost of waiting. An action we can cheaply undo, or a decision we can revisit in six months, should not be treated like an irreversible one, and restraint is not free.
4. Whether the actors can coordinate. Distinguish at least three settings: a field with a shared venue, reputations, and slow-moving norms; a field with no venue and no way to verify what others are doing; and a field containing automated systems that can act at machine speed, where no norm could form in time to matter.
5. The lone actor who is right. Conformity counsels inaction, and sometimes inaction is the moral failure. Say what the procedure does when the majority judgment it defers to is itself the thing that is wrong.

Then run the procedure end to end on three proposals, showing each step's output rather than only the verdict.

(a) A grant to develop and publish an open, low-cost method whose foreseeable effect is to lower the barrier to a dangerous capability, proposed by a team whose stated aim is benign and whose work would also be genuinely useful.
(b) A grant to a team that is, by common agreement, the best in the world at this, whose central argument is that if we decline, someone less careful will do it, and that it is therefore better done here.
(c) A case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like. Construct it yourself, make it the kind of case a sympathetic program officer would find genuinely uncomfortable, and say plainly why the procedure comes out that way and what would have to be different for it to come out otherwise.

Three further things, all of which matter to me more than elegance.

First, separate the curse from two things it is confused with: the bystander or diffusion-of-responsibility effect, which predicts too little action rather than too much, and commons problems, where the damage comes from depleting or congesting something shared rather than from an act that is bad in itself. For each, say what my procedure does differently, or why it should not be run at all in that case.

Second, close with a short section on what this procedure cannot do, where I should override it, and what I would be pretending if I let it decide for me.

Third, give me a compressed version, under two hundred words, that I can paste into a memo and that still shows a reader where the real uncertainty sits.

Begin by thinking on paper for a few paragraphs about where the hard part of this translation lies: which distinctions from the underlying idea have to survive contact with an hour-long decision process and which can be dropped, and what a procedure of this kind will predictably get wrong. Then write the procedure. Do not pad it with hedges, do not tell me that everything is context-dependent, and do not write me an essay about the ethics of dual-use research — I want the procedure and the worked cases.
