I am a high school student preparing an extended essay for a seminar on the history of ideas. I would like you to help me think through a counterfactual: suppose Alan Turing could be brought forward to the present and asked to comment on machine learning and education, fields that have changed enormously since his death. I do not want a general summary of his famous views. I want a disciplined prediction of what he would think about specific developments that only arose after his time, inferred from the commitments and arguments already present in his writings on machine intelligence and education. Please distinguish throughout between claims that follow fairly directly from what he wrote, extrapolations that require filling gaps in his view, and points where modern evidence would likely have forced him to revise his position.

Here are the developments I would like you to address.

First, today's most capable systems are trained in a largely self-supervised way on enormous corpora of text, images, and other data before they are fine-tuned for particular tasks. This is sometimes described as giving a machine a broad background before any deliberate education. Turing suggested that a practical route to machine intelligence might be to build something like a child's mind and then educate it. Would he regard large-scale pretraining as a vindication, a shortcut, or a distortion of that proposal? What would he say about the fact that the childhood of a modern model consists largely of passive prediction rather than interaction, play, or curiosity?

Second, modern systems are often shaped by reinforcement learning from human feedback, instruction tuning, and training against written principles or rubrics. These look like deliberate attempts to inculcate values, habits, and standards of answerability. How would Turing think about this as education? Would he see it as closer to teaching, training, or indoctrination? What would he want to know about the difference between a model that has learned to satisfy a teacher and one that has learned to think for itself?

Third, there is a widely discussed claim in artificial intelligence that general methods which scale with compute eventually outperform methods built on human-crafted knowledge. Some people treat this as a deep law of the field. Would Turing have accepted it? What would he have thought about its implications for education, where the analogous question is whether we should teach general reasoning or specific content, and whether the goal is to produce flexible learners or reliable performers?

Fourth, a specific puzzle I keep returning to is in-context learning: a model can be given a few examples of a novel task in its input and then perform the task, without any change to its weights. This looks like learning without education in Turing's sense, or perhaps like a compressed form of education happening in the forward pass. Would he have regarded this as a genuine form of learning, and what would it imply for his distinction between the child-like machine and its later education? Relatedly, models can be prompted to produce intermediate reasoning steps, and educators debate whether students should be required to show their work. Would Turing have seen a substantive parallel there?

Fifth, schools are now debating AI tutors, personalized learning systems, automated grading, and the possibility that students will use language models to do work that was supposed to demonstrate understanding. If Turing were advising a school board, what would he say about the purpose of education in an age of increasingly capable machines? Would he favor using machines as tutors, as subjects of study, or as competitors to be outperformed? What would he think about assessment when the same tool can both help a student learn and conceal whether learning has occurred?

Sixth, I am interested in a more conceptual issue. Turing's work often treats learning as a process of search, modification, and selection, but modern systems raise hard questions about whether a model is genuinely learning or merely retrieving patterns from its training data. How would Turing have drawn the line between learning and memorization, and what would he have said about whether education should aim at the former rather than the latter? Would he have expected the internal workings of an educated machine to be legible to its teachers, or would he have been comfortable with the thought that a machine might learn in ways we cannot easily inspect?

Please organize your answer as a set of predictions, each with a brief statement of what Turing would likely say, the basis for that prediction in his arguments or writings, and a confidence level. I am less interested in whether your predictions are flattering to Turing than in whether they are faithful to the logic of his position. Do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I need the reasoning itself, and I cannot check sources. Where the evidence underdetermines the answer, say so plainly and explain what further consideration would resolve it.
