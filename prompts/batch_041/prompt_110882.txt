I am editing a manuscript and trying to keep an explicit, ledger-style credence in one hypothesis: that a particular marginal note is in the hand of the author who wrote the rest of the manuscript. Very little of my evidence arrives as a clean observation. The paleographer I hired returns one of three verdicts — attributing the note, attributing it with hesitation, or declining to attribute it — and I do not know how reliable she is; I suspect in addition that her three verdicts are distributed differently depending on whether the note really is hers, since she is slow to make firm attributions and quick to withhold them. I also have my own graded impression from comparing the note with specimens I trust, and her judgment and mine are plainly not independent: we attend to the same features and tend to go wrong together. So far I have handled both sources by treating them as uncertain evidence in the spirit of Jeffrey's rule — rather than conditioning on a definite fact, I have re-weighted my conditional probabilities across the cells of a partition, taking for granted that the update leaves my conditional probabilities within each cell as they were.

Please describe the five most important implications of that framework for how I should actually conduct the bookkeeping, in a case shaped like mine. Where an implication is settled among people who work on this material, say so plainly; where it turns on something genuinely contested — whether the rule is the right norm here, whether evidence of this kind can be represented as a partition at all, whether there is a determinate fact about which cell I am in — mark it as contested and do not present it as consensus. I would rather be told where the framework bites in a case like mine than receive a general defence of it. Give exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion.
