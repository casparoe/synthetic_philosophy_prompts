Act as an archaeologist who is also a philosopher of the historical sciences, and write for a philosopher with a decent general knowledge of Rome and none of the technical literature. Think the problem through carefully before committing to anything, and open with a short working plan — the sub-questions you take the case to raise and the answers you expect to reach — so that I can see the shape of the argument before the argument itself.

The case is Pompeii and Herculaneum, the eruption of 79 CE, and the two and a half centuries of excavation that have made the site the standing example of a city captured at a single instant: the plaster casts of the dead, the loaves still in the bakery ovens, the wine still in the jars. I want that case read through the assumption that archaeological theory knows as the Pompeii premise — that a deposit records one short episode of behaviour, so that what we excavate is a moment rather than an accumulation. The premise takes its name from my case, and that irony is why I am asking.

Facts to work from, supplied so that the answer need not rest on recall. Much of the town was still under repair from the earthquake of 62 CE when it was buried, so that the moment is also a building site. The Bourbon excavators sank tunnels, removed what they wanted and backfilled, so that later excavators often found an assemblage someone else had already rearranged. The electoral notices painted on the street facades are repeatedly whitewashed and repainted, which makes them look less like a snapshot of one campaign than a palimpsest of decades of competition. A wall painting of the brawl in the amphitheatre is generally read as depicting the same affair, of 59 CE, that Tacitus narrates. And the season of the eruption is itself disputed, the manuscript tradition of Pliny the Younger's letters giving late August while autumn fruit, braziers, and a charcoal graffito found in 2018 and dated by its editors to 17 October point the other way.

What I want, in order:

1. A precise statement of the premise, with its strands separated — depositional, chronological, behavioural — since I suspect it is several assumptions travelling under one name.
2. Why the site earned the name: what the premise gets right about Pompeii, and what it licenses.
3. Then the case against it: the classes of evidence that make the frozen moment untenable, and whether any weakened version of the premise survives.
4. The reconstruction of beliefs and institutions specifically. What do we claim to know about how the town was governed, what its households worshipped, and who was owned by whom — and how much of that rests on reading the deposits as a moment? On the palimpsest reading, what becomes of the electoral notices as evidence?
5. The theory in the other direction: what does this case do to the vocabulary of formation processes, of systemic versus archaeological context, and of middle-range theory? Does Pompeii refute the premise, show it to be an inescapable idealisation, or show that it was never one assumption at all?
6. A verdict.

No citations, bibliography, or reading list; I want the reasoning, not the apparatus. Where you are reconstructing rather than knowing, say so, and say what evidence would settle the matter. And I want a committed answer: a definite verdict on whether the premise survives the Pompeii evidence, and on whether the case tells for the law-seeking, hypothesis-testing programme of the New Archaeology or for the interpretive, context-bound reaction against it — with your reasons given, and a candid note on where your confidence is thinnest. Do not set out the options and leave the choice to me.
