Write a dialogue between an endurantist and a perdurantist, both of them competent and neither a strawman, on a test case that the usual exchange over temporary intrinsics does not settle. The case is an institution — a city — that has been treated as one and the same city for two centuries although its charter, name, boundaries, population, and every building have been replaced, and although it was dissolved for two years in the middle and re-founded. The endurantist must say whether the city that exists now is numerically identical with the city of two hundred years ago and what makes that true, without resting the answer on legal or linguistic convention; the perdurantist must say what the two-year interruption interrupts, given that the object here is either two disconnected worms or a single worm with a hole, and must also say what unifies an arbitrary sum of temporal parts into one thing rather than a gerrymander, given that if composition is unrestricted and never vague then indefinitely many overlapping worms contain this city's parts. Each should press the other on the cost of their answer, concede the point the other has earned, and meet the strongest objection to their own view that they can foresee; each reply must take up the specific claim just made rather than reassert a position. Neither side needs to cite literature. After the dialogue, add a short note stating how confident you are that each position has been presented as its ablest defenders would present it, and identifying the single point in the dialogue of which you are least sure.
