Bit of an odd one. I've been chewing over Goodman's grue thing — all the emeralds we've seen are green, but they're also 'grue' (green up to some future date, blue after), and both generalisations fit the evidence perfectly, so the puzzle is what licences us to project 'green' and not 'grue'. Fine, I get the puzzle. What I actually want to ask about is what a real, working practice presupposes about it.

Take insurance pricing. A home policy in a flood zone is priced off a 'one-in-a-hundred-year' flood level estimated from the historical record, and planning rules steer you away from building below it. A life insurer selling an annuity that pays out for thirty years is projecting mortality improvement decades ahead off a table of past deaths. An employer's liability insurer prices partly off the class average and partly off that particular employer's own claims history, and gives the employer's own record more weight the more years of it there are.

So what does this presuppose about which patterns in the past are projectible? The underwriter never states a theory of projectibility, but theyre clearly assuming something about which predicates — 'this population's mortality', 'this employer's accident rate' — carve the world at the joints and which are grue-ish artefacts. Reconstruct that implicit view for me. Which rival accounts of projectibility would licence the practice, and which does it quietly rule out? Does it actually need to settle the question, or is it underdetermined — several incompatible theories all fitting how insurers behave?

Then the bit I'm really stuck on. The actuary's implicit answer looks purely statistical — fit plus sample size — and Goodman's whole point is that fit and sample size can't do the work, since green and grue fit the same data equally well. In practice, when the record is thin they lean on physical models instead (hydrology, epidemiology, whatever). Is that a substantive answer to the riddle smuggled in, i.e. that the projectible predicates are the causal/mechanistic ones? And why do insurers happily extrapolate from a handful of years in some lines and refuse to write other risks at all on the grounds that the past tells you nothing — is that inconsistency a tell about what they really presuppose?

Last thing: if you couldn't solve grue, what would have to change about how insurance is actually done — pricing, reserving, which risks get written?
