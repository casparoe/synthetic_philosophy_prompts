I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on divine providence, and I would like you to write a dialogue that I can use as a teaching text. The background is the Molinist account of providence, on which God possesses, alongside His knowledge of necessary truths and His knowledge of what He has freely decided to create, a third and intermediate kind of knowledge — middle knowledge — whose objects are the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: true propositions about what any possible free creature would freely choose in any set of circumstances in which it might be placed, whether or not those circumstances ever come about. Because God is supposed to know such counterfactuals prior to any decision of His own, He can arrange circumstances so that His ends are served while the creatures' choices remain free. The difficulty I want the dialogue to bring out is this. Suppose God discloses to a prophet a true counterfactual concerning the prophet's own freedom — that, were he offered a particular bribe, he would freely refuse it. When the bribe is later offered, the prophet deliberates with a consideration that ordinary deliberation would not contain: he knows what he was told he would freely do, and he can try to honor the prediction out of reverence or to falsify it out of pride. The critic's complaint is that the counterfactual has become self-undermining, since the very fact that it is known alters the circumstances it was meant to describe, and that if a free act can be deflected by the agent's advance knowledge of it, then middle knowledge is neither as stable nor as providentially useful as the Molinist requires. I am aware of several replies the Molinist might attempt — that the counterfactual was indexed to a complete circumstance that simply did not include the disclosure, so nothing has changed; that God's middle knowledge already contains counterfactuals about what creatures would do when told the truth about themselves, so the case is settled in advance; or that in the self-referential case there is no truth of the matter at all, and God does not need one — and I would like each of them to be pressed rather than merely asserted.

Please write the dialogue between two characters: a Molinist and a critic who denies that there are any true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom and therefore holds that middle knowledge has no object, but who is willing to grant them for the sake of argument and then show that even so they cannot bear the weight the Molinist places on them. Both should be philosophically competent and neither should be a strawman; the critic should not simply recite the familiar grounding objection but should make the disclosure case the centerpiece of the exchange, and the Molinist should answer it with the strongest resources of his position rather than with a shrug. I want genuine back-and-forth over several rounds, with each character responding to the other's actual points rather than talking past them, and with neither side allowed to win by fiat: where a move is available but contentious, the other character should say why it is contentious. The dialogue need not end in agreement, but it should end with the disagreement sharpened to its clearest form, so that a reader can see exactly where the two stand and what each still owes the other. Several exchanges, rather than a single pair of speeches, would be ideal.
