Next Thursday I'm presenting at our cognitive science seminar and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a pep talk. Here's my line, so you can aim the objections at something concrete. I argue that IIT is really two theories wearing one coat: on top, the phenomenological axioms — the list of what consciousness supposedly is — and underneath, the Φ machinery plus the exclusion postulate. My claim is that the axioms do basically none of the work. Every actual verdict about which system is conscious and which isn't comes out of Φ plus exclusion, and exclusion is not derivable from the axioms at all; it's bolted on to stop the theory from saying that every overlapping complex is conscious. To press this I introduce what I call the two-bridge case: two complexes that the theory would each rate as conscious, joined by a thin bidirectional channel. Run the theory and the verdict flips depending on how finely you redraw the boundary — call it boundary drift — and I claim IIT has no principled tiebreak here, just a preference for the grain that gives the "nicer" answer. So my conclusion is that the defensible half of IIT is the axiom side, and the axiom side doesn't need Φ.

The room: mostly computational neuroscientists who build predictive-processing models and are openly skeptical that Φ is anything but numerology with a nice diagram; one experimentalist who runs the perturbational complexity index on coma patients and is genuinely sympathetic because the measure has clinical bite; and two philosophers, one an illusionist who thinks the whole phenomenal-consciousness frame is a mistake, one a panpsychist who likes IIT precisely because it makes consciousness fundamental and spread out. That's the audience. Prep me for them, not for the IIT people online who'll just tell me I've misunderstood exclusion.

What I want: the objections I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll come and ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it — the modelers' version of an objection sounds nothing like the illusionist's version of the same objection, so give me both when they diverge. For each one, a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is soft, i.e. where I'm bluffing. Tell me what to concede up front because it's true and cheap, what to hold because it's load-bearing, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices.
