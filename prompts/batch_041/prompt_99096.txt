I am a third-year philosophy student currently taking a course on philosophy of mind and cognitive science, and I serve as the student representative on my university's Access and Accommodations Review Board. The Board must decide, at its meeting this Friday, a case that has divided us, and I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion before the vote.

The case concerns a student with a documented episodic-memory impairment who requests permission to use, during closed-book in-person exams, a wearable device running a custom retrieval system. The device is not connected to the internet and contains only the student's own lecture notes, annotations, and previously solved problem sets, indexed by the student's own associations. It responds to subvocal queries by surfacing relevant entries and, when asked, paraphrasing them in its own words. The student reports that in trial runs she sometimes cannot tell whether an answer came from the device or from her own memory, and that the device once surfaced a note she had forgotten writing. She says it adds no new information, returning only what she has already studied, but at a speed and reliability her unaided memory cannot match. Our written policy has two relevant categories: accommodation, available to any student with a documented disability and not recorded on the transcript; and unauthorized aid, which is referable for academic misconduct. The policy permits assistive technology that compensates for a disability and forbids aids that give an unfair advantage.

The Board's chair has proposed adopting what she calls the Functional Integration Standard: an external resource counts as part of a student's cognition, and so as an accommodation rather than an aid, when it is reliably coupled, automatically endorsed rather than consciously consulted, and recruited by the student's own reasoning. A rival group favors the Internal Benchmark Standard: accommodations may restore performance only up to a baseline measured without external storage, because anything beyond that is enhancement and cannot be separated from cheating. A third member has raised what he calls the offloading asymmetry: the device helps only because the student already did the cognitive work, but the exam is supposed to measure retrieval under unaided conditions, so the help is real even if no new content is added.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete decision—grant, deny, or grant with conditions—and specify a time frame and any conditions the Board can actually administer before the student's exams begin the following Monday. It should apply the Board's existing categories rather than inventing a new policy wholesale, though it may propose a narrow clarification if the existing categories cannot handle the case. It should engage the strongest argument for the side you reject, especially the worry that the Functional Integration Standard would license almost any convenient external memory and make closed-book exams meaningless. It should say what the Board would be committing itself to about the boundary between a student's mind and a student's tools, and what later case would best test whether that commitment is coherent. The case has affinities with the extended mind thesis, but I do not want a literature review; the memo should be self-contained and usable by members who have not read the philosophical background.
