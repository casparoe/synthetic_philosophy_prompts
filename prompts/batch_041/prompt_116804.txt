Write a short story of roughly two thousand words that turns on a mathematical puzzle and treats it as a matter of consequence rather than as a brainteaser.

The puzzle is this. A circle is given, with an equilateral triangle inscribed in it. A chord is to be drawn 'at random', and one asks for the probability that it will be longer than a side of the triangle. Three equally natural procedures give three different answers. Choose the chord's two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference: the probability is one third. Choose a radius uniformly at random, then a point uniformly at random along that radius, and draw the chord perpendicular to the radius at that point: the probability is one half. Choose a point uniformly at random in the interior of the circle and let it serve as the chord's midpoint: the probability is one quarter. The arithmetic is not in dispute. What is in dispute is which procedure, if any, deserves to be called the drawing of a random chord.

Your story must put this to work. Some person or institution must actually produce a 'random' chord, or a random line, or a random division of a bounded region by a single straight cut, because something irreversible is going to be decided by it: who receives a share, who is excluded, which of two indistinguishable claims prevails. The choice of procedure must matter in the world of the story, not merely inside a calculation. The setting is yours, but the procedure must be something the characters physically perform or commission, with equipment, witnesses, and a record.

A few constraints, which are the point of the exercise:

- Do not name the puzzle. No character may cite, allude to, or paraphrase any book, theorem, journal, or author. The characters must arrive at the difficulty out of their own situation and materials, and the story must be intelligible to a reader who has never heard of any of this.
- At least three characters must each regard one procedure as the obviously neutral one, and each must be given a serious case in their own idiom, drawn from their trade or office. None of them may be a straw man, and none may be a mouthpiece for the author.
- Include a character who presses the following objection, in whatever terms the setting affords: the symmetry of the situation does not by itself select a procedure, because one and the same random object can be described in terms of different quantities — endpoints, a midpoint, a distance from the centre, an angle — and indifference over one description is not indifference over another. This character should demonstrate concretely, with the story's own apparatus, that the answer moves when the description moves.
- Include a second character who attempts a repair: the procedure ought to be required to be invariant under the transformations that leave the situation unchanged — rotating the figure about its centre, or changing its scale — and this requirement should be pursued far enough that both its force and its limits become visible. It is worth having someone in the story ask whether a line in the plane can be chosen uniformly at all, or whether the supply of lines admits no such thing, so that every line must come from some finite physical process, and every such process brings a measure along with it.
- The mathematics must surface through the action — a ritual, a survey, a machine, a disputed instrument, a rehearsal, an experiment — never through a lecture. No character may explain the puzzle to another for the reader's benefit.
- Do not resolve the story by declaring one of the three answers correct. If there is a resolution, let it come from a character who argues that the demand for a unique answer is a confusion about what fairness or impartiality amounts to, or who alters the question so that the ambiguity no longer bites. The ending should leave the reader holding the difficulty rather than a verdict.

Write in restrained, concrete prose, attentive to the physical details of how the procedure is actually carried out and to what the people doing it stand to lose.
