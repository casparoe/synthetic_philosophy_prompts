I am a second-year doctoral student in animal welfare science, and in three weeks I have to run a single ninety-minute session for a cohort of about twenty MSc aquaculture students. They will arrive with decent fish biology, almost no philosophy, and a professional stake in the answers, since several of them are heading back into production roles. The concept I need them to leave with is the one my own thesis turns on, and I have found it unexpectedly hard to teach: that "the evidence that decapod crustaceans are capable of suffering is inconclusive" and "we have no positive reason to think they are capable of suffering" are not the same claim, and that deciding which way the inconclusiveness should cut is a values decision wearing empirical clothing. What I want from you is not an explanation of that idea but a plan for getting it across: the order in which the components have to be introduced and why each depends on the last, the single case that should carry the whole session and what that case must have and must lack in order to bear the weight, the misconceptions this particular audience will arrive with or develop on the way, and precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off.

A few constraints on the plan. I want the carrying case to be eyestalk ablation in penaeid shrimp broodstock, the routine removal of one or both eyestalks from breeding females in maturation facilities in order to accelerate spawning, rather than the octopus, which is the example they will all reach for and which concedes the argument too cheaply. Tell me why that choice is right or wrong, and what the ablation case must be made to display, and to stop displaying, if it is to do the work. Among the misconceptions I am expecting, though I would rather you generated the list than merely confirmed mine: that pain requires a cortex; that nociception and pain are the same thing; that an animal which grows and spawns normally is thereby faring well; that "precautionary" must mean either a total prohibition or nothing at all; and that the whole disagreement is an empirical one that better data would settle. I also need to know what I should openly decline to cover at this level, and to say so at the time, and one question I can put to the room in the last ten minutes that would actually tell me whether the distinction has landed rather than whether they have learnt to nod at it. Assume I know the literature; I do not need a reading list or citations.

Please write in British English, and tell me how confident you are in the pedagogical claims you are making, singling out the one point about which you are least sure.
