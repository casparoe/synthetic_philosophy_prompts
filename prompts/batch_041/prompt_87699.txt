I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group and we got onto pain asymbolia, which I'd only vaguely heard of. The way it was described: patients can still detect and localize a painful stimulus, they can say where it is and roughly how intense it is, and they'll even report that it's pain -- but it doesn't bother them. No distress, no urge to pull away, sometimes they laugh or chat through it. Our discussion leader called this a dissociation between the sensory-discriminative side of pain and the affective-motivational side, which sounds right, but then someone else cut it off with 'pain hurts' -- as in, if it doesn't hurt, it isn't pain, so asymbolia is just a fancy name for something else. I think that's too quick, but I also can't tell if 'pain hurts' is a deep truth or just a tautology doing no work.

Can you lay out the different precise claims that one-liner could be making? I mean things like: is it a claim about the meaning of the word 'pain', or about the necessary structure of any pain experience, or about what pain does to you motivationally, or about why pain is bad, or about how we should treat reports of pain? For each reading, say whether it's trivial, substantive but defensible, or just false, and importantly whether that reading actually gets my friend his conclusion that asymbolic patients aren't in pain. I'm less interested in a verdict on the clinical case than in a menu of readings I can bring back, so please keep them distinct and say which one people usually mean when they use 'pain hurts' to shut down a question like this. Also if some readings make the slogan true but irrelevant to his argument, flag that.
