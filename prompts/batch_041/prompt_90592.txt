I'm a novelist — two books out, third one stalled somewhere around page 90 — and I've somehow talked my way into a one-year fellowship at an institute that's mostly philosophers and cognitive scientists. The catch is they want a two-page research proposal from me by the end of the month, and they've been generous enough to let me propose something I actually want to think about rather than a chunk of the novel. So here's my half-formed idea, and I'd like you to turn it into the actual proposal. I've been reading alot around the exclusion argument for mental causation — Kim's version, I think, though I'm honestly hazy on who else is in that fight — and what keeps nagging at me is that everyone seems to be arguing about whether my belief that the kettle's boiling 'causes' my hand to reach for it, while quietly using two different senses of cause. One is the difference-making sense: the belief has to make a difference to what happens, over and above whatever the neurons are doing. The other is more like a relevance or explain-why sense: the belief is the sort of thing you cite when you explain why the hand moved. My hunch is that the exclusion argument only really bites against the first sense, and that most of the escapes from it work by sliding into the second — which looks like it rescues mental causation but may just change the subject. So the question I want the proposal to pin down is this: can we state a notion of mental causation that is both immune to exclusion and still strong enough to underwrite what we actually need it for — blame, responsibility, the explanatory practice of psychology, all of it — or is there some proof-shaped reason you can't have both at once? I want that set up as a genuine dilemma with two horns, not a vague worry.

The proposal needs to do the usual work. State the question so it has a determinate answer. Say why the existing positions leave it open — and please don't give me a literature review or a reading list, I'll fill in the citations myself; just tell me what the positions are and where the gap is. Then the method: what I'd actually do, month by month, across the year. My instinct is that the interesting part is building a small taxonomy of causal notions and stress-testing each one against the messy cases — preemption, overdetermination, double prevention — rather than only the clean ones, since the clean ones are where everyone already agrees. Tell me what would count as this project failing, and be honest about whether it's too much for someone who hasn't done formal philosophy of mind since undergrad. And say what the deliverable is at the end: a paper? a taxonomy plus a dilemma argument? I'd rather know now than in month nine.

One more thing, and I don't know if it belongs in a research proposal. I write fiction, so I care about this partly because if the mental really is doing no work, then the whole business of character motivation is a kind of lie we tell on purpose, and I'd like to know whether the lie is harmless. If thats a silly thing to put in front of a funding committee, leave it out — but if there's a way to make it a real stake in the project rather than a confession, I'd rather have it in.
