I am an undergraduate taking a course on judgment and decision making. We have just finished the unit on sunk costs and escalation, where we read Staw's 1976 study of escalating commitment and, earlier in the term, Shubik's dollar auction as an illustration of the same phenomenon. I am writing a short paper on how these two things relate, and I would like help getting the comparison straight.

The two accounts, as I understand them:

(A) The structural account. In Shubik's game a dollar is auctioned off under a rule that both bidders pay: the highest bidder pays what they bid and receives the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also pays what they bid and receives nothing. On this account escalation requires no error on anyone's part — once you are the second-highest bidder, dropping out means paying for nothing, so raising is the locally better move, and the sequence of locally better moves carries the bidders past the value of the prize.

(B) The motivational account. In Staw's sort of case — a failing project that keeps getting funded, a war that keeps getting more troops, litigation that keeps being appealed — escalation is explained by the decision maker's unwillingness to accept that the earlier investment is wasted: sunk cost reasoning, self-justification, saving face.

What I want from you:

1. What each account has to explain. Set out the phenomena that any account of escalation should cover — the cases it must get right, the features it must reproduce (an entrant who would not have entered ex ante; persistence in the face of bad news; the role of personal responsibility for the original decision; the fact that observers call the behavior irrational while the actor can usually give reasons) — and say which of these each account handles well and which it handles badly or not at all.

2. Two variants I invented to find out where the trap actually lives. First, keep the prize and the bidding but let the losing bidder pay nothing — an ordinary English auction for the same dollar. Second, keep the second-highest bidder's liability but refund half of it. In each case: does the escalation survive? In what form, and bounded by what? What do the two variants together tell you about which feature of the original game the escalation depends on? I would rather have your reasoning here than a verdict.

3. The bridge back to the real world. Real escalation — the project, the war, the lawsuit — has no auctioneer and no explicit rule that the loser pays. What plays the role of the second bidder's liability in those cases? If the answer is "the sunk cost itself," has the structural account quietly become the motivational one, or does it still tell us something the motivational account obscures? Be specific about what is left of the distinction.

4. Verdict. Which of the two is more fundamental — in the sense that the other could be dispensed with if certain things turned out to be true — and in what sense, if any, is the dollar auction special as a model of escalation? I have a suspicion that the auction is most illuminating exactly where it is least like a real case, because nobody in it is making a mistake, but I want you to tell me whether that suspicion survives contact with the details.
