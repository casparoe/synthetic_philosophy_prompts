I'm a graduate student in a small reading group that mixes statistics and philosophy of science, and this term we've been circling the frequentist/Bayesian divide in the light of the replication crisis. In our last session I raised a worry about the Bayesian reanalyses of failed replication attempts: a lot of them turn on the choice of prior, and it's easy to move a Bayes factor from decisive support for the null to decisive support for the alternative just by changing a prior that nobody bothered to justify, which makes the supposed "Bayesian fix" for the replication crisis look less robust than its advocates suggest. A senior student waved this away with the familiar line — just get more data, and with enough data the prior washes out. I couldn't tell whether that was a mathematical triviality, a substantive bet about how the world happens to be, or a debating move dressed up as a theorem, and I suspect the phrase is doing several quite different jobs depending on who says it.

What I'd like from you is a menu rather than a verdict. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "with enough data the prior washes out" could actually be making, ordered from the ones that are true almost by definition, through the ones that are substantive and defensible but only under conditions that have to be spelled out, down to the ones that are simply false or are rhetorical sleight of hand. In particular I want the readings that hinge on the fine print: what counts as "enough" and how the answer changes with sample size versus dimension; what happens under misspecification, when the model is wrong in a way that no amount of data repairs; what happens with weakly identified or unidentifiable parameters, where the likelihood is nearly flat and the prior keeps its grip however much data arrives; and the difference between a genuinely informative prior and a supposedly neutral default one. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive, or false, and then say which of the readings would actually license the use my colleague put the phrase to — namely, dismissing my concern that prior choice can flip the conclusion of a replication reanalysis — and which would not. Keep it conceptual rather than computational: I'm not after code, a simulation, or a worked numerical example, and I don't need citations or a reading list, since I can track down the literature myself. I want a clear-eyed sorting of the readings and, at the end, a note on which one I should press him on when the group meets again, and why the others don't get him the conclusion he wanted.
