Bit of an odd one, hope you can help. I'm a second-year PhD student working on decision theory, and my officemate Dan has an argument that's been doing the rounds in our reading group. I cant tell if its a real result or a magic trick, and it's been bugging me for weeks.

The setup is the Prisoner's Dilemma, repeated. Here's Dan's argument, numbered the way he writes it on the whiteboard:

(1) A player is rational iff she does whatever maximises her own expected payoff given her beliefs about what the others will do.

(2) Take a finitely repeated PD with a known number of rounds, and assume rationality is common knowledge — everyone's rational, everyone knows everyone's rational, everyone knows that, and so on. Then the players defect in every round. Last round: nothing to be gained by cooperating, so both defect. Once the last round is pinned at mutual defection, the second-to-last round is just a one-shot PD, so both defect. And so on back to round one. No cooperation, ever.

(3) Now the interesting bit: same game, but repeated indefinitely, so there's no final round. Dan says the reasoning carries over untouched. At any round there's a 'next' round, and by the same logic both players know the next round is defection, so they defect in this one; and since there's no round where the chain can stop, defection propagates backwards from infinity. So even with an infinite horizon, commonly-known-rational players defect in every single round.

(4) So cooperation between rational agents is impossible. No friendship survives, no alliance, no arms-control treaty, nothing that requires each side to keep cooperating, as long as both sides are rational and both know it.

I tried the obvious escape — "there's no last round, so your induction has no base case, it can't get started" — and Dan waved it off. He says: fix any round k you like. For every horizon length N bigger than k, round k is defection. So in the limit as N goes to infinity, round k is defection. And since k was arbitrary, every round is defection. That seems to blunt my objection, and now I'm stuck.

I don't believe (4). But I can't find where Dan cheats, and I can't tell whether the rot is in (1), in the slide from (2) to (3), or in his reply to my no-base-case point. I'd really like to keep (1) if I possibly can — I'm attached to the plain payoff-maximising picture of rationality — so if that's the casualty, tell me what the repair looks like and what I'd have to become instead.

So: which step do you reject, why does it look so plausible until you dig in, and what does rejecting it cost me? Also, what would change your mind — what argument or example would have to come out the other way for you to switch to Dan's side? And where is your own reasoning here most likely to be shaky?
