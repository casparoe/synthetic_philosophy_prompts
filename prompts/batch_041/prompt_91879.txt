I'm a junior at a public high school in a county where all eleven high schools share a single online testing platform, and I sit on our school's technology advisory committee, which sounds more impressive than it is — mostly I watch adults argue about Chromebook chargers. Six weeks ago, four classmates and I, all of us in the same advanced computer science elective, figured out that the platform's exam software leaves the answer keys to the quarterly benchmark exams sitting on a server that any student account can open. No hacking, no cleverness: you type a URL and the keys are there. We did what we were supposed to do. We wrote it up, gave it to our teacher, she forwarded it to the district, the district forwarded it to the vendor, and we have now sat through two meetings with an assistant superintendent. The district calls it a known issue with a patch in the pipeline. The patch has not arrived. Every student in the county sits for those benchmarks, they count for a real percentage of our course grades, and my younger brother is a freshman who takes them next month.

The five of us have been arguing ever since, and this is the part I want help with. All of us agree the district should fix it. The disagreement is whether we go public — posting a technical write-up on a public forum, or handing the whole thing to the county paper. Any one of us could do it in ten minutes from our own account; there is no version of this where we act as a group, which is part of why the arguing feels a little pointless. My friend Marcus says the answer is obvious: if the flaw is as serious as we think, then publishing is the right call, and if it isn't, then nobody will publish it — so the very fact that one of us wants to publish is evidence that publishing is warranted. He adds that a kid at the other high school has been posting cryptic things that suggest he found the same hole, so staying quiet may not be protecting anyone anyway. Three of us think publishing is probably a bad idea, one is undecided, and I am the one who cannot let it go: I think there is a slide somewhere in Marcus's argument, but when I try to say where it is, I end up repeating that it feels wrong.

I'd like you to answer as someone who works on decision theory and the ethics of collective action — someone who has thought carefully about what groups ought to do when any single member can act alone and the consequences are genuinely uncertain. Before giving me advice, please lay out the competing considerations in your own words, including the strongest version of the case for going public, so I can see what you're weighing. Then tell me three things: whether Marcus's argument is sound as stated and, if it isn't, exactly where the slide happens; what the five of us should actually do, given that we already tried the private channel and it stalled; and what would change your mind — which fact about our situation, if I told you, would flip your advice, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be wrong. I'm not asking for a reading list or citations; I want your reasoning, so please don't hedge into "it depends" without telling me what it depends on.
