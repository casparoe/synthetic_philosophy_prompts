My friend Nadia and I went at it for like two hours last night about whether there's any coherent version of "you can't do X even to prevent more X," and I woke up still annoyed, mostly at myself, because I didn't have a real answer.

Her argument, roughly: if the reason not to kill someone is that killing is bad, then refusing to kill one person in order to stop two other killings looks like not caring about the badness of killing — it looks like caring about your own hands. She called it a fetish, compared it to turning down a free trade on principle, and the only thing I managed to say back was "but you're not seeing the point," which is not an arguement.

So before I go back at her, I want the spec. Not a defense of any particular view — the list of what anything claiming to answer this has to be able to do. For each item, the case or consideration that makes it a genuine requirement and not just something I happen to want to be true, and a call on which items are negotiable (a preference of one camp) versus non-negotiable (drop it and you've changed the subject).

Some things I think belong on the list, but cut whatever doesn't. It has to say why the constraint holds even when breaking it would leave fewer violations in the world, and it has to do that without assuming from the start that it's fine for reasons to be agent-relative, since that's exactly whats under dispute. It has to explain why the person who gets sacrificed has no valid complaint, or else admit she does have one and say what follows. It has to say whether it's allowed to cave in catastrophe cases, and if it does, why that isn't just conceding her whole point in installments. It has to have something to say about moral residue, if we're keeping that idea at all. And it has to survive the case that actually stumped me: a hundred of us each tip a little something into the river, each contribution is negligible, together we kill the fish in the whole valley. Each of us keeping our hands clean is what produces the disaster. Does the same machinery that handles the one-against-two case handle that one, or is that a different theory wearing the same name? And when the agent is a company or a state, "keep your hands clean" doesn't obviously mean anything, so whatever the requirement is really about has to be something a committee can have — or the view has to own up to applying only to people.

Two things I especially want out of this. Flag any pair of requirements you think can't both be met; I suspect at least one of them is doing its work by smuggling in the conclusion. And tell me which single requirement does the most to kill off otherwise popular fixes — if it's cheap, one line each on which family of answers dies first, but don't spend the prompt on that. No reading lists and no "see the literature on X," I can't look anything up right now and I don't want names anyway, I want the demands themselves. One more thing: should the list include "show that one of the assumptions generating the problem is false" as a legitimate way of satisfying everything else? Is the target a solution or a dissolution? I honestly can't tell, and I'd rather the list settle it than me.
