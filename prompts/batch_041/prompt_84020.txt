Last night a friend and I spent four hours arguing about whether culture evolves in a Darwinian sense, and we ended exactly where we began, which is also where the literature seems to have sat for decades. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on which of us is right.

The shape of it. He is a population geneticist by training and holds that a Darwinian process requires a replicator: a unit copied with enough fidelity that its descendants resemble it more closely than they resemble unrelated variants, so that selection has something to work on before transformation washes it out. Cultural transmission, he says, is reconstructive rather than replicative — people rebuild what they hear out of their own priors — and he thinks this shows up in the data as convergence: the same folktale episodes, the same melodic contours, the same tool forms recur in traditions with no contact, while idiosyncratic details die within a few retellings. On his view, what the cultural evolution literature calls selection is mostly the repeated pull of shared cognitive biases toward the same attractors, and the population-genetic vocabulary is a formalism draped over something closer to epidemiology than to descent.

My side: Darwinian dynamics need heritable variation in fitness, and heritability is a matter of degree and of statistics rather than of mechanism. The standard bookkeeping, which separates change into a selection term and a transmission term, applies whether or not there are discrete high-fidelity replicators, and models with biased transmission plus selection do generate the signatures of cumulative adaptation rather than mere recurrence. Nor do I think fidelity has a threshold that nature has to clear: if selection is strong enough, moderate fidelity will do, and how much fidelity is enough is something you set in a model rather than discover in the world. I should add that I think the word 'meme' is a distraction and I am not defending it.

We collided on three things and moved on none of them. First, the transmission-chain experiments in which material becomes steadily easier to learn over generations: I read that as selection among variants, he reads it as evidence that the material is being pulled toward a learnable attractor, and we each think the result is decisive for our side. Second, his circularity charge — that a selection model can never be wrong, since fitness is defined by what in fact spread, so 'the fitter variant spread' explains nothing. I say the models earn their keep by predicting cross-population and counterfactual patterns; he says the parameters are estimated from the very distributions the models are meant to explain. Third, what the case is even about: he keeps asking how a mind comes to hold a representation, I keep asking how a distribution of representations shifts across generations, and each of us treats the other's question as beside the point.

My hypothesis, held loosely, is that this is mostly a verbal dispute about how far the word 'Darwinian' stretches, and that once we agree on neutral terms — variation, heritable transmission, differential retention — we will turn out to agree about the phenomena. He insists the disagreement is empirical, about how much fidelity cultural transmission actually has. I don't trust either of us here, partly because we each have a stake in the answer.

Diagnose it for me. Give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no closing summary.

1. Whether my verbal-dispute hypothesis is right: how much of our disagreement survives restatement in neutral vocabulary, and what is left over.
2. Whether what remains is a hidden empirical question, and if so what measurement or experiment would settle it — or whether the fidelity threshold is a modeling choice rather than an empirical matter at all.
3. Whether he and I are answering different questions under a single heading, and whether that makes the dispute idle rather than substantive.
4. Whether either of us is leaning on something we cannot argue for — a criterion for what counts as a real evolutionary process, say — and whether the impasse shows the question is ill-posed or merely hard.
5. What would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question — or an argument that none should be expected, and why.
