I write about ideas for a living, science and philosophy mostly, and I'm putting together a magazine piece on the strange ethics of outsourcing your moral judgment: obviously it's fine to trust someone else about which antibiotic to take, and yet letting someone else tell you what's right feels off in a way that's weirdly hard to pin down. There's a whole philosophical literature on this, moral testimony and so on, but the book I keep circling back to is Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day, and I want to read it through that lens. Stevens is basically a case study. He hands his moral judgment over to Lord Darlington wholesale, the two Jewish maids get dismissed and he keeps his head down, he keeps serving dinner while his father is dying upstairs, and thirty years later he's driving across the west country trying to convince himself it was all fine and that he'd been, as he'd put it, dignified.

What I want from you is an actual reading, not a plot summary or a book report. Where does the novel dramatize the thing the philosophers are worried about, and where does it complicate or resist it? My hunch, which I'd like you to push on hard, is that the interesting bit isn't that Stevens defers, it's the particular shape of the deference. Nobody's forcing him, he's not obeying orders, he's trusting a man he thinks is better placed than he is to know what's worth doing with a life, and he's deferring about what to feel as much as about what to do. So: is there a real difference between borrowing someone's answer to what should I do and borrowing their answer to what should I care about, and does the novel make that distinction matter or just collapse it?

Then the thing I keep flip-flopping on. Does the book even think deference is his problem? The rival diagnosis is dignity, that he's committed to a professional ideal which happens to require self-erasure, and the tragedy is that the ideal is coherent but empty. Or maybe the real problem is that he had no decent alternative, which would make the whole moral-deference frame a bit of an anachronistic imposition on the novel and I should say so in the piece.
