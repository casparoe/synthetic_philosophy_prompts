Okay so last night I spent two hours in an arguement with my friend about moral trade — the idea that if you care most about X and I care most about Y, we can both do better by our own lights if I fund your thing and you fund mine, instead of each of us just funding our own. He thinks it's the most underrated idea in practical ethics. I think it mostly amounts to whoever stakes out the most extreme position getting bought off. We didn't settle it, and now I have to, for a boring reason: I run the swap desk for our giving circle.

What that means in practice: people send me proposals — a handful a month — where two of them agree to change where they give, or agree to do or skip some other thing, and I'm the one who has to look at it and either wave it through or kill it. Money involved is usually a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. I get maybe twenty minutes per proposal, I'm not a philosopher, and I'm definately the one whose name is on it if it goes wrong. Our one house rule so far is no cash to the people doing the trading — money has to go to organizations, not to the traders — which I suspect is doing more work than we realize and might also be quietly dodging the interesting cases.

Please turn the moral trade idea into a procedure I can actually run. In order: the questions to ask, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure just stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is values and not facts. Keep the distinctions that matter and tell me which ones you dropped to make it fit on a page. No citations, no reading list, I cant go look things up — and don't assume I know the vocabulary, spell it out.

Then run it on three proposals:
1. The clean one: I give $800 to your animal welfare org if you give $800 to my AI safety org, or some version of that.
2. Someone offers to go vegetarian for a year if the circle pays $2000 to a farmed animal org. Feels different from case 1 and I can't say why.
3. A local company offers to keep a factory open for two more years — real jobs — if we drop our campaign against them. Also feels different, also can't say why.

At least one of these should come out the way I don't want it to. If all three come out the way I'd have guessed on my own, the procedure isn't earning its keep.

Two things about how to pitch the answer. My friend's version of the test was "would you make the same trade with someone who holds the exact mirror image of your view" — use it if it's any good, but don't use it just because he said it. And if I've got this wrong at the root — if the idea doesn't actually support a general procedure, or if what I'm describing isn't moral trade but something else wearing its coat — say so straight out rather than writing me a checklist anyway. Same if the procedure is going to end up blessing whatever I already wanted.
