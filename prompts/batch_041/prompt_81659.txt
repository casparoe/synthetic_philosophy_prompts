Here is the chapter's account, in full: "Fitch's paradox begins from the anti-realist's principle that every truth is knowable — that for any true proposition, it is possible that someone should know it. Fitch showed that this innocent-looking principle has a startling consequence: if every truth is knowable, then every truth is in fact known. The proof is short. Take any proposition that is true but not known — say, 'it is raining and nobody knows it.' If every truth is knowable, then this proposition, being true, must be knowable. But suppose someone did know it: since knowing a conjunction means knowing each of its parts, this knower would know that it is raining and would also know that nobody knows it; and because knowledge is factive — you can only know what is true — knowing that nobody knows it would mean that nobody knows it, and the knower, being somebody, would then not know that it is raining. So the knower would both know and not know the same thing, which is impossible. It follows that the proposition 'it is raining and nobody knows it' can never be known; and since it can never be known it is not knowable, so by the anti-realist's own principle it can never be true — which is just to say that whenever it is raining, somebody knows it. Generalising, the knowability principle entails that every truth is already known, that is, that we are omniscient. The paradox therefore refutes verificationism: the knowability principle cannot be held in the form its defenders want, because it collapses into an absurdity. The one escape is logic. The reasoning is classical, resting on the law of excluded middle and on the classical equivalence between 'it is not known that p' and 'it is known that not-p'; an intuitionist who refuses those steps can keep the knowability principle exactly as it stands."
