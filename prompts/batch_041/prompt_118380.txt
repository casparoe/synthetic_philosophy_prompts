I am a novelist, and I have written a short essay — for a literary magazine, as a companion piece to a book of mine — that I need to get right, and I have reached the end of what I can do by myself. I have no philosophical training; the essay is not a work of scholarship and I have no intention of letting it become one. What I want from you is comment of a substantive kind: where the argument is loose, where it is confused, where it is merely a familiar move dressed in fresher words, and where it is wrong. The essay follows in full. Beneath it I set out the particular things I want commented on, and the form I want the comments in.

THE LEDGER AND THE FLAME

I came to this essay by way of a novel, and by way of a problem the novel left in my hands. The book concerns a woman who in one lifetime is the author of a considerable cruelty and who, some years later, is the victim of a cruelty she had no part in. She can see no connection between the two, and I did not mean one. My first readers, however, kept asking whether the second was intended as payment for the first. I said no. Then I tried to explain why not, and found that I had walked into a doctrine I had always taken to be a species of moral bookkeeping and had never once examined: karma.

Stated as a theory of moral desert, the doctrine requires two things. It requires that the one who receives the outcome be the one who performed the deed; otherwise we are not distributing desert but merely causing harm in a pattern. And it requires that the outcome be deserved — that the agent be the kind of thing that can deserve, and the outcome the kind of thing that can be deserved.

The difficulty is that the setting in which the doctrine does its work is precisely the setting in which the first requirement is hardest to satisfy. The deed is done in one life; the reckoning arrives, by hypothesis, in another. Memory does not cross over. And on the analysis I take to be standard in the Buddhist case, there is no owner to cross over either: a person is a process, a stream of constituents, not a thing that persists behind them. So the recipient of the reckoning is, in every sense that matters to a novelist — memory, temperament, the felt continuity of a life — a stranger to the deed. If desert requires that the reckoning land on the one who acted, karma appears to punish an innocent.

I can see three ways out, and I distrust all of them.

The first is to abandon the desert reading altogether. On this account karma is not a verdict but a causal law of character: what you do makes you into a certain kind of person, and a person of that kind suffers. No ledger is required. But then the moral vocabulary has quietly been withdrawn, and what remains is a natural history of suffering — true, perhaps, and of no more moral interest than the fact that suffering breeds suffering. It also leaves unexplained why this particular causal law should track fitness rather than mere regularity. The tyrant's misery is then no more significant than his gout.

The second is to insist that the stream itself is the person for the purposes of desert, and that the absence of memory is neither here nor there. Desert is then attached to a causal continuum. But if a causal continuum is enough to make me answerable for what was done within it, I do not see how to stop: I am continuous with my parents, and with the century that made me, and with the country whose deeds I inherit. That way lies a doctrine on which I deserve the misfortunes of my ancestors and my neighbours alike, which is surely a reductio.

The third is my own, and I suspect it may be a verbal trick. It is the thought that the fittingness at issue holds not between a person and a fate but between a deed and an outcome, in the order of things; the person is only the place where the ledger surfaces. A debt that has been sold on is still a debt, and the new holder's liability is real without being his desert. On this view karma describes the shape of the world's response to what is done in it, and the individual is the site of that response rather than its addressee. Call it desert without a deserver.

What the third option buys is the fittingness; what it costs is the address. It cannot say: it should have been you. And a theory of desert that cannot say that has, I think, given up the thing we wanted. So I am left with a dilemma of my own making: either the doctrine is desert, and then the memory problem bites; or the memory problem is dissolved, and what remains is not desert. I would like to know whether the third option is a position or a pun, and whether the dilemma has an exit I cannot see.

COMMENTS WANTED

1. The characterisation. Is the doctrine as I have stated it a fair target or a straw man? In particular, is the desert reading one its defenders would recognise as theirs, and is my account of the discontinuity — no memory, no owner — the standard one, or a novelist's simplification? Do not send me to sources; tell me in your own words where I am wrong and how much it matters to the argument.

2. The dilemma. Is my trichotomy exhaustive? If there is a fourth option, state it as precisely as you can and say what it costs. If one of the three is not a real option, say which, and why.

3. The third option. Is it coherent, or is it an equivocation on the word desert — a description of causal fittingness wearing moral clothes? If it can be made coherent, say exactly what has to be true for it to work and what it would still fail to explain. If you think it is a pun, say so without softening it.

4. The essay as an essay. Which sections earn their keep for a general reader, and which are the novelist showing off his reading? Where should the argument begin? I am willing to cut a third of the piece and would rather cut the right third.

5. Revisions. Give me two or three concrete changes, at least one of which alters the argument rather than the prose.

Three constraints on the comments themselves. No bibliography, no reading list, no names of scholars, no citations: I will supply whatever apparatus is needed later, and a list I cannot evaluate is of no use to me. Do not summarise the essay back to me; I know what I wrote. Assume no technical vocabulary on my part, and if you must use a term, define it in passing. Aim for something in the region of a thousand words, and be direct rather than tactful — I would rather be told the essay fails than be congratulated on it.
