You are a philosopher of mind with a poet's ear, and I'm hoping you can help me with something that has been nagging at me all term. I'm in a seminar on self-knowledge, and a friend of mine — a card-carrying Williamsonian, for whatever that's worth — keeps insisting that two things I had always run together come apart. The first is the old idea, sometimes called the KK principle and traced back through Hintikka's epistemic logic of the early 1960s to much older sources, that knowing something brings knowing that you know it along with it: if I know that I'm in pain, then I know that I know that I'm in pain, and so on up without end. The second is the argument, given its most influential recent form in Timothy Williamson's Knowledge and Its Limits (2000), that nothing of the everyday sort is luminous in the sense the KK principle needs — that is, nothing is such that whenever you're in it, you're in a position to know that you're in it. The argument, as I understand it, walks you along a continuum — a bath heated by degrees, a light brightening by imperceptible steps — where each stage feels exactly like its neighbor and the condition is plainly present at one end and plainly absent at the other. If you could know you were in the condition at some stage, then at the next stage, which is indistinguishable to you, you would believe the same thing; since the condition isn't there, that belief would be false; so the earlier claim to knowledge would have rested on luck, which knowledge supposedly cannot do. Knowledge seems to require a margin for error, and a continuum is precisely a place where no margin is available.

What I'd like from you is a villanelle about this — nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, two refrains. The first refrain should be the kind of thing someone says when they take their own present state to be fully open to them, the declaration the KK principle licenses and then licenses again; the second should be the observation that the next case down the line is indistinguishable from this one, which is the fact that does the damage. The form should not be decoration: the whole point of a refrain is that it comes back and claims to apply again, which is exactly what the KK principle does, so the poem's returning lines should enact the iteration and the closing quatrain — the place where the two refrains finally have to stand side by side — should be where a reader sees that they cannot both hold. Put it in the voice of someone standing inside the series, feeling their way along it, rather than someone summarizing the puzzle from outside. I'd also ask that the poem never use the technical vocabulary — no "luminous," no "KK," no "margin for error," no "safety" — the ideas have to arrive through the images and through the pressure the refrains put on each other. If a line has to be padded to make a rhyme work, change the line rather than pad it; I would much rather the refrains be a little strange than smooth.

Before you write anything, please think the argument through carefully and try out several candidate refrains on the page, since the poem lives or dies on whether those two lines can bear nineteen lines of repetition without going stale. Then give me the poem, followed by a short account, in plain prose, of the choices you made — why those refrains, why that setting, and where exactly in the poem you take the argument to turn. Write in American English.
