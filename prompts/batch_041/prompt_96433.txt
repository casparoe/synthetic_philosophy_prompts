Since I retired from the laboratory I have been sitting in on a graduate seminar in the philosophy department on rational choice, and the instructor has asked me — as the resident outsider, a physicist who spent his working life on statistical mechanics and on the interpretation of noisy measurements — to contribute one essay question to the take-home final. What I want the question to test is something I have now watched a dozen bright students fumble. They can all reproduce the result that an agent whose preferences satisfy certain axioms can be represented as maximizing expected utility, and they can all recite the money-pump and Dutch-book stories, but when pressed they slide from "your preferences can be represented that way" to "rationality requires that they be" without noticing that this transition is the whole question. I would like a question that parks them exactly on that seam and gives them nowhere to hide — one that a student cannot answer by reproducing a lecture, however accurately.

Two further twists, both of which I have been turning over in our informal reading group, should be built into the question. The first is the case of an agent who is genuinely decisive about most of her options but refuses to rank two of them at all — she does not prefer the one, does not prefer the other, and does not consider them equal — which raises the question of what the coherence arguments do or do not say about an agent whose preferences are incomplete rather than inconsistent. The second is a fact I take to be important and which the students appear never to have considered: the exploit in these arguments is always staged by a counterparty, in a setting where trade and repetition are possible. I would like the question to force them to say whether the normative force of "coherence" survives in an environment where no such counterparty exists, and, if it does, whence that force derives. Neither twist should require the students to cite or summarize any particular author; I would far rather they argue than report, and the question should be answerable without appeal to the literature.

Please write the question, a model answer of roughly seven hundred words that argues rather than asserts, and a marking scheme that will let a colleague who has never taught the material grade it consistently. I want the marking scheme built around five must-hit points, five marks each for a total of twenty-five, each point phrased so that a merely competent answer which misses it is visibly distinguishable from a strong one, and each accompanied by a sentence naming the specific mistake — not the generic weakness, the specific mistake — that costs a student those marks. Size the question for a seventy-five-minute sitting. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables; if the marks must be enumerated, do it in sentences.
