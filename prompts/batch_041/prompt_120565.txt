I've been reading about the solar neutrino problem and it's turned into my favourite test case for something I've been calling the two-lock picture of identifying unobservable things. Setup, in case any of it matters: Davis puts a tank of dry-cleaning fluid in a gold mine in South Dakota, chlorine-37 catches electron neutrinos and becomes argon-37, they count the individual argon atoms, and they get roughly a third of what Bahcall's solar model says should be arriving. So you've got three families of response -- the sun is wrong (maybe the core is cooler than modelled), the experiment is wrong (extraction efficiency, the cross-section, take your pick), or the neutrino is wrong (oscillations, mass, mixing; Pontecorvo had floated the idea years earlier and basically nobody cared). Then Kamiokande, a completely different detector, sees a deficit too, which squeezes the experiment-is-wrong door. Then SNO with the heavy water seperates electron neutrinos from the other flavours and the total flux comes out right, so the missing ones were changing flavour en route. And the deficit turned out not to be uniform -- much later measurements showed it was larger for the high-energy neutrinos and barely there for the low-energy ones, which I take to be the tell.

The two-lock picture: an unobservable thing is identified only once you have (a) a causal handle -- something that wouldn't be there if the thing didn't exist, so you can say something's there -- and (b) a discriminating handle -- something that comes out differently depending on what kind of thing it is, so you can say which thing rather than merely that thing.

What I want is the episode run through that, and then the framework run through the episode. Did the neutrino have a causal handle long before any of this (missing energy in beta decay), and when if ever did it get a discriminating handle -- is the solar deficit one, or does it only count as one retroactivly, after you've closed the other two doors? And in the other direction: the obvious embarrassment is that the winning alternative was already on the shelf and ignored for decades, which makes the stock worry -- that past scientists couldn't even frame the alternatives that turned out to be right, and neither can we -- look like the wrong diagnosis of why we get these things wrong. Does the two-lock picture handle that better or worse? And be explicit about where it's just silent, e.g. it seems to have nothing to say about when to revise an existing entity's properties versus positing a new one; they did later posit a sterile neutrino for a different set of anomalies and that one still isn't nailed down. Don't be polite about it, I'd rather find out where the framework is dead than get a victory lap.
