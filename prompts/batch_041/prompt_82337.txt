The claim I want you to build a case against: a rational agent can never be made worse off in expectation by free, reliable information received before they act. I have seen this referred to as Good's theorem. The proof I have been shown is short — for any act a, the law of iterated expectations gives E[u(a)] = E[E[u(a)|X]], and the maximum of a set of averages cannot exceed the average of the maxima, so the value of the problem after observing X is at least the value before. I follow the proof and I believe it under its assumptions. What I want is not a discussion of it but a designed case, together with a demonstration that the design works.

Specifications for the case:

- The agent is a textbook expected-utility maximizer with a fixed utility function over ordinary outcomes — money, time, health, career — who conditionalizes correctly. No regret aversion, no ambiguity aversion, no weakness of will, no self-deception, no error about the probabilities. Nothing about the agent's reasoning is defective.
- The information is free, perfectly reliable, arrives strictly before the agent must act, and the agent remains free to act exactly as they would have acted without it.
