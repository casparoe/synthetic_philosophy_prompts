Q3. Friedman's 1953 essay on the methodology of positive economics is best known for a claim his critics christened the F-twist: that the significance of a theory increases with the unrealism of its assumptions.

(a) Reconstruct the argument that would make the claim good, saying what "unrealistic" has to mean for the argument to go through, and saying which of the two kinds of assumption distinguished in lecture the argument needs — the kind that the theory itself gives you a way of relaxing, because the idealisation is a limit case and the theory tells you what happens as you move away from it, and the kind that simply identifies the case the theory is about, so that there is nothing to relax. (6 marks)

(b) The same essay insists that a theory is to be judged by the accuracy of its predictions rather than by the realism of its assumptions. Reconcile (a) and (b), and say what would count as a theory that satisfies (b) while failing (a). (6 marks)

(c) Does the doctrine commit Friedman to instrumentalism about theories? Argue for your answer. (8 marks)

Answer. The F-twist takes the ordinary presumption — that a theory whose assumptions are false is in trouble — and reverses it. The argument runs roughly like this. A theory is a device for turning what we know about a situation into predictions about it. Its assumptions are not extra premises that have to be true in order for those predictions to be licensed; they are the conditions under which the machinery runs. So the way to appraise a theory is to feed in the relevant facts and see whether what comes out the other end matches what we observe. Read that way, "unrealistic" cannot mean "false", because a theory with false assumptions can be an excellent predictor; it must mean something like "not literally true of any actual case", as with the assumption of no friction or the assumption that firms know their marginal costs. Unrealism in that sense is a mark of ambition rather than a defect. A theory that assumed everything about one particular economy would predict that economy and nothing else; a theory that abstracts gets a grip on a class of cases, so the more it abstracts the more it can be asked to explain, which is why the most significant theories tend to have the least lifelike assumptions.

That is also the answer to (b). The realism of the assumptions is not a second standard competing with predictive accuracy, because the second is the standard and the first is only a way of describing how a theory manages to meet it. An assumption is doing its job when the predictions that hang on it come out right. A theory that satisfied (b) while failing (a) would be something like an elaborate curve-fitting exercise: it gets the numbers right, but it has no reach beyond the cases it was fitted to, which is just the low significance that (a) is about.

As for the distinction from lecture: I don't think it does much work here. Friedman's test is predictive accuracy either way, so an assumption of the second kind — one that identifies the case the theory is about — is in no worse a position than an idealisation. A model of a market with exactly two firms is false of almost every actual market, and that is no mark against it, and we do not need a way of relaxing the assumption to see why: we judge the model by whether the predictions it generates are accurate for the markets we use it on. The distinction tells us how a theory would be modified if we wanted to modify it; it does not tell us whether the theory is any good.

(c) The doctrine is a claim about how theories are tested, whereas instrumentalism is a claim about what theories are. So the F-twist does not by itself commit Friedman to instrumentalism. But the essay does go further in that direction than the doctrine requires: the talk of behaviour as if agents were maximisers, and the insistence that assumptions are not tested directly, are the moves that make instrumentalism tempting. The only reason I would hesitate to call him a full instrumentalist is that he still wants the predictions to come out true, and a thorough instrumentalist would not care about that.
