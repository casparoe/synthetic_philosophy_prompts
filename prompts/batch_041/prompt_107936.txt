Ok so I teach a third-year undergrad seminar, 16 students, and I've got one 80-minute slot I'd rather spend on a game than a lecture. They've done expected utility and a little Nash equilibrium, but nothing on cooperation in symmetric games yet, so they won't have the vocabulary going in and I want to keep it that way.

What I want them to feel, without me announcing it first: the pull of the move where you stop asking what's best for me given what they do, and start asking what would we all do if we're all the same kind of reasoner. Then the moment that move stops working. The specific contrast I'm after is this: there's a version where the sameness assumption flips the answer to cooperation and it lands like a genuine discovery, and a version where the sameness assumption gives you no answer at all, because every common choice pays the same, and the whole thing unravels into a bidding war. Hofstadter set this up originally -- he had the Platonia Dilemma, and then a Luring Lottery where readers sent in postcards and the prize was a fixed pot divided by the total number of entries, and a postcard could name any integer, so anyone could inflate their own entries. I don't need the history, I need the mechanics staged so my students hit both halves inside one session.

So design it. Rules, materials (assume I can photocopy, I have a bag of tokens, and about 15 bucks for a silly prize -- no real money beyond that), a payoff scheme that works with tokens, and a timeline that fits 80 minutes including a break if you think it needs one. Anonymity seems important, so tell me how to run the slips or sealed envelopes so that reputation and social pressure don't swamp the incentives. I'd like them to play, then play again with one thing changed, then a third round where I break the symmetry on purpose -- but you decide whether that's the right arc.

Then the debrief. Map each round onto the idea, including why the same sameness argument rescues them in one game and not the other, and what the actual escape hatch is. I have a hunch it's some publicly agreed, arbitrary convention or a pre-committed coin flip, and I'd like them to arrive at that themselves rather than hear it from me. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it -- I've never done this and 16 undergrads is alot of coordination -- and how to recover mid-session. And be honest about the part the game can't do for me: I assume there's some piece of the argument about identical reasoners that no payoff table can make real, since no two people actually reason identically, and that I'll have to just tell them. What is that piece, exactly?

No readings, no citations, no reading list -- I just want the thing itself.
