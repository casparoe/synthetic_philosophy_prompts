I'm a novelist so maybe this is above my paygrade, but a friend who teaches philosophy handed me this argument and I can't stop chewing on it. He called the interesting bit "free-floating rationales." Here it is:

(1) A thing acts for a reason when the best explanation of what it does is that doing it brings about a certain outcome.
(2) Natural selection, trial-and-error learning, and market competition all explain a creature's or a firm's behavior by the outcome it brings about.
(3) None of those processes require the thing to represent the outcome, or to want it.
(4) So reasons can exist without reasoners — they float free of any mind.
(5) But a river carving a canyon is also explained by the outcome it brings about (the lower, easier state), and so is a stone rolling downhill.
(6) So the river acts for a reason, and so does the stone.
(7) And if rivers and stones act for reasons, then "acting for a reason" marks nothing distinctive at all — every event whatever is done for a reason.
(8) So one of the earlier steps has to go.

Where does it break? I'd love to keep (4), because the whole reason it excites me is that it would let me write a beehive, or a whole forest, as a character with aims, without cheating. But if something has to give, tell me which step, why it looked so obviously fine while I was nodding along, and what I lose by dropping it.
