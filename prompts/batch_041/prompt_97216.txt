In a reading group on the evolution of morality I keep running into the one-liner "you can't get an ought from an is," and I've started to notice that it does very different work depending on who is wielding it. The occasion was an argument by a colleague — call him Ravi — who claimed that because fairness dispositions evolved in groups that outcompeted less cooperative ones, fairness is a genuine standard we ought to respect; a second colleague dismissed the whole thing with the slogan, and Ravi shot back that the objection is empty, since every normative view has to start from descriptive facts about what we care about. I suspect the two of them are talking past each other, but I can't articulate why, and for once I'd like a menu rather than a verdict.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making — say, a claim about logical entailment (that no valid argument can have only descriptive premises and a normative conclusion), a claim about epistemic justification, a claim about the meaning of moral terms, a claim about the metaphysics of moral properties, and any others you think are actually in play — and for each say whether it is a trivial logical point, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false? Please be careful about a few distinctions I keep fumbling: whether it forbids only entailment or also weaker relations such as supervenience; whether "is" premises are supposed to include institutional facts (as in the inference from "you said you would come at noon" to "you ought to come at noon") and teleological or functional facts (as in "the heart's function is to pump"); whether it rules out instrumental oughts (as in "you want to get well, and this is the only way, so you ought to take the medicine"); and how the slogan relates to the so-called naturalistic fallacy, which I have a suspicion is a different accusation wearing the same coat. Finally, tell me which of these readings my colleague would have to be relying on for the slogan to do the work of actually killing Ravi's argument, and which readings would leave Ravi entirely untouched. Lay it out as a numbered list I can bring to the next session.
