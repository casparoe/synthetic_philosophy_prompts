I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I sit on the standards committee that rules on contested accuracy questions in our own pages. The case before us on Thursday is our technology desk's account of a customer-service chatbot that, as the story had it, 'decided' a caller was attempting fraud, 'believed' her account of the transaction was false, and 'steered' her toward a scripted refusal, under a headline saying the system 'lied' to her. The vendor has demanded a correction on the ground that there are no beliefs or intentions there to report, and the committee has agreed to settle, once and for all, how this magazine may describe what such systems do. Two of us have already staked out positions. One wants mental-state vocabulary struck from our AI coverage as either a category error or a lazy shorthand; the other, who keeps invoking the intentional stance, argues that the whole point of such vocabulary is prediction — that if describing a system as wanting something lets you anticipate its behaviour better than any mechanistic description can, the description has earned its keep, and that a rule against it would make our reporting less accurate rather than more. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. I need a ruling the desk can apply without a philosopher in the room: what a reporter must be able to show before a mental verb goes into a sentence about a system; whether the bar should be higher for verbs that implicate deception or intent — which seem to require representing someone else's state and aiming to produce a false one in them — than for verbs like 'chose' or 'preferred'; whether the rule can be framed without the magazine taking a stand on contested metaphysics, which I suspect it cannot, since a blanket ban is itself a stand and silence leaves the intentional reading in place by default; and what weight to give the fact that when we write that a system lied, the blame in a reader's mind lands on the software rather than on the people who shipped it. Give me a concrete rule with a grace period for copy already filed and a date for review, and test it against a hard case: a system whose behaviour really is best predicted by treating it as having aims, even though nothing in it stores a proposition over time. Engage the strongest version of the objection from my colleague, who is not a fool and whose view commands roughly half the newsroom.
