I'm a philosopher who works on machine learning, and I've got a seminar next week where I'm defending a line that I know is going to get me in trouble, so I need a Q&A prep sheet. My line: the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness (the ones showing you can't have a score that's calibrated within groups and also balanced in its error rates across groups unless the base rates happen to match, and so on) aren't obstacles to be cleverly routed around. They're a sign we've been asking the wrong question. What we should be equalizing isn't a statistic of the classifier but the ex ante distribution of who bears the cost of its mistakes, and no single deterministic classifier can do that -- so the honest object of fairness is a randomization over classifiers (and, in the limit, over which way the genuinely marginal cases fall), whose weights are set by a deliberative process rather than by the model. The fix isn't a better algorithm, it's relocating the fairness question from the classifier to the politics of residual risk. A lot of my motivation comes from the fact that even a perfectly calibrated score, thresholded, can carve out wildly different subpopulations.

The room is going to be rough, and it's a mixed one. Roughly: three econometricians/statisticians who've worked on recidivism and credit risk scores and care a lot about calibration -- they'll want every term defined and will smell hand-waving from a mile away; two critical algorithm studies people who think the whole fairness-metrics literature is a technocratic laundering operation and will read my proposal as more of the same; a couple of ML practitioners who've actually shipped a scoring model and want something they can do Monday; and one philosopher of science who'll want to know what work the phrase construct validity is doing in my argument.
