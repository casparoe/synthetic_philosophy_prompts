And this is what one of them wrote:

> Benacerraf's dilemma comes from the fact that a good account of mathematics has to do two jobs at once. It has to tell us what makes mathematical sentences true, and it has to tell us how we can come to know that they are true. The problem is that the best answer to the first question seems to rule out the best answer to the second.
>
> The first horn is the semantic one. When we assert that there are infinitely many primes, we are on the face of it talking about a domain of objects, the primes, in the same way that a sentence about three chairs talks about chairs. So a semantics that treats mathematical language like the rest of language will treat numerals as referring expressions and quantifiers as real quantifiers, and that seems to commit us to numbers as objects which exist independently of us. That is platonism.
>
> The second horn is epistemological. Knowledge requires some kind of reliable connection between the knower and the thing known, and Benacerraf's version of this is a causal theory, where knowing that p requires an appropriate causal chain from the fact that p to your belief that p. Numbers are abstract, so they are causally inert and outside space and time and can't stand at the end of any causal chain. So we can't know anything about them, and mathematics turns out to be a body of truths that nobody could be in a position to know. (A lot of people have given up the causal theory since Benacerraf was writing, which I think takes some of the sting out of this horn.)
>
> The horns pull apart because the semantics pushes us towards platonism and the epistemology pushes us away from it, and we can't have both. Keep the objects and you lose the knowledge; keep the knowledge and you seem to have to give up the objects.
>
> My view is that Benacerraf is right, and that the way out is the one he gives in the same paper, which is to stop treating numbers as objects at all. He points out that there is no unique way of identifying the natural numbers with sets: you can build them the Zermelo way, so each number is the singleton of the one before it, or the von Neumann way, where each number is the set of all the smaller numbers, and either way you get a full sequence satisfying the axioms. Since neither construction is more correct than the other, numbers can't really be any of these sets, and what arithmetic is about is the structure, i.e. the pattern of relations, which any system of objects can have. So we keep the semantics without the mysterious objects.
>
> I'd also add that this only really matters for platonists. A nominalist like Field denies that mathematical sentences are literally true descriptions of objects, so there is no realm of abstracta for him to be causally cut off from, and the second horn never gets going. So the dilemma is best read as an argument against platonism rather than against mathematical truth as such.
