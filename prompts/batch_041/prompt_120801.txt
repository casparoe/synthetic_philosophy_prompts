I teach a 3rd-year seminar on history and explanation -- 18 students, one 90-minute session a week -- and I'm sick of teaching hindsight bias with the usual slide about a study where people misremember their predictions. I want them to feel it, and I want them to feel the sneakier half too: not just that they misremember what they thought, but that the *explanation* they produce feels so satisfying precisely because it was assembled after the fact. Those are seperate failures and my students conflate them every single year.

Design me a runnable activity. I'll write the case material myself (a fact-sheet of maybe 12-15 facts about one episode, plus two different endings), so don't worry about getting any history right -- instead tell me what the fact-sheet has to contain and what properties the two endings need. Groups of four or five.

Rough shape of what I want to happen: first everyone commits to a forecast, one number plus the one fact they think is decisive, sealed in an envelope. Then half the groups are told the episode ended one way and half are told it ended the other way, and each group writes the causal story, marking which facts were "the causes". Then we compare. I want that comparison to be nasty -- the same facts recruited as causes of opposite outcomes, the same trait read as a strength in one room and a fatal flaw in the other, the ambivalent facts quietly dropped or reinterpreted. Then the envelopes come back and we put what they said they thought next to what they actually wrote.

Give me the exact sequence and timings, the materials, the wording of the instruction cards (this matters more than I'd like to admit -- one vague sentence and everyone hedges into mush), how to seed the ambivalent facts, a comparison scheme for the group debrief that isn't just me waving at the whiteboard, and a control condition for the leftover groups (facts, no ending, forecast only) so the narrative groups' "how predictable was this really?" ratings have something to sit next to. Also tell me whether the two endings should be one real outcome plus a fabricated one, or two real cases that genuinely diverged, and what the disclosure looks like either way -- I don't want them feeling tricked in a cheap way.

Then the debrief: what I say, in what order, and how I make the memory failure and the narrative failure come apart without lecturing. Be honest about what the activity can't do -- I suspect the toy case will leave everyone thinking all historical explanation is post hoc storytelling, which isn't what I believe, and I need to know exactly where to repair that, or whether there's a version that doesn't leave that taste.

And tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time. I'd bet money on at least one group asking why they're being made to explain an ending that's obviously manufactured, and I don't have a good answer ready.
