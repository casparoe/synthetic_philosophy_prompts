Interactive proof assistants are built on the rule that nothing counts as proved unless the kernel derives it from the axioms, yet every such system ships with a way out: an axiom to be postulated, a lemma admitted without proof, a component trusted rather than verified. Practitioners police that line sharply, treating some crossings as legitimate and others as cheating, and ordinary mathematics does something similar whenever a theorem is proved from assumptions stronger than the statement needs and then reported as a result about the original objects.

What does this practice presuppose about the relation between a formal system and the truths it is meant to capture? Does it require a standpoint outside the system from which the system's verdicts can be assessed — the move the MU-puzzle invites, where one counts the I's, observes that no rule changes their number mod 3, and concludes that MU is underivable, without deriving anything? Or is the weaker thought sufficient, that every outside is itself a system, so the standpoint is only ever deferred? The MU argument is itself elementary mathematics, so its outside is another system's inside; does that collapse the distinction or merely relocate it?

Reconstruct the implicit view, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates, and say what would have to change if no outside standpoint existed at all.
