I'm a 3rd year PhD student in epistomology, TAing a 90-minute session of a formal epistemology seminar next week — 12 students, mostly first-years, fine with probability, haven't read anything on acceptance or belief. I want a game that makes them feel why you can't have all three of: accept any claim that clears your threshold, accept the conjunction of whatever you accept, and never accept a contradiction. And I want them to feel the lottery case and the preface case pulling in different directions, because everyone nods along at the lottery and then treats the preface as obviously fine, and I don't think they've noticed the difference is structural.

Design: two rounds, same scoring rule, cheap tokens rather than money. Round 1 is a real draw — one winning ticket out of 20 in a hat — and each student privately declares a set of claims of the form ticket k loses. Round 2 is the same game but the claims are that each chapter of a book is correct, chapters independent and each about 90% likely on the author's own lights. Both rounds include the auditor rule: whatever set you declare, you're taken to have declared its conjunction as well, and you're scored on that too. Tune the numbers so a single claim is a good bet and a big batch is a bad one — I want the squeeze to bite, not to be arithmetic they can shrug off. No setup lecture; they should hear the names only in the debrief.

Debrief: map what happened onto the three-way squeeze, and then onto why the lottery round feels like a rigged trick — the claims form a partition, so ruling out all but one is equivalent to asserting the last — while the preface round has no partition and no such escape. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my bet is they declare nothing and think they've beaten it) and how to handle that live, and be honest about what the activity can't reproduce: I assume it can't give them any feel for degrees of belief as against flat-out acceptance, but say so if that's wrong.
