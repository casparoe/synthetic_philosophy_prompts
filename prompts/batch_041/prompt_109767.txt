Until recently I worked in compliance in London, where corruption was whatever the rulebook said it was. I have now taken a post as a governance adviser attached to an international development programme, and the question has become a live one for me almost daily. I am asked to adjudicate on payments, favours, gifts and appointments, and the working definition I inherited — in substance, that corruption is the misuse of public position for personal advantage — keeps returning verdicts I do not trust. It makes a cash envelope to a customs officer corrupt but treats a lavish gift to a minister's household as a cultural matter; it counts a nephew placed in a public agency as corruption but calls a departing official's well-paid board seat something else; it has nothing to say about favours traded inside a private firm, where no public office is involved; and it falters altogether when the benefit does not go to the official at all but to her village, her party or her congregation.

So I have stopped asking which of my cases are corrupt and started asking what a theory of corruption would have to accomplish to be worth using. Please give me that specification: the full list of desiderata that any adequate account of corruption must satisfy, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and your view as to which are genuine requirements and which are merely the prejudices of one tradition. I suspect a good deal of the literature writes Western public-administration norms into the definition and then presents the result as universal.

I should like the list to say which cases the account must get right, beyond the ones I have described; which distinctions it must not collapse — corruption as against mere illegality, as against gift-exchange and hospitality, as against patronage that is perfectly legal and openly practised, and the line between a bribe and a tip, a facilitation payment and a fee, a lobbying payment and a campaign donation; and what it must be consistent with, including the awkward fact that one and the same transaction may be corrupt in one jurisdiction and unremarkable in another without the concept thereby becoming merely local. Please also say whether the account must be able to condemn a practice that those who live inside it do not regard as wrong, and whether it must be able to exonerate conduct that an outside body's rules forbid; and what it should make of the official who breaks a rule for admirable ends, or whose supposed principal — the public — appears not to mind.

If some of these requirements cannot be met together — if, for instance, no account can be both faithful to how a practice is understood by its participants and usable as a standard for criticising it — I would far rather be told so plainly than handed a definition that quietly drops one of them. That possibility is the part of the answer I care about most. If one requirement does most of the work in ruling out the usual candidates, I should like to know which, but I am not asking for a scorecard of existing theories.

Please write in British English, and no reading lists or citations — I want the specification argued for, not a survey.
