I've gotten really into pottery this year — got a little kiln in the garage and I've been making these little figures — and it's definately got me hung up on a question I can't shake. When I make a figure out of a lump of clay, and then it dries out and cracks and I mush it back into a lump, is the figure gone? Is the figure even a different thing from the clay to begin with? My sister, who minored in philosophy, lent me this pop-philosophy book about everyday objects, and it had a section on exactly this, and now I want to know if I can trust it.

The book's take, as best I remember it, goes roughly like this. It calls it the problem of material constitution. The puzzle: a lump of clay and a statue made from it are obviously two different things — you can squash the statue and still have all the clay — yet they're made of exactly the same stuff and fill exactly the same space at the same time. The standard solution, the book says, is that the statue and the lump are really just one single object, and 'statue' and 'lump' are two ways of describing it, so the whole puzzle is really about language rather than about what actually exists. On this view there's no fact of the matter about whether the statue survives being squashed — it just depends on which description you prefer. The book pins this standard solution on a philosopher named van Inwagen. The opposing view, which the book calls eliminativism, says that only the fundamental physical particles are truly real and that composite things like statues and lumps don't exist at all. The book closes by saying the whole debate is basically about whether everyday objects like tables and statues are real or just convenient fictions, and that most philosophers these days think the common-sense view wins.
