I keep noticing that the same city will run two price controls with opposite designs, and nobody treats it as a puzzle. On housing, rent regulation characteristically caps increases on sitting tenancies but exempts buildings constructed after a cutoff year, and often lets the rent reset to market when a unit turns over. On labor, the minimum wage applies to every worker regardless of when the job was created; the exemptions that exist are carve-outs for categories of worker -- teenagers, apprentices, tipped staff -- never for categories of job. Reconstruct the theory of the wrong of a price control that this pair of practices presupposes.

Specifically: does the housing design presuppose that the harm of a price ceiling is dynamic and supply-side -- that the thing to avoid is the chilling of new construction, so a ceiling that spares new supply has dodged the wrong -- while the wage floor is treated as a matter of the worker's entitlement, which attaches to the person and so cannot be dodged by exempting new positions? Or is the asymmetry better explained by something more mundane, such as the relative political power of developers versus low-wage workers, or the fact that a house is an asset whose price is a stream of future rents while an hour of labor is consumed as it is sold?

Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is underdetermined -- I expect several quite different theories would license the same statutes, and I want to know which ones the design actually rules out. Then take the analogy seriously in the other direction. If we carried the new-construction logic over to wages, we would get a minimum wage that does not bind on newly created positions: a new-hire exemption. Sub-minimum training wages for young workers are sometimes defended on roughly this logic. Is that a coherent extension of the housing design, or does the analogy fail somewhere -- in what a rent ceiling does to the incentive to build versus what a wage floor does to the incentive to hire, or in the moral status of the two things being priced? I have read enough of the econ-and-philosophy blogosphere to know that people usually defend these two policies with incompatible implicit models and never notice the seam; I would like the seam mapped. Finish by saying what would have to change in either policy if we swapped the presupposed theory for its leading rival.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
