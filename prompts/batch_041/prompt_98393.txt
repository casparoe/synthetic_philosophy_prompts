I have been assembling a family of arguments that share a single form, and I would like to know whether that form has an established name, and if not, what it ought to be called. The form is this: someone wishes to establish that some purported distinction or entity is not real, and the argument proceeds by observing that the description of the world — or of the theory that describes it — is entirely unaffected by the distinction. Exchange the two candidates, shift the whole configuration, or relabel the parts, and nothing in the description changes; from this it is concluded that there is no further fact of the matter to be had.

I keep meeting the same form in places that otherwise have little to do with one another. In the dispute over whether two perfectly similar spheres in an otherwise empty universe are one object or two, the question is pressed by asking what would be different in the world if the two were exchanged, and the sense that nothing would be different is used both to defend and to attack the claim that they are genuinely two. In the older question whether the whole universe could have been displaced in space or time while remaining qualitatively identical, the move recurs. In general relativity there is a puzzle in which the equations appear to allow many distinct assignments of the metric to the same underlying points, differing only by a rearrangement, and this is taken to bear on whether those points have any identity of their own. In quantum mechanics, two particles of the same kind cannot be told apart, and the formalism treats their exchange as no change at all. I can construct a fresh instance as well: take a single featureless, perfectly homogeneous sphere alone in space; rotating it about its centre yields a configuration indistinguishable from the original, and one feels the pull both ways on whether that is a genuinely different state of affairs.

What I cannot pin down is precisely what is being inferred, and how far the inference is licensed. Sometimes the conclusion drawn is modest — the theory does not represent the difference, so the difference is not part of what the theory is about. Sometimes it is immodest — there is no difference in reality whatsoever. And the same premise about symmetry or indiscernibility seems, in different hands, to support the reality of structure rather than the unreality of objects.

So: does this argumentative form have a name? If it does, I want to know exactly what it covers and what it excludes. If it does not, I would like a term and a definition, with the edges marked. In particular, tell me how it stands to the principle of sufficient reason, to verificationist reasoning, to the denial of merely haecceitistic differences, and to the various no-difference principles that circulate in the philosophy of physics, and say which of my instances fall inside the form and which merely resemble it. I am after the carving, not the label; a bare name will not satisfy me, because what I need to know is what is really being claimed, from what premise, and where the inference is sound rather than merely seductive.
