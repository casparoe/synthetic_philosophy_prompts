I teach a 90-minute upper-level undergraduate seminar in metaphysics and ethics, with sixteen students. I want to run a structured deliberation that makes them feel the force of the Scheffler-style afterlife conjecture: the thought that human extinction would be bad not only because of the deaths it involves, but because it would deprive us of a future in which our projects, commitments, and values are still alive. The idea should emerge from what the students do, not from anything I announce at the outset.

Design a runnable activity built around a fictional institution, the Long Now Trust, and a sealed prognosis. The Trust is a small foundation that funds projects whose benefits are deliberately deferred by at least a century. Students act as trustees in four or five small groups. Each group receives a portfolio of future-directed projects, such as a language revival, a seed vault, a slow astronomical survey, a manuscript conservation programme, and a civic archive. Each project carries a schedule of project-value points that accrue only after the original trustees are dead. Groups must allocate a fixed budget, draft a short charter, and write a letter to their successors. Halfway through the session, I distribute the prognosis: a credible report that human civilisation will end within forty years, after the natural deaths of everyone in the room. Groups must then decide whether to continue, revise, or abandon their projects, and justify the decision to the others. One further wrinkle: the prognosis comes in two versions. Some groups learn that a small isolated community will survive for two further generations; others learn that no human beings will survive at all. I will not tell them about the difference until the debrief.

I need the complete design: group roles or structure; materials; the budget and project-value scheme; the timing and staging of the reveal; rules for the final deliberation; and a debrief. The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the afterlife conjecture, distinguishing the badness of the deaths from the badness of losing a future in which our values are still alive. It should also identify the points at which a group could resist the thought that the projects lose their point, and what it would cost to resist there. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle that, and be frank about which feature of the conjecture the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion.

Do not include reading lists, citations, or attributions. You may use a name for a position in the debrief only as a label for something the students have already felt. The activity must stand on its own for a facilitator who cannot search online, and it should require no real money, special technology, or more than ordinary classroom materials.
