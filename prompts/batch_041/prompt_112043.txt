I have no academic training in philosophy or decision theory, but I have been chewing on the absent-minded driver problem on my own for some months and have ended up with a set of opinions about it that I would like audited rather than confirmed.

The setup as I understand it: a driver is heading home along a road with two exits. He cannot tell the exits apart when he reaches one — at each he has exactly the same information, so he cannot know whether he is at the first or the second. Getting home by the second exit is best; crashing past both is in the middle; taking the first exit is worst. He must decide at each exit whether to take it or keep going, and he cannot condition that decision on which exit he is at, because he does not know. The exact numbers do not matter to my question; the structure does.

Here is where I have landed, and I would like to know whether it holds together.

First, the thing to evaluate is a whole plan rather than a single choice. He should settle on one probability of exiting, apply it at both exits, and pick the probability that makes the overall trip go as well as possible. Asking what he should do when he reaches an exit is the wrong question, because at either exit he has the same information and therefore the same reasons.

Second, for problems that unfold in stages, the right method is to work backwards: settle what is best at the last decision point, then at the one before it, and so on. That is how I would approach any multi-stage decision, and I do not see why this case would be an exception.

Third, a rational plan should be one the agent would not want to abandon partway through, given the information he will actually have at the time. A plan that looks good from a distance and bad from close up has something wrong with it.

Fourth, the driver's uncertainty is uncertainty about the world — about which exit he is currently at. He is not uncertain about what he is about to choose. Those are different kinds of uncertainty, and I do not think they should be mixed.

I have a nagging sense that at least one of these is in trouble, but I cannot tell which, and I do not want to be told that it all depends on which decision theory I adopt without being told what it depends on. So: which of the four are genuinely in tension with each other, which only look like they are, and how serious is each real conflict? For each genuine tension, tell me which commitment has to be weakened or dropped, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs me elsewhere — I care about the cost, not just the formal fix. Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and if one revision would clear up the most while costing the least, say which and why.

Finally, if my framing is confused in a way that makes this hard to answer well — if I am using plan and choice in a way that hides the real issue, if one of these four is not a position anyone would actually hold, or if the question as posed cannot be answered without first settling something I have not mentioned — please say so directly rather than working around it. I would rather be told the question is malformed than get a tidy answer to the wrong question.
