I am a philosopher at a small liberal arts college, and I have been asked to give this year's commencement address: eight minutes, outdoors, to a graduating class of about two hundred and their families, with the faculty seated behind me. The dean, who knows my work, asked for something warm and practical. I would like to accept that brief without saying anything I believe to be false.

The difficulty is that the standard register of commencement advice presupposes a ranking I do not think exists. Weigh your options; find the path that is best for you; you'll know when it's right; trust that the pieces will fit together — each of these assumes that a person's alternatives are commensurable, that somewhere beneath the noise there is a fact about which one is better, and that the graduate's job is to discover it. For most of the choices that actually keep my students awake, I do not believe there is such a fact. And the standing alternative, that it doesn't really matter and one may as well just pick, is neither kinder nor truer.

I have come to think the honest thing to say turns on a distinction among three ways a choice can be hard. Some choices are hard because you do not yet know enough: more information would settle them. Some are hard because the two things at stake are not measurable against each other at all — you can no more say that one is better than you can say that a length is better than a weight, and the difficulty lies not in a deficit of knowledge but in the absence of any common scale. And some are hard because the alternatives genuinely are comparable and genuinely are neither better nor worse: there is nothing to discover, and it is the choosing itself that makes one of them the better one for you. My students' hardest choices, I suspect, are mostly of the third kind, sometimes of the second, and they have been trained by every form they have ever filled out to treat all of them as the first.

I want a speech that enacts this difference rather than explains it. Please write it.

It has to land for the whole field. The philosophy majors will recognize the distinctions and will be listening for whether I fudge them; the grandparents will be listening for whether it is kind. So: no technical vocabulary — in particular, none of the terms I have just used — no lecture, no arguing with anybody, no names of thinkers.

Constraints on what the speech may say:

- It may not present any of these hard choices as a puzzle with a hidden answer, and it may not suggest that failing to find the answer means the graduate has not looked hard enough.
- It may not offer the consolation that it doesn't matter, that every road leads somewhere, or that they will end up where they were meant to be.
- It may not advise trusting the gut, following the heart, or waiting for clarity, since each of those is a way of pretending the ruler is there.
- It may not resolve a hard choice on the audience's behalf.
- It must nonetheless give them something to do: an account of what choosing is when there is nothing to find, and of why that is not a demotion.

It should include at least one concrete choice of the kind graduates actually face, handled so that it shows, without labeling, which of the three kinds it is. Invent that example — not the trolley, not the lifeboat, not Sophie's choice, and nothing that requires the audience to have read what I have read. Near the end it should say something about the regret that survives a choice well made, the claim that is not cancelled by the option you did not take, and treat that regret as evidence of seriousness rather than of error. Aim for roughly eleven hundred words.

Then commit. I do not want a speech that keeps its options open or that says some philosophers think this and others that. Choose one account of why these choices are hard, build the speech on it, and let it dictate what can and cannot be said at the podium; where the three kinds pull in different directions, pick the one you think is true and make the speech consistent with it.

Finally, after the speech, add a short note, not to be read aloud: say where the speech leans on a reading of the idea that someone could reasonably reject, state the strongest objection to it, and say whether the speech survives that objection or would have to be rewritten if it did not.
