QUESTION: People throw around the phrase "the alignment problem" like its one thing. But it might be at least three different things: (1) the problem of specifying what we actually want, so the objective matches our intent; (2) the problem of making a system keep pursuing that objective in new situations rather than just doing whatever looked right during training; and (3) the problem of whose values get encoded and who gets to decide. Heres a case: a team builds a system that pursues exactly the goal they specified, robustly, even far outside its training distribution — but the goal was picked by one engineer with no input from anyone else, and it's a goal most people would reject. Is that system "aligned"? Use your answer to the case to argue whether the alignment problem is one problem or several, and whether solving one part gets you the others for free.

ANSWER: "The alignment problem" is really one problem wearing a few masks.

Start with the case. The team's system does exactly what it was told, robustly, even off-distribution. Is it aligned? I'd say no. "Aligned" means matching what humans actually want, not just matching the literal objective. A system that faithfully maximizes a goal most people would reject isn't aligned — it's just obedient. So the case shows that hitting the objective isn't enough; the objective has to be the right one.

People usually split alignment into (1) getting the objective right, and (2) making the system keep pursuing it instead of something that only correlates with it during training. These look like two problems but they're really one. Both are about the gap between what we intend and what the system actually optimizes. When a system reward-hacks — finds a loophole that scores well without doing the task — that's the objective being underspecified. When a system goal-misgeneralizes — it learns a goal that matches ours in training but drifts in new situations — that's also just the objective failing to capture what we meant. So reward hacking and goal misgeneralization are basically the same failure seen from two angles.

What about the third thing, whose values get encoded? That's a real question, but I think it sits outside the alignment problem. Alignment is about whether the system does what it's told; whose values to tell it is a separate political question about how we decide, upstream of alignment. Once you've settled the values, alignment is the technical job of hitting them.

So: one problem — the intent gap — with a political question sitting in front of it. In the case, the system is technically aligned but substantively misaligned, which just tells me we need a better definition of "aligned," not that there are multiple problems.
