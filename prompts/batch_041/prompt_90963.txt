I'm writing a novel set in the 1850s about a woman who takes on a translating job to keep the household afloat while her own beleif quietly comes apart — loosely inspired by what I know about Marian Evans and her Englishing of Feuerbach.

Here's the bit I keep circling, from the preface to the second edition of the book she translated:

"I do not generate the object from the thought, but the thought from the object; and I hold that alone to be an object which has an existence beyond one's own brain. I am nothing but a natural philosopher in the domain of mind."

The first part I get, roughly — it's the anti-Hegelian move, you start from the world and not from your own skull. What I can't get is what he's doing with "natural philosopher in the domain of mind," and whether it's more than a fancy way of saying he's a materialist about religion. Is there a methodological claim in there — that you study religious consciousness the way a naturalist studies a beetle, from the outside, by what people actually say and do rather than by what the doctrine is supposed to mean? And if so, why would someone who's loosly losing her faith find that a relief instead of a cold shower? My character is supposed to feel let out of prison when she finally understands this sentence, and I want to know wether that reaction is psychologically honest or just something I'm putting on her.

So: help me understand the passage, and then tell me what it plausibly did to the person translating it.
