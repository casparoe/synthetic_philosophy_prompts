I teach a final-year seminar that sits awkwardly between political philosophy and AI ethics, and last week a colleague dropped a distinction on us that I still can't shake. We'd been going round the usual question of whose values an aligned system should serve — the individual user's, some aggregate across everyone, or a fixed public set — and she suggested the whole debate quietly imports a picture of neutrality from the state that doesn't survive the move. A state can be neutral because it issues one law and a thousand citizens go off and live differently under it; the neutrality lives in the reasons it offers, not in the outcomes it produces. An assistant that answers a question, by contrast, produces exactly one output per query, and that output *is* an outcome — so the design has to commit to some weighting of values in the very act of being helpful, and neutrality of justification doesn't get you off the hook the way it does for the legislator. I think she's onto something, but I'm not convinced it's fatal, and I'd like the students to argue it out in writing rather than nodding along.

So here's what I want. Write an essay on whether a single aligned AI can be neutral in the liberal sense at all, or whether the one-output-per-query constraint means it must always already be serving somebody's conception of the good. Bring the aggregation side in properly: any system built on a preference model is aggregating, and aggregation is exactly the place where neutrality is usually thought to be achievable procedurally — fair weights, a legitimate rule, no thumb on the scale. And there's one twist I want taken seriously rather than waved at: the preferences being aggregated are partly *produced* by the system itself, since what people come to want is shaped by what they've already been given. If that's right, the ex ante aggregate that was supposed to be neutral is a moving target, and I want to know whether that makes the neutrality ideal self-undermining or merely harder to realise — say which, and why.

To keep it from floating off into the abstract: picture the pilot my faculty is actually considering, a study-and-advice assistant that first-years can ask anything, where one student's family background pulls hard one way on a question about loyalty to relatives and another's pulls the other, and the university has to decide whether the thing conditions on a user profile or holds one public-reason-compatible line. Weigh the strongest case for "personalise to the individual and stop pretending there's a public answer" against the strongest case for the single public stance, then reach a verdict and defend it — I don't want a both-sides-in-the-end essay, I want to know what you'd actually build and why, including what your verdict costs whoever ends up on the losing side. No citations, no reading list, no bibliography — I've read the literature, I want your argument. Please write it in British English (I'm marking for it and my colleague is a stickler about it).
