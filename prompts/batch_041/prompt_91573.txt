For most of a semester I have been defending a view in a reading group on moral psychology and metaethics, and a colleague has finally persuaded me that I should find out what could be said against it. The view is this. Cross-cultural moral variation is real, and sometimes deep, but it is not what it appears to be. Beneath the variation lies a thin, shared core of moral concern — roughly, that suffering and flourishing matter, that persons are owed something simply as persons, and that members of one's group stand in relations of special obligation — and what actually varies from society to society is how that core is specified, weighted, and extended to marginal cases. Nor is the variation random: it tracks local conditions such as subsistence mode, kinship and residence patterns, exposure to threat and to pathogens, and relational mobility. That is why I prefer to describe the divergence as ecological calibration rather than as moral disagreement in the strict sense. If all of this is right, a modest moral objectivity survives the cross-cultural record: some claims are true for everyone, cross-cultural criticism remains possible at the level of the core, and most thick moral claims are true only relative to an ecology. The large cross-cultural survey built around self-driving-car dilemmas seems to me to support precisely this picture, since it found strong global agreement on a few dimensions alongside systematic regional variation on others.

Now attack it. What would count against the view? I would like the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, and drawn from the empirical record wherever that is possible rather than from the generic argument from disagreement, which I already know how to answer. In particular: are there well-documented cases in which a society does not merely weight the core differently but seems to lack one of its elements altogether — where the moral status of out-group members, or of the dead, or of animals, or of children is so different that describing it as a different application of a shared core is strained, and where the difference cannot be explained by ecology or by a difference in factual belief? Which findings should make me suspect that the core is a projection of the investigator's own intuitions — those of the WEIRD sample, Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic — rather than a human universal? And which findings would suggest that the calibration half of the story is doing all the explanatory work, so that objectivity has quietly dropped out and I am left with relativism in empirical dress?

Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and separate the ones a defender of the view would have to concede from the ones a defender could dismiss as question-begging or as trading on the gap between description and evaluation. Finally, tell me honestly whether the position is well insulated: whether it makes a genuine empirical bet that could fail, or whether its two clauses can absorb any datum whatsoever. If it turns out to be insulated, say so plainly, and tell me whether that is a defect in a view of this kind. What would move me most is a single well-documented case of a society that has no use for one element of the core, where the absence is not an artifact of translation, sampling, or factual disagreement but a genuine alternative to the element itself.
