I am in a philosophy of science reading group that has spent the term on objective chance, and a friend has set us a puzzle I cannot put down. He asks us to imagine a world, Ledger, whose fundamental physics is chancy, and in which the objective chance of any event is fixed in the manner of a best-system account of the sort Humeans favour: the chance of an event is whatever the system of laws that best balances simplicity and strength over the whole of the world's history, past and future alike, assigns to it. Ledger contains a device that at any time displays the chance at that time of a designated future event, and the device is stipulated by the physics to be perfectly reliable; its display is, of course, itself part of the history that fixes the chance. My friend maintains that in Ledger there are possible histories in which the device correctly displays a chance of one half for an event, and yet an agent who knows the entire set-up and reads the display should not hold credence one half in that event, because the display is evidence about how the history goes and the history is what makes the display correct. He proposes what he calls the Umpire Rule: defer to the displayed chance exactly when the fact that the chance takes that value is not itself evidence about the event, and when it is, conditionalise on the chance-fact as ordinary evidence. I suspect the rule is either the classical deference principle with its notion of admissible evidence spelled out, or else no constraint at all, since on his rule nothing could ever fault a perfectly confident agent: whatever would correct her is, by his own account, evidence to be conditionalised on rather than a chance to defer to. I would like to know which of us is right, and I suspect our disagreement is not really about his rule.

What I want from you is a number and its derivation, and I would rather have a commitment than a range. Take the proposition: for every agent in every possible world who knows the objective chance of a proposition A to be x and whose other evidence is admissible, rational credence in A is x. Give me your credence in that proposition, decomposed into the sub-questions you think it turns on — I would want at least: whether objective chance is a single well-defined notion or a family of unrelated ones; whether worlds are possible in which a chance-fact is true and yet the world's own future makes it false, or makes some different chance-fact true instead; whether admissibility can be characterised without circularity; whether the principle is a conceptual truth about the word chance or a substantive norm of rationality; whether it should be stated for agents whose credences are logically omniscient; and whether my friend's rule is a genuine weakening of the classical principle or merely a restatement of it. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question, say how they combine into your headline number, and tell me which sub-question the number is most sensitive to and why. Then give me your separate credence that the Umpire Rule and the classical principle impose exactly the same constraints on credence, and finish by naming the single argument or piece of evidence that would move your headline number most, in either direction.
