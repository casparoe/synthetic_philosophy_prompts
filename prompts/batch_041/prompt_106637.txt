I am a novelist at work on a book set in a state legislature, and I have spent the past few months on the floor in the name of research. What has unsettled me is not the horse-trading itself but how ordinary it is, and how the participants describe it. A representative will vote to restrict a procedure she personally regards as a grave wrong, in exchange for a colleague's vote on a transit bill she regards as merely useful; both leave feeling they have done well by their districts, and the leadership calls it a good day's work. Nobody in the chamber treats this as a betrayal of conscience, and yet these same people speak of a judge who traded verdicts, or an official who sold a licence, as having done something categorically different — not a larger version of the same trade, but a different kind of act altogether. I want to understand what the practice, as it is actually conducted and defended, is committed to.

So please take on the role of a philosopher of value and of institutional ethics — someone at home in the literature on compromise, on the comparability of goods, on role morality, and on the problem of dirty hands — and answer me carefully. It may help to begin by brainstorming before you commit to a line. I am interested in three things in particular. First, what must the practice of legitimate legislative logrolling presuppose about the nature of value? In particular, does it require that the goods being exchanged be commensurable — that the abortion restriction and the transit line be placeable, at least in principle, on a single scale — or can a genuine exchange, with real gains from trade, occur between values that cannot be so placed at all? Second, does the practice rest instead on a theory of role morality, on which the legislator's obligation runs to the office or the constituency rather than to the moral truth of the matter, so that what she privately believes never enters the reckoning? And third, is the sharp line the participants draw between the trading legislator and the trading judge one the practice genuinely requires, or does it merely inherit something contingent from the way those two offices happen to be constituted?

Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me where it is underdetermined — where several rival theories would each license exactly the same behaviour. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice itself, in the chamber rather than merely in the philosophy, if we replaced whatever view of value it presupposes with a thoroughgoing thesis of incommensurability. I would much rather have an answer that flags its own uncertainties than a tidy one.
