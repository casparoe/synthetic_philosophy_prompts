I spent the better part of last night arguing with a friend who is finishing a psychiatry residency, and we ended up exactly where we always end up. I take what I understand to be the naturalist line: there is a fact of the matter about whether a part or process is functioning as it should, fixed by biology — by what the heart is for, by the species-typical organisation of the organism — and our talk of disease tracks that fact, however imperfectly we detect it. He takes the normativist line: that calling something a disease is a value judgment, that the boundary of the pathological is drawn by what we take to be bad and worth treating, and that the biology in my position is a laundered version of my preferences about how bodies ought to work. We are not confused about the facts; we agree about what the heart does and about what happens in every case we discuss; and yet we get no closer. His declassification examples — the conditions medicine once called diseases that we now regard as ordinary variation or as identities — do not move me, and my favourite cases of unambiguous malfunction do not move him. He says the unambiguous ones are precisely the ones whose values are uncontroversial, which is what he would predict; I say his examples are cases where medicine was simply wrong about the biology, which is what I would predict. We have both noticed that our quarrel has the shape of a long-running dispute in the literature rather than a private one, so what I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

My own hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that three things are tangled here. The first is an equivocation on "norm": the norm that a function is supposed to supply, in the sense of the species-typical or the design-standard, and the norm of a prescription, in the sense of what ought to be done about a body. Each side hears the other's sense, so the naturalist's claim that function is normative sounds to the normativist like a smuggled ought, while the normativist's claim that disease is a value sounds to the naturalist like a claim that medicine's categories are arbitrary. The second is a difference of question: the naturalist may be asking what makes a disease ascription true, while the normativist is asking what makes a diagnosis warranted — a question about a property against a question about a practice, both conducted under a single word. The third is a genuine question about the reference class. If the fact of the matter really is species-typical functioning, then whether a given trait counts as dysfunctional seems to depend on what we compare it with — the whole species, a local population, the person's own developmental history, the environment they now inhabit — and that choice is not obviously dictated by biology rather than by what we already care about. If the third is doing the work, I would expect this to be a dispute that is in principle decidable but only by a great deal of biology, and to have been mistaken all along for a dispute about values.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, really, and what would count as progress given that answer? I do not want a survey of the positions or a reading list — no citations, I will take your word for it — but a diagnosis supported by what each side actually commits itself to, a verdict on which of my three hypotheses, or what fourth possibility I have not seen, is doing the stalling, and an honest statement of what would move it: a distinction, a piece of biology, a change of question from whether something is a disease to something else, or an argument that this one should be abandoned because there is nothing here to move. And if you conclude that my friend and I are simply asking different questions under one word, tell me what the two questions are, and whether either of them is the one I am actually asking when I say that a failing heart is a diseased heart.
