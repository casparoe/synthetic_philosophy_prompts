Suppose we could put to Charles Darwin a question his own century never framed. Contemporary philosophy of mind contains a family of theories of intentionality — often gathered under the label teleosemantics, or biosemantics — on which an internal state has the content it does not because of what it currently correlates with, nor because of what its bearer would report, but because of the biological function of the mechanism that produces it: a state represents whatever it was selected to indicate. On this view content is fixed by selection history, much as Darwin taught us to treat an organ's function as fixed by what it was selected to do.

Now consider a case I have not seen the theory's defenders forced to confront. A synthetic organism is assembled from scratch in a laboratory by deliberate design rather than by descent with modification. Its internal state reliably tracks some feature of its environment because its engineers built the mechanism to track that feature. The organism has no phylogenetic history; it was designed yesterday. The question is whether its internal state represents that feature in the full-blooded sense, or merely carries information about it.

I want you to predict what Darwin would have said about this case. I do not mean what he would have said had he been briefed on a century of analytic philosophy; I mean the position his actual commitments, as a naturalist who made function a matter of selection, would have led him to, whether or not he would have recognized the question as one worth asking.

In answering, do three things. First, state the verdict you attribute to Darwin and defend the attribution by appeal to the parts of his thought that genuinely support it. Second, give the strongest reason he might have gone the other way — in particular, weigh his insistence on the continuity between artificial and natural selection, and his own willingness to speak of contrivances as having purposes, against the reading you favor. Third, say plainly how confident anyone could reasonably be in such a prediction, and on what that confidence turns.

If you think the exercise is methodologically confused — that pinning a determinate verdict on a man dead since 1882, about a debate whose very vocabulary postdates him, is a category mistake rather than a hard question — then say so directly and explain why, instead of manufacturing a confident answer to satisfy me. A well-argued refusal to play along is more useful to me than a fluent guess.
