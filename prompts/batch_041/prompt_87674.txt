I'm a retired cell biologist — spent 35 years on RNA splicing, never read a word of philosophy until I started auditing courses at the local university two years ago, and now I can't stop. The one that's really gotten its hooks into me is G. E. Moore, the Principia Ethica guy (I think that's the title — the 1903 book anyway), especially where he says the two best things we can even imagine are personal affection and the enjoyment of beautiful things, and the stuff about "organic unities," where the value of a whole isn't just the sum of the values of its parts and can in fact be less than the sum. Meanwhile, my oldest friends' daughter is getting married in six weeks, and they've asked me to give the toast. I said yes before I'd finished the Moore chapter and now I'm stuck, because about half the room will be her parents' crowd — retired teachers, a couple of farmers, lovely people who have never heard of Moore and don't want to — and the other half is her grad school cohort plus two of her old professors, all moral philosophers who will absolutely catch every move I make. I don't want to give a lecture in a tux, but I also don't want to hand them a toast that's just warm noise.

So here's what I'm after: write the toast, about four minutes spoken, and let Moore's ideas actually do the load-bearing work rather than being name-dropped. That means a few things, and I keep tripping over them. First, because of the organic-unity business, I can't do the lazy arithmetic everyone does at weddings — "you're each good, so together you'll be twice as good" — the whole's value isn't the sum, and I gather it isn't automatically greater either, so whatever I say about their union has to be about the *kind* of unity they've made, not a guaranteed surplus. Second, Moore seems to think affection only counts as a great good when it's directed at real qualities of the person, not diffuse goodwill, which is awkward because I genuinely can't stand the standard toast line about how "you two just fit." And third, the naturalistic fallacy — I can't list their shared love of hiking and their good communication and pretend I've shown their marriage is *good*; those are natural facts and I've read enough to know Moore thinks the value doesn't follow from them, so the toast has to affirm the goodness some other way without becoming an empty compliment. There's one more thing I half-remember and may be mangling: something about a beautiful thing nobody ever perceives having almost no value compared to the consciousness of it, which actually seems useful here — maybe the point is that what's precious isn't the two of them existing side by side but the fact that they each *see* the other. If I've got that wrong, just fix it in the toast rather than explaining it to me. Family-friendly, no jargon, a couple of real laughs if you can manage it, and nothing in it that the philosophers would wince at.
