In the reading group I attend, one participant regularly closes off discussion by declaring that if you know something, you are entitled to disregard whatever is urged against it — that once you know, any counterevidence can be set aside as misleading by the very fact of your knowing. I spent forty years as a working scientist, where the operative norm was the reverse, and this strikes me as a recipe for never revising anything; yet I cannot put my finger on the precise step that fails. Please separate the distinct principles that one-liner could be expressing: what the word 'know' is doing in it (bare truth, justification, entitlement to certainty, knowledge that one knows); what 'disregard' amounts to (not entertaining the evidence at all, entertaining but discounting it, refusing to let it change one's view); and how the entitlement is supposed to arise from the knowledge. For each reading, say whether it is a truism, a substantive and defensible claim, or plainly false, and identify which reading makes the position genuinely troubling. Lay the readings out as a menu rather than settling on one, and note which of them would actually license the conversation-stopping use my colleague puts it to. Answer in no more than 400 words.
