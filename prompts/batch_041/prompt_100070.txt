I'm a security engineer, and I spend my days building the consent and privacy plumbing for a mid-size consumer app — the OAuth scopes, the signed consent tokens, the audit trail, the dialogs that pop up when someone toggles data sharing. I know the vocabulary of my trade cold: authentication versus authorization, integrity, provenance, trust boundaries, threat models, capability tokens, revocation lists, replay. My philosophy, on the other hand, is strictly amateur. A colleague on our legal team keeps telling me that a user "validly consented," and I keep noticing that what she means by that and what my system actually attests to are not obviously the same thing. I'd like you to take the philosophical accounts of what makes consent valid — the standard conditions people cite (competence or decision-making capacity, adequate information or understanding, and voluntariness, meaning freedom from coercion), together with the live disputes philosophers have about what each of those conditions really requires — and restate them, one condition at a time, in the vocabulary I actually use. Treat competence as something like authentication of the principal; treat information and the absence of deception as questions about the integrity and completeness of what was presented; treat voluntariness as a claim about the threat model and about who controls the environment in which the token was issued. Be as faithful as you can: don't simply map "competent" onto "authenticated" and declare victory, and don't quietly import my field's assumptions — that validity is a property of a token, that provenance is enough — and then present them as the philosophers' view.

Then give me the residue report, which is really why I'm asking. Which parts of the philosophical debate survive the translation cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in how my systems think? I'm especially interested in the cases where the two senses of validity come apart: a request can be perfectly authenticated, integrity-protected, and unrevoked — my system would call that valid consent — while the philosopher might say the person was coerced, or deceived, or lacked capacity, and so never consented at all. Is that gap a defect in my vocabulary, something about the will or about the moral standing of the person that protocol language simply cannot represent? Or is it a sign that the philosophers are packing into "consent" something that isn't really about consent to begin with? For each piece of residue, tell me which way you'd call it, and be explicit about where the analogies to revocation, scope, or attestation are doing honest work and where they are just a metaphor that flatters my framework. Please answer in American English.
