I teach a 400-level epistemology seminar and I'm writing the final, and I want one essay question on luminosity that can't be answered by reciting the margin-for-error argument. Every year I get the same paper back: definition of luminosity, the margin-for-error move in four sentances, a shrug, and a closing paragraph about how knowledge isn't transparent. They can recite it; they can't tell me what it actually needs. So I want a question that makes them seperate what the argument uses from what it merely mentions.

Here's what I'd like the question built around, and treat these as the working definitions -- they're my own, invented for the exam, so nothing needs to be attributed to anybody and the exam is closed-book anyway, no citations expected. Say a condition is luminous if whenever you're in it you know you're in it. Say it's almost luminous if whenever you're in it you know you're in it, except for the cases sitting within some small distance of the boundary -- call that distance epsilon and leave it vague. And say a condition is band-shaped rather than threshold-shaped when its extension is a stretch of the underlying scale with an edge on both sides, so being comfortable or being in a normal room temperature counts, whereas having fewer than a hundred hairs on your head is threshold-shaped. One more thing I want them to be forced to confront: a couple of students every year insist that the whole argument quietly assumes KK, i.e. that if you know something you know that you know it, and that a defender of luminosity can just give up KK and walk away whistling.

So the question should have three parts. First, reconstruct the margin-for-error argument in a form where it's explicit which premises it actually leans on, and say whether KK is among them. Second, ask whether almost luminous conditions survive the argument, and if they do, whether that's a real victory or whether the escape hatch costs the notion whatever made it worth caring about in the first place. Third, ask whether the argument runs the same way for band-shaped conditions as for threshold-shaped ones, and what the answer tells us about whether the argument is really about vagueness and sorites structure or about margins as such. Aim it at about 50 minutes of writing, so roughly 900 to 1100 words of student prose -- three parts is the right number, don't turn it into a five-part question. Then give me a model answer of 700 to 900 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 points where the marks attach to specific moves rather than to mentioning names, and where for each of the four or five key moves you tell me what a student who has memorized the topic but doesn't get it typically writes instead and how many points that costs them. Make it obvious where the A/B line falls -- I'd much rather have three moves that clearly separate the two than ten little ones. Write all of it in American English, and remember the question has to stand on its own, since they'll only have the assigned chapters in front of them.
