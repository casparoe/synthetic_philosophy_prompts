ok so I'm a novelist, not an academic, so bear with me. I'm about 200 pages into a book that's turned into a long, ugly war of attrition between two people, and I've realized I don't actually know what I'm talking about when it comes to escalation. My research keeps handing me the same three or four stories — the dollar auction, the Concorde, Vietnam, the guy who sits through a film he hates because the ticket was expensive — and the standard line is always that people throw good money after bad because of the sunk cost fallacy, or because they can't bear to admit they were wrong. That gets me nowhere. In the dollar auction itself (you're bidding for a dollar bill, and the top *two* bidders both have to pay, so a bid of 60 cents can cost you 60 cents and get you nothing) nobody is confused about sunk costs, because nobody has spent anything before the first bid. And yet the thing reliably runs away, and each step of the run-away looks defensible from where the bidder is standing, even though getting in was stupid. So before I write another scene, I want the spec, not the story. What does any account of escalation actually have to deliver to be worth a damn?

Give me the list of requirements, each with the case that puts it there. Mine so far, and tell me what I've got wrong or left out:

- it has to explain runaway escalation with no prior investment and no ego on the line (the dollar auction, arms races, waiting in line, litigation nobody wins);
- it has to keep apart cases where the past outlay is genuinely irrelevant (the bad film, the bad meal) from cases where it isn't (a contract with penalties, a war of attrition where the one who blinks loses more, an asset that's worth nothing to anyone but you);
- it has to say what actually stops these things, since they do stop — budgets, a boss, a deadline, someone agreeing to split the loss — and whether the stopping points are features of the game or just exhaustion;
- it has to survive the awkward fact that escalation often pays, or at least that whole institutions are built on doing it deliberately (armies, VC funds, drug pipelines, strikes);
- it has to say whether escalation lives in the person or the position — hand the whole mess to a new manager with no ego invested in it and the theory should predict which way that goes, and mine doesn't.

Two wrinkles from the book that I think matter and that the usual write-ups skate past. First, my escalator isn't spending money, he's spending years, on something with no market price, so the theory can't quietly lean on money being fungible or on there being a going rate for the thing he's chasing. Second, one character is deliberately bad at quitting: he's decided that if he's known as a man who never walks away from a position he's paid for, people will stop testing him. Is that coherent, or is it the same fallacy in a hat? Because if a theory has to accommodate honoring a sunk cost on purpose as a commitment device, it also has to tell me how to tell that apart from the ordinary self-justifying kind, and I suspect you can't have both cleanly.

Also tell me which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the prejudices of one camp. I have a feeling some of them can't all be satisfied at once, and if that's right I'd rather know up front, because that's the actual result. If it's cheap, put the main existing accounts (the self-justification one, the loss-aversion/framing one, the game-theoretic war-of-attrition one, the organizational-politics one) against the list at the very end, but keep that part short — the list is what I need, and I'm using it to decide whether my guy is a fool or a player. Casual is fine, I'm not going to cite you.
