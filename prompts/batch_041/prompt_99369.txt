I write a fortnightly Ideas column for a general-readership science and ideas magazine, and I am building a reader quiz around the old puzzle of choice without preference — the creature that starves between two identical options. (The versions differ: in the oldest surviving one a man who is equally hungry and thirsty stands midway between food and drink; in the medieval commentary the animal is a dog; the donkey is a later joke at the philosopher's expense. None of that belongs in the quiz, and I don't want any of it attributed, named, or cited.) The column's hook is that this ancient curiosity keeps turning up in unglamorous places — an automated system that must break a tie, a lottery for scarce slots, an editor who has to pick one of two equally good headlines — so the quiz should feel like it is about something readers have actually met. My editor's brief is that it be a genuine sorting instrument rather than a personality test: readers should end up classified by what they say about concrete cases, not by the label they would pick for themselves. Write the quiz and its scoring key, in the voice of the column: plain language, no jargon without a gloss, no reading list, no names of philosophers, no citations.

Specification:

Nine questions. Each presents one concrete case and offers exactly three answers, phrased so that a reader can choose without knowing where the answer leads. No question may ask, in effect, whether free will exists or whether the animal should starve, and none may be answerable by recalling a famous example.

Your cases should include at least the following, though you may change the setting and invent the details:

1. The plain case: two options the chooser regards as exactly as good as each other, with no cost to waiting and no clock running.
2. A case in which the options are neither equal nor rankable: the chooser cannot say that either is better, cannot say that they are equally good, and would not give up one for the other without a sweetener thrown in. Treat this as a distinct state from indifference, and make the case carry that distinction for a non-specialist reader.
3. A case in which the chooser has a preference but cannot locate it — she finds that her behaviour reveals a leaning she could not have reported. This is a gap in self-knowledge, not a gap in value, and the quiz must be able to separate it from case 2.
4. A case in which waiting is itself costly, so that deciding arbitrarily acquires a reason the plain case did not supply.
5. A case in which the choice manufactures the preference: whichever option the chooser takes, she will come to prefer it, and there was no fact beforehand about which she would have preferred.
6. A case in which the chooser is deciding on behalf of others who are symmetric with respect to her, and the decision must be justifiable to the one who loses.
7. A case in which two choosers face the same tie and their choices have to mesh — a coordination problem wearing the costume of a tie.

The scoring key must map answer patterns onto at least these positions, each named descriptively rather than attributed to anyone, and must give for each, in one sentence, the case that gives it the most trouble:

- The will cannot move without a preference, so a perfectly rational chooser simply stays put.
- The will can choose regardless, and the capacity to do so without a reason is the clearest exercise of freedom rather than a failure of it.
- No preference is needed, because a chooser may adopt a tie-breaking rule or device in advance, and the rule then supplies the reason.
- No preference is needed, because which option is chosen is beside the point: any option is good enough, and the real task is to stop deliberating.
- Rational choice requires a complete ranking of the options; where the ranking is incomplete, any option that is not worse than another is permissible, and that is the whole story.
- There is never a genuine tie: some fine-grained preference is always present, merely below the threshold of awareness.
- The options are on a par rather than equal, and what the situation calls for is not a reason but a commitment — the chooser has to decide what to make of the choice.
- The puzzle dissolves: preference-free choice is ordinary, ubiquitous and unproblematic, and only the artificial symmetry of the old example made it look otherwise.

Then, in a short section following the key:

(a) Flag any two positions the quiz cannot separate on any of the nine cases, and say why — whether the cases fail to reach the difference or the positions genuinely coincide.

(b) Lay out the underlying distinctions as a small tree. Include at least these spines: whether the puzzle concerns what the will can do, what it is rational to do, or what the chooser knows about herself; and whether the chooser's state is indifference of value, parity, or ignorance. Mark the combinations no position occupies and diagnose each empty cell in a clause or two: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

(c) Close with three questions a reader could put to someone defending one of these positions, each phrased so that answering it would force the holder onto a case rather than a restatement of the view.

Aim for roughly 1,800 words. The register should be that of a well-edited column for non-specialists: curious, exact, and free of throat-clearing.
