My friend and I got into a stupid-long argument last night about whether you can say anything about 'national character' without being a jerk, and I ended up writing this little essay to try to settle it. I'd like you to tear into it — not a bibliography or anything, just: is it actually right, is it interesting, and where does it overreach? Here it is:

Everyone thinks there are two positions on national character: either it's real and you can generalize, or it's a fiction and you can't. Both are wrong, and the real action is in a distinction neither side makes.

The coherent version of "the Germans are orderly" is a statistical claim: sample Germans, measure orderliness, the distribution is shifted relative to some other population. Understood that way it's not incoherent at all — it's a claim about a distribution, and it can be true or false. Call this the distributional reading.

The version people actually mean is different. They mean that being German is *why* the orderliness is there, that there's something shared, an essence or a disposition each German carries around. Call this the essentialist reading. It doesn't follow from the distributional one, and it's almost always false — because what generates a distributional shift is usually institutions, history, incentives, not a shared inner quality. Two people can be pushed the same way by the same cause without sharing anything inner.

Then the kicker: within-group variance almost always swamps between-group variance. Even if the means differ, knowing someone's nationality tells you almost nothing about them as an individual, because most of the spread is *inside* the group. So the distributional claim, even when true, is nearly useless for the thing people want it for, which is sizing up a person.

Second problem, the one that actually bugs me: the reference class problem. Any individual belongs to indefinitely many classes — nationality, region, occupation, generation, family, whatever. There's no fact of the matter about which class is 'the' right one to generalize from. You have to pick, and the pick is a value-laden choice, not a discovery. Choosing nation is a political act dressed up as a descriptive one.

And third: these generalizations can make themselves true. If everyone expects a people to be cold, the coldness gets rewarded and reproduced. The description becomes a cause, and you can't cleanly seperate describing from enforcing.

Ethically I want to say: a distributional generalization is fine as a description, and sometimes as a screening heuristic when you genuinely can't do better, but it's never a justification for how you treat a particular person, because the person in front of you is a sample of one and the distribution doesn't reach them. The essentialist reading is the one that licenses contempt, and it's also the one that's false — which is convenient, since it means the thing that's unjust is also the thing that's unsupported.

So: national character isn't incoherent. It's just that the only coherent reading is boring, the interesting reading is incoherent, and people keep switching between them mid-sentence without noticing.

A few things I actually want you to push on: (1) is the distributional/essentialist distinction really doing the work I think it is, or is it just a fancy way of saying "stereotypes bad"? (2) does the within-group variance point get me where I want, or is it a non sequitur — a claim can be a weak predictor of individuals and still be a legit description of a group, and I might be conflating those. (3) is the reference class problem really value-laden, or can you sometimes just say nation *is* the relevant class for a given question and be done with it? And (5) is any of this interesting, or is it alot of dressing on something everyone already knows? Be harsh — I'd rather find the hole now than in front of my friend.
