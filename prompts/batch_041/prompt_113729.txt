Ok so I'm the copy chief at a mid-size regional daily, and every April we put out a spoof issue (it's called the Piffle, it's a whole thing, last year's lede was "Area Man Reads Terms and Conditions, Feels Nothing"), and this year I want the centerpiece to be my favorite entry in our house style guide — the Belden Rule. Background: in 1971 one of our managing editors got spooked by a libel suit and wrote a rule that we never run a first-person belief sentence in a straight news story. Fine, whatever. But the rule has grown these weird tendrils that nobody on staff can either defend or repeal. We kill "I believe the zoning board took a bribe" — sure. But we also kill "The zoning board took a bribe, and I don't believe it did," which is a sentence that can be perfectly true in a world where the board took a bribe and our reporter just hasn't heard. We also kill "I believe the board took a bribe, and it didn't," which feels like a different animal again. Meanwhile we run "She believes the board took a bribe and it didn't" on page one without a second thought, we printed "I believed the board took a bribe, but it hadn't" in a retrospective last month and nobody blinked, and the sports desk gets to print "I believe this team can go all the way" every Sunday under a nineties-era exception that says that one's an avowal of a commitment, not a report about the inside of somebody's head. The night desk has started calling the whole tangle the Belden Rule, and I've got a filing cabinet of corrections that show it in action.

So: write me the stylebook entry. Voice is a copy chief who is definately a reasonable man and thinks he's being clear-headed about all this, deadpan the whole way through, no winking. Format it the way a real stylebook does — a main rule, numbered sub-rules, three or four "corrected copy" examples in our corrections format (the crossed-out version then the approved one), and a short FAQ at the bottom for stringers, maybe four questions. Around 650-800 words. The jokes have to come from getting the cases right: someone who's actually thought about this should finish the piece feeling it's fair rather than embarrassing, and a reader who's never heard of the phenomenon should still laugh, because the running joke is that the paper has a strict rule about something it can't name. So: no naming any philosopher, no jargon like "pragmatics" or "implicature," and please don't have the copy chief explain the underlying theory — he should misuse the word "contradiction" at least once, in a way the examples quietly refute. The rule should hold up on the cases he cites and then visibly strain on one he doesn't resolve. And stick in a dissenting footnote from a night editor that the entry just leaves hanging there, plus one line where the copy chief admits the sports desk exemption is doing work he can't replicate anywhere else in the paper. If you need a name for the 1971 editor, Belden is fine, he's invented.
