Two friends of mine got into it at a bar last night and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. One said: any set of degrees of belief that violates the probability axioms is irrational, full stop, because if your credences are incoherent there's a set of bets you'll accept that guarentees you lose money no matter how things turn out. The other said that's a shell game: the sure-loss book only exists if you're willing to take each bet on its own terms, and someone with incoherent credences can just say no to the whole package, so all you've really shown is that coherent credences are the ones a bookie who already knows your credences can't exploit — which is a fact about bookies, not about rationality. The first guy said fine, swap money for utility and the arguement goes through anyway, and the point was never that you *will* be exploited, just that your credences don't hang together. The second said that's the tell: if it's only about hanging together, then any coherent assignment is as good as any other — a flat 0.5 on everything is coherent — so coherence can't be what makes a credence rational, and the sure-loss move has quietly assumed you care about money in a straight line, which is exactly the thing the argument was supposed to establish. The first said the sure-loss argument was never meant to tell you which credences to have, only that whatever credences you do have had better be coherent, and that this is a constraint on the means, not the ends. The second said that just restates the problem: if the only constraint is not being exploitable, then someone who doesn't care about being exploited isn't irrational, they're just a different kind of agent. Then they both looked at me. Can you sort out who's right at each step — the sure-loss argument itself, the 'you can just refuse the package' move, the 'any coherent assignment is fine' move, and the 'constraint on means not ends' move — and say what each of them is missing? I'd rather have it move by move than one overall verdict.
