I have been arguing for months with a friend who does condensed-matter theory, and we keep circling the same disagreement. He insists that the laws of nature are real entities that reach down and make each later event happen — that when we say a dropped stone falls because of gravity, we are naming a genuine causal governor. I lean toward the opposite picture: that there are only the local facts arranged in spacetime, and that what we call a law is the most economical summary we can write of that whole mosaic, the axioms of the shortest and most informative system we can devise, with no additional ingredient of necessity layered on top. Mid-argument he offered a hybrid of his own: keep the summary story about what laws are, but drop the usual demand that a good system be both simple and strong, and instead say that the laws are simply whatever description minimizes the length of the total history of the world under a fixed coding scheme, letting predictive power fall out as a byproduct rather than a constraint. He thinks this is the cleanest possible version of my own view; I suspect it collapses into something incoherent, but I have not been able to say exactly why.

What I want from you is not a survey of the literature but the most important implications of taking either of these positions seriously for a specific practical task. I am writing a popular book on the history of physics, and I keep reaching for sentences like "Newton discovered that gravity is a law that governs the planets" or "the second law of thermodynamics dictates that entropy increases." If the summary view is right — in either the standard form or my friend's coding-based variant — which of our ordinary ways of talking about laws survive intact, which have to be abandoned or reinterpreted, and which of those revisions would actually change what a general reader takes away? I am interested in the whole family: governing, dictating, compelling, being "discovered," possessing modal force, licensing counterfactuals, explaining why a particular event happened, and the sense in which a law is "responsible" for the regularity it describes. Rank the implications by how much they matter for the writing, and tell me which ones are merely verbal and which would force a real change in the book's claims.

Finally, tell me how the list shifts if my friend's variant is the correct one rather than the standard summary view — in particular, whether dropping the strength requirement (so that a law need only compress the actual history, and need not predict or support counterfactuals about unactualized possibilities) removes or adds casualties among the locutions above. I care most about the implications that would change how I write; I am content to leave the adjudication of who is right for another day.
