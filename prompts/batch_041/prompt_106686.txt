I retired a couple years ago after 35-odd years as a paleoclimatologist (ice cores, mostly), and I've been filling the free time with archaeology and philosophy of science, which has been humbeling. I want to write a short research proposal and I'd like you to draft it for me. Please don't hand me references or a reading list — I'll track those down myself.

Here's my half-formed idea. In my old field we talk about regime shifts and critical transitions: push a system past a threshold and it reorganizes into a different state, and sometimes it doesn't come back when you reverse the push (hysteresis), sometimes it does. The archaeology and history people talk about "collapse" — the Bronze Age, the Maya, Rapa Nui — and there's a long-running fight about whether those societies actually collapsed or merely changed, decentralized, reorganized. I've come to think a good chunk of that fight is a fight about the concept, and that nobody has said clearly what work the word is doing. The skeptics treat collapse as a moralizing story we impose; the threshold people treat it as a physical fact about a system. Both sides seem to be answering different questions.

So the question I want the proposal to pin down: is collapse a structural/dynamical predicate — roughly, a system's loss of the capacity to reproduce its own structure — or is it ineliminably evaluative, i.e. collapse-for-whom, relative-to-what-baseline? And can we define it so the empirical cases stay decidable without smuggling in a value judgment about what a society is supposed to look like?

My hunch is the productive move is to treat it as a two-part predicate: a structural part (something like a regime shift in a specified variable) plus a normative part (that variable was valued, or the system had a specified function). Then collapse and transformation come apart cleanly, and the interesting cases are exactly the ones where the two parts disagree. But I'm not sure that isn't just relabeling the problem, so I want the proposal to say what would show it's more than relabeling.

Make it a real proposal: state the question so it has a determinate answer; say why the usual framings — collapse-as-myth on one side, doom-and-threshold on the other — leave it open; describe what I'd actually do, month by month; say what a good result looks like and what would count as the project failing; and be honest about whether a one-semester paper can carry this or whether I'm kidding myself. Don't put me in the field, I'm 68 and my knees are done. Keep it to one page, 800 words max, and lead with the question in the first sentence.
