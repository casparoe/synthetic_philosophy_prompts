I want you to write a piece of sustained, deadpan satire. Before you begin, take a few minutes — in a scratchpad, or just carefully in your head — to think through what the procreation asymmetry actually commits its defenders to, where those commitments become awkward or embarrassing, and which of them a bureaucracy could plausibly mistake for ordinary common sense; then write the piece. Assume the role of someone who knows the population-ethics literature well enough to be bored by it and who is also a competent parodist. I have read enough rationalist blogs to be tired of the recycled version of this debate, so the piece should live in the details rather than in the headline.

The deliverable: a public-facing FAQ page — the kind an agency posts when it is legally obliged to explain itself and would rather not — for a fictional government office that administers the procreation asymmetry as licensing policy. Give the office whatever name such an office would plausibly give itself. Do not name, quote, or cite any real philosopher, book, or paper. The voice is a mid-level civil servant who regards the asymmetry as too obvious to require defense and is mildly annoyed that anyone would ask. Around 650–750 words, formatted as numbered questions and answers.

The humor has to come from the asymmetry being administered exactly as its defenders state it. Exaggerate the real view; do not substitute a strawman for it. Specifically, let the FAQ quietly encode:

- the claim that coming into existence can be a harm but cannot be a benefit, so the office maintains an elaborate procedure for suffering and no procedure at all for happiness — and presents the absence of a procedure as an obvious economy rather than a gap;
- the comparison problem: press on what being better off could mean when one of the two options contains no one at all, and let the functionary answer with a confidence that clearly exceeds the available tools;
- the slide between the axiological reading (this state of affairs is bad) and the deontic one (you had a duty), presented as if it were ordinary drafting;
- at least one conditional permit of the form granted if and only if, which reveals the asymmetry's structure without ever announcing it;
- the natalist objection — that the view appears to forbid, or at least never require, bringing anyone into existence — handled as a communications problem rather than a philosophical one;
- one moment where the functionary very nearly sees the whole thing, and files it under see above.

Keep the satire legible to a reader who has never heard the term, while getting the details right enough that someone who has argued about the asymmetry laughs rather than winces. Stay in character throughout: no winking, no aside explaining the joke, and no final paragraph stepping outside the FAQ to tell the reader what they just read.
