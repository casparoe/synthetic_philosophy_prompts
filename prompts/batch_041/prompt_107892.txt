I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy writing on collective responsibility, and I've hit a snag in my own thinking that I'd like help with. In seminar I'm perfectly comfortable saying that the inefficacy objection is too quick — that individual contributions to climate change, to factory farming, and to elections can be wrong even when they make no difference at all. But when I interrogate my gut verdicts on particular cases, they don't line up the way my seminar self insists they should. Here they are, as honestly as I can state them. Driving my aging gasoline car to campus every day for a year: mildly wrong, and I do feel a small amount of guilt, though not enough to reorganize my life. Flying once a year across an ocean to see my parents: not wrong at all, and I would resent being criticized for it. Flying the same distance in a private jet I happened to be offered: clearly wrong, and the wrongness doesn't seem to scale merely with the carbon. Buying and eating a beef burger in a crowded restaurant: wrong, but weakly — the sort of thing I'd defend a friend for doing. Buying the last beef burger of the evening at an almost empty restaurant, where the kitchen would otherwise have thrown the patty out: nearly innocent, even though the animal was raised and killed for it all the same. Voting in a national election where my single vote is one in tens of millions: I feel straightforwardly obligated, and I would think less of a well-informed friend who skipped it, even in a safe state. Adding one drop of dye to a swimming pool when a thousand others are each adding one: I cannot get myself to call it wrong. Being the one whose drop tips the pool past the threshold and turns it visibly green: wrong, and categorically worse than the previous case, not merely a bit worse. Boycotting a company that will never notice my absence: feels meaningful and worth doing, though I can't claim it changes anything. Taking the one empty seat on a repositioning flight that the airline would have flown anyway: I feel nothing at all about this. And declining meat when I am the only vegetarian at a table where the meat has already been ordered: no obligation whatsoever, though the animal is no less dead.

My tentative principle was this: an individual act is wrong only if it is the marginal cause of a harm — that is, only if the harm would not have occurred without it — and otherwise it is at best a matter of character, integrity, or solidarity. That principle seems to get the private jet, the threshold-tipping drop, the leftover patty, and the empty seat, but it also licenses the daily driving, the safe-state abstention, and a general shrug at the crowded restaurant, which I can't accept. A friend of mine puts the point by saying that by the time I act the harm is already overdetermined; another replies that this is just the fallacy of division run backwards, and I can't tell which of them is right. So what I want from you is the principle, or small set of principles, that actually captures the verdicts above. I'm interested in whether a single account can do it — perhaps one that distinguishes acts which trigger a harm from acts which sustain a harmful practice, or that weights each participant's contribution by the share of the harm they are individually answerable for — and if no single account works, which of my verdicts I should give up and why. Please also say what your preferred principle implies about two cases I haven't listed: buying a single cheap item I know was made in a sweatshop, and going vegetarian in a country where, for demographic reasons, my abstention would genuinely reduce production by one animal's worth. I don't need a literature survey or citations; I want the principle stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on the cases above, and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
