I need a decision tree for a graduate seminar handout on the value of information. Build it around a standard result: for an agent who maximizes expected utility, whose options and preferences are the same before and after she learns, and who faces no other agents, free information can never be expected to leave her worse off — the expected value of information is non-negative, since the act she would have chosen anyway remains available to her. Since it is nonetheless plausible that some information is bad for a person, every such claim must either locate the harm outside expected utility or deny one of the result's assumptions. Take as the root the claim that some information is bad for me and that I may be rational to avoid it, and let each branch node be either one of those assumptions (the option set is fixed; preferences are stable, so the same agent chooses at both stages; the chooser maximizes expected utility at both stages, with no regret, anxiety, or weakness of will about having learned; no one else observes what she learns; value is measured ex ante, in her own utility) or a question about where the alleged harm sits (in the outcome, in the epistemic state itself, or in her standing as an agent). Leaves should be named positions — orthodox expected-utility Bayesianism, regret-based and other non-expected-utility accounts, accounts on which information changes the agent or the decision problem, strategic and signaling accounts, the literature on the value of knowledge, and whatever else belongs — each carrying one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Where a branch is empty, say whether the view that would live there is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves the tree cannot separate. Use these as the diagnostic cases: a person who does not want to know her risk for a disease she can neither prevent nor treat; a chess player who would rather not be told his opponent's plan, because knowing it will make him play defensively; a patient who asks his doctor to tell him the result only if it is treatable; and the pair of cases in which a true belief is formed by a lucky guess and by reading the test result, which some hold to differ in value even though both are true. Keep it to a handout — five to seven branch nodes and eight to twelve leaves — and make clear which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it, rather than presenting one side of a live disagreement as the consensus.
