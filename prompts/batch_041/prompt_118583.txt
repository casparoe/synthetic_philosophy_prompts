I missed a seminar last week on incommensurable values, and a colleague who attended sent me her notes, reproduced below. I want to know how much of this I can trust before I build anything on it. She is a careful note-taker and the lecturer is a specialist, so I am not expecting howlers; what worries me is that parts of it sound like the kind of thing that is true in a loose sense and false in a strict one, and I don't know this literature well enough to tell the difference.

Her notes read:

"The lecture was on incommensurable values. Some goods have no common measure, which means you cannot compare them at all, and that is what makes a hard choice hard. It is not the same as uncertainty: with uncertainty you don't know the facts, whereas here the facts are all in and there is still no answer. His example was a woman choosing between staying in her hometown to care for her father through a long illness — she is the only one who can — and taking a two-year post abroad that is her one real chance at the career she trained for. He said the two options were roughly equal in value, not exactly equal, and that this is different again from one being better than the other. Because of this there is no fact of the matter as to which is better, so whatever she chooses is beyond rational criticism: she cannot choose wrongly, and no one can tell her she chose worse. He also mentioned an argument in which you take one of the options and improve it slightly and it still isn't better than the other; he said this proves the same point. He drew the moral that values cannot be objective, since if they were there would have to be a common measure. And he ended with what he called the standing objection to utilitarianism: if some values are incommensurable there can be no single scale, so the theory collapses."

What I would like is an audit rather than an exposition. Go through the notes claim by claim and tell me, for each, what a philosopher working on value theory would accept as stated, what they would wince at as a loose or misleading way of putting something true, and what they would reject outright; where a claim distorts a real position, say what the accurate version is and why the difference matters. Three things in particular I'd like pinned down. First, whether "no common measure" really entails that the two options cannot be compared at all, and how that sits with the lecturer's remark that they are roughly equal rather than exactly equal. Second, what the small-improvement argument actually establishes and whether it supports the conclusion she draws from it. Third, whether anything about objectivity, or about utilitarianism, follows from whichever parts of the notes survive. I don't need citations or a reading list — I need to know which sentences I can safely repeat and how to phrase the ones I can't.
