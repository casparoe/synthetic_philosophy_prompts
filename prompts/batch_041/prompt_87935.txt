The question was:

'Peirce and James both counted themselves pragmatists about truth, yet Peirce went out of his way to distinguish his position from James's. Set out what each takes truth to be, and say whether the difference between them is a real disagreement about the nature of truth or a difference of emphasis. To focus your answer: suppose a belief guides a line of research successfully for twenty years and is then abandoned as false. What does each of them have to say about that belief while it was working?'

The student wrote:

'Both are pragmatists, so both hold that the meaning of a concept lies in its practical consequences and that the test of a belief is the difference it makes to practice. Peirce's version is the pragmatic maxim: to get clear on an idea we must consider what effects it could conceivably have on conduct. For him truth is what the community of inquirers agrees on — he defines it as the opinion that everyone who investigates is fated to end up agreeing to — so a true belief is one that inquiry will not overturn. He later renamed his own view "pragmaticism" precisely because James had taken pragmatism somewhere he didn't want it to go.

James agrees that truth isn't a static property of propositions but something that happens to ideas. For him a belief is true if it's useful, if it pays, if it works for the person holding it. Since different people find different beliefs useful, truth here is relative to the individual believer, which is why James's pragmatism is often called a form of subjectivism. His essay on the will to believe is the clearest statement of this: he argues we're entitled to believe what we need to believe when the evidence can't settle the matter, which is really the same point as his theory of truth.

On the abandoned belief: Peirce would say it was never true, because the community eventually disagreed, so it fails his definition; James would say it was true for as long as it worked, since usefulness is all truth amounts to. So the disagreement isn't merely verbal — they're giving different accounts of what makes a belief true. Peirce's is backward-looking, resting on what inquiry ends up settling, while James's is forward-looking and rests on consequences in experience. Where I'd push back on Peirce is that his fated agreement is doing the work of a correspondence theory, so it's not really about consequences at all.'
