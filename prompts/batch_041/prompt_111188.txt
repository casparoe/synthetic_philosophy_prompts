A research group I advise runs two evaluation regimes side by side. Before a high-stakes release, a model must pass a process review: it has to lay out an inspectable reasoning trace, follow a documented procedure, and clear a checklist, and a model that reaches the correct answer by an undocumented shortcut is recorded as failing even though its outputs were sound. In deployment, the same models are graded only on outcomes — task success and the absence of incidents — and nobody reads the traces. After an incident, the review asks whether the procedure was followed, not what harm came of it. What conception of justification does each half presuppose? Does the process gate treat good reasoning as constituted by the documented steps or merely as indicated by them, and does the outcome half treat outcomes as the thing we ultimately care about or only as a proxy for it? Which rival views does each half quietly rule out, and can a single institution hold both at once, or does the process half end up doing the real justificatory work while the outcome half merely supplies the number? Reconstruct what the practice strictly requires, distinguish that from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is underdetermined. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study AI evaluation, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
