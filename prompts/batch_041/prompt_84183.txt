I wonder whether you could help me untangle a single paragraph in Blackstone that has had me stuck for the better part of a week. I am midway through a seminar on Anglo-American constitutional thought, and my presentation a fortnight from now has to be on the first chapter of the Commentaries — the one on the absolute rights of individuals. Our instructor has warned us twice, with some vehemence, against reading eighteenth-century texts as though they were briefs in a present-day dispute, which may be precisely why I can no longer tell what Blackstone is actually asserting. The paragraph, as it stands in the facsimile of the first edition of 1765 (Book the First, chapter one, page 139), reads: "The fifth and last auxiliary right of the subject, that I shall at present mention, is that of having arms for their defence, suitable to their condition and degree, and such as are allowed by law. Which is also declared by the same statute I W. & M. st. 2. c. 2. and is indeed a public allowance, under due restrictions, of the natural right of resistance and self-preservation, when the sanctions of society and laws are found insufficient to restrain the violence of oppression." What I cannot sort out is what kind of right he takes himself to be describing, and how the parts of the sentence hang together. On one hand he calls it a "natural right of resistance and self-preservation"; on the other he calls it a "public allowance, under due restrictions," and confines the arms in question to those "suitable to their condition and degree, and such as are allowed by law." That sounds as though the legislature is being invited to fix the scope of a right that is supposed to be natural — and natural, moreover, in a way whose whole point seems to be that the subject may resist when law and society fail. It also strikes me as significant that he calls the right "auxiliary" and numbers it fifth: the word suggests an instrument serving something else, which makes me wonder whether the right to arms is meant to be derived from, and so perhaps adjustable in light of, whatever rights it is auxiliary to. I looked up the statute he cites ("I W. & M. st. 2. c. 2.," the 1689 measure ordinarily called the Bill of Rights) and found the clause he must have in mind: "That the subjects which are Protestants may have arms for their defence suitable to their conditions and as allowed by law". The same document, in its list of grievances against the deposed king, complains of his having caused "several good subjects being Protestants to be disarmed at the same time when papists were both armed and employed contrary to law". That discovery has only deepened my confusion, since the clause begins to look less like the announcement of a general right than like a rule about who may be disarmed, tied to religious allegiance and to one's "conditions."

So, four things, in whatever order seems clearest. First, explain in plain prose what work "auxiliary right" is doing in that sentence, and what follows from the right to arms being one of a set of such rights rather than a freestanding one. Second, take a position on the apparent conflict between "natural right" and "public allowance, under due restrictions": is the right recognized and regulated, or created and licensed, and which reading does the wording better support? Third, say what the 1689 background — a grievance about Protestants disarmed while papists were armed — ought to do to our reading of the sentence, and whether it pushes toward a general right or toward a privilege attaching to a class of subjects. Fourth, address the conditional at the end: if the right comes into play only "when the sanctions of society and laws are found insufficient to restrain the violence of oppression," is it dormant the rest of the time, and if so, what does that imply about ordinary legislation restricting weapons within an otherwise functioning legal order?

I would also like you to settle a disagreement between two of my classmates, since I suspect it is really the same question in a sharper form. One holds that because the right is auxiliary — instrumental to security — a legislature that restricts arms in order to reduce violence is working within Blackstone's framework rather than against it; the other holds that a natural right of resistance is precisely a check on the legislature, and so cannot be calibrated by the very power it is meant to check. Tell me which reading the paragraph itself, including its placement in a chapter on absolute rights, better supports, and what in the wording would decide the matter. Please confine yourself to the text I have given you and to what a careful reader could extract from it; I do not need a reading list or citations, and I would much rather have the reasoning than a survey of opinion. Where the passage is genuinely ambiguous, say so plainly, and distinguish those places from the ones where a reader is simply importing a modern question.
