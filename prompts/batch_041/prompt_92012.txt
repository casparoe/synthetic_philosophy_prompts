I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy, working on value theory and rational choice. Next month I will present a paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop, and I want to walk in prepared for the question period rather than improvising my way through it. The audience will be split roughly in half. One half consists of economists and decision theorists, for whom the transitivity of preference is not a substantive thesis to be defended but part of what it means to be rational at all; the other half consists of ethicists who care about value but have little formal training and are instinctively suspicious of importing axioms from economics into ethics. My paper argues that the relation better than is not transitive, and — this is the part I expect to be unpopular on both sides — that the familiar spectrum arguments establish this conclusion more robustly than either camp is willing to admit. My claim is that the intransitivity survives once we set aside the two standard escape routes. It does not depend on vagueness in the relevant predicates, and it does not depend on the items in the sequence being incommensurable, since I stipulate that every adjacent pair is comparable and that one member of each pair is determinately better than the other. I go on to argue that the appropriate response is to abandon transitivity as a constraint on betterness, rather than to restrict the domain of the relation or to declare the problematic sequence ill-formed.

Please draft my question-and-answer preparation sheet. I want the objections I will actually receive from this particular audience, phrased the way each faction would phrase them — the decision theorists reaching for money-pump and Dutch-book considerations, the ethicists reaching for the thought that no sane person would trade across the whole sequence at once — ordered by how much damage they do to my position rather than by how likely they are to come up. For each objection, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Separate the objections that genuinely target my argument from the ones that are really about the unpalatability of my conclusion; I am willing to concede that accepting the conclusion is costly, but I do not want to be argued out of the conclusion by an appeal to its costs. Tell me which one or two points I should concede up front to establish that I am not being cavalier, which I must hold onto, and finish with the single question I am dreading — the one I have not yet found a good answer to.
