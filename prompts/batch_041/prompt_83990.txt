For the past term my epistemology reading group has been stuck on the cluster of problems that travels under the heading of easy knowledge: the suspicion that any theory which lets a belief source confer justification before we are justified in trusting that source will also license a subject to certify that source by using it. We have re-litigated the same two or three stock examples until we can recite one another's moves, and we have finally agreed to do what we should have done months ago and write the document that a proper adversarial collaboration would have produced. There are two camps. The permissive camp — we have been calling them the Bootstrappers — holds that the subject's procedure is a legitimate way of coming to know that her instrument is reliable, and that the appearance of viciousness is an artifact of importing a demand for an independent route that no basic source, including perception and memory, could ever satisfy. The restrictive camp — the Circularists — holds that the procedure is viciously circular and cannot yield knowledge of reliability, and that any theory which says otherwise is refuted by the case. I would like you to draft the joint statement the two of them could put their names to.

Because we are bored of the canonical examples, please frame the whole statement around cases we invented for the occasion. Case A: Nadia, a field ecologist, works with a handheld acoustic rangefinder, the Bat-1, which displays a distance in metres; on each of several hundred nights she reads the display and, on that basis, comes to believe how far away the bat she is tracking is, and she is disposed to accept the conditional that if the display reads 4.2 then the bat is 4.2 metres away; at the end of the season, never having checked the device against a tape measure or any other independent standard, she infers by enumeration that the Bat-1 is reliable. Case B: a small laboratory owns two sensors, Kappa and Lambda, made in the same batch by the same process; with no external standard available, the lab uses each sensor to certify the other and treats their agreement over hundreds of trials as confirming that both are reliable. Case C: everything is as in Case A, except that Nadia has read in the manual that her particular unit came from a production run with a known high defect rate, a fact she never revisits.

The statement should have two parts. First, a numbered section of claims that both camps can sign: about the structure of the inference in each case, about what is and is not at issue, about what the cases do and do not show, and about the cost each side's position incurs. Second, a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement gets a formulation of each side's view in wording that side would accept, the crux it turns on, and the test, case, or argument that both sides agree would bear on that crux, with each side's prediction about how it would come out recorded in advance. I would like the table to include at least the following rows: whether the objection turns on a premise-circularity or a rule-circularity, the former being circularity in the premises relied on and the latter circularity in the inferential rule being relied on; whether the anti-bootstrapper's complaint, if sound, generalizes to memory, testimony, and induction itself; whether Nadia and her counterpart, whose device is a dud but whose evidence at every step is identical, occupy the same epistemic position; and whether the fact that the Bat-1 really is reliable does any work in Nadia's justification or only in the explanation of it. Please say explicitly which portions of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal — for instance, if the two sides turn out to mean different things by justified, or to be answering different questions — and which portion is substantive, and close with what each camp would count as evidence or argument against its own position, and whether there is anything both would accept as actually moving the debate.
