For a couple of decades now the extended mind debate has had the feel of something stuck rather than something unresolved. The proponents hold that when an external resource is reliably coupled to an agent and used the way internal memory is used -- consulted automatically, trusted, endorsed without deliberation -- it is part of the cognitive process, not merely a tool that aids it, so that Otto's notebook stands to Otto's beliefs roughly as Inga's biological memory stands to hers. The critics reply that coupling is not constitution: a process depending on an external resource no more makes the resource cognitive than a calculation depending on a pencil makes the pencil part of the arithmetic, and the proposed criteria prove too much, admitting notebooks, phones, and anything else a person happens to lean on. Both sides seem to accept the same cases under the same descriptions and still not move.

My hypothesis -- and I want it treated as a hypothesis, not a premise -- is that the dispute is not really about Otto or about coupling at all but about the word 'cognitive': one side uses it more or less as a natural-kind term for the processes that occur in brains, the other as a functional-role term defined by what a state does for the system, and once that is fixed each conclusion follows by definition. A third possibility seems at least as plausible: the disagreement is about where the boundary of the cognitive system falls, and the two sides are using different criteria of system individuation -- causal coupling versus functional integration -- so that they can agree on every fact and still be describing different systems.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on who is right. Say what kind of disagreement this actually is, using what each side's arguments do rather than what they say they are doing, and consider whether some hidden question -- empirical, about how cognition is implemented, or formal, about how a mechanism is individuated -- is carrying the weight. Then bring in a case the debate usually passes over: a standing, dispositional resource that is shared with another person, or one that a third party could alter, so that reliability and trust come apart. Does that case favor one diagnosis over another?

Finally, answer what I actually care about: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress -- a distinction, a change of question, an empirical result -- or is this a dispute that should be dissolved rather than settled? Take a definite position and give your reasons rather than laying out the options for me to choose from.
