My ethics reading group keeps going around in circles on the paradox of deontology, and I want to make a handout so we can actually get somewhere. Heres the setup as I understand it. There's a constraint against killing, and the constraint says you mustn't kill one person even when that's the only way to keep five others from being killed. But the whole point of the constraint is supposed to be that killing people is bad -- and here it's telling you to let more of it happen. So the theory looks like it undercuts its own rationale.

Rate each of the following on 0-10 for how well it actually dissolves that, where 10 means the paradox turns out to be an illusion and 0 means it's just the constraint restated in a fancier voice. Then put them in order. I numbered them just to keep track; the order I've written them in is random.

(a) The constraint was never about minimizing killings. It's about you not being the one who does it. You're answerable for what you do in a way you aren't for what you merely fail to prevent.
(b) Same thought, but put as a point about deliberation: what should I do, and which outcome should obtain, are different questions, and constraints answer the first one, so there's no head-on clash for a paradox to be built out of.
(c) Constraints express the equal standing of persons. Expressing something isn't the kind of thing that can be weighed against more of itself, so the arithmetic that generates the paradox never gets off the ground.
(d) Constraints are a long-run commitment device. Agents who refuse to kill even when killing would minimize killings do better overall than agents who redo the math each time.
(e) The constraint is real but has a threshold: at some number of threatened deaths it lapses, and below that number it holds absolutely.
(f) The constraint governs what may be done to a person, and each person has something like a veto that other people's interests can't cash out. The five don't get to spend the one.
(g) The constraint is a device for allocating responsibility rather than for minimizing harm. If you kill one to save five, the one death is now yours, while the five become nobody's in particular; so where the constraint lets more deaths happen, it also makes sure more of them have an owner.
(h) No dissolution, and none needed. Practical reason is irreducibly plural and first-personal; the demand that morality come out looking like one maximizing scale is the thing to give up.

Then tell me which of these are really the same move wearing different hats, and which ones are just slogans that sound like an answer. Also, people in the group keep tossing around the separateness of persons as if it settles the whole thing -- does it? If so, which letter does it collapse into?
