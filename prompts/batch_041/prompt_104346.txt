Descartes is to be brought forward into the present and seconded to a parliamentary committee drafting two instruments: a statute that extends welfare protection to invertebrate animals on the strength of a behavioural and pharmacological battery, and a body of rules assigning legal and moral standing to artificial systems that converse fluently and adapt to novel tasks. Assume that he has been fully briefed on everything that has happened in physiology, ethology, and computing since his own day, and that he retains his core commitments unaltered: substance dualism; the immaterial rational soul as the only seat of thought; animal bodies as machines whose operations require no mind; and the two criteria by which he proposed, in the Discourse on Method, to tell a machine from a thinker — the capacity to use language appropriately across an unbounded range of topics, and the capacity to act appropriately across the full range of situations that arise.

Deliver the following, in continuous prose under the four headings.

1. The invertebrate statute. Its qualifying test has three parts: the animal forgoes something it otherwise pursues in order to avoid a noxious stimulus; it will incur a cost to obtain relief after such a stimulus; and both patterns are abolished or modulated by the same agents that modulate them in mammals. Would Descartes support the statute? Would he treat passing the test as evidence of experience? Give his reasoning in his own mechanist idiom, and say whether the statute's purely functional criteria create any difficulty for the distinction he wished to draw between the brutes and human beings.

2. The artificial systems. The committee is shown a system that passes both of his criteria: it converses appropriately on any topic, and it responds aptly to situations for which nothing prepared it. Would Descartes revise the criteria, deny that they were ever sufficient, or maintain that they were always diagnostic rather than constitutive? If he retreats from the criteria, name the Cartesian consideration he would put in their place — candidates include the indivisibility of the mind, the certainty each thinker has of his own thought, the conceivability of mind apart from body — say which does the load-bearing work, and why the others are weaker. State whether his original argument against machine thought is refuted or merely outflanked.

3. The mechanization of human cognition. Assume that the same explanatory style he applied to animal bodies now covers human reasoning, memory, and decision, so that everything a human being does is in principle produced by the operation of organised matter. He need not concede that dualism is refuted; explain instead whether this development destroys his argument for animal automatism, given that the argument moved from mechanism to absence of mind. Identify the premise the argument requires and say what becomes of the argument when that premise is denied.

4. The reckoning. Identify which of these developments damages his position most, and explain why the most damaging one is not the one usually held against him. Say which of his commitments he could abandon at the least cost, whether the surviving position is stable, and what it slides into if it is not. Where his answer turns on the disputed question whether he allowed animals any sensation at all, state the dispute and give his reply under both readings.

Constraints: no citations, no references to scholarship, no reading lists — the brief above is the whole record. Do not report what is commonly said about Descartes; reconstruct the position from the commitments stated. Mark every point at which you are extrapolating beyond what those commitments determine. He is not to be made into a modern animal-welfare advocate, nor into a proto-materialist; where a recognisably modern conclusion is unavoidable, show the Cartesian route to it. Roughly 800 to 1000 words.
