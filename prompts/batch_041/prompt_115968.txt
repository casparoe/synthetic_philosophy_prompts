I teach in a philosophy department, and a colleague in the sciences has drafted me for a single seventy-five-minute session in a cross-faculty doctoral methods course. The audience is roughly thirty PhD students drawn from molecular biology, health economics, and epidemiology, none of whom has taken a philosophy course since secondary school, and most of whom will arrive believing that inference to the best explanation is either an ornamental name for what they already do in the lab or a soft, pre-Bayesian gesture in the direction of rigour. I have taught the topic to philosophy students often enough, but this room is different: they will not be moved by the mere form of the inference, only by whether it earns its keep, and they will be quietly resistant to the thought that the explanatory virtues — unification, simplicity, consilience, the absence of contrivance — might come apart from the probability that the hypothesis is true. My aim is not to convert them to anything. I want them to leave able to state the inference, to see why comparing candidate explanations is doing work that likelihoods alone do not do, to hold apart the inference as a story about how hypotheses are actually generated from the inference as a rule about which of them to believe, and to feel the pull of the objection that we are only ever choosing the best of the candidates we happen to have hit upon. There is a whiteboard and no projector, so anything I want them to see has to be built in front of them.

I do not want a lecture outline or a handout; I want the teaching plan. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. First, the order in which the components must come — what has to be in place before what, and which later idea collapses if it is introduced too early; I am particularly unsure whether the distinction between an explanation's being attractive and its being likely to be true should arrive before or after they have worked through a case, and whether they need the notion of a rival hypothesis made explicit before or after they have felt the need for it. Second, the single example that should carry the whole session: what it must have and what it must lack, why it should come from outside the audience's own disciplines, and why not the obvious candidates — the diagnostician, the detective, and the celebrated historical episodes that everyone reaches for. Third, the misconceptions to expect and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, including the one I expect to be hardest to dislodge, that the explanatory virtues amount to a list of aesthetic preferences with no evidential standing, and the related one that a formal probabilistic treatment simply supersedes the whole business. Fourth, the one question to put to them at the end that would show whether the inference has landed rather than merely been memorised. Fifth, what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and how to say so without the refusal looking like evasion.
