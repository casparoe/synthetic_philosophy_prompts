I'm in this seminar that's half phil of science and half stats — officially it's called Probability and Evidence but really its 90 minutes of the prof handing you one scrap of info about a single person and going "so what's the probability?" and then telling you which class you were supposed to have used. My friend Priya keeps a spreadsheet of his verdicts (she's like that) and honestly reverse-engineering his rule has turned into the only part of the course I actually care about. So far: a coin we know is fair, he says 1/2, obviously. A 58-year-old man who smokes, has high blood pressure, and whose father had a heart attack at 55 — he says use the class of men with exactly that profile (a registry of about 45,000 of them, ten-year risk 14%), not the class of all 58-year-old men (three million, 9%), even though the big class has a hundred times the data; and his reason is that the 9% is a mush of everything from 4% to 20% depending on which 58-year-old men you mean, so narrowing costs you nothing. Then some guy in the back suggested we also condition on whether he drinks diet soda, since there's a study of 4,000 people where the diet-soda group comes out at 17%, and the prof got a little testy and said the diet soda isn't doing anything, it's a marker for other stuff. And when a different person wanted to use the class "people born in the same hospital as him" (90 of them on record, 33% of them had the outcome) he just laughed and said use the disease class. I feel like I've got the shape of it, but every example he picks is one where the narrow class is also the one with good numbers and the broad class is a mess, so I have no idea what he does when those pull apart.

Here's Thursday's. A 44-year-old woman with a rare subtype of soft-tissue sarcoma. On the table: (a) everyone with that cancer type in her age band, about 250,000 patients, five-year survival 55%, and that number is boringly stable — same in every decade, every region, every registry you slice it from; (b) patients in that same age band and cancer type whose tumour samples happened to be sequenced at one particular large commercial lab, about 90,000 of them, five-year survival 62% — but the lab is just where the sample got mailed, it has nothing to do with her biology or her treatment; (c) the 40 patients on record who carry her specific mutation, five-year survival 80%, except it's 40 people and the confidence interval is enormous. Which number does he give her? What I'd definately find useful: the rule he seems to be running, the other rules that fit every verdict above just as well, which of those the new case actually pulls apart, and the single further case I could put to him that would settle it. And if you have a view on which class is actually the right one, say so — I have to defend something in section Friday and I'd rather not just guess his taste.
