I'm a retired geochemist — thirty-five years at a state survey — and since I quit I've been reading philosophy on my own, and its changed how I think about one thing I can't stop chewing on. Back in 2003 a colleague of mine, Dan, found that the plating plant upriver had been dumping chromium for years and that the county knew and sat on it. He'd signed a confidentiality agreement on that contract; he broke it, got the sampling logs to a reporter, and was fired, sued, and pretty much erased. The numbers turned out right — the state eventually confirmed the plume. Now the survey is finally naming its clean-water lab after him, fifteen years late, and they've asked me to give the dedication speech. Half the room are the old scientists — some of them voted to let him go and still say he should've gone through channels. The other half are his widow and kids, who just want him called a hero.

What I keep circling back to is Kierkegaard's Abraham — the teleological suspension of the ethical, the knight of faith versus the tragic hero like Agamemnon, the way Abraham can't explain himself or be understood, and how from the outside you can't tell him apart from a man who'd just murder his son. Dan broke a promise. Being right about the chromium doesn't touch that; inside the ethical, he did a wrong thing, period. The only way he comes out justified is if the ethical itself was suspended for him, and that's exactly what nobody else can certify — maybe not even something a public ceremony can honor.

So write the speech for me. It has to actually land for the widow and the kids and for the guy in the third row who signed the termination papers. And be straight with me about which parts of this Kierkegaard reading are really settled among people who know the text and which are up for grabs — don't hand me one view like it's the consensus, because I'll be saying it out loud in front of people. No headings, bullets, or tables; just prose, like a letter to me.
