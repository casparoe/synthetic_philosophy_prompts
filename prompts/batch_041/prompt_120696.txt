I'm a retired chemist — spent 35 years on enzyme kinetics, and since I hung up the lab coat three years ago I've been sitting in on the local university's philosophy and economics seminars, which is the most fun I've had in decades and also the most confusing. Here's what I keep bumping into. The moment the conversation turns to rent control, or to raising the minimum wage, or to price gouging after a hurricane, somebody says 'price controls don't work', or 'you can't argue with the law of supply and demand', and everyone nods and the discussion is over. Its the same ring to me as someone saying 'you can't argue with the second law of thermodynamics' — except that when a student in my old lab said that, I could always make them tell me which formulation they meant, what the boundary conditions were, and what a violation would actually look like on a bench. Nobody can do that with this one, and I suspect it's because the sentence is doing five different jobs and nobody notices when it switches.

I don't want you to tell me whether rent control is good. I want the menu. What are the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be standing for, laid out so I can tell them apart? Sorted roughly from the readings that are almost tautologies — true but too weak to win an arguement — through the ones that are real empirical claims with identifiable scope and boundary conditions, to the ones that are false, or not the kind of thing that could be true or false at all.

A few things I've picked up that make me think the readings genuinely come apart, and that I'd like you to say something about:

- A former student of mine is now doing an econ PhD. He tells me the standard model contains a case where a wage floor raises employment instead of cutting it — something about the employer being the only game in town and holding wages down to begin with. If that's right, then at least one reading of the slogan has to be stated so carefully that it excludes that case, or it's just false.

- I've heard of a famous comparison of fast food employment in New Jersey and neighbouring Pennsylvania after New Jersey raised its minimum wage in the early nineties, which supposedly found no drop in employment at all. I have no idea what one comparison is worth, but it seems relevant to which reading is on the table.

- Someone mentioned a theorem to the effect that when there are distortions elsewhere in the economy, fixing one price here needn't make things better overall — that you can make the whole thing worse by getting one market right. If that's true it cuts against the reading where 'works' just means 'the uncontrolled price is the efficient one'.

- And I notice the scope slides around wildly. Sometimes it's about one city's rent law over five years, sometimes about every price control ever attempted anywhere, including wartime ones where the controls came bundled with rationing, subsidies and conscription. Those can't be the same claim.

- Some people, I think, mean something about justice rather than consequences — that the clearing price is what people deserve, or that a shortage is a wrong regardless of whether it's efficient. If that reading is in the mix I want it on the list too, clearly marked as a different kind of animal.

What I'm after: the list, each reading stated precisely enough that I could say what would count against it, with a verdict for each — trivially true, true only under conditions you name, contentious, false, or not a claim at all. Then tell me which reading is actually needed for each of the three uses I keep hearing (killing a minimum wage proposal, killing rent stabilisation, killing a wartime freeze), and whether the needed reading is one of the defensible ones. And if the honest answer is that the sentence does no work and the real argument has to be made some other way, say so — I'd rather know that than get a tidier answer.
