I'm in third year and taking a course on ethics, and we spent two weeks on the Taurek thing — whether five lives really do outweigh one, or whether that's just a number talking. Annoying timing, because next month the lifeboat station is naming a boat after my uncle, and they've asked me to say a few words. Ten years ago he took the boat out in a storm and went for one man in the water instead of a group of five further out. He got the one; the five didn't make it. At the inquiry he kept saying that five wasn't more than one to him, not because he'd read anything, just how it looked from the wheel. Half the town never forgave him. The families of the five will probably be there, and so will the man he pulled out, and my seminar tutor is coming because I wrote my essay on it.

Write the speech, about five minutes. It has to work for the whole room — the crew, the families, people who've never heard of a trolley problem — and it can't pretend his view away or quietly turn him into a utilitarian by the back door. I recieved a lot of advice that I should just say he saved who he could, but that's not what he thought he was doing and I don't want to launder it.

After the speech, add a short note for me, clearly not part of it, on where I'd be overstating things: which claims about what people who work on this actually agree on would be a stretch to say out loud, and where the disagreements really are.
