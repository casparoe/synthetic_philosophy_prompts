I teach a graduate seminar on the history of analytic philosophy, and for several years I have used Frege's political diary — the entries he set down near the end of his life, with their antisemitic and anti-democratic content — as a case study in how a philosopher's extramural commitments relate to his philosophical work. A colleague has now pressed an objection that I find I cannot answer to my own satisfaction, and I would be grateful for your adjudication.

The objection is this: whatever the diary reveals about Frege the man, it cannot bear on Frege the philosopher, because any inference from an author's politics to the merits of his doctrines commits the genetic fallacy. Since I certainly do not wish to hold the absurd view that a philosopher's prejudices make his theorems false, I have drawn what seems to me the only remaining conclusion, namely that the diary is philosophically inert and that the scholars who treat it as significant are merely dressing up the genetic fallacy in hermeneutic clothing.

Their argument, as I understand it, is that the diary's obsessive vocabulary of purity, contamination, and the illicit mixing of realms is continuous with the logic's demand that the logical realm be kept free of psychological and empirical intrusion; on this reading, the same sensibility that produced the diary also produced the conception of logic as the science of the most general laws of truth, so that the politics and the logic are two faces of a single project. But even granting that continuity, it seems to me to establish something about Frege's psychology and nothing about the truth or the content of his logic.

Would you agree that the diary is philosophically irrelevant, and that the genetic fallacy disposes of the case for its relevance? If not, I would like to understand exactly what kind of bearing on a body of philosophical work a document of this sort can legitimately have, and which of the inferences my colleague and I have been exchanging are sound and which are fallacious.
