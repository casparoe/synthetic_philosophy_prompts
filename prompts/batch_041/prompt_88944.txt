Right, so I'm taking this module on philosophy of the social sciences — mostly the measurement-and-comparison end of it — and one of our assignments is to write a short story that dramatises a conceptual problem instead of writing another essay. Sounded like a skive until I actually sat down to do it. Anyway, here's my idea, and I'd like you to just write the story.

The domain we're working in is the "is Sweden more liberal than Norway, or Canada more than the US" sort of question — the one where everyone has an intuition and nobody can say what would settle it. So: two neighbouring countries, both invented, so I don't have to get real facts right. Call them Valdoria and Skerryholm. Same size, same language, split off from each other after some forgotten war, a river between them. A joint commission has been convened over fishing rights, and the treaty text — drafted by someone being cute a century ago — says the dispute goes to arbitration in whichever of the two countries is "the more liberal". So a junior analyst on the commission has to actually answer that question.

I want the story to follow her through the attempts. Opinion surveys, where Valdorians say the more tolerant things. Laws on the books, where Skerryholm has no established church and near-absolute speech protections, while Valdoria legalised same-sex marriage a decade earlier and has the permissive drug laws. Administrative practice, where the surveys and the paperwork quietly disagree with each other. And underneath it all, the two senses of "liberal" sliding past each other — the political-theory sense (individual rights, state neutrality, markets) and the colloquial Anglo-American sense (roughly left-of-centre), plus the fact that neither is a single scale.

I'd like it to end without a clean verdict, but I don't want it to be a cop-out either. By the end the reader should see exactly what further evidence would or wouldn't help — what kind of thing could settle it, if anything could, and whether the commission's real problem is the data or the question.

Two things to avoid: don't make it a Socratic dialogue where two characters just explain philosophy at each other, and don't let it turn into a lecture. There should be actual plot and texture — deadlines, a hard-nosed economist colleague, a translator who's seperate from the whole thing, whatever you like — and at least one scene where she gets close to an answer and then realises it doesn't survive a change in what's being measured.

Then, at the end, add a short note in your own voice, not the narrator's: say how confident you are that the story handles the underlying conceptual issues correctly, and single out the point you're least sure of — the place where you're most likely to be leaning on a contested reading of what "liberal" means, or of what comparability between societies actually requires.
