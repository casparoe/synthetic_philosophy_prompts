I'm drafting a short essay on the frame problem and could use your comments before I go futher with it. Here's the argument in brief. The standard way of posing the frame problem is: when you act, how do you know which facts about the world change and which stay put? I want to argue that's the shallow version and that the real frame problem is about relevance, not updating. Any representation has to pick out some features as the ones that matter and treat everything else as background — I'm calling this a frame wager. The catch is that a frame wager is only good if the regularities it banks on hold across the situations you'll actually meet, and working out which regularities hold is exactly the thing the frame was supposed to hand you in the first place. So the frame problem isn't a missing piece of knowledge you could in principle go and supply; it's the problem of induction wearing a different hat. And the "solution" in modern learning-based systems — just let the model learn its own features — doesn't dissolve it, it just relocates the wager into the inductive biases and the training distribution, which is why these systems look fine right up until the distribution shifts.

Comments please. I care most about (a) whether the reduction to the problem of induction is actually sound or whether I'm just gesturing at a resemblance, and (b) whether I'm being unfair to the idea that a learned representation could genuinely dodge the circularity rather than just hide it. Also tell me whether the "frame wager" label is doing any real work or whether it's just a rebranding of stuff people already say. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasons — no preamble, and don't restate what I said back to me.
