I convene a cross-cultural ethics reading group that has spent the term on envy, admiration, and the social management of success, and I would like to close the final session with a poem rather than a summary. Please write one.

The subject is the collision between the American Dream and the Nordic complex that runs from the Law of Jante through lagom to the tall-poppy instinct. Two things should be kept straight. The Law of Jante is not a statute: it is a satirical invention — ten rules plus a menacing eleventh — put into a 1933 novel by the Danish-Norwegian writer Aksel Sandemose, and since become shorthand for a real attitude, the suspicion directed at anyone who claims to be more than the people around them. And the American Dream should not be flattened into boosterism; it is a claim about what a person is for, and a poem that treats it as mere marketing will miss the argument. What I want the poem to show is that the two codes are answers to one question — who do you think you are — and that the celebration and the cutting-down can be the same sentence in different mouths.

The form should carry that. Write a pantoum: four quatrains in English, the standard interlocking scheme, with the last line of the poem returning to the first. Let two of the recurring lines be the poem's hinges — one an encouragement to rise, spoken in the voice of the Dream, and one a second-person prohibition against rising above the rest, spoken in the voice of Jante. Invent that prohibition yourself; do not quote Sandemose's rules. The point of the pantoum is that a line cannot change its words when it comes back, only its setting, so arrange the quatrains such that by the last one the encouragement is audible as the prohibition, and the prohibition as a kind of grief. Use lagom — the Swedish word for just enough, no more than the occasion requires — exactly once, as the pivot of a line, and do not gloss it.

Give the poem a single speaker and a single scene: someone raised under Jante who left and prospered somewhere that rewards rising, back home for a family occasion. Let one concrete domestic object — a dish left on a step, a certificate, a trophy, whatever fits — recur and turn over in the same way the refrains do. The speaker should not know which code they are speaking in by the end; the uncertainty is the subject, not a flaw to be resolved. Avoid archaic diction, and avoid naming the syndromes as labels; the poem should earn whatever it gestures at.

A reader who grew up under Jante and then lived in America should find it true. A reader who has known neither should still be able to follow it, and should finish it having understood the argument rather than merely the mood.
