For the final portfolio in my rational-choice and welfare-economics course, I have to write a poem that engages one of the central concepts, and I would rather it be an actual poem than an illustrated definition. Please write it to the following brief.

Write a pantoum of at least four quatrains, in the standard form — the second and fourth lines of each stanza become the first and third of the next, and the closing quatrain restores the poem's opening line — spoken in the voice of a person whose choices have been read, by someone else, as a complete and exact account of what they wanted. The two lines the form keeps handing back and forth should be the inference itself: one a settled, confident line to the effect that whatever was taken is what was preferred, the other a quieter line that merely records what happened to be on offer. The form should carry the argument rather than decorate it. The pantoum's compulsion to repeat its own lines, and its return at the end to the line it began with, should enact a theory one cannot get outside of, in which every apparent counterexample is reabsorbed as fresh evidence of preference: the choice made because nothing else was available, the choice made under someone else's pressure, the choice that cost the chooser and benefited another. The poem should let a reader feel that one and the same transcript of choices could have been produced by very different inner lives, and that the account on offer is committed to not caring which. It should not name the theory or its authors and should avoid the vocabulary of the field; someone in the course should recognize the argument the form is making, while someone who has never heard of it should still find it a good poem. A single recurring image to organize it around — a menu, a shelf, a bowl of fruit — would help, provided it stays subordinate to the repetition.
