I am a third-year undergraduate currently taking a course on African philosophy and conceptions of personhood, and I have been asked to give the toast at my older sister's wedding next month. The room will divide sharply in two, and that is the whole problem. My sister is marrying into a family from South Africa; several of her new relatives are steeped in Ubuntu, and two of them in particular — an aunt who has written on the subject and an uncle who is a community elder and has presided over gatherings like this for years — will be listening closely. The rest of the room is our side: our parents' friends, largely secular academics and engineers, who have never encountered the idea and who will hear any strong talk of persons existing through one another as either sentimental or quietly authoritarian. I would like the toast to be built on the relational conception of personhood rather than merely gesturing at it, and I need you to keep several distinctions straight that the course has drilled into me.

First, on this view personhood is not a status one simply has at birth but something one becomes, and even something one can fail to attain or can lose, through the quality of one's relationships and one's standing in a community. My lecturer insisted on a further point that I want you to take seriously: the relevant community is not only the living present but extends to the dead and to the not-yet-born, so a person stands in relation to ancestors and to future generations as well as to neighbours. Second, this creates an obvious trap for a wedding toast. The standard romantic script — two self-sufficient individuals who complete each other and henceforth need no one — is precisely what a relational view denies, since it denies that a person is ever a self-sufficient individual and locates a marriage inside a wider web rather than treating it as a sealed dyad. So the usual sentiment about the couple completing each other is unavailable to me, and I need the speech to find its warmth elsewhere: in the thought that each of them became the person they are through others, and that the marriage is one more relation within that ongoing work. Third, I do not want to overcorrect. The elder relatives would be offended by a speech implying that the couple is merely a node in a collective with no standing of its own, and the secular half of the room will read any thoroughgoing communitarian claim as a threat to the individuals they see standing in front of them. I gather there is a live dispute in the literature between a more radical and a more moderate reading of communal personhood, the moderate one trying to make room for individual responsibility and autonomy within the relational picture; I would like the toast to be consistent with that middle position without turning into a lecture about it.

Please write the toast, of roughly three minutes spoken. It should be such that the aunt who has written on personhood would find nothing loose or overstated in it, the elder uncle would find it recognizably in the tradition rather than a foreign paraphrase of it, and the engineers' table would find it moving rather than obscure. Do not quote anyone and do not cite any works; I have no sources in front of me, and neither will anyone listening.
