I sit on the acquisitions committee of a mid-sized regional art museum — I'm the outside member, the one who isn't in the art world; I cover science and philosophy for a living, so the director figured I'd be useful when the arguments get abstract. Problem is that roughly once a quarter we hit a case where the committee can't even agree on whether the thing in front of us is art, and the arguement eats the whole ninety minutes we set aside for everything else. Two meetings ago it was a birchbark canoe from the 1870s, beautifully made, that a dealer wants to sell us. Last month it was a set of images a local graphic designer made with an AI generator and entered in our open call. Before that it was a piece of driftwood a curator picked up on a beach and put on a plinth; she says it's a sculpture now, the trustee who used to run a bank says it's a log, and a canoe is a boat, and round and round. Meanwhile the whole time we're also quietly voting on whether the thing is any good and whether we can afford to keep it forever, since we almost never deaccession, and those three questions keep getting collapsed into one vague show of hands. So what I want is a procedure we can actually run in the room, with the director signing off afterward but in practice never overruling us: an ordered list of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure can stop with a clean no, and where it has to stop and hand the thing to a person because what's left is values rather than facts. It has to be runnable by a curator, a local painter, a donor's widow and me, none of us with any training in this, in under ten minutes per object.

I know the standard moves roughly — the family-resemblance line that there's no definition to be had at all, the institutional line where the artworld just confers the status by fiat, the historical line where what matters is whether the maker was working in the wake of earlier art and expected to be looked at that way. What I don't have is any of that converted into something a bank president can apply, and I'm not sure the historical version survives contact with an anonymous 19th century canoe maker who may not have had our concept at all, or with a machine that has no intentions to be in the wake of anything. A friend who teaches aesthetics keeps insisting the whole question is malformed and we should stop asking whether a thing is art and ask instead what kind of attention it's answerable to and who's entitled to be the judge of that; tell me whether that's a genuine solution or just a dodge, and build the procedure so it stands up either way, including for the first object of its kind where there's no precedent to lean on.

Then run it in full on all three — the canoe, the AI images, the driftwood — and be blunt about which one it handles worst, and which answer the committee (me included) is going to hate. Skip the citations and the reading list, I can't chase them down and they won't help the five of us on a Thursday night; what I need is the thing in plain words, and honesty about the steps that are just judgment calls dressed up as tests.
