I'm setting the final for my third-year seminar on Davidson — the language side of him, not the action theory. I want one essay question, 60 minutes, and I want it to be the one that actually sorts people out, because last year everyone just wrote out the principle of charity like a prayer and nobody could say why its supposed to do any work.

What the question has to force is the circle: you can't fix what a speaker means without fixing what they believe, and you can't fix what they believe without fixing what they mean, so what precisely is charity meant to be doing that gets the whole thing off the ground? In lectures I've been drawing a distinction between what I've been calling thin charity and thick charity, and I want them to use it. Thin charity: you maximise agreement, i.e. you count the speaker's beliefs as true by your own lights wherever you can. Thick charity: you don't care about agreement at all, you maximise how reasonable the beliefs are given the evidence and circumstances the speaker had — so you'll happily count a great many of their beliefs as false, provided they were sensible to hold them. (I think there's a version of this kicking around in the literature, someone's principle of humanity or something, but I don't want them naming it or citing it — I want them to argue the thing itself.) The question should make them say whether thick charity can do the job thin charity was meant to do, or whether it just pushes the circle back a step, and make them earn the answer on a concrete case where the two principles come apart.

Then for the top end I want a bit at the end about the later Davidson — the essay where he uses Mrs Malaprop-style slips to argue that there isn't really any such thing as a language, just this ad hoc business of working out what someone meant on the day. Does that leave radical interpretation standing as a project, or does it quietly dissolve the problem rather than solve it? A good student should be able to say what the earlier project was even trying to construct once you give up on the idea that speaker and interpreter share a language.

Deliverables: (1) the question itself, worded the way it'd appear on the paper — the three moves should be visible in it without turning it into a checklist of sub-parts; (2) a model answer of about 700 words, written as an answer rather than as an essay plan, and with no quotations at all — students get no marks for quoting him and I don't want the model answer faking a quotation it can't check; (3) a marking scheme out of 25, broken down into the specific moves a strong answer has to make, with the marks attached to each, and for each move a one-liner on the mistake that costs the marks; (4) a short note on where the A/B line falls.

British English spelling throughout, and don't ask them for any reading, sources or citations — it's a closed exam, they've nothing but their heads.
