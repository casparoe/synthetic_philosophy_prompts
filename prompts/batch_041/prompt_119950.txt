For a 75-minute session of an undergraduate political theory seminar (about 20 students, one room), design a game in which the players must build a political organization and win something concrete — a vote, a share of a fixed budget, the right to speak for a constituency — under rules that make Simone Weil's diagnosis of parties true of the players themselves. Weil's case, in her note proposing the general suppression of political parties, is not that parties are corrupt or badly led: she defines a party as a machine for manufacturing collective passion, as an organization constructed so as to bring collective pressure to bear on the thought of each of its members, and as something whose first and in the end only aim is its own unlimited growth, and she concludes that belonging to one forces a person to lie. The activity should let each of those three mechanisms do its work through ordinary play — the need for a single line before acting together, the price of dissent, the need to recruit in order to survive — so that participants feel the pressure as a cost they are incurring rather than as a thesis they are being told, and so that the first person to think for themselves loses something by the rules rather than by anyone's malice. Then run a second round on Weil's own proposed substitute: loose groupings formed around a written review, no binding line, candidates standing in their own name — and let the group discover what that structure can and cannot win. Give me the roles and rules, materials, scoring, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps particular moments onto each of Weil's three claims and her conclusion. Say plainly which of her claims the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to argue for at the end, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and tell me what to do if the group refuses to form the organization at all.
