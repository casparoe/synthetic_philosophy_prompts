ok so I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the "what is life" question and I keep landing in weird places. I want to know if there's actually a principle behind my gut calls, because right now I can't tell if I have a view or just vibes. Here's where I come out on stuff, roughly:

- Flu virus sitting on a doorknob: not alive. Even though it has a genome, mutates, evolves, and is currently ruining my week.
- Prion: not alive, and this one doesn't even feel close. It's a misfolded protein that makes other proteins misfold.
- Viroid (just a naked loop of RNA, no coat, no genes for proteins, wrecks potato crops): not alive. But I'll admit this one makes me uneasy, because structurally it's not that far from the virus in case 1 once you take the coat off.
- Rickettsia, a bacterium that literally cannot replicate outside a host cell: alive. No hesitation, not even a little.
- Mule: alive. Obviously alive. Also completely sterile, can't reproduce at all.
- Tardigrade dried out into a little tun, no measurable metabolism for thirty years: alive the whole time. Not dormant-and-dead, alive.
- Frozen human embryo: honestly I don't know. Gut says not alive, but I can't tell you why the tardigrade is and this isn't.
- Mitochondrion inside one of my cells: not alive. It has its own genome, its own ribosomes, its own division schedule, and it's descended from a free-living bacterium. Still not alive — it's a part of me.
- A free-living bacterium of that same ancestry, sitting in a puddle: alive. So the verdict flips based on where it's sitting?? That bugs me.
- HeLa cells: alive. They're cells, they've been dividing for decades, they've outlived the woman they came from and are arguably on their own evolutionary track now. Though maybe what's alive there is the cells and not any organism.
- A self-replicating computer worm that mutates and gets selected for: not alive. But it does the evolution thing better than the mule does.
- Fire: not alive. Boring, everyone agrees, moving on.
- An RNA molecule in a test tube that's been evolving for years and has shrunk to a tiny stub that does nothing but copy itself: not alive.

The only principle I've managed to write down is something like "it's alive iff it's part of a lineage that maintains itself far from equilibrium and can be shaped by natural selection," which gets the virus and the fire right and then falls apart on the tardigrade, and also seems to let the worm in.

So: is there one account that gets all of these, or close? If it has to be two — a definition plus some separate story about why I keep wanting to rule out the embryo and the mitochondrion — say what the second part is. Tell me which verdict I have to give up, and don't just tell me my intuitions are confused, tell me which one to drop and why that one. Then say what your principle predicts for some cases I didn't list, in particular: a lab-made lipid vesicle with a self-replicating RNA inside it that grows, splits, and evolves but never makes a single protein; whatever the current minimal synthetic cell is; something living on another planet with completely different chemistry; and the biosphere as a whole. And if the honest answer is that "life" isn't a natural kind and I'm just doing stipulative bookkeeping, fine, but then tell me what the bookkeeping is for.
