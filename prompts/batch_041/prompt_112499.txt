I'm teaching a seminar on early modern astrology and I want to use Kepler's own Somnium as the set piece — read it through Kepler's own reformist astrology rather than the textbook kind. The bits I want kept in view: Duracotus, the Icelandic boy, and his mother Fiolxhilde, who sells charms and can summon daemons; the daemons carry travelers to Levania, the moon, on a trip with cold, damp sponges for breathing, and a point partway where they have to slow you down; the moon's two hemispheres, Subvolva and Privolva, whose names turn on whether they ever see Volva; the whole thing is a dream Kepler wakes from mid-description; and he added explanatory notes years later, around the time his own mother was prosecuted for witchcraft.

The astrology to test it against: Kepler thought astrology needed reforming. He kept casting charts and reading aspects, but he wanted the effects to run through the soul's response to harmonic proportions — the same ratios that show up in music and in the varying angular speeds of the planets — not through planets pushing on bodies; he thought configurations incline rather than compel; and he was scornful of popular predictive astrology with its houses and signs, even while insisting that astronomy is the wise mother and astrology the foolish daughter who nevertheless gave birth to her.

So: does Somnium dramatize the theory's causal story, or paper over its gap? Kepler's astrology needs some account of how a geometrical ratio in the sky reaches a soul, and in the story that job is done by daemons — is that just the occult placeholder the reformed theory promised to get rid of, and does the fiction know it? Then the mother: she's the astrologer-charmer sitting right at the edge of witchcraft, and the witch-trial material is real. Does the story treat her craft as continuous with what Kepler does, or does it split the wise astrology from the foolish one by fiat? And the Copernican vantage — if you're watching the sky from Levania, are the angles that define an aspect even the same angles? Does the fiction quietly expose a geocentric measuring practice still riding along inside a heliocentric system?

I want a reading thats fair to the story as a story and doesn't just turn it into a diagram of the theory. Where does it resist the astrological reading, and what does it show about the tension in Kepler's position that his arguments dont?
