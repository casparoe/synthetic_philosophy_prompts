I chair the Civic Honors Board of a mid-sized river city, and next Thursday we vote on whether to rename the central library after a former mayor, Ada Fenwick. Fenwick is, on the public record, the best mayor we ever had: she rebuilt the riverfront, desegregated the fire department, and balanced the budget in a recession, and a local foundation has offered to pay for the renovation if we attach her name to it. But a graduate student has just published a dissertation chapter showing that during the 1974 outbreak of a waterborne disease, Fenwick authorized the health department to announce a death toll roughly half the true one and to deny that the contamination came from the municipal water supply, because the state epidemiologist warned her that a general panic would overwhelm the hospitals and cause a second, larger catastrophe. The deception held for eleven days, until the outbreak was contained; at least forty people who believed the official reassurances kept drinking the water and died, and the true source was not disclosed for twenty years. Fenwick never admitted the lie. Her private papers, which the student also found, show that she regarded it as a necessary but regrettable instrument, and she seems to have felt no guilt worth recording. The board's charter says honors go to those whose public service reflects the city's highest ideals, and I have to circulate the opinion that will frame the vote. The council can overturn us, but only if we look indecisive, so the opinion needs a bottom line I can defend.

I have been reading about the problem of dirty hands, and my colleagues are split in a way I find philosophically interesting rather than merely political. One faction says a deliberate wrong, however necessary, disqualifies a person from civic honor, because the city would be teaching that ends justify means. Another says that if the deception really did avert a greater catastrophe, she did what the office required, and refusing to honor her is a self-righteous refusal to acknowledge the tragic dimension of governing. A third colleague proposes a distinction I want the memo to test: that the honor should turn not on whether the act was wrong but on whether she owned it—whether she carried the guilt, at least privately, or treated the lie as a mere instrument. I was talking this over with a friend who teaches political theory, and she said the interesting question is not whether Fenwick was justified but whether a city can honor someone it cannot fully describe. So I need you to adjudicate, not just survey: (i) was Fenwick's deception a wrong that remains wrong even if it was the right thing to do, or was it fully justified and therefore not a wrong at all? (ii) does the answer to (i) settle whether she should be honored, or is the honor a separate expressive question about what the city publicly affirms? (iii) does her failure to confess—and the fact that the act had to remain secret—bear on the decision, and if so, how? (iv) what, if anything, would a plaque or resolution have to say for the honor to be honest?

Draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend one of three courses—proceed with the naming, refuse it, or proceed with an explicit acknowledgment of the wrong—and it should say in substance what the acknowledgment would have to contain if that is the recommendation. It must give the reasoning and treat the strongest argument on the losing side honestly, not as a straw man. End with a short set of criteria the board could reuse for future cases, including one criterion that would have decided this case the other way. Keep it under 800 words, and put the bottom line in the first two sentences.
