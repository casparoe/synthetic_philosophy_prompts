so I host this little podcast out of my garage about decision making and why everyone is so bad at it, and my guest next month is a philosopher who works on bounded rationality and satisficing. His thing — and we've gone a few rounds about this over dinner — is that satisficing isn't the cheap fallback you settle for when you can't afford to maximize. He thinks it's the other way around: satisficing is the basic notion and maximizing is just the special case where you set the bar at the very top. On his picture every real choice is made against a threshold, an aspiration level, and that level drifts up and down depending on how things have been going lately. He also likes to say that needing a complete ranking of all your options is a fiction invented by people who have never had to pick a health insurance plan.

Give me eight to ten interview questions that actually dig in, plus a follow-up for each one keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I don't want the what-is-satisficing softball stuff, I definately want the questions that make him work. At a minimum I need one each on:

- where the threshold comes from. Picking your aspiration level looks like a choice you could get right or wrong, so is he secretly maximizing one level up, or is the theory just incomplete?
- the re-description worry. A friend of mine who does econ says you can take any satisficing pattern and call it maximizing under a utility function that has the cost of thinking baked in, so what does his view predict that plain old maximizing with a weird utility function doesn't?
- the stopping problem. If searching one more time is always possible and always has some chance of turning up something better, when is it rational to stop? Does he have a principled stopping rule, or is it just fatigue and deadlines wearing a fancy hat?
- big world versus small world. The point I always took from Simon is that in a real mess you can't even list the options, which is different from the options just being expensive to compute. Does satisficing answer the first problem, the second one, or is he running the two together?
- the drifting aspiration level. If the bar rises with success and falls with failure, doesn't that ratchet you back toward maximizing eventually? And can you game yourself by lowering the bar on purpose — is that rational or just giving up?
- incommensurable options. If two things both clear the bar and neither is better, is he happy saying just pick either one? Is that a feature, or does it mean the theory goes quiet exactly when it matters most?
- the local versus global thing. Seems like you satisfice on the big dimension and then maximize inside the survivors — first apartment that is good enough, then cheapest among those. So is satisficing a whole theory of rational choice or just one move you make once?
- normative versus descriptive. Is this a claim about how we should choose or about how we in fact do? And if it is mostly the second, does the first half of the view survive?

Then end with one question about what would actually count as evidence against his view — what would he have to see to give it up — and tell me which of these hes most likely to dodge and how I should push when he does.

No need for citations or a reading list, I'm not going to read them anyway. And write it in American English please.
