I witnessed an extended argument between two colleagues at my company and would like an outside adjudication, because I genuinely could not tell who had the better of it and I have no stake in either side.

The occasion was the company's recent introduction of an internal points currency. Employees earn "credits" for taking unpopular shifts, covering holidays, and referring hires, and can spend them on extra vacation days, preferred parking, and first pick of desks. The scheme is run entirely in-house, the exchange rate against the local currency is set by management, and the credits disappear when an employee leaves.

(1) My colleague A opened by saying the credits are plainly not money. You cannot pay rent with them, they evaporate if you resign, and management can reprice or abolish the entire scheme by fiat. A token, he said, is not a currency.

(2) My colleague B replied that by that standard the dollar is a token as well. A dollar is worth something only because enough people treat it as worth something; if everyone stopped accepting dollars tomorrow, they would be decorative paper. The difference between the credits and the dollar, she said, is scale, not kind.

(3) A answered that scale is not the only difference. The dollar is backed by a state that can demand taxes in it and by courts that will enforce debts denominated in it, whereas the credits are backed by nothing but a company's continued goodwill, which can vanish in a bankruptcy or a change of management. One of these is enforceable, he said, and the other is merely a promise.

(4) B responded that enforceability is itself a social achievement — it is just a larger number of people agreeing, together with the threat of force — and noted that currencies collapse all the time. She added that the credits are already doing the work money does: people are trading shifts to accumulate them, hoarding them, and growing anxious about their balances. Once a thing organizes behavior that way, she said, calling it "not real" is just snobbery about who is permitted to issue money.

(5) A came back with two points. First, he accused her of conflating whether something has real effects with whether it is the kind of thing it claims to be: a placebo has real effects, and that does not make it medicine. Second, he observed that we readily call race and gender socially constructed precisely in order to insist that they could be otherwise, and that no one proposes abolishing money — which suggests, he said, that we do not actually believe money is constructed in that sense.

(6) B replied that the placebo example helps her rather than him, since a placebo produces its effects precisely because people treat it as a treatment, which is the very structure she is claiming for money. She added that the revisability of a category is not evidence that it was never real; it is simply what it means for something to be socially real rather than natural — and that this is exactly the point A keeps denying.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating, stating explicitly which distinction the move turns on and whether that distinction is being applied correctly. Then say what both parties are missing — in particular, whether "real versus fake" is even the right frame for the question they are arguing about, and what a more precise version of their question would be. Finally, give an overall verdict on who has the better of the argument, and why.

Write your answer in American English.
