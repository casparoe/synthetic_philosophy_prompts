I'm taking a graduate seminar on dispositions this term, and a classmate has spent the past two weeks defending an argument that I keep wanting to reject but cannot quite dismantle. He begins from three cases he invented. The first is a tablet pressed from precisely the same compound as an ordinary soluble tablet, but engineered so that the instant it touches water it extrudes a film that seals its surface; it will never dissolve, however long it is left. The second is a wire drawn from an excellent conductor, fitted with a device that strips its conductivity the very moment it is touched; touch it and it will not conduct, though left alone it conducts perfectly well. The third is a pebble of a thoroughly insoluble mineral, encased in a shell that disintegrates on contact with water and releases an acid that consumes the pebble; it dissolves every time it is dropped in. He groups the first two under one heading (an interferer that prevents the manifestation, whether by blocking it or by removing the disposition at the critical moment) and the third under another (an interferer that manufactures the manifestation in something that lacks the disposition), and he insists that the tablet is nonetheless soluble, the wire nonetheless conductive, and the pebble nonetheless insoluble. He adds that cases of this shape can be manufactured for any disposition whatever. From there he argues as follows.

(1) An object x has a disposition D, whose characteristic manifestation is M under stimulus condition S, if and only if x would M if S were to occur.
(2) For every disposition D there is a possible case in which x has D and an interfering arrangement ensures that x does not M when S occurs, and a possible case in which x lacks D and an arrangement ensures that x does M when S occurs.
(3) In the first sort of case, "if S were to occur, x would M" is false; in the second, it is true.
(4) Hence there are cases in which x has D while the counterfactual fails, and cases in which x lacks D while the counterfactual holds.
(5) So the biconditional in (1) fails in both directions, and the conditional analysis of dispositions is mistaken.
(6) The only way to save the analysis is to build an exception clause into the antecedent: x has D if and only if, were S to occur and nothing to interfere, x would M.
(7) But "nothing interferes" can mean only "nothing prevents D from being exercised" — that is, "D is neither blocked nor removed" — and to say that is already to say that x has D.
(8) So the amended analysis presupposes the very thing it is meant to explain; being circular, it explains nothing.
(9) Therefore no conditional analysis of disposition ascriptions can be correct.
(10) A disposition ascription can be determinately true only if it can be analyzed; there are no brute, unanalyzable dispositions.
(11) Therefore no disposition ascription is determinately true, and there are no dispositions — only categorical structure, with our talk of what things are "disposed" to do a projection laid over it.

He is confident that (7) is where an opponent must dig in, but I am not so sure, and I would rather not surrender (2), since his three cases strike me as correct, nor accept (11). Which single step should I press on his behalf, why did it look innocent enough to pass unchallenged, and what does rejecting it cost? For each of the steps adjacent to the one you choose, tell me what a defender would have to say in order to keep the rest of the argument standing without (11). I would also like to know whether the general claim in (2) can really be had as cheaply as he assumes, and whether he has quietly been trading on one reading of "would" in one place and a different reading in another.
