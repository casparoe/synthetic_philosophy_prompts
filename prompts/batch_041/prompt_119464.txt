I have never studied philosophy formally, but I have been thinking about Newton's bucket for some months now, and I would like you to write a poem about it. Please take the role of a poet who also knows this debate well enough to keep the poem honest, and think carefully before you begin: get the physical situation straight, then draft the refrains, then check the constraint below, and only afterwards write the verses.

Here is the situation as I have assembled it, so that we are working from the same picture. A bucket of water hangs from a twisted cord. When the bucket first spins and the water has not yet been dragged along, the surface stays flat, even though water and bucket are in their greatest relative motion. Later, when the water has been caught up and is turning with the bucket, so that it is perfectly still relative to the pail, the surface has climbed the walls into a bowl. So the bowl in the water does not track the water's motion relative to the bucket. Newton takes this to show that the water is genuinely turning, full stop, and he adds a second case: two globes joined by a cord, alone in an otherwise empty universe, where the tension in the cord is supposed to reveal that they are revolving about their common centre even though there is nothing left for them to revolve against. The other side answers that the bowl is real but the "full stop" is not: what the water is really doing is turning against the whole sky, and if the stars were removed the bowl should flatten. I understand a bishop raised something like this before Mach did, and that the star-side version is usually credited to Mach, though I may have both of those wrong, so do not lean on the names.

I want the poem in the voice of the water itself, not of a commentator. The formal constraint is this: exactly five numbered stanzas; every stanza ends with the same refrain; each stanza's refrain is one word shorter than the refrain of the stanza before it; the first stanza's refrain is exactly five words, so that the refrain of the fifth stanza is a single word. That last surviving word must be "still" — I want it because it means both "not moving" and "even so", and I think that is the entire disagreement compressed into one syllable. Let the physical detail arrive through what the water undergoes rather than through explanation: the flat beginning, the slow dragging, the moment of being at rest inside the bucket and yet climbing, the suspicion that it is climbing against the farthest stars, the suspicion that the stars have nothing to do with it. Somewhere the poem must make room for the two globes and their taut cord, since that is where the two sides are supposed to part company. Leave the question open at the end; do not let the poem decide whether the bowl is the water's own motion or a conversation with the sky. Accuracy matters more than prettiness here: a reader who knows this debate should find their own position recognisable in the poem and should not wince at the physics. Do not use quotation marks and do not attribute lines to anyone.

Give me only the poem, as exactly five numbered stanzas of two to four lines each, with no title, no epigraph, and nothing written before or after it.
