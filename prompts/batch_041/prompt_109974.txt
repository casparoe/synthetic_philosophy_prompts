Hey, I'm running next month's session at my reading group and I need a plan, not a lecture. Its nine people, mostly non-philosophers (two economists, a statistician, three software folks, a retired judge, and my neighbor who just likes arguing), fifty minutes, one whiteboard. Topic: counterfactual mugging, plus the blackmail case that's supposed to be the same structure with the sci-fi stripped off.

Quick version of both, in case you're thinking of something else. Mugging: a superintelligence flips a coin before you're born, or whenever, and decides its move by predicting whether you'd pay if asked. Heads, it will ask you for $100. Tails, it won't ask you for anything — but it gives you $10,000 if and only if it predicted you'd pay. You've just been asked. Do you pay? Blackmail: same shape, no superintelligence, just a person — a letter turns up explaining that in the other branch of the coin flip you'd have been getting the money instead of getting shaken down, and that whoever set this up knew in advance what you'd do. Pay up or they publish.

Don't explain the problem to me. Plan how I should teach it. Specifically:

1. The order the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what. My gut says people can't even feel the force of the question until something else is already in place, and I want to know what that something is and why it has to come first.

2. The one case that carries the whole hour, and what it has to have and has to lack. I had an idea and I want you to tell me if it's right or if I should just use the standard version. Mine: cut the superintelligence entirely, make the predictor an ordinary earlier measurement — a researcher scans my brain a week beforehand, writes down whether I'd pay, then flips the coin. Heads, I get asked for the $100. Tails, nobody asks me for anything, but whether I get the $10,000 depends on what the scan said. My reasoning is that the sci-fi framing lets people wave the whole thing away as impossible, and a mundane measurement doesn't. But I'm worried I lose the thing that makes it bite. Tell me honestly.

3. The misconceptions, in the order they'll actually show up, and exactly where in the sequence I head each one off. My guesses: "you can't change the past, so paying is pointless"; "this is Pascal's wager with extra steps"; "it only works if the predictor is perfect" (and here I want to know how much of the hour the reliability question deserves, and whether the answer slides smoothly or flips as accuracy drops); "this is just extortion, and rewarding extortion is bad for everyone"; "so which one are you, causal or evidential" — the labels showing up before anyone can state what the disagreement is about; and "expected value is expected value, you compute it from what you know." That last one feels like the actual crux to me and I want to know if I'm right about that or if I'm the one with the misconception.

4. One question to ask at the end that tells me whether they got it, as opposed to whether they can recite my summary back at me.

5. What to cut. I have a bad habit of trying to cram the whole decision-theory zoo into fifty minutes. I'd rather name what I'm leaveing out and say so out loud than rush it. Tell me what's genuinely not needed at this level.

One more thing: I want the blackmail half to do real work. My sense is the whole reason to teach the two together is that they're the same structure but the intuitions go opposite ways, and if I just announce "look, same structure" I've given away the punchline and wasted the case. So how should the two halves be sequenced so they feel the flip themselves?

Answer in American English please.
