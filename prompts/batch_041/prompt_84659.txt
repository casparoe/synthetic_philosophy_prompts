Argue for the following position: the lesson of Bertrand's paradox is not that the principle of indifference is incoherent, but that indifference is a relation between an agent and a class of generative procedures, not a relation between an agent and a set of possible outcomes. When a chord of a circle is said to be chosen at random, nothing determinate has yet been said. Once we specify the procedure — two points drawn uniformly and independently on the circumference, or a radius drawn uniformly together with a point drawn uniformly along it, or a point drawn uniformly in the disk and used as the chord's midpoint — each procedure induces a well-defined distribution over chords and a different answer (one third, one half, one quarter) to the question whether the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. The paradox is generated by the pretense that randomness names a single procedure, or that indifference over one parameterization must agree with indifference over another parameterization related to it by a transformation we have no independent reason to treat as irrelevant.

Defend the stronger claim that follows: that indifference, relativized in this way, remains a genuine principle of inference rather than a mere consistency constraint on a family of credences — that once a procedure class is fixed, indifference within it is forced, and that this yields a determinate answer in cases where classical indifference was silent or ambiguous. Then take seriously the objection that this is a verbal rescue: that by making the procedure class part of the input you have renamed the problem rather than solved it, since the cases that matter — a coin of unknown bias, a manufactured cube whose side length is unknown, a hypothesis about the world — arrive without a procedure class attached, and indifference over procedures is then either empty or a covert way of smuggling in an arbitrary parameterization.

Finally, give an honest accounting of the cost. Say which classical applications of indifference the view preserves (for instance, a uniform prior over a finite set of hypotheses that the agent regards as perfectly symmetrical) and which it loses (a determinate prior over an unknown continuous quantity, or the style of reasoning that drives the two-envelope problem and the doomsday argument). Where the view loses an application, say whether the loss indicts the view or indicts the application.
