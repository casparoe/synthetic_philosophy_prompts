I'm putting together a short piece for my department's decision-theory reading group on Good's theorem — the claim that a Bayesian with a fixed decision problem can never be made worse off in expectation by free information. Draft below. I mainly want comments on whether I've got the theorem right and whether my alleged counterexamples are really counterexamples.

The theorem: V(X) = E_X[max_a E(U | a, X)] − max_a E(U | a) ≥ 0, since for every value of X the best act given X is at least as good as the act that was best before you looked. Fine, I'm not disputing that.

The move I want to make is that the usual hand-wave — you can always just ignore the information — is a cheat. A Bayesian agent can't ignore evidence; she conditions on it. What actually makes the theorem go through is that she gets to re-optimize after seeing it. Once you notice that, the theorem looks less like a fact about information and more like a fact about having more options. And the cases where information genuinely hurts are exactly the cases where one of those two things breaks: she can't re-optimize, or her payoff depends on her beliefs rather than only on outcomes.

Three cases: (1) belief- or state-dependent utility — if you'd rather not know your prognosis, or you care about being a certain kind of person and then learn something about your dispositions, information can lower expected utility; (2) the decision problem isn't held fixed — a test result can remove an option (you now have a disclosure duty) or even change the state of the world (tell students a statistic about their group and the statistic starts becoming true of them), and then the "same problem" clause is doing all the work; (3) aggregate cases — I have a memory of a result in financial economics where public information makes everyone worse off ex ante because it destroys the risk-sharing that was possible under ignorance, so the social value of information comes out negative even with all agents Bayesian.

Also, the theorem is about expected value, not realized value: you can look and then wish you hadn't.

So: is my re-optimizing-not-ignoring reading right, or is there a standard statement of the theorem on which ignoring really is part of the proof? Are (1)-(3) counterexamples to the theorem or just rejections of its assumptions, and does that distinction do any real work? If you had to cut one, which? I'm least sure about 3 — I remember the shape of the mechanism but not the details or the assumptions, so don't worry about naming it or citing it, just tell me if the mechanism is even coherent as stated. And is any of this interesting, or is it the sort of thing everyone in decision theory already shrugs at?
