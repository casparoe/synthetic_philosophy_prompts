(b) Does the argument succeed? Be explicit about what has to be true of the agent over time for the pump to work, and about the best reply available to someone who insists her cyclic preferences are perfectly rational."

And here's what the student wrote, verbatim:

"(a) Take an agent whose preferences cycle: she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. Give her C to start. Since she prefers B to C she'll pay a small amount, say 1 cent, to trade up to B. Now she has B, and since she prefers A to B she'll pay another cent to trade to A. Now she has A, and since she prefers C to A she'll pay another cent to trade to C. She's back where she started, minus 3 cents. Repeat and she's pumped.

(b) The pump doesn't actually need time in any deep sense. All three of her preferences hold simultaneously -- she prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A at the same instant -- so we can think of the whole cycle as one incoherent package she's paying to move around in. The diachronic part is just bookkeeping: each trade is one she endorses at the moment she makes it, so at no point is she doing anything she doesn't want to do. That's why it's really a synchronic argument: it shows her preference set is internally incoherent, like a set of credences that violates the probability axioms, which is why the Dutch book argument for probabilism and the money pump are the same argument in different clothes.

If she's rational she should look ahead and refuse to start. That's the 'sophisticated' response: the sophisticated agent sees the whole sequence coming and sticks to her original plan, which is why sophistication is also called resoluteness -- she resolves not to be moved by the offers. The alternative, sometimes called 'naive' choice, is the agent who just takes each trade as it comes and gets pumped.

Is she thereby irrational? Alot of people say yes, and the intuition is strong: she ends up with less money and the same good she started with, and she'd have been better off refusing every offer, so her preferences can't be rational. But someone could hold out and say her preferences are fine and it's only her choices that are irrational, since an ordering doesn't have to be transitive to be an ordering. I think that reply fails, because if we find out what someone prefers by looking at what she chooses, then a choice set that gets pumped is just what an intransitive preference looks like from the outside. So the money pump does show that cyclic preferences are irrational."
