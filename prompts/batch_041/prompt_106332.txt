Bit of an odd one this, but I'm stuck. Reading group on formal epistemology, and a mate of mine built an argument that's bugging me because the conclusion is obviously bollocks and I can't find the dodgy step.

Here it is more or less as he wrote it:

(1) Jeffrey conditonalisation is the rule for updating on uncertain evidence. When what you learn isn't certainty in a single proposition but a shift in your probabilities across a partition {E_i}, your new probability for any proposition A is the sum, over the cells, of your old conditional probability of A given E_i multiplied by your new probability of E_i.
(2) Perception hands you uncertain evidence: when you seem to see or hear something, you're left with a high-but-not-certain degree of belief in its content, not certainty.
(3) So perception updates are run by (1).
(4) Fix any partition into E and not-E, and any A. Your old conditionals P(A|E) and P(A|not-E) come from your prior. Your new probabilities for E and not-E are just the degrees of belief you wind up with in the evidence once the experience is over.
(5) The new probability of A is a weighted average of those two old conditionals, weighted by your new degrees of belief in the evidence. So as your new confidence in E slides from 0 to 1, your new P(A) slides smoothly from P(A|not-E) up to P(A|E).
(6) Nothing in the rule pins down your new confidence in E. It's whatever you find yourself with.
(7) So for any target belief in A lying between those two old conditionals, there's a permissible update that lands you exactly on it, and since nothing pins your new confidence in E, nothing stops you landing on whatever you fancy.
(8) So Jeffrey conditionalisation lets a rational agent end up believing anything at all, simply by letting their confidence in the evidence settle wherever suits them. A rule of rationality that permits every outcome isn't a rule of rationality. So either Jeffrey conditionalisation is wrong, or uncertain perceptual evidence can't really count as evidence.

To make it concrete he put numbers on it: say P(A|E)=0.9 and P(A|not-E)=0.1. Then your new P(A) is 0.1 plus 0.8 times your new P(E). Land on 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, whatever you like, just by fixing your new confidence in E accordingly.

I'm pretty sure (1) is the right rule for uncertain evidence, and I don't want to give up (2), because I'm not willing to say perception is certain. So which step is rotten, and what does it cost me to cut it out? If more than one is rotten, tell me the order you'd sacrifice them in. British English please, and don't worry about being gentle about it.
