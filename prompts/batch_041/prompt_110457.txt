For a seminar I'm taking on the philosophy of AI, each of us has been asked to write down our own position on how deliberation should be idealized, so that the rest of the group can press on it. Here is where I've landed, and I'd like an audit before I circulate it.

(1) When we deliberate about anything hard, the process is — or is best modelled as — a tree. The top-level question gets answered by answering sub-questions, each sub-question is handed to a bounded agent, and the answers are combined on the way back up. I mean this as a claim about what deliberation is, not merely a way of computing it.

(2) The ideal version of that process is what the alignment literature calls HCH, a recursive acronym for "humans consulting HCH": I answer a question by consulting an idealized copy of myself on whatever sub-question I face, and that copy can consult a further copy, and so on. I take the answer HCH would give to be the correct answer to the question — equivalently, to be what my considered judgment really is.

(3) I don't claim that any real process attains HCH. It's a regulative ideal: our actual answers count as correct or incorrect by how they stand to it, even though nothing ever arrives at it.

(4) But an acceptable idealization of deliberation has to be defined out of resources a deliberator could in principle have. If an ideal is defined by a procedure that would take an infinite regress of self-consultation to run, it isn't an idealization of deliberation; it's a replacement for it.

(5) The tree only preserves correctness when the sub-questions are separable. When the answer to one sub-question depends on how the others come out, the process can be right at every node and wrong at the root. And the questions I care about most — what to do with my life, whether to have a child, what one person owes another — are precisely the interacting ones, where the sub-answers are entangled.

(6) HCH is nonetheless a well-defined target, because the regress converges: the limit of consulting idealized copies doesn't depend on the order in which the sub-questions are taken up, so there is a single answer for it to be.

I suspect these don't all hang together, but I can't see where the seams are, and I don't want to overcorrect by treating every friction as a contradiction. Please tell me which of the six actually pull against each other and which only appear to; rank the genuine tensions by severity; and for each, say exactly what would have to give. Then give me a verdict rather than a menu: name the one commitment you would abandon and what abandoning it costs. I'd rather you commit to a reading and defend it than lay out the options and leave the choice to me.
