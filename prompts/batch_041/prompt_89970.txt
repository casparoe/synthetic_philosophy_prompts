Augustine wouldn't budge on lying: no exceptions — not to save an innocent man, not to protect someone from rape, not even to catch out heretics. You're allowed to keep quiet and let a truth go unspoken, but you're never allowed to say anything false. I want an essay on whether that absolutism survives contact with the hard cases. Please skip the usual Nazi-at-the-door example, it's been done to death. Go instead for the cases where the deception is built into the exchange and both sides half-know it: bluffing at poker, the set-piece flattery of a dinner party, a lie told to someone who has definately asked not to be told the truth, and a public statement that no one in the room believes and everyone knows no one believes. Augustine's own criterion is that what makes a lie a lie is the will to deceive rather than the falsity of what's said — so on his view all of those are lies, and he'd condemn them. Compare that with the modern account on which a lie is simply an assertion you believe to be false, and say which of the two handles these cases better. Then reach a verdict: either Augustine's ban survives and these cases don't really touch it, or it needs an exception of exactly the kind he refused, or the better analysis of what lying is makes the hard cases dissolve. Don't end on the fence — take a position and defend it. British English, please.
