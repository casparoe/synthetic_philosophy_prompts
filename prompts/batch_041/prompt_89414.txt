I spent thirty-odd years doing physiology — vision mostly, cats and then monkeys — and since I retired I've been reading philosophy of mind, which is a lot more fun then I expected. Anyway, I've gotten attached to a version of the higher-order theory of consciousness and I want you to try to knock it down for me, or at least show me where the load-bearing walls are.

Here's my version. A mental state is conscious when a higher-order representation is directed at it. But I want three things that I notice a lot of people resist. First, the higher-order representation doesn't have to be a thought with concepts in it — I think it can be a bare analog signal, more like a pointer or a re-entrant copy than a proposition. Second, it doesn't have to be conscious itself; the whole point is that it does its work from behind the scenes. Third, I'll bite the bullet on accuracy: if a higher-order signal targets a state and gets the content wrong, the state is still conscious, just misrepresented. I actually think that's a feature, because it explains how we can be wrong about our own experience. And I want consciousness to come in degrees — a state is more or less conscious depending on how much information the higher-order signal carries about it, sort of a resolution or bandwidth thing.

So: what would count against this? I dont want the textbook list of objections to higher-order theories in general, I want to know which specific observations or arguments bear on my version — the non-conceptual, unconscious-pointer, graded one — and how hard they hit. Rank them. Which ones should actually move me, and which ones only look threatening because they're aimed at a different version of the view?

A few things I'm worried about; tell me whether they're real worries or whether I'm just confused. (1) If the higher-order signal is unconscious and non-conceptual, what stops me from saying a thermostat, or a spinal reflex arc, has a little bit of consciousness? Where's the principled line? (2) If misrepresentation is fine, can I still distinguish a conscious state from no state at all — what does my view say about a higher-order signal that targets nothing, or targets a state that isn't there? (3) Does the degrees story collapse into just attention, or reportability, or some other thing I could have said without the higher-order machinery at all? (4) What does my view predict that its rivals don't — is there any result, in blindsight or masking or the split-brain work, that would come out differently under my version than under a first-order view or a global-workspace view? Be honest if the answer is basically no.

And last: suppose I keep adding hedges — the signal can be unconscious, non-conceptual, inaccurate, graded. At what point have I made the view unfalsifiable, and is that a fatal problem for a view of this kind or just what happens when you're careful about a hard case? I'd like to know which parts a sympathetic higher-order theorist would themselves admit count against them, not only what the critics say. And tell me straight if you think the thing is well insulated — I'd rather hear that than get a polite list.
