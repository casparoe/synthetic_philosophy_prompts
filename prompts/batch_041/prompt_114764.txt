I'm in a graduate seminar on epistemic logic and I've been assigned the deflationary side of the logical omniscience debate, so I've ended up defending a harder line than I expected and I want it stress-tested before I commit to it. The line is this: logical omniscience is not a fixable defect of possible-worlds epistemic logic but a consequence of what that logic is a theory of. Knowledge is a relation to a proposition, propositions are individuated by their truth conditions, and an agent who knows something thereby knows everything true in the same set of worlds — so closure is not an extra assumption bolted on, it is the semantics working as designed. On this view the standard repairs all pay in the same currency: impossible-worlds semantics (worlds where contradictions hold), awareness operators (a modality that tracks which formulas the agent can even entertain), and the implicit/explicit belief distinction either smuggle closure back in through whatever generates the worlds or the awareness sets, or they stop describing a knowledge relation and start describing a computation, a proof search, or a database. Hence my claim: the only genuinely omniscience-free semantics are ones that have changed the subject, and there is nothing left to repair. I don't want a reading list and I don't want citations — I know the literature exists. What I want is a map of my exposure, and I'd like it sorted by how decisive each threat would actually be.

So: what would count against this? (a) I need to know whether any repair blocks closure while still treating the objects of the attitudes as truth-conditionally individuated propositions; if one does, the changed-the-subject move collapses, and I want to see exactly where it pays the cost instead — in the semantics of the attitude verb, in the underlying logic, or in the notion of a proposition — and whether any of those prices is one I could afford. (b) I want a case where the idealization does not merely misdescribe real agents but distorts what the theory predicts about agents we care about: whether it makes learning by deduction, or knowledge of one's own ignorance, unrepresentable in a way that matters beyond ideal-agent bookkeeping, and whether it wrecks the intellectualist reduction of knowing how to knowing that — if knowing how to swim is standing in the knowledge relation to a proposition under a practical mode of presentation, does the agent thereby know all its logical consequences under that mode, and is that absurdity a defect of the reduction or of the idealization?

(c) I want to know whether closure, kept together with factivity, positive introspection, and the principle that every truth could in principle be known, generates the result that every truth already is known — because if that collapse is real, closure is not an innocent idealization and I cannot wave the problem away. (d) I want to know how much of my position survives if someone exhibits a resource-bounded, computable belief state that is provably exploit-free, learns logical facts over time, and at any finite stage assigns no probability 1 to unproved theorems, yet is never deductively closed; if that is a coherent non-omniscient doxastic state, then my claim that everything short of omniscience is either incoherent or a change of subject looks like a false dilemma. A friend who works on automated theorem proving keeps telling me the idealization is harmless because the provers themselves are resource-bounded and nobody models them as knowing all consequences of their axioms — I can't tell whether that is a point against me or beside the point, and I'd like you to say. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, tell me which of them a defender of my line would concede as a genuine cost rather than dismiss, and be willing to tell me that the position is well insulated and that this is (or is not) a problem for a view of this kind. I would rather have the one thing that should actually make me give up the view than a survey of everything anyone has ever said against it.
