Last night a friend and I argued for hours about whether a theory can be "right but early," and we got nowhere, so I would like the question worked through properly rather than settled by whoever talked loudest.

The episode we kept circling is the 1980 wager between the biologist Paul Ehrlich and the economist Julian Simon. Simon offered to bet that the inflation-adjusted prices of whatever raw materials Ehrlich named would fall rather than rise, over any period more than a year out; Ehrlich and two colleagues picked five metals -- chromium, copper, nickel, tin and tungsten -- staked $200 on each at the prices of 29 September 1980, and named 29 September 1990 as the settlement date. Ehrlich lost: every one of the five was cheaper in real terms a decade later, and he mailed Simon a check for $576.07. The decade in question was also the one in which world population grew by more than 800 million, the largest absolute increase in a single decade on record. My friend treats the outcome as a clean refutation of the Malthusian outlook Ehrlich was speaking for, and regards "right but early" as the standard loser's evasion; he adds, more interestingly, that the bet was rigged, since the start date sits just after a commodity boom, so the basket was a mean-reverting one bought cheap, and that in any case prices measure the wrong variable. My own view -- which I could not defend at the time -- is that a decade of metals prices is a strange proxy for a claim about the long-run relation between population and subsistence, and that "right but early" is either a real epistemic category or a euphemism for unfalsifiability. I would like to know which.

So please analyze the wager as a test of the Malthusian claim, using the machinery from philosophy of science that deals with refutation and the rescue of theories from it: Popper on severe tests and immunizing stratagems, the Duhem-Quine point that no hypothesis is ever tested in isolation but only together with auxiliary assumptions, the Lakatosian contrast between a progressive and a degenerating response to a failed prediction, and the older question of whether a theory earns extra credit for a prediction it made in advance rather than a fact it accommodated after the event. I want the mapping done explicitly, not gestured at:

- What was actually on trial? Separate the Malthusian mechanism (diminishing returns to a fixed factor, population growth absorbing the surplus), the resource-depletion prediction, and the price index used to operationalize it. Which of the three did the bet test, and does a real price series track scarcity, or extraction cost, market structure, and the business cycle?
- Was this a severe test? Consider the choice of start date, the number of commodities, whether Ehrlich's team had committed in advance to a specific magnitude, and what Popperian severity actually requires of a test.
- Enumerate the rescues available to a Malthusian after 1990 and sort them honestly: which are ad hoc in the technical sense of testing nothing new, which are principled amendments that generate fresh risky predictions, and which merely relocate the claim's horizon. Then say whether "right but early" can be distinguished in principle from "unfalsifiable," given that Malthus's original prediction carried no date.
- Run it in the other direction. If the bet was a bad test of the right theory, does that vindicate the Malthusian or simply indict the test? And is there any stopping rule for how many rescues a research programme may absorb before it counts as degenerating, or is that a judgment the framework never made precise?
- Say where the machinery is silent -- what falsificationism and the research-programme framework cannot adjudicate here, rather than forcing them to speak.

Please state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of. I am not looking for reading recommendations or citations.
