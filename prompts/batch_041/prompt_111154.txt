You are a professional philosopher with a specialism in classical Chinese thought — in particular the School of Names (Mingjia) and the White Horse dialogue attributed to Gongsun Long — and you also write comedy for an audience of philosophy graduates. I should like one joke. It should be funny to readers who actually know this material, and merely baffling to readers who do not.

So that nothing here depends on your recall of the sources, here is the material you need. The White Horse dialogue, a chapter of the Gongsun Longzi, has two unnamed speakers dispute the claim that a white horse is not a horse. The advocate's case runs roughly as follows. Horse is the term that designates shape; white is the term that designates colour; what designates colour is not what designates shape, and so a white horse is not a horse. He then adds a sharper move. If you ask for a horse, a black horse or a yellow horse will do; if you ask for a white horse, neither will do. Were a white horse simply a horse, both requests would be answered by the same things; but they are not, so the requests differ, and what answers to the one does not answer to the other. Later in the dialogue the speakers worry about whiteness that is fixed to a particular thing as against whiteness considered on its own, and about whether white and horse can be prised apart within a white horse. The surrounding tradition of debaters, whose paradoxes survive mainly in the last chapter of the Zhuangzi, is full of cousins of this puzzle: a chicken with three feet, the shadow of a flying bird that never moves, a dog that is not a dog, a one-foot stick halved every day and never used up. Deadpan absurdity is very much the house style.

Now the task. Write a single short joke, set in the present day, whose punchline turns on the actual structure of the advocate's argument rather than on the general oddity of ancient philosophers saying strange things. Put it wherever you like — a lost-property office, an online shop's search filter, a border checkpoint, a zoo's inventory list — provided the setting makes the argument's moves visible. The joke should run to no more than about 150 words; dialogue or narration, either is fine, but it needs a clear punchline and it must not require a footnote in order to be recognised as a joke. The punchline must depend on one specific move in the argument — the shape-and-colour distinction, the two-requests move, or the fixed-whiteness move — and not merely on the conclusion that a white horse is not a horse. Do not misrepresent the argument; if the joke trades on a deliberate misreading, make it evident that the misreading is the point. Avoid the obvious soft targets: philosophers being confusing, Chinese being vague, or the whole thing being a silly riddle.

Before you write, work out for yourself two or three candidate punchlines and discard any that merely restate the conclusion. Then give me the joke, followed by no more than two sentences explaining which move it turns on. If a second joke on a different move comes to you, you may append it clearly marked as an alternative, but the first must stand on its own. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, citations, or quotations from any source: if you need to refer to the argument, paraphrase it in your own words. Write in British English throughout, and keep the register light — this is comedy, not an exegesis.
