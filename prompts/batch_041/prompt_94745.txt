Many institutions now open meetings and events with a land acknowledgment: a scripted statement naming the Indigenous peoples on whose traditional territory the gathering takes place, often read aloud by someone with no personal tie either to the land or to the peoples named, and just as often dismissed as merely "performative." Treat the land acknowledgment as a speech act, and reconstruct the theory of language it presupposes. The word "performative" is doing double duty here: in Austin's technical sense it names an utterance that does something rather than describes something, while in ordinary usage it means an empty gesture that substitutes words for action. Say which sense the practice itself relies on, which sense the critics' charge invokes, and whether the practice can survive the collision. Is a land acknowledgment a constative — a true-or-false claim about history and territory — or a performative, an act of honoring, acknowledging, committing, or apologizing? Then work out its felicity conditions: does the speaker need standing or authority to perform the act, must a conventional procedure be followed, and must the utterance be taken up — by the audience, or by the peoples named — before the act counts as done? Distinguish the failure modes: does the typical acknowledgment misfire, failing to come off as the act it names, or does it come off and merely prove hollow, insincere, or an abuse of the procedure — and does that distinction bear on whether the practice is defensible? End with a verdict rather than a survey: is this a coherent way of using words to do something, or does it presuppose a power of language we should not grant, and what would an alternative practice, built on a sounder account of what performatives can accomplish, look like? Take a definite position and argue for it.
