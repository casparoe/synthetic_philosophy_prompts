I have no training in philosophy, but for about a year now there has been a thought I cannot put down, and I would like you to write me a poem about it.

Two people who have never met take the same corner of the same road on the same wet evening, and each of them looks down at a phone for the same three seconds. One of them drives home and sleeps. The other does not, because on that corner, at that moment, a child was crossing. Nothing about the two of them differs — not their haste, not their carelessness, not the fraction of a second their eyes were away — and yet they are now different people in the eyes of the law, of their neighbours, and, I suspect, of themselves. I gather that philosophers have a name for this, and that they do not agree about what it shows. Some hold that our unequal reactions are a confusion we ought to train out of ourselves; others, that those reactions answer to something real in what a person has to answer for. I cannot decide between them, and I would rather have a poem that holds the difficulty open than an argument that closes it.

Here is what I am asking for. Two stanzas of eight lines each, in the same meter and the same rhyme scheme, the second repeating the first as nearly as possible — line for line, image for image, and in the same order — with exactly one line changed. That line is the one that reports what happened, and it should be metrically identical to the line it replaces, so that the ear hears no difference at all and only the sense turns over. Nothing else may be altered: not the driver's hand on the wheel, not the rain, not the song on the radio, not the speed. I want the whole moral difference between the two of them to arrive in a line that occupies exactly the space of the line before it. Then a coda of two to four lines, in the same voice, that says what the difference was worth — or confesses that it cannot say.

The poem should not use the term "moral luck" or any other clinical label for the phenomenon, and the coda should not turn into an explanation. It should be honest enough that someone who has spent years on this question would recognize the difficulty rather than a slogan, and open enough that it does not tell me which of the two views to hold.

After the poem, in a few sentences of plain prose, tell me which of the things the poem implies are common ground among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and flag any place where the poem has quietly taken a side in a live dispute — I do not want to mistake its mood for a consensus.
