You are a philosopher who works on rational choice, time, and constitutional theory. Please think this through carefully before writing: first work out for yourself where the argument below could plausibly break, and only then commit to a diagnosis. Write the reply under the headings given at the end.

A friend of mine, who has no academic training but reads widely, has been arguing me into a corner. His argument:

(1) A precommitment -- Ulysses having himself tied to the mast, me putting money in a trust I cannot touch until I am fifty, a constitution's entrenchment clause -- gets whatever authority it has from a preference of the person who sets it up. There is nothing else for it to get authority from.

(2) The person who sets it up and the person who is later bound are the same person. There is no second self issuing an order and a first self obliged to obey it.

(3) So if the earlier preference has authority over later conduct, it is because it is the preference of the person who is later bound. But the later preference is also the preference of the person who is later bound -- the same person, at a later time, standing in the situation.

(4) When a person's preferences conflict across time, the later one wins: she is the one who has to live in the situation, and she knows more about it than she did from a distance.

(5) So the later preference always wins, and the earlier act of binding has no authority over it.

(6) A Ulysses contract therefore binds no one. It is at most a device for making the later choice expensive; the later self may pay the price if she wants, and is under no obligation not to.

(7) The same reasoning covers constitutions. An entrenched clause cannot bind a later generation, because the later generation's preference is the preference of the same collective person, later in time and better informed.

(8) So "binding" in both cases is a fiction, and what we call precommitment is only a toll the current majority has to keep paying.

(9) But that cannot be right. A constitution revisable by simple majority whenever the majority felt like it would not be a constitution, and I do not believe entrenchment is always just the dead hand of the past.

He calls the principle in (4) "temporal majoritarianism," and says anyone who rejects it owes an account of why an earlier preference deserves more weight than a later one, given that it is the same person either way.

I would rather keep (9) than (5) through (8), but I want to know what I am paying. Which step should I give up? Do not just name it -- explain why it looked convincing, what work it was doing in the argument, and what giving it up costs me elsewhere, in the individual case and in the constitutional one, which I suspect are less alike than he makes them. If the honest answer is that the argument is sound and (9) is the step to drop, say so and tell me what that leaves me with.

Format:
