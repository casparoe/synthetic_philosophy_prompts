I'm a software engineer — I build databases, no philosophy training beyond one undergrad ethics class I mostly slept through — and a couple of years ago I volunteered to rebuild the collections-management system for our university's small art museum. Somehow that turned into a seat on the museum's collections and conservation committee, which meets monthly and decides what the conservators are actually allowed to do to objects in the collection. There's a case coming up at the next meeting and I'm the one who has to circulate the committee's written opinion, but I keep going around in cirles on it, so I'd like you to draft the thing for me.

The case: we own a large wooden sculpture, roughly 500 years old, in terrible shape — rot, insect damage, maybe 40 percent of the original wood is gone or structurally dead. The conservation lab has a proposal that's more aggressive than anything we've approved before. They want to use a 3D scan to carve replacements for everything that's lost, and here's the part making everyone uneasy: they want to grow the replacement wood from cell cultures taken from the surviving original wood itself, so the new material is the same tree stock as the sculpture, not merely the same species. Their pitch is that after treatment you wont be able to tell original from new without the lab's records, and they say that's the point — the object gets to keep being the object, instead of being a ruin with a modern sculpture grafted onto it. The donor funding the whole thing wants it displayed as the artist's work under the existing accession number, and wants the dead wood cut away during treatment deaccessioned, either discarded or handed to a teaching collection. The curator is fine with all of it. Two of the conservators think this crosses a line, that whatever comes out of the lab is a new thing which happens to contain old parts, and should be re-catalogued and labeled as a reconstruction. My problem is that I built the database, so I know exactly what's at stake in the cataloguing question — the accession number is basically the primary key for "the object," and everything else hangs off it, provenance, loan history, insurance, the artist attribution, so calling this a new object versus the same object isn't a bookkeeping detail, it changes what the record is a record of. And I can't shake the feeling there's a real question under the paperwork, something like: what is the artwork exactly — the wood, the shape, the history, or the thing that has all three? The lab keeps saying the wood they grow is "the same wood," and I sort of get it and sort of think it's nonsense.

So: please write the opinion I'll circulate before the meeting. It needs to recommend a specific course of action — approve, approve with conditions, or reject, and if approved, exactly what we're approving and on what timeline, since the donor's offer lapses at the end of the fiscal year and we can't just table this forever. It should take the dissenting conservators' position seriously rather than waving it away; they'll be in the room and if I can't answer them the meeting will just stall. Don't assume I know the standard philosophy vocabulary for this — if there's a name for the puzzle I'm circling, explain it in plain terms instead of name-dropping it. And please write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables, just the way you'd write an email or a letter, since that's how these opinions actually get passed around.
