I've spent a lot of time in the collapse-adjacent corners of the internet, and I keep circling the same disagreement without settling it: when we say a society "collapsed," are we describing something that happened in the world, or just drawing a line around a system and then mourning that the line no longer holds? I'd like you to work that question through one well-documented episode rather than in the abstract.

Take the Late Bronze Age Collapse — the wave of destructions and disappearances around 1200 BCE in which the Hittite empire, the Mycenaean palace centers, Ugarit, and the long-distance trade networks binding them together all came apart within a generation or two, while Egypt, by most accounts, weathered the transition and survived. Assume I know the broad outline; don't recite it, and don't cite sources — I want the argument, not a bibliography.

Run the episode through two theories of collapse, steelmanning each before you adjudicate between them.

The first is the diminishing-returns-to-complexity view associated with Joseph Tainter: societies add complexity — bureaucracy, specialization, infrastructure, energy subsidy — to solve each new problem, until the marginal return on further complexity turns negative, at which point collapse is a rational simplification, an involuntary shedding of complexity rather than a failure of nerve or a stroke of bad luck.

The second is the resilience / adaptive-cycle view associated with C. S. Holling and the panarchy school: systems pass through growth, conservation, release, and reorganization; what we call collapse is the release-and-reorganization phase, and the real question is whether reorganization returns the system to its previous regime or to a new one.

For each theory, do the mapping explicitly — what is the "system," what counts as complexity or as the conserved regime, what is the release, what is the reorganization — and say plainly where the theory has to be stretched or is simply silent. Then tell me which theory does better on three specific puzzles: (a) why so many politically unconnected societies came apart within a generation or two of one another; (b) why recovery took centuries rather than decades; and (c) why Egypt's political order rode out a shock that destroyed its neighbors.

I have also been using a rough working criterion, and I want you to test it rather than accept it: a society has collapsed only if its population falls substantially, its central political authority dissolves, and its achieved complexity — literacy, urbanism, long-distance exchange — is lost for several generations. Does the Bronze Age case meet that bar? And is the criterion doing real work, or is it smuggling in a "failure" verdict under the guise of a definition?

Finally, step back. The two theories look as though they disagree about whether collapse is a failure or merely a phase. Is that a substantive disagreement about the world, or a verbal one about where we draw the boundary of a "society" and at what level of analysis we are looking? Say what would have to be true for it to be substantive, and give me your own view.

End with a final line in exactly this form: Verdict: <COLLAPSE|TRANSFORMATION|LEVEL-DEPENDENT> — <one sentence naming which theory you would keep, and why>.
