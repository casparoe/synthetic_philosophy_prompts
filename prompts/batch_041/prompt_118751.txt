I'm a novelist, third book, and the first two were the kind of thing where a marriage comes apart in a house near a lake, so I'm trying to write something with more machinery in it this time. What I keep circling is two people who would both be better off cooperating, who both know it, and who still can't pull it off — and I want the reason to be about knowledge rather than about their personalities. Not a betrayal story. Nobody lies. Everybody is being perfectly reasonable and it falls apart anyway.

I've spent a few weeks reading around the epistemic game theory stuff and I've got just enough of it to be dangerous. The bit that's stuck: everyone knows X is much weaker than everyone knows that everyone knows X, and that ladder keeps going up, and nothing much happens until you get to the top of it. And there's a puzzle where two players who are rational, where it's common knowledge between them that they're both rational, and where the game has a known last move, get eaten from the back by backward induction — they defect on move one, in games where defecting on move one is the worst thing either of them could do. That's the feeling I want in the story.

Here's the wrinkle I want to build on, and I should flag that I only half understand it. I've seen it called team reasoning: the agents stop asking given what she'll do, what should I do, and start asking what should we do, and then each of them does her part of that answer, and cooperation can come out as the rational thing without anybody being irrational or secretly generous. I want a character who finds her way into that mode of thinking, or finds she can't hold onto it, and I want the reader to feel the difference between the two modes from the inside rather than be told about them.

What I need from it:

- 2,000 words or less. Tight beats baggy.
- The knowledge structure has to be load-bearing. Something should turn on a character not knowing whether the other one knows that she knows, and it should cost her something real. I also want one moment where no private fact changes at all, but the thing gets said out loud in front of both of them and suddenly it's something they have to act on. I've always found that creepy and I want it in there.
- No lectures. No professor character explaining the puzzle. If someone has to name the thing, let them get it a bit wrong.
- Dont end with a verdict about which mode is correct. I'd rather it not. But don't leave me a riddle with no floor either.
- Don't steal the shape from a famous story or film. I'll notice.
- Set it somewhere mundane and specific. A kitchen, a shop, a car. Stakes small enough to be real — two people deciding whether to sell the house, whether to keep the shop open, who moves out.

Give me the story first. Then a section titled What I was doing, 250 to 400 words, where you tell me which parts of the epistemic material are actually carrying weight in the story, where you were deliberately vague, and what you'd change if I told you I wanted more knowledge and less people.
