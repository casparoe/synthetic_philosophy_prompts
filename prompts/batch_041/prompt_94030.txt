I am putting together a session for a graduate seminar on self-knowledge, and I want to open it with a specification rather than with any particular theory. Let us say that a condition is luminous when a subject who is in that condition is thereby in a position to know that she is in it, and treat the KK principle as the claim that knowing that p always puts one in a position to know that one knows that p. What I want from you is the full list of requirements that a theory of our knowledge of our own current condition would have to meet in order to count as adequate for the purposes of the seminar and for evaluating the recurrent claim that introspection is a reliable route to one's own states: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the constraints it cannot contradict, each accompanied by the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. Mark clearly which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the house preferences of one camp, and say which single requirement, in your view, does the most to exclude the popular candidates.

Several pieces of the territory need to be in the list, because they are the ones my students keep mishandling. First, the incremental case: a subject is exposed to a series of conditions, say descending water temperatures or rising light levels, each adjacent pair differing by a step too small for her to discriminate; she begins in a state she can confidently identify and ends in one she can equally confidently identify, while no single step is noticeable to her. The theory has to say what she can and cannot know at each point, and explain why nothing in the middle of the series is luminous, without making her earlier and later confidence simply mistaken. Second, the phenomenal case: it looks overwhelmingly plausible that a subject can always know how things currently appear to her, even when she cannot know how they are, and the theory must either accommodate that or explain the appearance away without simply denying that experiences have a character. Third, a case I have invented for the class: over the course of a minute the light in a room dims by imperceptible steps while a subject reads; she never notices the change and cannot say when it occurred, though at every moment the room has some definite brightness. Two further things I want built into the specification, since they do not appear in the material I know and I want to see whether the familiar requirements even have room for them. The first is a distinction between luminosity of states and luminosity of transitions: a subject might be unable to know how bright the room is at any moment yet be able to know that it has just become brighter, or the reverse. The second is a graded notion: suppose someone proposes that a condition is luminous to degree d just in case a subject in that condition is in a position to know she is in it in at least proportion d of the cases. The theory must either make sense of this proposal, or show why it collapses into the binary notion, or say which degrees are attainable and why.

So the deliverable is the requirements list with its motivating cases, together with an explicit flag on any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; I expect the interesting result to be that the phenomenal requirement and the incremental-case requirement pull in opposite directions, and if so I want that stated as a result about the standard rather than as a problem with any one theory. After the list, score briefly three views against it: the KK principle itself, an account that analyses knowledge in terms of the absence of close possibilities of error, and an account that treats our access to our own experience as a kind of direct acquaintance. Keep the scoring short, since the requirements are what I will actually be teaching from.
