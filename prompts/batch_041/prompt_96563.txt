A colleague of mine teaches decision theory and is very settled about cases where someone has no preference between two options, and I need to predict what she'll say about something live. Her track record: two identical umbrellas, she grabs the left one without deliberating — fine, rational. Two job offers, months of research, still a dead tie, she flips a coin — fine. Same tie, but she decides to wait for a sign and both offers expire — she says thats not fine, that's a failure. A tie between two restaurants broken by an irrelevant fact about which chef has an earlier birthday — she shrugs, silly but rational. A guy who refuses to use any tie-breaker and keeps hunting for a real reason until his window closes — she says he's irrational, and specifically that the irrationality is in the continuing to deliberate, not in the not-choosing. She also thinks the donkey starving between two identical hay piles only works if you assume a psychology with no tie-breaking dispositions at all, which she finds implausible. Now the live case: our hiring committee has two candidates scored exactly equal on every criterion. The chair wants a standing rule — ties go to the alphabetically earlier surname — arguing that a rule is transparent, ownable, and can be announced to candidates; one member says the alphabet isn't neutral (surnames track nationality, gender, plenty of other things) and that only a lottery is defensible; a third says that if we're exactly tied our criteria are just too coarse and we should go sharpen them instead. Predict her verdict on the standing alphabetical rule, tell me the rules she might be running — any tie-breaker is fine; ties must be broken symmetrically or randomly; what matters is that the tie-breaker is stable and ownable; the tie is itself an epistemic failure and the real error is upstream — what each predicts here, which you'd bet on and why, and what further case would actually tell them apart.
