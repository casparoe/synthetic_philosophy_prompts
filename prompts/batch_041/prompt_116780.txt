So my brother-in-law Wes and my friend Marisol got into it at our place on Saturday over a painting I have up in the hall — just a print, nothing fancy — and I've been chewing on it alot all week, because I honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it and they wouldn't let it drop. They're both smart and neither one was being dumb, which is why I'm stuck. Can you walk through it move by move and say who's right at each step?

The rough shape of it. Wes said you can't argue about beauty, full stop: if he hates the thing and Marisol loves it, nobody's wrong, it's like vanilla versus chocolate. Marisol said then why does anyone ever talk about somebody having good taste, and why does a book sometimes get better on a second read — you don't "develop" a taste for vanilla. Wes said getting better is just changing, not getting closer to anything.

Marisol came back with Hume, who she'd read the essay on taste by in college. She said the standard isn't in the painting, it's in the verdict of the best judges — people who've looked at tons of art, who compare things, who put their own hang-ups aside. When the qualified ones agree, that's what beautiful means; someone who calls a bad movie a masterpiece is like someone who can't taste the difference. Wes said that's circular, you've just defined good judges as the ones who like good things. She said no, the qualifications are things you can check on their own — how much someone's looked, how closely they pay attention, whether they're biased — without knowing what they'll pick. Wes said fine, but take two people who both pass those tests and disagree about the same painting: now your standard has nothing to say, and if you fall back on the majority of qualified judges, that's a vote, and a vote about feelings isn't a fact. Marisol said Hume's whole point is that it can be a sentiment and still have a standard, because people are built roughly the same, so the qualified ones will tend to land in the same place. Wes said that's a bet about how the world turns out, and he doesn't think it's true.

Then Marisol brought up that old story Hume borrows from Don Quixote — two guys taste a barrel of wine and say it tastes of iron and leather, everyone laughs at them, and then somebody fishes a key on a leather strap out of the barrel. She said that's the point: there was a fact of the matter and the picky ones got it right. Wes said that's about wine going off, not about beauty, and anyway here's what bugs him. A guy at his work read a long essay describing my exact painting — every color, every brushstroke, what it's supposedly doing — and now he's certain it's a masterpiece, but he's never actually stood in front of it. Marisol says he can't really judge it. Wes says if the beauty were in the painting, why can't a good enough description get you there? The only reason to say he can't judge is if beauty is just the feeling you get standing there, which is his side. Marisol said judging is a skill you practice on the thing itself, like tasting wine, not reading about wine. Wes said but then two equally skilled tasters should agree, and she keeps needing it to be a skill and also not to have a right answer, and those don't go together. Marisol said sure they do — chess skill is real and the better player usually wins, but not every position has one best move.

Last round, Wes said the thing that really gets him is that all this training and learning might just be Marisol talking herself into liking stuff, like acquiring a taste for cigarettes, and calling that better taste is just flattering the version of her that did the work. She said the difference is that with cigarettes you don't start seeing something you were missing.

So — who's right where? I'd like each of their moves rated, from clearly wrong to clearly right, and a straight answer on a few specific points: is the qualified-judge idea actually circular or not; does the wine story really help Marisol's side, or is Wes right that it's about a different kind of thing; the guy who only read the description — who has the better of that one; and whether improving your taste is a real thing or just changing. And if they're each right about different pieces, tell me what both of them are missing that neither one is seeing.
