I'm a reporter on the science-and-ideas beat, and I'm working up a piece about the late-life conversion story — the thinker who supposedly recants at the end and hands the biographers a tidy third act. Right now I'm on Mill, because I keep tripping over the line that the great theorist of individual liberty died a socialist. I was arguing about this at a bar with a friend who teaches Victorian lit and she just laughed and said "everyone knows Mill went soft," which is exactly the kind of thing I'm trying to write about and not repeat. The version I've been working from is the editor's introduction to a reissue of his political essays. I've read it closely enough to be suspicious of it and not closely enough to argue with it. Here's roughly what it says: Mill's final writings on political economy show him far more sympathetic to socialism than the textbook Mill; he came to think that private ownership of capital has no defensible basis, and that a socialist organization of industry is the only arrangement a rational person could now endorse. The remedy he has in mind, the introduction says, is common ownership, with the state holding the means of production on everyone's behalf. Mill concedes that this means letting go of the harm principle from On Liberty — that doctrine, he supposedly says, was fitted to a society of competing private owners — and that liberty will need redefining once production is socialized. His one real hesitation, on this reading, is whether people are morally ready for it. The resistance comes entirely from the rich, and he expects the old Malthusian anxiety about overpopulation to dissolve under socialism, since the competitive scramble that drives population growth will be gone.

Something about this doesnt sit right with me, but I don't know the Mill literature well enough to say what. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a Mill specialist would nod along to, what they'd wince at, and what's just not in the texts — and in particular where the introduction has taken something Mill was describing or weighing and reported it as something he was recommending? I'd also like to know which of the apparent errors are actually live disagreements among interpreters rather than mistakes, since I don't want to accuse the editor of something the field hasn't settled. Then the bigger question: once the summary is fixed up, is there still a story here about Mill converting, or is the arc itself mostly a biographers convenience? Tell me how confident you are in each of your claims and single out the one you're least sure about. American English, please — this is for a US audience.
