I'm putting together the final for my 4th-year formal epistemology seminar and I need one essay question on the Condorcet jury theorem. The problem is every year students just regurgitate the standard result—if each voter is better than random and votes independently, bigger juries are more likely to be right—and then they stop. I want a question that forces them into the weeds. Here's the setup I want to build it around: a hospital ethics committee of nine clinicians, each individually right 60% of the time on a binary life-or-death call, but because they were trained in the same diagnostic culture their errors are positively correlated. There's also a junior clinician who is only 55% right but whose errors are independent of the nine. I want students to figure out when adding the junior to the nine helps, when it hurts, and what that says about the independence assumption in the theorem. They should have to do at least qualitative math, not just name the assumption. If you need to make the correlation structure concrete to make the question answerable—say a common-shock model where either everyone is exposed to the same biasing factor or they vote independently—feel free, as long as the math stays simple enough for a 90-minute closed-book exam. Give me the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 built around five must-hit points, with a one-line note on the mistake each point catches. Dont ask for citations or readings; they wont have them. And if my setup is actually confused—like if nine people each being 60% accurate plus positively correlated errors doesn't pin down the joint distribution enough to answer—say so plainly and either repair the question or tell me why it cant be done well. I'd rather get pushback than a politely padded non-answer.
