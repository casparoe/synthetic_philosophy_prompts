I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and I need to turn a half-formed idea into a research proposal for a small internal grant that would buy me one semester of teaching relief. The funder asks for two pages, but the real audience is my committee, so I want it to read as a prospectus for what I hope will become the middle chapter of my dissertation. The chapter concerns the informational vocabulary that saturates molecular biology — the ubiquitous talk of DNA coding for traits, of genes carrying information, of signals and messages — and the fork in the literature that I have come to think is not a real fork. On one side, "genetic information" is treated as a semantic notion, in something like the sense in which a sentence can be true or false, so that a gene can misrepresent, and the arbitrariness of the codon–amino acid mapping (the mapping is fixed only by the translational machinery and could, in principle, have been otherwise) is taken as the hallmark of genuine coding. On the other side, it is treated as a purely causal or structural notion — the correlation-based sense in which any cause carries information about its effects — in which case misrepresentation is a category error and most of the philosophical excitement evaporates. My half-formed idea is that these camps are trading on two different things that both get called a "code": a relation of encoding, specifiable without any reference to users, conventions, or functions, and something closer to ciphering, which cannot be specified without them. If that is right, much of the dispute about whether genes represent is verbal, and the interesting residual question is quasi-empirical: which of the two notions is doing the explanatory work in actual biological practice, and does the answer shift when we look at cases where the informational description seems to earn its keep — error correction, alternative splicing, transgenerational epigenetic effects, or genetic elements that spread without benefiting the organism?
