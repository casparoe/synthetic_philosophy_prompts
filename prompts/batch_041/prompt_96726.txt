Argue for the following thesis: that the distinction between a moral preference and a moral bias is not a single psychological distinction that better empirical work could draw more sharply, but a shift between two standpoints on a moral outlook — the deliberative standpoint, from which the outlook is the agent's own set of commitments, and the explanatory standpoint, from which it is the output of causes — and that every attempt to fuse the two into one criterion of bias fails in one of two ways: it smuggles the deliberative standpoint back in, or it forfeits the normative force that makes the label worth applying at all.

Take the following stipulations as fixed and use them throughout. An agent prefers a form of conduct, in the sense at issue, when her attitude toward it is stable under reflection, endorsed by her, and answerable to the moral considerations she acknowledges. She is biased toward a form of conduct when her attitude toward it is produced by something she herself would not count as a moral consideration — disgust, the order in which cases were presented, the identity of the person affected — and would not survive her learning that this was its source. Call this the self-endorsement criterion, and treat it as the criterion most often relied on in practice whenever a moral response is classified as bias rather than judgment.

Then do the following, arguing throughout rather than surveying.

First, show that the self-endorsement criterion cannot bear the weight placed on it, by pressing at least two difficulties: that the reflection supposed to certify a preference is itself shaped by the dispositions under review, so that an agent whose outlook is thoroughly distorted may endorse it without remainder; and that there are cases in which an attitude is unstable under reflection precisely because the agent is being pressured out of a judgment she is entitled to keep.

Second, examine the two natural repairs and argue that each fails. The first replaces self-endorsement with an objective criterion: a disposition is biased just in case it is caused by a factor that is in fact irrelevant to the moral truth of the matter. The second replaces it with a counterfactual-stability criterion: an attitude is a preference just in case it would survive the agent's coming to know its causal history in full, and a bias otherwise. Say which of the two comes closer, and argue that both either presuppose the distinction they are meant to explain or collapse bias into moral error, losing the contrast the distinction was introduced to draw.

Third, test the result against two cases and say what each shows: (a) a person who judges a harmless but taboo practice to be wrong, whose attitude is traceable to disgust, who can give no reason for the judgment that does not beg the question, and who nonetheless endorses it on reflection and would not revise it; and (b) a person whose judgment that a just but socially costly arrangement is required wavers under the contempt of others, and who is highly susceptible to that pressure, but whose judgment is arguably correct.

Fourth, formulate and answer the strongest objection you can to your own conclusion — I take it to be that without a psychological criterion bias becomes a term of abuse, so that calling a view biased adds nothing to disagreeing with it, and that this is a cost the position cannot pay.

Finally, say what follows for practice: whether debiasing exercises, implicit-bias training, and the common advice to reflect on the origins of one's moral convictions can be justified on your account, and if so, on what grounds.
