I'm running a session on Ian McEwan's Atonement for a reading group that's half philosophers and half lit people, and I want to do the whole thing through moral luck — Nagel, Williams, and the messy aftermath, not the watered-down version. Everyone gives the obvious reading (13-year-old lies, three lives wrecked, is she guilty or just unlucky) and Im bored of it, so I want the in-the-weeds version. Also please be honest where the book fights the reading instead of just making it fit, and don't lean on the film adaptation — the Joe Wright version shuffles the reveal around and I forget exactly how.

Stuff I definately want in there:

- The epistemic condition. Briony was thirteen and genuinely believed she knew what she'd seen. There's a whole literature on culpable ignorance, and on tracing a blameworthy act back to an earlier failure of the agent — does either apply here, or is the epistemic condition just relabeling luck? Worth noting Robbie himself, in the retreat section, decides she was too young to blame fully: does the novel agree with him, or is that just his own softness talking?

- Agent-regret vs. ordinary guilt. Williams's lorry driver who runs over a child through no fault of his own and is haunted by it is the standard case. Briony's lifelong penance — is that agent-regret (which Williams insists is not a moral emotion at all) or plain guilt? And does the distinction survive a case like hers, where she's guilty of the false accusation no matter how the luck falls?

- Law as institutionalized luck. Same intent, different outcome, different sentence (attempt vs. completed crime); and the doctrine where a death during a lesser crime counts as murder even when the death was a fluke. Also doli incapax — the old rebuttable presumption that a child of roughly ten to fourteen doesn't know right from wrong. Does the novel's moral psychology line up with any of that, or is the legal analogy a distraction?

- The coda. Briony invents the reunion, giving Robbie and Cecilia the ending the facts denied them. I've been calling this luck laundering: rewriting the outcome so the agent gets the ending they'd have chosen. Is that atonement, or is it a rerun of the girl at the window — the novelist claiming to know other people's insides — or both at once?

- Where it breaks. Which scenes resist the whole moral-luck frame, and what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that a paper on resultant luck can't?

After the piece, give me a short structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields: thesis_one_line (the whole reading in one sentence); luck_inventory (an object with keys constitutive, circumstantial, resultant, each a sentence on how that kind of luck shows up in the book); scenes (an array of 3 to 5 objects, each with scene and point, naming the scene and the one thing it shows); epistemic_condition_verdict (a sentence saying whether Briony's ignorance was culpable and why); coda_verdict (an object with label, one of atonement, evasion, both, or neither, and why); where_the_reading_breaks (one or two sentences); what_the_novel_adds (one or two sentences); confidence (an object with level, high, medium, or low, and why).
