In his Letter to d'Alembert on Spectacles (1758), written against d'Alembert's proposal to give Geneva a theatre, Rousseau singles out Molière's Le Misanthrope as the proof of his case: a work of genius and morally backwards, he says, because the honest man Alceste is made ridiculous while the complaisant Philinte is held up as the wiser party, so that an audience comes away flattered in the very vices it came to laugh at. I'd like a reading of the play through that indictment, for an essay on Rousseau's aesthetics. Where does the charge land — is Alceste's integrity a principle or a wound, and is Molière himself already exposing the vanity of the misanthrope, so that the play is on Rousseau's side rather than against it? Where does the play resist him: the sonnet scene, the unmasking of Célimène, Alceste's final withdrawal from the world? Does the comedy invite our contempt for him, our complicity with the salon, or something more uncomfortable than either? And note the awkward fact that Rousseau, denouncing the salon from outside it, is himself a sort of Alceste, which may make his defence of the honest man a defence of himself. End by saying what the play shows about the moral work of the stage, and about the laughter it turns on virtue, that Rousseau's argument by itself cannot reach.
