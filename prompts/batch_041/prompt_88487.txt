Two legal philosophers have spent years disagreeing about whether a constitution can bind its own amendment, and neither has ever managed to state the other's position in a form the other would sign. I want the document they should have written together instead.

Take the following case as the shared object of dispute. A constitution contains a single amending clause: it may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the legislature, and any amendment adopted under it may itself be altered only by a unanimous vote. In year one, the legislature by a two-thirds vote adopts an amendment that replaces this clause with one requiring only a bare majority for all future amendments. In year two, by a bare majority, it restores the old unanimity requirement. The two parties agree on the bare facts of the sequence and disagree about whether any of the three acts is legally valid, and about whether that question even has a determinate answer.

The first party holds the constituent-power view: an amending authority cannot validly limit its successor's power to amend, so a clause purporting to entrench itself is either void or merely declaratory; on this view the puzzle dissolves once one sees that a later genuine exercise of constituent power replaces the rule of change rather than being judged by it. The second party holds the social-fact view of validity: entrenchment is perfectly coherent because legal validity is relative to a rule of recognition that is itself only an accepted social practice, and a practice can be structured so that the later enactment is simply invalid; on this view the appearance of paradox comes only from treating legal rules as propositions with truth-values and then asking which rule governs its own creation. This is the structure the game Nomic makes vivid, by putting the rules of rule-change themselves in play.

Write their joint statement. It should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can sign — about what a rule of change is, about the difference between repealing a rule and exercising it, about what makes a legal system's ultimate criteria of validity ultimate, about what would count as a change of legal system rather than a change within one, and about which parts of the three-act sequence are uncontroversial.
2. A table of the residual disagreements, each side's position stated in wording that side would accept as its own.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I suspect one is whether being valid under the old rule and being valid under the new rule are the same predicate, and another is whether an act can be legally authorized by a rule it simultaneously destroys; but identify the cruxes yourself if you think I have missed the load-bearing ones.
4. For each crux, the argument, hypothetical, or variation on the case that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction about the outcome recorded in advance.
5. Your judgment on which parts of the dispute are merely verbal and which are substantive, and why.

Then, in a closing section, turn the same scrutiny on the document itself. State which of the agreed claims might not really be agreed; which crux I may have misidentified or which you may have misidentified; whether you think one side in fact has the better of it, and on what grounds; and what argument or variation on the case would lead you to revise your verdict on whether the dispute is verbal. Be explicit that this closing section is your own view rather than a joint position, and flag the step in your reasoning where you are least confident.
