Ok so I need a thought experiment built to order, and I want it in the weeds a bit. You know the discursive dilemma — the thing where every single person in a group has a perfectly consistent set of views, but when you take the majority vote on each point separately, the group ends up committed to something contradictory, even though everyone agrees about how the points connect to each other. I want a case that does that, but with a few twists, and I want you to show your work.

The specs:

- It has to be a real institutional setting, not a philosophy seminar — a committee, a board, a panel, a tribunal, something with an actual written decision rule and an actual decision to make. Keep the setup to a paragraph.
- Five people, not three. I'm sick of the three-judge version. All five have to be individually consistent, and at most two of them can have the same judgment set.
- Everyone in the group accepts the same connection between the propositions, so nobody can blame the trouble on a disagreement about the logic. The written rule should make that connection explicit.
- Here's the twist I actually care about: make the connection disjunctive rather than conjunctive. I want the group to end up endorsing the conclusion by majority while majority-rejecting every one of its grounds. The usual textbook case runs the other way, so this should be harder to build.
- Also make the two aggregation procedures disagree about the final decision: vote on the grounds and derive the decision, you get one answer; vote on the decision itself, you get the other.

Then walk me through it. List each member's judgments, the majority verdict on each proposition, and the inconsistent collective set that falls out of them. Show that each individual set is consistent and the collective one isnt. State what the premise-based procedure outputs and what the conclusion-based procedure outputs on the final decision, and say which of the two the group's own written rule seems to favor. Finish with an honest note on which feature of the case is doing the work, whether the whole thing collapses if you drop that feature, and what the best move is for someone defending straight issue-by-issue majority voting — not just that they'd reject the case, but their actual strongest response.

The point of all this is to convince someone that the paradox isn't an artifact of 3-person examples, so if you cheat by stipulating that the group ends up with a contradictory set, the case is worthless — the contradiction has to fall out of the individual judgments you list.
