Last night I argued with a close friend for several hours about whether a life can be too morally good, and we ended exactly where we began, so I want a careful adjudication rather than another exchange of intuitions. The dispute concerns two rival ideals of a life, and I want them compared as theories, not summarized as moods.

The first is the maximizing ideal. On this view the good is good without limit, so a life is better the more thoroughly it is organized around producing the good: someone who gives away most of their income, treats every discretionary hour as a resource owed to others, and lets no project or attachment stand outside the moral ledger is living the best life available, and any life that stops short is by that much worse. My friend pressed this hard and added a principle she called the parity of moral opportunity — if a better use of your time always exists, then every life that declines to take it is diminished by precisely that much.

The second is the moderated ideal. On this view the moral is one domain among several, and a life that leaves nothing outside it is not fuller but thinner; the person who keeps a reserve — for friendship, for work not justified by its consequences, for idleness — is not falling short of goodness but protecting something goodness itself cannot supply. I held this line, and called the thing the maximizing ideal would destroy the integrity of the unclaimed life.

What I want from you is a genuine comparison of these two as conceptions of the good life. What does each presuppose about the nature of value — is the good additive, and can it be maximized at all? — about the self, and about the relation between the moral and the non-moral? Where do the two ideals genuinely conflict, and where do they merely talk past each other? Then adjudicate: which has the better of it, and on what grounds?

Test the ideals rather than restating them. Tell me what each would say about one concrete hard choice — say, whether to spend a free decade on a personally absorbing but morally neutral project, or to give that decade to the most effective cause — and say whether a person can coherently hold that the maximizing life is morally best while denying that it is the life to be chosen. Finally, say what would have to be true for your verdict to reverse.

End with a single line of exactly this form: Verdict: <one sentence, stating which ideal has the better of it and why>.
