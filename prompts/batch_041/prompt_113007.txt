Be sharp, not a strawman, concede small points about evidence or causation if you have to, but never give up the main claim. Stay in character until I say stop. Here's my opening. I've been calling something the moral opportunity set: the set of morally loaded actions actually available to an agent, given their circumstances, talents, and information. Take two people, A and B, with identical characters, identical commitments, identical decision procedures. The only difference is luck of birth. A is born somewhere the only way to save a drowning child is to swim out and risk her own life; B is born somewhere a well-funded lifeguard service already pulls every drowning child out before anyone else can act. A saves one child and nearly dies. B saves nobody because there's nobody left to save. You want to say their moral worth is the same, because each would have done the same thing in the other's shoes. But their moral opportunity sets differ, and the difference is pure luck. If you say their moral worth is identical, it looks like actually saving a child adds nothing to moral worth—only the counterfactual disposition matters. If you say A's moral worth is greater, you've let constitutive luck in through the back door. So which horn do you take, and what does the control principle look like on that horn?
