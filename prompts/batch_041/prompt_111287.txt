A friend in my reading group defends Moore's proof of an external world, and I have drawn up the following argument to show that it proves far too much. I suspect that I am the one who has slipped somewhere, but I cannot see where.

(1) I know that here is one hand and here is another — I know it in the ordinary way in which I know where my own feet are.
(2) So I know that at least two physical objects exist.
(3) If I know that, then I know that there is an external world, that is, that some things exist whose existence does not depend on any mind.
(4) So I know that there is an external world.
(5) My friend's rule, which he calls the ordinary-way rule, states: whatever I know in the ordinary way, I know to be true whatever it entails.
(6) The rule is indifferent to the example. It gives me knowledge that I am not a handless brain in a vat, and — since I know that I had breakfast this morning — knowledge that the world did not come into existence five minutes ago complete with my apparent memories.
(7) I do not know either of those things, and no amount of staring at my own hands can give me that knowledge.
(8) So the ordinary-way rule is unsound, and Moore's proof goes with it.

My friend's reply is that (2) to (4) is nothing but the inference from a known premise to what that premise entails, so that if the argument is bad the fault can only lie in (1) — which he finds absurd. I should much rather keep (1) than keep (5).
