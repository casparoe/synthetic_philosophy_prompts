I teach a combined upper-level course on early modern philosophy and the history of economic thought, and I'm writing the final. I want one essay question on Berkeley's views on money — the Querist material, where he runs the whole thing as a long string of questions and is basically campaigning for Ireland to get a national bank and paper credit. What I want the question to force is this: he treats gold and silver as having no worth in themselves, as mere tickets or counters that stand for goods and labour, whose value is set by convention and whose real job is to excite industry — and the students will want to say "well, he was an idealist, so of course he thought money was imaginary" and stop there. I want them to actually sort out whether his economics hangs on his immaterialism or just rides along beside it — i.e. could a materialist (Locke, Hume, take your pick) hold the very same account of money as a conventional token? Does immaterialism force money to be unreal, or does it not? And what, if anything, does the metaphysics actually add — is there a principled link (maybe through his thing about signs and language) or is it just two separate projects sharing a temperament?

So: write the essay question, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves separating an A from a B, with a one-line note on how students typically blow each point. And tell me where your own model answer is most likely to be wrong — what a Berkeley scholar would push back on, and what would actually change your mind. I don't want an answer that sounds more confident than the scholarship can carry.
