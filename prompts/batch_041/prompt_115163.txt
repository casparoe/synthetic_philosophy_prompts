Consider the Challenger disaster — specifically the sequence of teleconferences and the final launch decision on the night of 27–28 January 1986, in which engineers from Morton Thiokol raised concerns about the performance of the O-rings at low temperatures, were asked to reconsider, and the launch proceeded the next morning. I want you to analyze that episode through I. J. Good’s theorem on the value of information: roughly, that under expected-utility maximization, costless information cannot have negative expected value, since an agent can always ignore it and retain the expected utility of the prior decision. Do not treat the theorem as a slogan. Explain what the theorem actually requires of an agent — a well-defined prior, a likelihood for the signal, a decision problem whose utility is fixed in advance, and the capacity to condition on the signal — and then ask whether the Challenger decision-makers satisfied those conditions. The interesting question for me is not the familiar one about managerial pressure or schedule risk. It is whether the episode is a counterexample to Good’s theorem or instead a case where the theorem’s antecedent conditions fail in a way that is philosophically revealing. To sharpen that, I want you to use a distinction I have been calling the acquisition–integration gap: information can be free to acquire but costly to integrate, because integrating it requires revising a model, reopening a decision that has already been socially ratified, or accepting a conclusion one has strong institutional reasons to avoid. Treat that gap as a proposed amendment to the theorem’s notion of “free,” and test it against the episode.

In working through this, keep the following features of the case in view: the engineers’ information was not a single crisp signal but a set of probabilistic judgments about temperature and O-ring erosion; the relevant decision was not simply “launch or not” but a nested set of decisions about whether to seek more data, whether to delay for better data, and whether to treat the existing data as decision-relevant; and the utility function of the decision-makers was itself contested, since the cost of a delay was weighed against a risk that some participants regarded as unacceptable. I do not want a general essay on groupthink, and I do not want a list of cognitive biases. I want a disciplined mapping: what the theorem says an ideal Bayesian agent would have done at each stage, where the actual agents departed from that ideal, and whether the departure is best described as a failure of information acquisition, a failure of information integration, or a failure to have a determinate utility function at all. Then tell me what the episode shows about the theorem’s scope — whether it is a theorem about rational agents that simply does not apply to organizations, or whether it exposes a gap in the theorem even for individual agents, namely that information can be free in the economist’s sense and yet not free in the sense required for the theorem’s proof to go through.

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. The five items should be: (1) the theorem’s conditions and what they demand of an agent; (2) what the ideal Bayesian would have done in the Challenger decision sequence; (3) where the actual decision-makers departed from that ideal; (4) whether the acquisition–integration gap is a genuine amendment to the theorem or merely a restatement of the requirement that information be costless; and (5) what the episode ultimately shows about the theorem’s scope, including whether it is a counterexample, a scope restriction, or neither.
