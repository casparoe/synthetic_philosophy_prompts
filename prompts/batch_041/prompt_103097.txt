I write about philosophy and the mind for a general-interest ideas magazine, and I am preparing to interview a philosopher who has spent years defending the strongly intentionalist line on self-deception: roughly, that the phenomenon is both real and genuinely paradoxical, in that the self-deceiver must in some sense already hold the belief he is setting out to acquire the opposite of, and must intend to acquire it by methods he has reason to regard as unreliable. His book-length defence leans on a partitioned or compartmentalised mind, with the deceiving and the deceived sub-agents drawn from one and the same person.

Please draft nine or ten interview questions. I want to stay away from well-trodden ground: nothing along the lines of what is self-deception, and no invitation simply to summarise the book. Instead, build a sequence in which the standard moves are made to do real work. At a minimum I need one question on the static paradox (how a single subject can both know and not know the same proposition without quietly equivocating on knows), one on the strategic or dynamic paradox (how one can deliberately set out to be convinced by evidence one regards as inadequate), and one on whether the partitioned-mind solution covertly reintroduces two persons, or, failing that, collapses into ordinary weakness of will.

For each of those three, supply the follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, whether that answer appeals to levels or degrees of belief, to acceptance as distinct from belief, or to the difference between the process and the product of self-deception, and press it one step further.

I should also like two questions on the asymmetries the account has trouble with: first, the difference between deceiving oneself into believing what one hopes for and deceiving oneself into believing what one dreads, and second, twisted cases in which the content of the deception is itself an attitude or a belief about one's own beliefs rather than a plain factual claim. Finish with a question about what would count, on his own terms, as evidence that no such partitioned machinery is needed, that is, what an entirely deflationary, motivationally biased account of belief formation would have to get right before he would concede that the phenomenon is less paradoxical than he claims.

Please write in British English. Keep the register that of a serious ideas interview rather than a gotcha: the questions should read as informed and sympathetic, but genuinely probing.
