My brother-in-law and I have a running arguement that's about to ruin Christmas dinner again. He reckons that if you had to choose between one person being tortured for fifty years and an absolutely gigantic number of people each getting a single speck of grit in the eye for a second, then once the number gets big enough the grit is obviously the lesser evil — a speck of grit is basically nothing, and there's only one of the tortured bloke. I think that's monstrous and I can't say why. Neither of us has read any philosophy, we've just been going round in circles for three years.

Before we go at it again I want to know what an actual answer would even have to look like — not an answer, the checklist. What would any decent account of weighing up loads of tiny bad things against one enormous bad thing have to get right? The cases it has to give the right verdict on, the distinctions it can't just squash (does it matter whether the tiny harms are seperated across millions of people or all land on one person? does it matter whether they're harms that actually happen or only a risk of happening? does it matter if the tiny harms are so small nobody notices them?), and anything it can't contradict. For each item tell me why it's on the list, and be straight with me about which ones are genuine requirements and which are just how one side likes to set the board up. If some of them can't all be true at once, say so — that's the bit I actually want, because I suspect that's why we never get anywhere. And which single requirement knocks out the most of the obvious answers?

Two things keep nagging at me and I don't know if they're silly. First: is there such a thing as a harm so small it doesn't count at all? Because if there is, then there's a biggest amount of grit-in-eye harm you could ever pile up — there's only so many people who'll ever exist — and maybe that total is still smaller than one torture, which would settle the whole thing. But if harms can be as tiny as you like, then his number has to be bigger than any number of people who could ever exist, and I want to know whether that's even a sensible question or whether the example falls apart before we start. Second: does it change anything if it's one person's life instead of millions of people? Like one bloke getting a speck of grit every few seconds for eighty years versus one bloke being tortured for fifty — same puzzle, or a different one?

Can you write it in British English please. And don't worry about being tactful about his argument.
