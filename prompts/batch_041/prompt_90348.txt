Me and two friends spent most of a dinner last week re-litigating the MIU/MU puzzle from GEB and I still can't tell who actually won. In case it's rusty: you start with the string MI and you've got four rewrite rules (stick a U on the end of anything ending in I, double whatever comes after the M, replace III with U, delete UU), and the question is whether you can ever produce MU. You can't, and the usual reason is that you stop playing the game and notice something about the game instead -- the number of I's is never divisible by 3, since doubling a non-multiple of 3 keeps you off the multiples and subtracting 3 doesn't change that, while MU has zero I's. So the proof only exists if you hop out of the system and talk about it rather than in it. Which is where we started yelling. Friend A thinks this hop is the whole secret of intelligence and basically what self-improvement *is*: any system can always jump to a bigger system, so there's no ceiling, and that's why minds (and maybe AIs) can bootstrap past their own rules. Friend B says that's cheap -- the hop is just a proof carried out in a bigger system, and if you formalize the bigger system it's still a system, so nothing non-mechanical happened and the phrase explains nothing. Friend C thinks the hop is a genuinely non-mechanical act of insight and that Goedel is on his side, since every consistent system has a truth it can't get to, so something has to be able to step outside. I kept dragging in the Loeb-obstacle / tiling-agents stuff from the self-modifying-AI world -- the bit where a system can't prove the soundness of its own successor, so the trust has to be imported from outside and the regress never actually closes -- and I couldn't tell if that was a decisive hit on A or just B's point in fancier clothes. Someone also cooked up a variant where you can additionally turn UU into I, and the mod-3 trick obviously dies on the spot, which somehow made everyone *more* confident rather than less.

What I want is an essay that actually settles this rather than surveying it. So lay out the MU case properly first -- what's inside the system, what the invariant argument does, why it has to happen outside -- then pull apart at least three readings of the jump: the deflationary one (it's just a proof in a bigger system), the system-relative one (the jump is real but never final, and the genuinely creative part is *choosing* which bigger system, or which property, to look at), and the romantic one (a non-mechanical insight, licensed by incompleteness). Say which of them survives contact with the details. Two things I specifically want you to chew on, because I think that's where the real disagreement is hiding. First, the asymmetry: the invariant move can only ever prove that something is *unreachable*; it can never show that something is reachable, which takes an actual construction. So jumping out seems to name two very different activities -- one defensive and negative (find a preserved quantity), one productive and positive (build the thing, or see an analogy that wasn't there before) -- and I suspect half the fight is people running those together. Second, whether the always-a-bigger-system reply really answers the tiling/Loeb worry or just restates it: fine, a bigger system exists, but the system you're in can't certify the jump, so who is doing the jumping, and does always-a-bigger-system buy you anything if the regress never terminates? Then give me the verdict, not a hedge: does jumping out of the system name a real cognitive act, and can it carry the weight people hang on it (no ceiling on self-improvement, creativity as escape from any formalism), or is it doing less work than the phrase suggests? No cites or reading lists needed, I just want the arguement, and please don't end on it's-complicated, actually commit.
