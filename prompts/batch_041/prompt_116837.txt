I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar on the nonidentity problem, and the bit I keep redoing is the ranking at the end. Heres what I need.

Below are the main moves people make to try to take the sting out of the problem — either to explain why the risky policy really is wrong after all, or to explain why our reaction to it is a mistake. Give each one a score from 0 to 1 for how much of the problem it actually dissolves (0 = it just restates the intuition in fancier words, 1 = accept it and the puzzle is genuinely gone), then rank them from most to least dissolving. One line of justification each, please, not an essay.

1. Wrongness needs a victim: an act is wrong only if it's worse for someone, and the kid under the risky policy isn't worse off than they would have been, since otherwise they wouldn't have existed at all.

2. Identity doesn't matter: compare the outcomes by how well off people are in them, whoever those people turn out to be.

3. A life worth living isn't a harm: being created can't be held against the creator so long as the life is worth having, so the complaint has nowhere to land.

4. Harm without the worse-off test: keep harm at the centre but drop the requirement that the person be worse off than they would otherwise have been — you can be harmed by being created in a certain condition even if you couldn't have existed any other way.

5. Look at the action, not the outcome: the choice is wrong because it's reckless, or because it shows insufficient concern for whoever ends up existing, and that's the end of it — no seperate victim required.

And then a sixth, which I'm not sure I made up or not, and I want you to place it: the contingency discount. A complaint about having been created gets weighted by how much the person's existence depended on the act in question — fully contingent, tiny weight; partly contingent, partial weight; so the kid in the risky policy has a small but nonzero complaint and the act is wrong to a correspondingly small degree, instead of the all-or-nothing thing. Score it, tell me where it lands, and tell me honestly whether it's a distinct move or just one of the five wearing a hat.
