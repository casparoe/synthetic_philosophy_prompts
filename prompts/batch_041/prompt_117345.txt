I am convening a graduate seminar on the idea of societal collapse, and I should like you to work through a problem with me. In the first half of term we read around the end of the Late Bronze Age, the Terminal Classic period in the Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui, and our discussions kept snagging on the same question: are we dealing with a genuine kind of event, or with a dramatic word for ordinary political and economic change? One of the students has circulated a scorecard, which I should like you to apply. It scores a case on five axes, each from 0 to 1, where 0 means the feature carries on without appreciable discontinuity and 1 means it is thoroughly and durably lost. The axes are: (i) settlement and demography, that is, whether the previous range of settlement sizes and the population the landscape can support persist; (ii) political integration, that is, whether any authority above the level of the locality survives; (iii) connectivity, that is, whether long-distance exchange and the shared elite styles that travel along with it persist; (iv) administrative and symbolic apparatus, that is, writing, calendrical and record-keeping systems, and the monumental display of authority; and (v) ritual and cosmological infrastructure, that is, whether the physical plant and the legitimating cosmology of the old order continue to be used and believed in.

Please apply the scorecard to four cases: the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Terminal Classic Maya lowlands, Rapa Nui, and, as a calibration case, the western Roman Empire in the fifth and sixth centuries. Give me five numbers per case, state the rule by which you aggregate them into a single collapse score for each case, and then rank the four cases by aggregate score, allowing ties where you think the underlying evidence genuinely does not separate them. For each case, say in a sentence or two what drives the number on whichever axis it scores most distinctively, and flag any axis where you judge the evidence too thin to support a number at all, rather than supplying one anyway. Then step back and give me two further judgements: first, a figure between 0 and 1 for how much each classification depends on the choice of baseline, that is, on the century one selects as the point of comparison; and second, your own verdict on whether collapse picks out a natural kind of historical process or is a category imposed by the observer who has decided in advance what counts as a society's proper form.

I am interested in your reasoning rather than your reading, so please do not supply references or a bibliography. Kindly write in British English, and keep the whole answer to roughly 1200 words.
