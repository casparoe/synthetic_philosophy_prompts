One of my colleagues in the economics department, who teaches our social choice course, offered the following thumbnail of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem over lunch, and I have been repeating it more confidently than I should:

"Harsanyi proved that if you accept the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms for individual preferences and for social preferences, and you accept the Pareto principle — that if everyone prefers A to B, so does society — then the only coherent way to aggregate is a sum of individual utilities. So classical utilitarianism, equal weights and all, falls straight out of rationality plus Pareto, and there is no room left for giving extra weight to the worst off. It is also why Arrow's impossibility theorem is not the last word: Harsanyi simply sidesteps it by letting utility be cardinal instead of ordinal. And it settles the old quarrel with Rawls, since maximin is a rule for the pathologically risk-averse, and once you set up the veil of ignorance correctly you are forced to be a utilitarian."

I suspect this is broadly in the right region and wrong in ways that matter, and I would rather not build a lecture around it until I know where the seams are. Please audit it sentence by sentence: what a careful welfare economist would accept, what is a permissible simplification, and what is a misstatement — in particular, whether the premises the thumbnail lists are the ones the theorem actually needs, and whether the equal weights, the Arrow remark, and the Rawls conclusion follow from anything in the result itself. I am inclined to think the last sentence overreaches, but I cannot say why. No citations or reading lists, please; reconstruct the theorem in your own terms and tell me which premise is doing the work.
