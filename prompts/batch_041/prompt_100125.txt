For most of a working life I did experimental condensed-matter physics, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science with more enthusiasm than competence. At a seminar last term, in a discussion of whether the properties of a solid can in principle be read off from those of its constituents, a colleague brought the matter to a close with the observation that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. I found myself nodding, and then, on the drive home, unable to say what I had nodded at. I would like help turning that sentence into something I can actually examine.

Please treat it as a piece of language rather than a settled doctrine, and lay out the distinct claims it could be making, as a menu rather than a single interpretation. I am chiefly interested in the several senses that greater than might carry here: the mereological sense, in which the whole has parts or features that a bare inventory of the parts omits; the explanatory or derivational sense, in which the low-level description fails to entail the high-level one; the nomological sense, in which the higher-level regularities are not consequences of the lower-level laws; the causal sense, in which the whole is said to act back upon its parts; and the merely epistemic sense, in which we cannot in practice compute the whole from the parts even though nothing metaphysical is at stake. For each reading, please say whether it is trivially or definitionally true, substantive and defensible, or false or incoherent, and what it would take to make it precise.

Then tell me which of these readings would actually license the conclusion my colleague drew — that the reductive programme is unnecessary, or impossible, and that we may stop asking the question — and whether any reading really does the work he needed, or whether the sentence only appears to settle the matter because the equivocation between readings is invisible at speed. I would also like to know which reading people most often have in mind when they say this in a seminar, even when they could not state it if pressed, and whether the conversational usefulness of the phrase depends on leaving that reading unspecified.

Two further things would help me. First, the phrase has a mirror image that I meet just as often — that some higher-level phenomenon is nothing but its lower-level constituents, or that life is nothing but chemistry — and I would like to know whether the two slogans are contraries, contradictories, or simply about different questions. Second, give me two or three diagnostic questions I can put to the next person who says it, so that I can find out which of the readings is in play before the conversation moves on.
