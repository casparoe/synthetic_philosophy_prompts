The question was: "Critics have charged that evolutionary psychology trades in just-so stories rather than genuine science. Explain what that charge amounts to, assess how well the leading defenses of evolutionary psychology answer it, and say whether the charge applies with equal force to evolutionary approaches to human behavior generally." The student's answer reads as follows: "The charge that evolutionary psychology trades in just-so stories is really a family of charges, and it is worth separating them. The first is that the field assumes that any interesting feature of human behavior must be an adaptation, that is, a trait that selection built because it raised fitness in ancestral environments. The second is that the explanations are untestable: whatever the data turn out to be, a fitness-based story can be constructed after the fact to accommodate them. Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper on the spandrels of San Marco is the canonical statement of both charges. Their target was evolutionary psychology's picture of the mind as a bundle of modules designed in the environment of evolutionary adaptedness, or EEA, which they identify with the Pleistocene savanna of roughly 1.8 million to 10,000 years ago. Their central example is the spandrel: a feature that was originally selected for one function and then co-opted for a different one, so that its current form is a byproduct of the earlier adaptation rather than an adaptation in its own right. Defenders of evolutionary psychology offer two main replies. The first is that their hypotheses are testable and have been tested. Cosmides and Tooby's work on social exchange is the standard illustration: people perform poorly on the abstract Wason selection task but much better when the same task is framed as a problem of detecting cheaters, which they read as evidence for a dedicated cheater-detection mechanism, a result that is now accepted by most researchers in the field. The second reply is that the charge of untestability, taken literally, proves too much, since it would also impugn population genetics and much of behavioral ecology. My own view is that the objection is best stated as the claim that practitioners rarely specify in advance what would count as disconfirming evidence, rather than as the claim that no evolutionary hypothesis about the mind could be tested. Where the charge does bite is on the breadth of the claims rather than their testability. If cheater detection is a real adaptation, the same style of argument can be run for jealousy, gossip, status-seeking, and so on, and the proliferation of modules eventually becomes a liability. The same worry applies to human behavioral ecology, which shares evolutionary psychology's commitment to massive modularity and differs mainly in studying small-scale societies instead of undergraduates. So the label is too blunt to do much work, but the underlying complaint about post hoc storytelling is a real one."
