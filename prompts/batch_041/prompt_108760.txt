I want a case analysis of a real episode — the global stock market crash of Monday, 19 October 1987, Black Monday — read through the no-trade theorem and the surrounding literature on what a market price knows. Take the following as given rather than trying to recall it: the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 508 points that day, about 22.6 per cent, from 2,246.74 at the open to 1,738.74 at the close, the largest one-day percentage decline in its history; the S&P 500 fell about 20 per cent; the fall was near-simultaneous across the major world markets; and no public announcement that day is generally thought commensurate with a repricing of that magnitude. The prior week had brought an unexpectedly large trade-deficit figure, a tax bill aimed at leveraged buyouts, and a falling dollar, but the standard complaint is that nothing on that list carries the weight. Two mechanisms are usually credited with the amplitude — portfolio insurance, which mechanically sold index futures as prices fell, and a self-reinforcing contagion of fear — and you may take both for granted.

The theory to use: the no-trade theorem, in Milgrom and Stokey's 1982 formulation, holds that when the market is in an efficient equilibrium, when there are no noise traders or other non-rational participants, and when the way traders acquire information is itself common knowledge, then traders holding private information cannot profit from it, because the mere willingness to trade reveals that information and no one will take the other side. Restate the result in your own terms first, and say which of its assumptions are doing the real work.

Then map the episode onto it. Who are the traders, what is the state of the world they are uncertain about, and what exactly is common knowledge among them? Does portfolio insurance sit inside or outside the theorem — an exogenous liquidity or hedging motive of the kind the theorem sets aside, or an informational trade of the kind it rules out — and does the answer change if we treat it as a rule-following non-rational participant? Next, the epistemological question: what does the closing price of 19 October tell an outside observer about fundamentals? If a 22 per cent repricing can happen with no news, is the price still a sufficient statistic for the state, and if not, what is it? Then the disagreement premise: every completed trade implies a difference in belief, and for agents with common priors such differences cannot survive being common knowledge, so the observed volume must mean that some assumption fails. Which failure fits the evidence best — genuinely heterogeneous priors, a failure of common knowledge, or non-informational demand? Finally, consider the price as a public signal that participants read and trade on, so that a move can justify itself without news, and set that against the Grossman–Stiglitz point that a fully revealing price destroys the incentive to gather the information it reveals, so that a market needs noise in order to function as a knowledge-generating institution. Is the crash an instance of this, or something the framework does not reach?

Then reverse the direction: what does the episode do to the theory — refute it, mark its boundary, or fall outside what it was ever meant to address? Where is the theory simply silent, and I mean silent about the dynamics, about the speed of the contagion, and about whether the new price was right? I would rather have a disciplined account of the gap than a forced one.

Close with a verdict on a single question: was Black Monday a failure of the market's epistemic function, or the market working as a coordination device whose output was never a knowledge claim in the first place? Say which reading the theory supports and which the episode supports, and flag any step where your answer turns on a contested empirical claim rather than on the theory.
