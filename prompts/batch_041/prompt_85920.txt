I am a second-year doctoral student in a science, technology, and society program, and I sit on the reading group my department runs on research ethics. Last week we took up a proposal to pause a line of dual-use work — the familiar case in which the same technique that might improve biosecurity might equally be repurposed — and something happened that I have not been able to put down. The proverb "better safe than sorry" was invoked on both sides of the room, and it appeared to do real work each time. One colleague argued that we ought not proceed until we can be confident the findings cannot be misused; another argued that we ought not create a world in which an entire class of defensive research goes unstudied, since refusing to act is also a choice and one we may be sorrier for. When I asked each of them what precisely "better safe than sorry" was claiming, they both treated the meaning as self-evident, and their answers turned out to have almost nothing in common.

What I would like is a menu rather than a verdict. Set out the distinct precise claims the proverb can be making when it is put to work in a policy or research-ethics argument, ordered from the nearly vacuous to the very strong, and give a verdict on each: which readings are trivial — true, but carrying no weight in the argument they were used in — which are substantive and defensible, which are false or self-undermining, and which reading each of my two colleagues must be relying on if their conclusions are to follow. I am particularly interested in the joints I suspect the proverb is hiding. Is it a claim about expected value and asymmetric loss — a tie-breaker that says something only once the probabilities and payoffs are already fixed? A claim about who bears the burden of proof, and against what threshold? A claim about what to do under uncertainty as distinct from measurable risk, and if so, what notion of uncertainty is doing the work? A deontic claim that certain harms may not be risked at any probability, however small? Or an institutional claim that we systematically underestimate the risks of the novel and the benefits of the familiar, so that a bias toward caution is simply a corrective? I would also like to know whether any of these readings survives the objection that inaction has a body count of its own, and whether any survives the objection that the same logic would forbid the remedy as readily as the harm.

Finally, a colleague floated the rule that we should take only reversible steps: tell me whether that is a precisification of the proverb, a rival to it, or a change of subject; and tell me which single reading is the one that actually yields a moratorium rather than another meeting. For what it is worth, my own guess is that only one reading is both non-vacuous and defensible — the one that combines an asymmetry of losses with a claim that our estimates for novel risks are biased low — and I would like to be told whether that reading collapses two claims that ought to be kept apart.
