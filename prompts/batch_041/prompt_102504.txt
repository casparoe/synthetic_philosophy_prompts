I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and last week I sat through a conference panel where two historians went at each other for forty minutes and I genuinely still can't tell who won. I'd like you to referee it.

Here's the shared background, which neither of them disputes. A mid-sized port town lost its main industry over about five years in the 1890s. Three things happened: a big storm wrecked the harbor works, a tariff change killed the export market, and a bank failure took out the firms that might otherwise have retooled. That's it. No factual disagreement.

Then it went like this.

A: The collapse was overdetermined — any one of those three would probably have done it, so there's no interesting question of "the cause." Asking which one *really* did it is close to a category mistake.

B: He's hiding behind a word. Overdetermined covers two totally different things. One is redundancy — two independent bullets, either of which kills. The other is robustness — a pile of factors, none sufficient alone, that together make the outcome stable and hard to avoid. A is using the second sense while borrowing the rhetoric of the first, and that's teh sleight of hand. Real redundancy is rare in history because the factors interact: the storm made the tariff bite harder, and the bank failure was partly downstream of the tariff.

A: Fine, call it robustness, but it still answers a real question. "What made the difference" isn't the only thing explanation does. Sometimes you want to know why it *didn't* go otherwise — why this is the sort of town that keeps getting wiped out. And "the cause" is relative to the question anyway. The cause of a fire depends on whether you're asking why it started or why it spread. Once the question is fixed, the answer is objective.

B: That's exactly how you smuggle politics into history. If causes are relative to interests, then "the cause" is whatever story sells. The Marxist says the tariff, the free-trader says the storm, and there's no fact left to appeal to.

A: No — interest-relativity fixes the *question*, not the answer. Given that you're asking why it spread, there's a fact of the matter about what made it spread.

B: Then show me the fact. To say the tariff made the difference, you need to know what happens in the nearest world where the tariff didn't pass, and historical counterfactuals are hopelessly underdetermined.

A: That's a problem for every causal claim in history, including your bullet test.

B: Granted. But then the honest report on this town is "we don't know what did it," and *overdetermined* is just a dignified way of saying that.

A: Overdetermination isn't ignorance. It's a positive finding: no single factor was necessary.

Please adjudicate this point by point rather than picking a winner, and be upfront about where your reasoning is shakiest and what would push you to a different verdict. I don't have a dog in this fight — I just want to know who's right about what.

Use these sections, in this order:

**Where they actually agree** — short, about 150 words. I want to see how much of the fight is verbal before we get to substance.

**Move by move** — the bulk of it, maybe 700-800 words. Go through the exchange in order, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say what the person should have said if they wanted to be right.

**The verdict** — about 200 words. Who has the better overall position, and what is each of them missing.

**What would change your mind** — about 150 words. Which argument or consideration would flip your verdict, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail.
