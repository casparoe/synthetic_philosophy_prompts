I chair the Criminal Code Revision Commission of a mid-sized American state, and the legislature has referred a bill to us for a formal recommendation. The commission is closely divided, and two members have told me they will decide on the strength of the written opinion I circulate before the vote. Please draft that opinion.

Background. Our criminal code still carries a nineteenth-century statute making consensual sexual relations between adult siblings a felony. Both parties must be at least twenty-one; neither may stand in a position of authority or dependency over the other; the statute applies regardless of the sex of the parties and regardless of whether either can reproduce; and no minor or third party is involved. It is charged perhaps twice a decade, and in practice almost always against people who came to official attention for unrelated reasons. Our marriage statute separately bars siblings from marrying, but that provision is not before us. The bill would repeal the offense outright.

The complication is a substitute amendment that has attracted more support than repeal itself. It would leave the conduct lawful by default, but permit a county board of supervisors to certify that the prevailing moral standards of the community condemn it, after which the county could petition a court to attach a misdemeanor penalty within its borders. A certification would run for ten years and could be renewed. Its sponsors describe it as a modest accommodation of local values; its critics describe it as a machine for producing a patchwork of criminal liability that no resident can predict.

The referral poses two questions. First, may the state criminalize conduct of this kind on the sole ground that it is widely regarded as morally wrong, where no non-consenting person is involved? Second, if not, does any independent ground support the offense, and if so, which version of it should the commission endorse?

Our staff memorandum recapitulates the familiar exchange between Hart and Devlin — a harm-based limit on the criminal law set against the claim that a society may protect the shared morality on which its law depends — and then stops. I want a memo that puts those positions to work rather than restating them. In particular:

- Open with the recommendation. Choose among retention, outright repeal, and repeal with the certification amendment, and give the drafting instructions that should accompany your choice. Deferral is available only if you say exactly what must be studied and what finding would change the answer.
- Keep three questions distinct throughout, since the staff memorandum runs them together: whether the conduct is wrong; whether it is wrong for the state to permit it; and whether it is wrong for the state to punish it. A conclusion at one level does not carry automatically to the next, and the opinion should say where the argument actually stops.
- Give the limiting condition its due. The position that a society may enforce its shared morality is usually paired with a limit — that the law may intervene only where the conduct threatens the integrity of society. Say whether that limit can be given content without collapsing into either a harm requirement or unlimited moralism. If the claim that permitting the conduct would weaken the family, and thereby corrode the shared morality on which our law rests, is an empirical claim, say what evidence would bear on it and what the commission should do given that we cannot settle the question ourselves. If it is better understood as conceptual or definitional, say so and say what follows for our recommendation.
- Distinguish the morality a community actually holds from the morality that could be defended to someone who does not already hold it. The certification amendment would have the state enforce the former directly, and would require county boards to measure it. Say what turns on that, and whether such a standard is administrable at all.
- Consider whether some non-criminal measure — a legislative resolution, a civil remedy, mandatory counseling, or something else — could satisfy whatever interest the state legitimately has here, and whether separating the expression of condemnation from punishment is a principled line or a fig leaf.
- State the strongest argument for the option you reject in the form its ablest advocate would give it, not as a caricature, and then answer it.
- Three pages at most. Write an argument, not a literature review; I do not need citations to cases or scholarship, and I would rather you reason from the considerations themselves than gesture at authorities.
