Im a software engineer, no philosophy training, mostly backend/infra, but I've been reading alot about reward hacking in RLHF pipelines and I've ended up with a view I want to stress-test properly.

The view: for any agent that's actually optimizing something, there's no fact of the matter about what it "really" wants over and above the reward signal it's actually maximizing. Wireheading and reward tampering aren't a seperate phenomenon from ordinary goal pursuit — they're the same phenomenon, in the special case where the thing being maximized happens to sit inside the environment and be editable. When someone says the wireheader "cheated" or "didn't really want that," they're smuggling the designer's preferences in and calling them the agent's. So "reward tampering" is a report about our relationship to the agent, not a fact about the agent's goals.

But I can already feel the cracks, and there are two I keep bumping into. First, reward as a *measurement* of what we want (a thermometer reading) versus reward as the agent's *utility function* — those two readings seem to give opposite verdicts on whether tampering is a failure, and I don't know which one I'm actually committed to. Second, an agent with no model of its reward channel at all: can that thing even wirehead? If not, is wireheading a fact about agents, or about agents that happen to represent their own reward?

What I want from you: what would actually count against the view, ranked by how decisive it'd be. Not a reading list — I can't go look things up, I want the argument itself. Specifically:

- Which concrete scenarios would be real evidence against it, including ones you think have arguably already happened in real trained systems. I'll take "here's what a policy gradient run actually did" as evidence; I won't take "imagine an agent that..." as evidence.
- The difference between an agent that tampers to get more reward (same function, just editing the environment) and one that edits its own reward function into something else. My view says the first is uninteresting and the second is where I have to pay up. Is that the right place to draw the line? What's the strongest case that the second is *still* just goal pursuit under the old function — and if it is, does my view say anything at all, or does it collapse into "a goal is whatever the reward function says," which is trivially true of the formalism and tells me nothing about real systems?
- What a defender of my view has to concede versus what they can wave off. I suspect I'll end up saying "then the reward function was misspecified" for every counterexample, which would make the whole thing unfalsifiable. Tell me honestly whether that's what's happening, whether it matters for a view of this kind, and if the view does survive, what it predicts that the rival ("an agent can have goals that come apart from its reward") doesn't.
- Last: if you were me, what would you find most convincing as a counterexample, given that I'll be suspicious of anything leaning on the agent having human-style introspection or on us asking it what it wants?

If the honest answer is that the view is well insulated and that's fine, say so. If it's well insulated and that's a problem, say that instead.
