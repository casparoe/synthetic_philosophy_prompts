Ayer's stuff about life after death, and I keep going back and forth on whether the pairing actually holds up or if I'm forcing it. Ayer's line, the logical positivist one, is that talk about surviving your own death is either straight-up meaningless or at least unverifiable — nothing you could ever observe would settle it, so "I'll still be around after I die" isn't really a proper factual claim at all. And then in his writing about persons he pushes the harder version: a person just is a living human being, identity runs through the body and the brain, so the idea of you persisting as a disembodied someone who still remembers being you and still wants things isn't even a coherent picture, it's a category mistake dressed up as a hope.

Here's what I want. Read the film as if its ghost-rules were a worked-out theory of what survival would have to be, and hold that theory up against Ayer. A few things I actually want you to engage with, not just gesture at:

- The twist only lands because from the inside Malcolm can't tell he's dead. His memories, his intentions, his sense of being mid-project all carry on exactly as before. Isn't that the film dramatizing Ayer's verification point rather than refuting it? The very reason he doesn't notice is the reason there's no test.

- But the film also gives us Cole, and Cole sees them. So inside the fiction survival becomes an observable, intersubjective fact with a detection method. Does that quietly convert Ayer's "meaningless" into a live empirical hypothesis — and does the film have any idea it's doing that?

- And the ghost-physics: the dead perceive, remember, feel, but they can't seem to move things, can't get the wife to hear them, can't get a locked door to budge. Is that a bodily criterion sneaking back in, the film conceding that to be a person you need causal grip on the world and that losing the body means losing exactly the thing that made you one? Or is it just a plot convenience so the reveal works?
