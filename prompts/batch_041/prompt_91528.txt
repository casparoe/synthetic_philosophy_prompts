I sit on the committee of our residents' association, and we are at last deciding how to spend roughly £9,000 that has accumulated over a decade of jumble sales and a small bequest from a former resident. There are five or six plausible projects — a ramp to the corner shop, brighter lighting on the path behind the garages, a bench and some planters on the green, soundproofing for the flats that face the ring road, and repairs to the communal shed — and we have to pick one. One of our number, who spent thirty years as a council officer, has produced a spreadsheet that is meant to settle the matter: for each project he scores, out of ten, how much better off it would leave each household, adds the scores up, and recommends whichever total is highest. He calls it nothing more than common sense with the arithmetic done properly.

Two things about it trouble me and I cannot put my finger on why. The first is that his totals only mean anything if a point gained by the retired couple at number 12 is worth exactly as much as a point gained by the family with three children at number 4; when I asked him to run it again with number 12's scores counted at double weight, on the grounds that they are the ones who would feel the change most, the recommendation flipped from the lighting to the ramp. He says the weighting is a detail. The second is that when I asked how he arrived at the scores, it turned out he had asked each household how much of their own money they would be willing to put towards each project and converted those figures into points; the households on the private side of the road, who have more money, mostly gave the larger numbers.

I am not against the spreadsheet, and I do not want to be the person who blocks a decision out of fastidiousness. But I would like to understand what is really going on — what his method is assuming, whether those assumptions can be defended, how much of the outcome is settled by his choice of scoring rather than by any facts about the projects themselves, and what I should actually propose at the meeting. Please write me an analysis I could read the night before, rather than a set of tips on handling difficult colleagues; I did a term of philosophy as an undergraduate, which is probably why this nags at me, though I could not tell you what it reminds me of, and I am happy to be taken through the argument properly rather than soothed. Please write in British English.
