I am a novelist several years into a book that has become, in effect, two books sharing a spine. It follows a single woman from a single childhood, but at one late scene she either boards a train or does not, and I have written both continuations in full. I now realize that the two halves keep making claims on each other: a debt incurred on one track is invoked on the other; a promise made before the fork is treated as binding after it; one sentence appears verbatim in both halves and is only intelligible if the woman who speaks it is one person rather than two. My editor says this is a structural error. I suspect it is the whole point, and that the question underneath it is the old one about what it means for a person in another possible course of events to be the same person at all.

I would like you to draft the joint statement that two philosophers could sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration on this question: a counterpart theorist, who holds that a person in another possible world is at best a counterpart of the actual person rather than that very person, and a transworld-identity theorist, who holds that the very same individual exists in more than one world and that identity across worlds is not a relation of resemblance. I want the document they could both put their names to, not a verdict in favor of either.

Please include, as a numbered list of agreed claims, whatever both can accept: about what a possible world is doing in each framework; about which de re modal claims each framework can and cannot paraphrase; about whether the necessity of identity is a substantive thesis or an artifact of how the semantics is set up; and about which of my narrative problems, if any, are settled by logic alone and which are matters of interpretation.

Then give me the residual disagreements in a table, with one column per camp, each formulation written so that the camp it describes would accept the wording as its own. I expect the cruxes to include at least these: whether talk of a person doing otherwise in another world requires a primitive this-worldly subject, or can always be translated into talk about a counterpart; whether transworld identity needs individual essences or bare particulars, or comes cheaply; and whether the objection that a person could not have done otherwise if their counterpart is defined by what they actually did is a real cost or a misunderstanding. For each crux, name a test, case, or argument that both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each predicts about it. Use my train-fork material, or invent sharper cases, wherever a concrete case helps; and if you conclude that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part, and why, in wording both sides could endorse.
