I have no academic training in any of this — no anthropology, no political science, no philosophy — so I apologise in advance for however I've put it. The question has been nagging at me for years and I have never managed to settle it by thinking on my own. What I would like from you is a dialogue, of roughly a thousand words, between two people who are both sharp and both decent, and neither of whom exists to be knocked down. Please give them ordinary names rather than labels, and don't let either of them announce which camp they belong to; let it come out in what they say.

The first speaker holds that corruption is corruption everywhere. Public office is a trust, and using it for private gain betrays that trust whether the currency is cash, a sack of rice, a job for a cousin, or a dinner at a nice restaurant. On this view, saying that it is simply their culture is usually the comfortable person's way of excusing what the poor endure most of. This speaker should be able to explain why relativism about corruption tends, in practice, to protect the powerful.

The second speaker thinks the word corruption is carrying more weight than it can bear. The standard definition — the abuse of public office for private gain — quietly assumes a public office that is actually doing its job. In a place where the clinic has no medicine and the clerk will not move your file without a payment, the payment may be the only thing standing between a sick child and nothing at all, and the person making it is neither a villain nor a dupe. This speaker should be able to explain why the outsider who denounces the practice is often denouncing the weak for coping, and should draw a distinction of their own: some payments open a relationship and create an obligation that flows back, while others close a transaction and end the matter — insurance in the one case, a toll in the other.

Then the actual work. Don't let them trade slogans; make them press each other on cases, and make each of them concede something real.

Case one: an elected representative in a poor district hands out roofing sheets and sacks of rice to households that turn out for him, they vote for him, and he gets the roads paved in his area. Is that a bribe, a gift, a welfare programme, a patronage machine, or all four? Which part is the corruption — the giving, the expectation, or the vote?

Case two: a nurse in a public hospital takes money from a family to move a patient up the queue. The queue is real, the money is real, and her salary has not been paid in four months. Does the answer change if she takes it from the richest family in the ward rather than the poorest? Does it change if the hospital director is skimming the drug budget?

Case three, which I want them to fight about hardest because I think it cuts both ways: a foreign lender makes a loan conditional on anti-corruption reform — new procurement rules, an asset register, a commission with subpoena power. Is that conditionality a cure, or is it itself a use of power for institutional gain? And if the commission turns out to be used against the opposition and never against the governing party, has anything been cured at all?

I want the second speaker to have to face the objection that the insider who defends the local practice is usually the patron, or the patron's cousin, and not the person paying. And I want the first speaker to have to face the objection that the same acts done in rich countries under other names — a lobbyist's dinner, a former minister's consultancy, a facilitation payment that is perfectly legal where it is paid — are not called corruption, so what exactly is the standard, and who is applying it, and why is the community whose judgment counts always the one that won?

Two last things, because they are the part I cannot work out alone. First, whether corruption is a cause of underdevelopment or a symptom of it — whether honest institutions can be built in a poor country or whether honesty arrives with wealth, and what that does to the first speaker's moral case if it is a symptom. Second, whether the poor are better served by a corrupt arrangement that works than by an honest arrangement that does not exist yet, and whether that trade-off is one the person making the argument is entitled to make on someone else's behalf.

End with each of them having moved, or with a clear statement of what each would have to give up to accept the other's position. I would rather have a dialogue that shows me where the disagreement is real than one that resolves it.
