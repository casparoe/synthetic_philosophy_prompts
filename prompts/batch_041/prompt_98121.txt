Last night a friend and I spent three hours arguing over which cause areas are worth working on, and I came away with a list of verdicts that I cannot reduce to a rule. I would like you to find the principle, or the best small set of rival principles, that captures them — and to tell me honestly which of my verdicts has to go.

My verdicts:

(1) Between two problems each of which will certainly harm many millions of people this decade — one with a dozen well-funded research groups making steady progress, one with almost nobody working on it and no known path to a fix — I would send the money to the neglected one.

(2) A third problem harms far fewer people, but a small grant would very likely solve it outright within a year, and no one is on it. I would fund this over both of the first two.

(3) A fourth problem is the most important of all in expected value — a small chance of an enormous catastrophe — but it is crowded with funders, and the marginal dollar accomplishes next to nothing. I would leave it alone and fund something unglamorous.

(4) Two problems are identical in scale and in how solvable they are. One is neglected because everyone who looks at it concludes it is simply hard; the other is neglected because no one has ever thought to look. I would treat these very differently and fund the second, not the first.

(5) A fifth problem would, if solved, produce a benefit far larger than any of the others, but the probability that any given effort succeeds is tiny and I cannot see how the field could ever be more than a lottery. It feels less urgent to me than the modest, near-certain win in (2), even after I multiply the expected value out.

(6) Between two equally important and equally solvable problems, one where a hundred people already work and one where three do, I would fund the one with three — but I would stop feeling that way the moment I learned the hundred are there because the field is heating up and a breakthrough is close.

The pair I keep going back and forth on is (1) and (4). In (1) I fund neglect with no route to a solution; in (4) I refuse to fund neglect that is explained by the problem being hard. To me those look like the same situation, and I cannot tell which of the two verdicts I actually believe.

Here is the tentative principle I would defend: neglectedness matters only instrumentally, as a signal of marginal returns — what I care about is how much more impact the next dollar buys here than elsewhere — so a cause neglected because it is hard has low marginal returns, while one neglected because it is unnoticed may have high ones. But that seems to make (1) a mistake, since I explicitly said the neglected problem there has no known path to a fix. Maybe the fix is that in (1) I am buying option value rather than progress, and in (4) I am not, but I am not sure that distinction survives contact with (6).

I would like a principle, or two or three rivals, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case, with each clause tied to the case that forces it, a prediction for at least one case I have not given, and a plain statement of which of my verdicts you would overturn and why. Please keep the whole reply under 800 words.
