I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and next week I'm interviewing a researcher who has become the most visible defender of one reading of the gender-equality paradox: the finding that countries scoring higher on composite measures of gender equality often show more, not less, occupational sorting by sex — a larger share of women in people-oriented and life-science fields, a smaller share in engineering and the math-intensive ones. His line is that this pattern reflects intrinsic average differences in interests and temperament that become more visible as material and social constraints loosen, rather than a failure of egalitarian culture. I want questions that force him past restating that thesis, and I want to be fair to him: I'll put the strongest version of each challenge and give him room to answer it.

Please give me eight to ten questions, each with the follow-up I'd ask depending on how he answers, in a form I can drop straight into my prep notes. Return a JSON array of objects, one per question, with these fields:

- "question": the question exactly as I'd put it to him.
- "presses_on": the specific claim or assumption in his position that the question targets.
- "likely_reply": the answer he is most likely to give.
- "follow_up": the follow-up keyed to that reply.
- "why_it_resists_deflection": one or two sentences on why the question is hard to wave away.

A few things I specifically want covered, at least one question apiece:

1. The leap from a country-level correlation to claims about individual preferences — what licenses reading a between-country pattern as evidence about what individuals want, given that group-level and individual-level relationships can run in opposite directions.
2. The dependence of the "paradox" on how gender equality is measured — some composite indices fold in political representation and economic participation alongside education and health, and the pattern looks weaker or different under other ways of scoring equality. I want him to say what he thinks the paradox is really tracking.
3. The mechanism he invokes: that affluence and security let choices track inner dispositions more closely. Ask what this predicts that the cultural explanation doesn't, and how we'd tell the two apart, since the cultural story can equally be told as preferences being formed differently in richer countries.
4. Whether any cross-national or cross-cohort data could bear on a biological reading at all, and what kind of evidence — not another correlation between countries — would actually move him.
5. A closing question on what he would personally accept as counting against his reading, and whether he thinks his position has been stated in a way that could ever fail.

Keep the questions concrete and pointed; no "what drew you to this topic" softballs, and no requests for reading lists or citations, since those aren't any use to me in the room.
