I cover science and ideas for a website, and for the last month I've been reporting on forecasting — prediction markets, superforecasters, base rates, all of it. A source of mine, a guy who runs a small forecasting shop, gave me the following argument over coffee and I wrote it down in my notebook because I genuinely couldn't tell if it was profound or garbage. I'm still stuck, my editor wants the peice Friday, and I've decided to just ask someone who thinks about this stuff for a living.

Here's the argument, cleaned up:

(1) When your inside estimate about your own project — your case-specific judgment about how it'll go — conflicts with the outside estimate, meaning the base rate for projects in its reference class, the outside one wins. That's the whole point of the outside view.
(2) Whenever I think I'll beat the base rate, my reason is always some version of "but my case is different."
(3) But "my case is different" is itself a reference class: the class of people who think their case is different. And the success rate of that class is no better than the base rate, probably worse, since believing you're the exception is exactly what the failures believed.
(4) So the very fact that I'm reaching for the inside view is evidence against my project. Any time the inside view shows up, it's already been discounted to below the base rate.
(5) Therefore you should never act on the inside view. Go with the outside view, always.
(6) But (5) is itself something I believe on the basis of my own case-specific reasoning about my own situation — namely, that I, unlike everybody else, should follow this rule.
(7) By (4), the fact that I arrived at (5) through my own inside reasoning is evidence against it, so (5) gets discounted below its base rate too.
(8) So the outside view, applied consistently, eats itself, and I'm left back with my own gut — which is definately not the lesson.

I'd like to keep (1) and (4); they feel load-bearing to me, and I'm willing to throw out pretty much anything else. So which step goes, and why did it look fine when I wrote it down? Then be honest about the price of giving it up: if I drop (3), can I still use base rates to deflate my own overconfidence, or does that go out the window too? Is there something I'm allowed to keep that does the same work?

Write it for a smart reader who isn't a philosopher — no jargon I'd have to look up, no footnotes, a few hundred words is plenty. US spelling please, I'm filing for a New York outlet.
