I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character — call her Vera — is a researcher who has spent a career defending one thesis: that moral status attaches not to organisms, species, or substrates but to a particular class of computations, namely those in which a system builds and continuously revises a model of its own ongoing states and uses that model to steer what it does next. She calls this self-indexing, and in the novel she is its most articulate defender. For the book I am interviewing a philosopher who holds a view close to hers, and I need questions she cannot answer with a paragraph from her own introduction.

Draft ten to twelve interview questions, with a follow-up attached to each, keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one question in each of the following areas, and I would rather have three good questions per area than a survey:

The boundary. Which systems qualify — insects, cephalopods, a language model that keeps a running summary of its own outputs, a person under anaesthesia, a split-brain patient, someone in a fugue state — and what is the principle that sorts them, stated so that it does not simply redescribe the verdicts she already wants.

The attribution. By what right do we say the computation is performed by the organism rather than by a subcircuit, by a subcircuit plus its scaffolding, or by a population? And if the answer depends on which description interests us, is the theory not circular — we choose the grain that yields the verdict, then cite the verdict as evidence for the grain?

The loop. For any self-monitoring system that recycles its own states there is a feedforward system with the same input–output profile. Does self-indexing require the loop to be causally real? If it does, the theory looks like architecture chauvinism; if it does not, what keeps the boundary from swallowing everything that maps inputs to outputs?

The gap. Self-monitoring is a cognitive achievement; suffering seems to be something else. Why should the morally relevant computation be this one rather than whatever underwrites valenced experience, and can she say what that is without circularity?

The novel. How should I write a scene from inside a system that just barely qualifies, and one from inside a system that behaves identically and just misses? What does she think it is like at the line?

For the three questions you judge sharpest, tell me what a competent defender could say that would show the question was mine rather than hers — I would rather cut a question than stage a straw man — and say where your reconstruction of this position is most likely to be a caricature. No reading lists, no citations; the questions have to stand on their own.
