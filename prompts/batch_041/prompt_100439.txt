I'm in this little reading group that's been chewing through some decision theory stuff, and last week we landed on risk-weighted expected utility, which I'd somehow never actually thought about. The idea (as I understand it, correct me if I've garbled it) is that you're not supposed to just multiply each outcome by its probability like a normal expected-utility maximizer; instead you line the outcomes up from worst to best, and then you weight each one by a "risk function" applied to the cumulative probability of doing at least that well — so the weight an outcome gets depends on how much bad stuff sits below it in the ranking, not just on its own odds. If your risk function is a straight line you're back to plain expected utility, if it's bent one way you get risk aversion, and if you bend it as far as it'll go you basically only care about the single worst outcome (maximin). My friend keeps insisting this is obviously the right theory because it explains why people turn down the Allais gamble, and I keep saying the risk function looks like a free parameter you can tune to justify any attitude you already had.
