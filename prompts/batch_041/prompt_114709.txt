I work as a software engineer and have no formal training in philosophy, and I have been asked to write down where I stand on expected-utility theory for a discussion group, so that the others can attack it. Here is the position I want to defend.

1. A preference ordering that violates the expected-utility axioms is defective, not merely unusual, and what makes it defective is exploitability: someone who holds such preferences can be walked through a series of trades, each of which they accept at the time, and end up certainly worse off than they started.
2. Completeness is not required of a rational agent. If I genuinely do not rank two options, that is a fact about me rather than a failure of rationality.
3. Exploitability has to be assessed against what I would actually accept. If I can see the sequence coming and decline to start it, or stop partway through, I have not been made worse off, and refusing is not itself irrational.
4. I am risk-averse about large one-shot stakes in a way that diminishing marginal utility of money does not explain. I would decline a coin flip that risks a month's savings to win two months', but I would accept that same flip if I knew I would be offered it fifty times over the coming year.
5. I would rather end up with less money than be the person who profits from a trade that the other party will later regret. What I care about there is the part I play, not only the distribution I end up with.

I suspect these do not all fit together, but I cannot tell which ones actually conflict. Please tell me which tensions are genuine and which only look like it, rank the genuine ones by how much it would cost me to repair them, and for each say exactly which commitment has to be weakened and what I lose by weakening it. For the pairs that turn out to be only apparently in tension, explain why the appearance is misleading. I do not need references; I need to know what, if anything, I have to give up.
