I am my mother's healthcare proxy. She was diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's two years ago, and while she was still clearly competent she signed an advance directive refusing antibiotics, artificial nutrition, and hospitalization for reversible conditions once she reached a specified stage of cognitive decline. She is now at that stage. Her physician is asking me to authorize antibiotics for a pneumonia that would likely be fatal without treatment. But she has begun saying, in moments that seem lucid, that she wants to live and that she would accept any treatment that might help. She does not remember signing the directive. I have been thinking about the usual framing of this problem as a conflict between the earlier self's autonomy and the later self's welfare, and I have come to think that framing is too crude. I want you to argue for a specific position: that the authority of the earlier self over the later self is best understood on the model of a trust rather than a contract. On the trust model, the earlier self is the settlor, the later self is the beneficiary, and the advance directive is the trust instrument. The proxy is a trustee who must interpret the directive in light of the beneficiary's best interests, not merely follow its letter. On the contract model, by contrast, the earlier self and the later self are treated as two parties, and the directive is binding unless the later self revokes it with the capacity to make a valid contract. Please argue that the trust model is superior. In doing so, explain what the trust model implies for my case: when, if ever, should my mother's current expressions of preference override the directive? What would count as evidence that her current preference is a genuine expression of her interests rather than a symptom of her illness or a momentary fear? Anticipate the objection that the trust model is paternalistic and that it fails to respect the later self's autonomy. Finally, tell me what the trust model would have me do if the directive and my mother's current wishes conflict in a way that cannot be resolved by appeal to her best interests.
