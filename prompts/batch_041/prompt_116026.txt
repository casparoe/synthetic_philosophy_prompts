I've been working through Samuel Butler's Erewhon on and off for a couple months — partly because people in my corner of the internet keep calling it an early piece of AI-alignment fiction, and I half suspect it's being overread by people who want a Victorian to have anticipated them, but I'd rather actually understand the thing before I decide. What's got me stuck is in the Book of the Machines, the stretch where the fictional treatise-writer is answering the obvious objection that machines might be strong but they have no will of their own. He writes: "But I have heard it said, 'granted that this is so, and that the steam-engine has a strength of its own, surely no one will say that it has a will of its own?' Alas, alas! if we look more closely, we shall find that this does not make against the supposition that the vapour-engine is one of the germs of a new phase of life. What is there in this whole world, or in the worlds beyond it, which has a will of its own? The Unknown and Unknowable only!" And then: "A man is the resultant and exponent of all the forces that have been brought to bear upon him, whether before his birth or afterwards. His action at any moment depends solely upon his constitution, and on the intensity and direction of the various agencies to which he is and has been subjected. Some of these will counteract each other; but as he is by nature, and as he has been acted on, and is now acted on from without, so will he do as certainly and regularly as though he were a machine."

I think I get the surface move — the objection assumes we've got some special will the machines lack, and he just denies that anyone has it — but when I try to lay it out as an actual argument it feels like it either proves too much or not enough. If nobody has a will of their own, then "will" has stopped being a difference-maker for anything at all, us included, so how is that supposed to get him to the engine being a germ of a new phase of life? And what's the deal with the one exception he carves out, the Unknown and Unknowable — is that just a stand-in for God, or is it pointing at some specific doctrine he's leaning on? There's also a sentence a bit later in the same paragraph, about how if we knew the actual past and present the future would lie open to us and we should "not be even able to distinguish time at all; but that is foreign," and I can't tell what the worry is there or why its waved off like that.

What I want is for you to walk me through it in order, like I'm an idiot: the actual premises, the inference, which steps are load-bearing and which are just Victorian decoration, and where you'd push back. The reason I care is the alignment stuff — I keep seeing a move in those debates that goes roughly "we have no privileged window onto our own minds, so we can't rule out the machine having one," and I want to know whether Butler's doing that move or a cousin of it, because it feels different to me in a way I can't articulate. One more thing that nags at me: the whole thing is presented as the narrator translating somebody else's book, so I'm never sure how much is Butler's own view and how much is the character's, or whether that distinction even matters for evaluating the argument itself.
