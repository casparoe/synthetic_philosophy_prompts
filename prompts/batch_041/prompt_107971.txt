All right, I need a hand with something that keeps derailing my concepts seminar. I'm doing the usual anti-essentialism song and dance — no necessary and sufficient conditions, family resemblance, all of it — and I use the letter 'a' as one of my stock examples. But honestly I've started to think I'm cheating, because there clearly IS something all the 'a's have in common. The double-story 'a' in a newspaper, the single-story 'a' in my kid's picture book, some insane blackletter 'a' — they're all 'a's. And no amount of fiddling with an 'o' turns it into an 'a'. So there's got to be a feature, or a bundle of features, that every 'a' has and no 'o' or 'd' or 'q' has. We just haven't pinned it down yet. Right?

So — what is it? I've tried 'one enclosed counter', but that overgenerates ('o', 'e', 'd', 'p'...). I've tried 'a bowl with a stem rising on the right', and that's just 'd'. Is there a better candidate, or a two-stage version where you first normalise the skeleton and then check something? I want something I can definately put on a slide.

Two things keep tripping me up. First: in plenty of sans-serif fonts you genuinly cannot tell a lowercase l from a capital I from a numeral 1 — identical shape, three different characters. I've been assuming that's just a font-design accident and doesn't threaten the essentialist picture. Second, I've got this puzzle I use on students: imagine a designer draws the 'a' as a perfect circle, exactly like that font's 'o'. Is the circle an 'a' or an 'o'? My line is that it's whatever the rest of the alphabet makes it — which I take to show the essence is real, just maybe spread out across the system rather than sitting inside any one glyph. A colleague tells me I've got that exactly backwards, and I honestly can't see how.

So which is it — what feature makes every 'a' an 'a', and where am I going wrong on the circle? I'd rather have a straight answer than another 'well, it depends what you mean by essence'.
