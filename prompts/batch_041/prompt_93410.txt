I'm working up a long feature on this argument that's been knocking about in decision-theory circles for the last few years — I think it gets called "evidential cooperation in large worlds", though I might be garbling the name — and I'd rather put a number on the central claim than just describe it, because the two people I've interviewed so far are miles apart and I want to see where the fault line actually is.

Here's the claim as I'd state it, so you can tell me if I've mangled it. The universe is probably enormous, and if it is, then there are lots of civilisations we could never contact, ever — no signal out, no signal in. The argument says that even so, we should act as though we were in a cooperative relationship with them. My choosing to be a certain kind of agent — one that doesn't grab, that keeps its bargains, that wants there to be more agents of that sort around — is *evidence* about what other agents like me do, including agents out in those unreachable places. So being decent here is a way of being decent to them; and since the unreachable places may hold nearly all the value there is, that evidential link could be the most important fact about my choice. (I know "evidence about" is carrying a hell of a lot of weight there. That's half the reason I want the working shown.)

What's your credence that that argument is sound — that in a big enough universe, a rational agent's choices really do stand in the evidential relation the argument needs, and that relation really does give her reason to be cooperative towards civilisations she can never affect or be affected by? I'm sitting at about 0.3 myself; the philosopher I spoke to yesterday put herself at 0.9. So: your own number, and then the decomposition — the sub-questions it hangs on, a rough probability for each, how they combine, and which one the whole thing is most sensitive to.

Off the top of my head the terms look like: whether evidential (rather than causal) decision theory is the right account of rational choice at all; whether the universe is big enough, and whether the measure/cosmology question leaves enough value out there for it to matter; whether the other agents are *similar enough* to me for my choice to be evidence about theirs — that one feels suspiciously like an old problem about which class of cases you're allowed to generalise from, and I'd like to know if that's a real worry or me being dim; whether cooperation even has determinate content once there's no channel to send anything down, or whether the argument quietly collapses into "believe whatever makes you feel like a good person"; and the nasty one, which is that if it works for cooperating it seems to work for caving in to threats from the same far-off agents, and I can't tell whether that shows the argument is powerful or broken.

Give me the numbers for those (or your own better set), the combination, and then tell me which single sub-question is doing most of the work in your final figure — and what would move it: a better decision theory, better cosmology, or just a better argument about whether any of this could ever change what we actually do on Monday morning.
