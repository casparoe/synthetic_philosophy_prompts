I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I'm drafting the final. I want a single essay question on Leibniz's claim that God actualizes the best of all possible worlds, and I need it to be unanswerable by reciting the standard package: sufficient reason, theodicy, evil as a condition of greater goods, and the familiar joke at Leibniz's expense. What I actually want to test is whether students can see that the whole position rests on a structural assumption that almost nobody states out loud.

To force that, I'll hand them a small framework on the exam sheet, and I'd like you to state it compactly inside the question itself. Suppose we model divine creation as a selection from a set W of possible worlds under a value ordering V. Three structures are available. First, W has a maximum under V. Second, V is unbounded above on W, so that for every world there is a better one. Third, V yields only a partial order: some worlds are neither better nor worse than each other, and not equal in value either. Then add two complications. The selection principle can be read as maximizing, as satisficing above a threshold, or as indifferent across the incomparable. And V is not external to its relata: a world's axiology is itself a feature of that world, so any fixed V used to rank W is one of the things being ranked.

The question should require students to say which of the three structures the Leibnizian argument actually needs, and why the other two make trouble for the claim that this world is the best; whether the reflexive status of V makes the ranking ill-formed or merely difficult; and what survives of the argument if no best world exists. I want the crux to be the inference from God's perfect goodness to God's actualizing the maximum, and I want a candidate who simply identifies the actual world with the best world, without asking what best quantifies over, to lose marks for it.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points totalling 20 marks. For each point, give the marks and one line on the specific way candidates typically forfeit it. The question should be answerable in about 75 minutes. No part of it may require recalling or citing secondary literature: assume students have only the headline claims of the primary text, and do not ask them for sources, reading lists, or attributions.
