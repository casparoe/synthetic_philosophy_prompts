Construct a thought experiment to order: a colony of social insects that is a candidate mind, engineered so that three named positions return different verdicts on whether the colony has a mind, and engineered so that the case isolates a single variable.

The purpose. This is the ant-colony question of the Ant Fugue dialogue, sharpened into a controlled case. It is a test of one specific claim — that the verdict on colony mentality turns only on the system's functional and causal profile, not on the medium in which that profile is realized and not on the nature of the system's parts. Build the case so that a defender of medium-neutrality cannot escape by alleging a difference in behaviour, and a defender of the opposing view cannot escape by alleging an unstated empirical difference. Stipulating the verdicts — writing into the scenario that the colony is, or is not, conscious — is cheating; each verdict must be derived from that position's own commitments.

Specifications:

1. Physical possibility. Nothing may require new physics, telepathy, or miracles. Every stipulated fact should be something a patient field biologist could in principle observe or measure. Prefer real ants or termites to invented organisms; if you invent, keep the invention within what selection could plausibly produce.

2. Behavioural parity. Stipulate a behavioural profile at least as rich as a normal mammal's — navigation of novel terrain, learning, memory, deceptive signalling, division of labour that adapts to injury, and decisions that no single insect's rule reproduces. No position may dismiss the case on empirical grounds.

3. Graph identity. State a coarse grain of description and, at that grain, stipulate that the colony's causal graph — which unit influences which, with what latency and reliability, including the global broadcast — is the same as that of a normal mammal's thalamo-cortical system. Hold the graph fixed.

4. The isolated variable: medium and publicity. The sole difference from the mammalian case is that every connection in the colony's graph is realized in a public external medium — chemical trails laid in the environment, read by whatever walks over them — rather than in private internal tissue, so that nothing inside the colony carries the integration loop. Build in the consequences: the colony's global workspace is a physical field that any insect, including a passing beetle that is not a colony member, can read; no ant has privileged access to colony-level states; and the colony has no state available to it and to nothing else.

5. A second, separable variable: nested subjects. Each ant is stipulated to be a subject in its own right, with its own experience, and not an automaton; no ant perceives or represents colony-level facts. This is a different variable from (4), and the case must let a reader see what each contributes on its own.

6. The scenario itself, no longer than 300 words.

Then produce:

(a) The scenario.

(b) For each of the following, the verdict — mind? beliefs and desires? experience? — and the reasoning from that position's own commitments: (i) intentional-stance interpretationism, on which to have a mind is for the intentional stance to be indispensable to a good predictive account of the system, with no further fact about mentality beyond the reality of the pattern; (ii) global-workspace functionalism, on which a state is conscious when it is broadcast to, and available for the control of, the system's subsystems; (iii) an internalist unity requirement, on which a system has a mind only if its integrating processes are bounded and internally coupled, so that the system's states are its own and its parts do not each have their own take on them.

(c) The debt each verdict incurs. For any position that answers yes, an account of how a subject can have subjects as proper parts without either absorbing their experiences into one subject or losing them. For any position that answers no, an account of why graph identity at the stated grain does not settle the matter.

(d) Which feature does the work. Separate the publicity of the medium from the nested subjects, and say which verdicts move when each is varied on its own. Then move the integration loop back inside the colony — a nerve cord running between ants, everything else held fixed — and say what the case collapses into, and whether any of the three positions turns out to be about the location of a system boundary rather than about minds.

(e) An adversarial note. For the position that fares worst, the strongest attack on the case, phrased as its defender would phrase it, and an honest assessment of whether that attack can be made without begging the question.

(f) If the specifications cannot all be met at once — in particular, if (3) and (4) cannot be held fixed simultaneously at any useful grain — say which specification must be weakened and why.
