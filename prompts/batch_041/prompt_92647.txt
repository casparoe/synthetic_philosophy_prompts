ok so I'm taking this seminar on social kinds (philosophy of social science, cross-listed with sociology) and we've spent the last two weeks on generations — boomers, millennials, gen z, all of it — and I have a paper due where I'm supposed to defend a view on whether generations are real kinds or just a handy label we slap on a birth window. Before I commit to anything I want the spec first, like the actual list of what a theory in this area has to do to count as any good, because right now everyone in the seminar just asserts their favourite view and then cherry-picks the examples that fit.

So: write me the desiderata. What does any adequate account of generations-as-kinds have to deliver?

Things I want in the list:

- the cases it has to get right. Say: why "the boomers" feels like a real thing in a way that "people born 1963-68" doesn't, if there's a difference; the fact that "gen z" was basically named into existence by marketing and media and yet people now genuinely identify with it; cases where the label gets exported to countries whose history doesn't match the American story it was built on and obviously fits badly; and cases where a cohort goes through something enormous together and still doesn't become a generation in any interesting sense.
- the distinctions it can't collapse: cohort (a birth-window fact) vs age effects vs period effects (a recession hits everybody, not one cohort); a cohort that merely shares a birth window vs one that actually develops a shared self-understanding and a common style; "is a generation" vs "is useful to call a generation"; the descriptive use vs the "kids these days" use. Also I guess the classic sociology move that a generation only really exists once the cohort goes through a formative experience together — I want to know if that's a requirement or just one tradition's say-so.
- results it can't contradict. The one I actually know about is the age-period-cohort identification problem: age, period and cohort are linearly dependent, so you can't estimate all three cleanly from the usual data, which means a lot of "millennials are like X because of their formative years" claims are doing something they haven't earned. Tell me what else constrains the theory — whatever demography forces on it, and whatever the social-kind literature about classificatory feedback / looping effects forces on it (the idea that classifying people as a generation can itself change the people classified, which looks like it matters a lot here).

Then two things I really need:

1. Mark which of these are genuine requirements and which are just prejudices of one camp. I suspect "generations must have sharp boundaries" is a prejudice rather than a requirement, but I want to be told, not guess.

2. Find me any pair or subset of the desiderata that can't be jointly satisfied. If a theory has to (a) make generational differences objective and (b) admit the boundaries are drawn by convention, is that already a contradiction? Or if it has to explain why cohort differences arise and also can't gerrymander the boundaries, does it just collapse into "any partition of birth years is as good as any other"? That's the part I most want. If the list itself turns out to be a result, say so.

Also, which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the popular accounts? Skip the full scorecard of every theory, I have to do that myself anyway.

Like a society where the birth cohorts are statistically identical in attitudes but everyone still self-identifies as a generation, versus one where the differences are huge but nobody has any generational self-understanding — what would each case show, and which of them is actually possible?
