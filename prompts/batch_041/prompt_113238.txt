I'm putting together a little anthology of short fiction for a reading group I run — the gimmick is that every story has to be built around one idea from the theory of collective decision-making — and I've got stuck on my own contribution. Could you write me a short story (1500 words or so, whatever the story actually needs) about an information cascade?

The textbook versions of this — urns and balls, two restaurants on a street — are exactly what I don't want. I want it in a town, with named people and something real at stake, and I want the mechanism carried by what people say out loud versus what they actually think, not by a narrator explaining it to the reader.

My half-formed setup, feel free to bend it: a small town has just sunk its money into a new well, and the question is whether to keep drinking from it. The council decides one at a time, in public, at a meeting, each person standing up and giving their call. The first two say keep it — and each is really just going on their own private bit of evidence (one tasted something off and talked themselves out of it, the other ran a cheap test strip that came back ambiguous and read it the way they wanted to). From then on everybody else is going on the fact that the first two said keep it, not on their own evidence, and by the end it's twelve to nothing. I want the last speaker to be the person who did the most careful test of anybody in the room and got a result pointing the other way, and I want it genuinely unclear what she should do — not a courage thing, an epistemics thing. And I'd like it left open whether she's holding back because of what she thinks the evidence shows or because of what breaking ranks would cost her, and whether those two can even be told apart from the outside.

There's also a reporter at the back of the room filing a piece that treats twelve votes as twelve independent checks. I'd like that to land as a real epistemic event in the story, not a punchline.

Then, after the story, a short note in your own voice — a paragraph, no citations or reading lists needed, I won't check them — saying how confident you are that the story actually gets the logic right, and naming the single point you're least sure of (e.g. whether someone in her exact position really should follow the room, or whether a run that long is as fragile as I've made it look). I'd much rather have the weak point flagged than smoothed over.
