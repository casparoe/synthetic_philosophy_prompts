I teach a seminar on evidence and rationality that sits awkwardly between philosophy and the forecasting world — half the students are epistemologists, half came in through prediction markets and calibration training — and I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, which has left me with very little patience for exam questions that can be answered by reciting the standard disagreement literature back at me. I am setting the final, and I want a single essay question on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia built around a case my students cannot possibly have seen, so that the only way through it is to reason.

Here is the case I want the question built on. Treat it as fixed; polish the prose if you like, but do not change its structure.

Nadia must assign a probability to a binary question Q. She deliberates carefully — base rates, the relevant evidence, the competing explanations — and lands on 0.85. Then three things reach her.

(i) A large, well-conducted meta-analysis of forecasters who work the way she works reports that when such forecasters land on 0.85 after deliberation on questions of this kind, the answer turns out yes only about 55% of the time. She has no methodological complaints about the study. Its sample includes her own past forecasts.

(ii) She keeps a personal ledger: of the 200 previous questions on which she landed on exactly 0.85, 88% resolved yes.

(iii) She learns that the meta-analysis's 55% is not uniform across its sample. It is driven largely by a subgroup of forecasters who, on their own account, deliberate carelessly on questions like Q — skimming the base rates, anchoring on the first plausible number. Reconstructing her own deliberation step by step, Nadia can see that she did not do any of that; from the inside, her process looks like the careful subgroup's. But she also knows that people in the careless subgroup report having been careful, and she cannot tell from the inside whether her sense of having been careful is itself reliable.

The question I want students to face: what credence should Nadia have in Q, and is there any coherent position on which she may keep 0.85 while conceding that her confidence in 0.85 is not epistemically justified?

Constraints on the deliverable:

- Ninety minutes, closed book. No citations are required and none are rewarded; grade the moves, not the names. Nothing in the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme may depend on getting an author, title, or date right.
- The question must not be answerable by summarizing conciliationism versus steadfastness, or by labelling Nadia a "conciliator" or a "steadfast". A student who does only that should lose most of the marks, and the marking scheme should say so explicitly.
- The case is the only source of detail. Do not add named studies, real forecasting tournaments, or real researchers.
- The three quantities in the case are not independent, and I chose them so that treating them as three readings to be averaged is the most tempting wrong answer. Make sure the marking scheme names that mistake rather than merely penalizing it.

What I want back:

0. A short brainstorm — three or four sentences is plenty — of the cruxes this case actually contains, so I can see what you take the hard part to be. Then:
1. The exam question itself, with the case reprinted inside it and any sub-parts lettered. A few sentences, not a page.
2. A model answer of roughly 800–900 words, written the way a strong student would write it under time pressure: a position, argued, with the opposing position stated fairly and answered. It should be willing to say that the case underdetermines the number if that is the honest answer, and to say what further fact would settle it.
3. A marking scheme out of 30 marks: five must-hit moves, the marks each is worth, and for each one a single line on how candidates typically get it wrong. Then a short paragraph on what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer, phrased as moves rather than adjectives.

You are a professional philosopher of epistemology with a serious side interest in forecasting and calibration research, and you have taught this material often enough to know which exam questions collapse into recall. Think carefully before you write: work out what the case is actually testing, and where a good student would be tempted to cheat. Begin with the brainstorm.
