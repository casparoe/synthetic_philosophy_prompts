Some context. I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years, and the torture-versus-dust-specks case is one of the pieces of that canon I know best. Next month I have been asked to run a seventy-five-minute session on it for an undergraduate ethics seminar. The students are second- and third-year philosophy majors. They have read some Mill and some Kant; none has encountered population axiology, and none has any formal decision theory. A handful of them read online rationalist writing and will arrive already convinced of one side, which I expect to be its own problem.

I do not want the case explained to me. I want the session planned around a reframing I have found more productive than the question of which world is worse. The reframing is this: for each answer to the case, what has to be true of the betterness relation? If one person's torture can be outweighed by enough people's trivial annoyances, then the significance of a harm must be a magnitude that trades off at some rate. If it cannot, then the significance of a harm must depend on its relation to the life it falls in — on whether it registers as a loss to anyone. Both options are already live in cases with no absurd numbers in them, and I want the session to get the students to see that before the absurd numbers arrive.
