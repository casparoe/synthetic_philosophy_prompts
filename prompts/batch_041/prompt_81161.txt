I write historical novels, real people in them, mostly dead Victorians. Theres a pattern that keeps happening to me and I've never found a name for it, so I'm asking. I make something up -- small, plausible, unverifiable: a sister says something cruel across a dinner table, an officer hesitates a beat too long before giving an order. I say it's invented in the afterword, I say it in every interview. Twenty years later I'm reading a biography and there's my dinner scene, no attribution, or footnoted to my novel as if a novel were a source. The made-up thing has turned into the record, and by then it isn't mine anymore.

The other half of it is the complaining. The only person with standing to object is the sister's great-grandaughter, who never met her and isn't her, and everyone acts as if the family speaks for the dead woman. The dead woman herself can't be asked, and what she'd have wanted is precisely the thing nobody knows.

Someone once told me the philosophical term for this is posthumous harm, but as I understand it that's about whether your reputation souring after you're gone counts as a harm to you, and it doesn't seem to cover the part where an invented detail quietly becomes evidence. So does the whole thing have a name? I don't just want a label, I want to know what the thing actually is -- whether the wrong, if it's a wrong, is to the dead person or to the family or to the readers who now believe something false; whether it matters that the invention is unflattering, or that the person was a nobody rather than a public figure; and how it differs from a historian simply getting something wrong, or from me making things up on purpose, which is the job.
