I'm a physicist by training, and this year I started leading an adult discussion group at my church. Someone in the group — a retired pastor, as it happens — has been pressing a reading of Galileo's letters on scripture that I cannot answer, and it has stopped being an academic question for me, because I find that I half believe his conclusion and don't want to.

He puts it this way:

(1) Where scripture speaks of the natural world, it accommodates itself to the capacities and settled opinions of the audience it addresses.
(2) Its first audiences took the sun to move and the earth to rest, so scripture speaks of the sun's rising and the earth's standing still.
(3) Such passages therefore express themselves in the language of appearance; they are not reports of how things stand in nature.
(4) On this reading, a demonstrated conclusion of natural philosophy cannot contradict scripture, since the appearance is true as an appearance and the demonstration is true of the world.
(5) But the ground of the accommodation is the condition of the audience, not the subject matter; the same ground operates wherever an audience's beliefs differ from the truth.
(6) So accommodation is not a peculiarity of cosmology but a general feature of how scripture teaches.
(7) It follows that what scripture teaches is calibrated throughout to what its first hearers already believed — about God, about the moral law, about the life to come.
(8) A teaching calibrated to what its hearers already believed cannot correct them where they are wrong.
(9) A revelation that cannot correct its hearers is not a revelation but a mirror.
(10) Therefore the principle that was supposed to rescue Galileo from the literalists dissolves the authority of scripture altogether.

He concludes that the churchmen who refused to let astronomers reinterpret scripture were right in principle, and that anyone today who accepts the accommodation principle has accepted a faith that tells its original audience nothing it did not already think. I don't want (10), and I don't want to give up (5), because (5) is what keeps the Galileo affair from being a lucky accident. Tell me where the argument goes wrong. If it doesn't, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what surrendering it costs — what I lose about scripture, or about the Galileo episode, that I would rather have kept.
