I help run a small reading group that has spent the past two months on transformative experience — the idea, familiar from work at the border of decision theory and epistemology, that some choices (deciding to have a first child, to enlist, to undergo a religious conversion, to take a drug that rewrites what you enjoy, to accept an implant that changes how you perceive) alter both what the chooser values and what the chooser is even in a position to know about the outcome. We have split into two stubborn camps that keep talking past each other, and I would like the document they should have written together. Call them the discontinuity camp and the continuity camp. The discontinuity camp holds that such choices are discontinuous with ordinary choice under uncertainty: because you cannot know in advance what the post-transformation life is like, the standard expected-value apparatus has no determinate input, and a rational agent is left with something non-standard — deciding on the basis of whether she wants to find out, or on the basis of a preference stipulated to survive the change. The continuity camp holds that transformative choice is continuous with ordinary choice: uncertainty about your own future values is just another kind of uncertainty, handleable through higher-order preferences, through testimony and evidence about how people fare, through disciplined imagination, or by pricing the change as one more risk, so that nothing here breaks decision theory in particular.

Please write the joint statement these two camps could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered section of agreed claims — about what makes an experience transformative, about what the standard decision-theoretic apparatus needs and when that input is missing, about which parts of the dispute are empirical and which conceptual, and about what either side would count as progress; then a disagreements table that states each surviving disagreement in wording the side holding it would accept as its own. I suspect at least these four are cruxes, and I want them all handled: whether deciding on the basis of wanting the experience, or of wanting to discover what it is like, is a genuine rational basis or an evasion; whether first-personal imaginative acquaintance with the outcome is required, or whether third-personal evidence and testimony can substitute for it; whether the pre-transformation self's preferences have any authority over the post-transformation self; and whether the puzzle is specific to transformation at all or is merely the general problem of preference change and unknown future values in disguise. For each crux, record a concrete case or piece of evidence that both sides agree bears on it — for instance retrospective testimony from people who have undergone the change and now judge whether it was worth it, cases where a person's preferences shift and the later self judges the shift a mistake, and cases where a preference is stipulated in advance to survive the change — and write down, ahead of the evidence, what each side predicts we would find and what it would take to make that side retreat. If you conclude that one of these disputes is really about words, say which one and why.
