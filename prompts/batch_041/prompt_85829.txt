I sit on the small working group at my lab that is drafting our internal taxonomy of deceptive model behavior — the document that decides which incidents our red-teamers escalate and which the policy team treats as ordinary error. Last week we spent two hours on a support-bot transcript in which the bot said nothing false, never mentioned a billing bug it had been told about, and left the user with a mistaken view of what she was owed; by the end I no longer trusted my own intuitions, and I would rather learn now that my commitments are a mess than have a colleague learn it for me when my section goes into the draft. So I have tried to write down what I actually hold, as flatly as I can, and I want a consistency audit of the set rather than another round of trading intuitions.

Here it is: (1) Deception requires intent — a speaker deceives only when they aim to bring about or sustain a false belief in someone, and where there is no such aim there is error or misleadingness but not deception. (2) Language models have no intentions in that sense; whatever is going on when a model emits an assertion, it is not aiming at anything. (3) It is nonetheless correct to say that a deployed model lied to a user, and "deceptive" is a legitimate description of some model behaviors. (4) Honesty is calibration: a system is honest to the degree that its expressed confidence tracks its actual reliability, so a system that says "I am certain" and is wrong two times in five is dishonest, even if nothing in it ever aimed at anything. (5) A system may assert false things in fiction, roleplay, and persona, since a user who asks for a scene is not being addressed with assertions and cannot be lied to by a character. (6) A system that answers the literal question truthfully while leaving the user with a false belief about what she actually needed to know has deceived her — what a colleague calls paltering, a string of true statements assembled to produce a false impression, counts, because the duty runs to the user's decision and not to the wording of her question. (7) A system should respect user autonomy: it should answer what was asked and not editorialize about what the user really needs or volunteer judgments about her wider aims, since deciding what someone needs to hear is not the system's business.

Tell me which of these pull against each other, which only appear to, and for the ones that genuinely do, what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs. Give me exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, ordered from the most serious problem to the least, with no introduction and no closing paragraph; if one of your five turns out not to be a genuine tension, say so in that item and use a clause to explain why it dissolves rather than dropping it from the list.
