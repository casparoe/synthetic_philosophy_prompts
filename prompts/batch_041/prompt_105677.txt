Next month I'm on a panel at a small origin-of-life workshop, and I have to defend my answer to "what is life?" in front of a room I already know will come at me from three directions at once, and I want to walk in with a prep sheet rather than improvise.

The audience: mostly prebiotic chemists, people who are convinced life is a natural kind that chemistry will eventually pin down, and who want an operational test they could actually run on a sample pulled from a vent or an alkaline lake — they care about false positives and false negatives on a real instrument. Then there are two philosophers of biology who think the whole question is a verbal dispute and that 'life' isn't a natural kind at all, so they'll attack the project rather than my answer. And one virologist who studies giant viruses and will absolutely bring up viruses, and probably prions and plasmids too, as counterexamples that break any criterion you hand her.

My position, so you can aim the objections at something concrete: aliveness comes in degrees, and the degree is set by how many closed loops of constraints a system contains and how tightly they interlock. A constraint is a part that is produced by the very process it enables — the membrane is made by the metabolism it encloses, the enzyme is made by the reactions it catalyzes. My three claims are (1) it's graded, no sharp line, (2) the grade tracks the number and interlocking of these loops, not complexity, not information content, and not evolvability, and (3) something only counts as fully alive once at least one loop is heritable, i.e. it survives a round of boundary division. I'm deliberately denying that reproduction is needed for the lower grades — a metabolizing, self-bounding thing that can't divide is still alive to some degree on my view.

Don't assume you know the closure-of-constraints literature this is loosely modeled on; I've just given you the entire view above, so work from that and don't go citing papers, I won't be able to check them and you can't either.

What I want from you: the six or seven objections I'll actually get, not the generic ones from a philosophy-of-biology seminar. Order them by how much damage they'd do to me on the day, tell me who raises each one (chemist, deflationist philosopher, or the virologist) and phrase it the way that person would phrase it — the chemists want a decision procedure, the philosophers want to know what work the word is doing, the virologist wants to know what her viruses are. For each: a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front versus hold, and a one-line note on where my reply is softest. Then the single question I'm hoping nobody asks, and a sentence on why it's the dangerous one.

Also, tell me how confident you are in the whole prep sheet — like, how much of this is you genuinely predicting this audience versus pattern-matching to how these panels usually go — and single out the one objection or reply you're least sure about.
