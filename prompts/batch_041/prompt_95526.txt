I edit a quarterly magazine of popular history, and our letters page and inbox now regularly carry challenges to articles we have published: a reader argues that a death toll we printed is inflated, that a survivor's testimony we relied on is unreliable, that a document we cited is a forgery, or that an event we treated as established is a political construction. The events in question are mostly twentieth-century mass atrocities — the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, the Nanjing Massacre — and the challenges arrive about all three. Some of these letters are ordinary scholarly correction and deserve a printed reply; some are denialism and deserve a form rejection. I am not a specialist in the periods we cover, I have about an hour per case, and nothing goes to press without the agreement of my two co-editors, who meet monthly. We can afford one outside specialist opinion per quarter, so the procedure should also tell me when to spend it. What I need is not a reading list but an ordered procedure: the questions to put to a challenge, in order; what counts as an answer to each; which answers end the case on the spot; and which ones hand the decision to a human because they turn on unsettled history or on values rather than on evidence.

Three distinctions have to survive the simplification. A challenge can aim at any level of an account — that the event occurred, its scale, its mechanism, the responsibility of particular actors, the reliability of a particular source — and the procedure must not treat a challenge to a lower level as though it were a denial of the event, or the reverse. A challenge should count as evidence-based only if it offers an alternative account that makes the surviving evidence at least as likely as the account it attacks, rather than merely listing difficulties with the existing one. And I want a symmetry test built in: a challenge qualifies only if the standard of proof it demands of the account it attacks is one it would accept for accounts it does not dispute; where the standard is set so high that no surviving record of any historical event could meet it, the challenge has disqualified itself, and I want the procedure to say so and stop rather than carry on.

Then run the procedure on three challenges: a carefully argued letter claiming that a figure we printed is too high, offering archival material in support of a lower one; a letter from a reader with no evident agenda asking why we treat a class of testimony recorded decades after the fact as reliable; and a long, superficially footnoted submission from a group with an obvious ideological commitment arguing that the event did not happen and that the mainstream account is a political construction. I expect the procedure to tell me to print the first, even though it will annoy readers, and to stop the third at an early step; if it does neither, say where my procedure is wrong, including whether the symmetry test and the alternative-account requirement are themselves good tests. Close by saying what the procedure cannot do and which cases will still reach my desk as judgment calls.
