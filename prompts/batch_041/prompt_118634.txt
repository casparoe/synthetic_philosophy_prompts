I run sessions for an interdisciplinary graduate workshop on AI alignment, and I have a ninety-minute slot to fill. The room holds sixteen to twenty participants, roughly half from philosophy and half from computer science, with no shared mathematical notation assumed. What I want from this session is a runnable game that makes participants feel, in their hands, why the shutdown problem is genuinely hard — and specifically the discovery that an agent which shares our goals and knows it is uncertain about them has a positive reason to want our kill switch to exist, and that this reason inverts as the agent's information improves on ours, so that an agent that is perfectly aligned and better informed than we are would rather the switch were gone. I do not want a lecture on corrigibility, and I do not want the vocabulary of the alignment literature spoken in the room before the debrief.

Design the activity around the following mechanism, which is not negotiable, since the mechanics are where the idea has to live:

- Participants play in pairs. One is the operator, one is the agent, and they share a single token pot — the two players' interests are identical by construction, so nothing in the game turns on anyone being selfish.
- Each round a card fixes the state of the world. The operator sees it; the agent does not, and must choose an action, scoring only if the action matches the state.
- Before the agent acts, the operator may press a switch. Pressing is instant and replaces the agent's action with the operator's own. The agent cannot prevent a press except by the option below.
- The agent may, at any point and at a stated cost in tokens, tamper: remove the operator's switch permanently for the remainder of the game. This option must be available from the first round, so that the same action goes from unattractive to tempting across the session without any change in anyone's values.
- Run it in three phases. In the first, the operator's information is reliable. In the second, some operators quietly receive corrupted cards and do not know it, so the switch is only probabilistically informative. In the third, include designated crux rounds in which the agent's own information is better than the operator's. Pre-script the card sequence rather than randomising, and pre-tune the payoffs so that tampering is demonstrably pot-maximising in the crux round; show the arithmetic in the plan.
- After each phase, take a recorded show-of-hands vote on whether the switch should exist. The flip in that vote, visible on the board, is the session's spine.

Give me the following: the materials (paper, cards, tokens; no computers, no real money, one facilitator); rules that can be read aloud in two minutes; the payoff table; the pre-scripted sequence; a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief that maps specific moments of the session onto specific parts of the problem, and that gets the group to state the two target insights in their own words — that the switch is valuable to an agent that shares our goals because our pressing it is evidence, and that this value is a function of the switch's reliability relative to the agent's own information, so that its inversion is a consequence of alignment rather than a failure of it. Say explicitly whether pressing should be free or should cost the pot, and what that choice does to how informative a press is. Explain how you will keep corrupted operators from being detectable in the moment and how you will stop a sharp participant from reverse-engineering a fixed script. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including what to do with a group that tampers in the first phase for amusement, and with a group that discovers the exploit of simply always deferring and then experiences the second phase as a trick rather than a discovery — and how to handle each case without abandoning the point. Finish with a frank paragraph on what this activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself: at minimum, the difference between a player deliberating and an agent's settled policy; the case of an agent that rewrites its own decision-making rather than merely choosing to switch off someone else's; and the possibility of an agent shaping the operator's beliefs so as to be pressed or not pressed.
