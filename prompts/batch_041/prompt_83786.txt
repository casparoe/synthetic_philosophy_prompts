My buddy Dave and I got into it for like two hours last night about the statue in our town square, and now I have to give a speech about it on Saturday, so I'm coming to you before I say something I can't walk back. Quick setup: I teach history at the high school in Wrenfield, and last fall the council voted 4-3 to take down the statue of Elias Wardwell, the mill guy — 1890s, paid for the library, the hospital wing, the bandstand, and also broke the strike in '94, had families evicted from company housing in January, and gave money to the nativist league that made life hell for the Polish and Italian families in the mill district. We've all watched this movie a dozen times by now, some slaver fished out of a harbour in Bristol, some general on a horse in a park somewhere, but it's different when the plinth is in your own square. The bronze itself is sitting in the DPW yard behind the salt shed under a tarp, but the plinth never moved, and it still has the original 1897 carving on it — his name and the years in two-foot letters and the whole grateful-town business. Saturday the commitee is dedicating what they're calling a reconciliation garden around it: planters, benches, a new plaque bolted onto the side. They asked me for the remarks, five minutes, standing right next to the thing. Wardwell's great-granddaughter will be in the front row (she's the one who's been paying to have the bronze cleaned every spring since the eighties), and so will two of the Kowalczyk sisters, whose grandfather was one of the ones evicted, and the mayor, who wants everybody holding hands by the end, and a reporter from the county paper who will quote exactly one sentence, so I need the spiciest sentence to be the one I actually mean.

Here's where Dave comes in, because he's the reason I'm not just writing "he was a man of his time" and going to bed. He's got this way of putting it that I can't shake: he says a monument isn't a claim, it's a promise — it's addressed to people who aren't born yet, and you don't refute a promise, you either keep it or you break it. So the whole fight we keep having in this town (was he really that bad, are we judging the past by today's standards, but what about the library) is the wrong shape, because that isn't how a promise gets answered. Then he drops his second thing, which he calls the footnote trap: you can't edit a statue, you can only sentence it to more attention. Bolt a plaque of corrections onto the base and you've made that base the most-read object in town, and the 1897 words still win anyway, because carved beats bolted-on every time — the correction is an annotation, and annotations live underneath the text they annotate. I told him the empty plinth is a monument too, and it's a monument to us, it says this is the kind of town that took it down, which is a promise about the present that'll look just as bad in fifty years, and he didn't have an answer for that, which honestly made me feel worse instead of better.

So: write me the five minutes. It has to land for that whole room without being mush and without saying anything I'd have to take back later, and it has to do something with the plinth itself — the carved words, the bolted plaque, the bronze under a tarp twenty feet from the salt pile. I'd like it built on one of Dave's two ideas, and I want you to tell me which one you picked and what the other one would have forced me to give up, because I think they pull in different directions and I haven't worked out where. Also say something about where the statue should end up — warehouse forever, museum, melted down, given back to the family — and what each of those would commit us to, because somebody is going to ask me and I definately don't want to wing it. No philosophy jargon, no quoting famous people (I'll get it wrong and there's no signal out by the salt shed), and don't put words in the mouths of anybody in the front row. Roughly 700 words of actual speech, then a short note after it on what you did and why.
