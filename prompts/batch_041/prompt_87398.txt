A science magazine article I read recently reports a proposal in animal cognition roughly as follows: researchers have now given a scientific answer to the question of which animals are conscious, namely that the mark of consciousness is the capacity for open-ended learning — the ability to acquire an indefinite range of new associations rather than a fixed repertoire of conditioned responses. Animals that have this capacity are conscious; those that lack it are not. Because the capacity first appears in the fossil record in the Cambrian, consciousness is a Cambrian invention. The practical upshot, the article says, is that fish, insects, and other animals that fail the test are not conscious, so the current wave of concern about their welfare is misplaced; and since the criterion is a threshold rather than a scale, no creature is more or less conscious than any other. I suspect the article is overselling, but I cannot tell where the line falls between fair reporting and embellishment, or whether the problem lies in the reporting or in the proposal itself. Please audit it sentence by sentence: say what a careful proponent of such a criterion would accept, what they would call a simplification, and what they would reject outright. Then say whether the ethical conclusion about fish and insects follows from anything that survives that audit. I am inclined to think the last two sentences are the magazine's own additions, but I am not sure. Please write your answer in American English.
