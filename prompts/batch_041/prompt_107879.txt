The panel I sit on — the enforcement advisory committee of a financial regulator, six members, one of whom is me and five of whom would prefer that I stop saying the word "entailment" — meets on the 14th, and I have been asked to draft the written opinion that will frame the vote, so I would like your help producing it. The case is a mid-sized bank whose two compliance files, taken together, entail that a client's payments breached a sanctions rule: the ownership file records who ultimately controlled the counterparty, the transaction file records the payments, neither file alone shows anything irregular, and the two sat in systems that nobody had ever linked. No employee ever joined them; a single query would have done it. The general counsel's office wants us to hold that the bank knew, on the principle that knowledge is closed under logical consequence: it knew what each file said, what the files said entailed the breach, so it knew about the breach. Our economist on the panel says that argument proves far too much — the same closure yields that the bank knows every tautology, that it knows absolutely everything once you notice that two departments filed incompatible beneficial-ownership forms (the contradiction is sitting in its own records, and from a contradiction anything follows), and that it knows every consequence of its records, including ones no program could be guaranteed to derive in time, since the consequence relation of the underlying logic is not decidable. The lawyers have also floated a third line, which I find harder to dismiss than the first: the bank did not merely fail to link the two systems, it arranged not to, and they say that arranging your records so that you cannot see what you would rather not know is not the same thing as ignorance.

What I need is a memo of roughly a thousand words that recommends a concrete attribution rule, states a bottom line the committee can actually vote on, and is candid about what that rule sacrifices. Specifically: it should say what threshold "readily available" inferences must meet, and whether the threshold is fixed by what this institution's systems could in fact have derived or by what any competent compliance function would have derived; it should say what we do with the contradictory filings — whether inconsistency triggers the full closure the lawyers want, or whether it makes the record unusable as evidence of what the bank knew; it should handle the timing problem, since closure makes knowledge timeless, whereas what we have to decide is what the bank knew on the day the money moved and what it merely could have worked out afterwards; and it should take seriously that the institution is not a person, so that no individual's state of mind settles the question, though the panel keeps reasoning as if it does. I wrote a dissertation on resource-bounded agents and closure, so I know the standard moves here, and I am uneasily aware that the memo I want to write is that argument with a deadline attached; what I need from you is not a survey of the problem of logical omniscience but the version of it that can survive a vote — including the strongest case for the general counsel's position, set out so that they would recognise it as their own rather than as a concession.

A few constraints, since the document goes into a file. No citations, no case names, no statutory references, and no reading list: the panel strips them and I cannot check them, so any appeal to authority will have to be made in the currency of argument. Structure it as a one-page rule, a justification, a short section headed what we give up, and a paragraph on what we should require of the bank before the 14th, with a date attached. And if the memo could have been written by reciting the textbook statement of the problem, it is not what I am asking for.
