I am a novelist at work on a book set in a hospital's genomic medicine unit, and I need your help with a problem that is more conceptual than clinical. The unit has adopted a policy of mandatory disclosure: any incidental finding from a patient's genome sequencing that has known clinical significance must be reported to the patient, whether or not the patient asked for it and whether or not anything can be done about it. The rationale offered is that patients have a right to know, and that knowing can only help them make better decisions. I am skeptical, but I lack the vocabulary to say exactly what the policy assumes. I would like you to act as an expert in decision theory and bioethics, think carefully, and help me reconstruct the philosophical commitments that this policy presupposes.

Begin by brainstorming the possible views about information, autonomy, and welfare that could underlie such a policy. Then narrow your focus to one candidate: the idea, often called Good's theorem on the value of information, that a rational agent cannot be worse off in expected utility by receiving additional information, provided the agent can ignore it and updates by conditionalization. Does the hospital policy presuppose something like this theorem? If so, what are the theorem's conditions, and does the policy satisfy them? Pay attention to cases where the information is about an untreatable condition, where the patient cannot un-know it, where the information changes the patient's preferences or self-conception, and where the patient would prefer not to know. If the policy does not presuppose Good's theorem, what other theory of information does it presuppose? Reconstruct that theory as charitably as you can.

Then distinguish what the policy strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Is the policy coherent, or does it contain tensions? For instance, does it treat information as always beneficial while also treating patients as fragile and in need of protection? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the policy if we rejected the presupposed view — if we took seriously the possibility that information can have negative expected value, or that the right not to know is as important as the right to know. Be specific about the institutional changes, and be honest about where the policy is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would license it. I am not looking for citations or a literature review; I want a careful conceptual reconstruction that I can use to give my characters arguments that feel real.
