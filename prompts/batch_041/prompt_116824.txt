I work as a software engineer on the digital side of a means-tested benefits system, and for the past year I have been building and maintaining the parts of it that enforce conditions on claimants. I have no formal background in philosophy, and I keep running into design decisions that seem to have arguments buried inside them. I would like help digging those out. Four features, which I believe are typical of workfare-style regimes generally:

First, claimants sign up to a commitment to perform a fixed number of job-search actions per period and to attend scheduled appointments. Miss an appointment, or log too few actions, and a sanction fires more or less automatically: payment is reduced or stopped for a set period. The claimant can escape it only by showing good reason, and the burden of showing it is theirs. The system cannot observe effort; it observes attendance and counts.

Second, overpayments are triaged by cause. Deliberate misstatement is fraud and can be prosecuted; the identical sum paid because the claimant misunderstood the rules is claimant error; if the department erred, it is official error. The money is the same in all three cases and the claimant's circumstances are the same; the consequences are not.

Third, there is a large apparatus for finding money paid out that should not have been. There is none for finding money never claimed that was owed. Non-take-up is not measured as a failure, let alone sanctioned.

Fourth, the claim process is deliberately wearing: repeated identity verification, documentation, waiting, and re-reporting of changes in circumstances. I have read the argument that this is not an oversight but a screening device, that making the transfer a little costly in time and dignity ensures that only those who really need it will bear the cost.

What theory of responsibility, of desert, and of the value of work does this arrangement presuppose? In particular: does the automatic sanction, with the burden of proof resting on the claimant, presuppose that non-compliance is within the claimant's control, and does that sit comfortably with the same system's acknowledgement elsewhere that claimants face barriers outside their control? Does the fraud and error triage presuppose that inner states are both morally decisive and reliably legible to an administrator who cannot observe them? Does the asymmetry between chasing overpayment and ignoring non-take-up presuppose that entitlement is something a citizen must activate rather than something already owed? And does the friction-as-screening argument presuppose that the transfer is a privilege to be rationed rather than a right to be delivered?

I would also like you to separate what the arrangement strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, since I suspect several different theories would license the same machinery, and to say where the design is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, if the desert-based picture were replaced with one that made need the criterion, what specifically would have to change in the rules, in the burden of proof, and in the way overpayments are triaged?
