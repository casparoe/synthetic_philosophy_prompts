I've been having a long arguement with a friend about when you're allowed to say there was a reason for something, and my gut verdicts don't seem to fit any one story. I want you to tell me what principle (if any) is behind them, and what I'd have to give up.

Here they are.

A chess engine plays a move because it sees the line wins — I say it had a reason for that move. It's tracking the position and it's after something.

My thermostat kicks the heat on and I say there's no reason anywhere in that. It's a strip of metal bending. "There was a reason for it to turn on, namely to keep the house warm" sounds like a joke to me — the only reason in the room is the one the manufacturer had.

A mousetrap snaps: same deal. The reason the bar comes down is the inventor's reason, not the trap's. I wouldn't say the trap had a reason to come down.

But now the weird one. A bacterium swims up a sugar gradient and I do think there's a reason for it to do that — and nobody has it. Not the bacterium, not any designer. The reason is just sitting there in the setup, floating free.

Same with a bee's waggle dance. There's a reason for it (it gets the foragers out), and I'm pretty sure the bee has no idea what it's doing. Floating free.

A beaver damming a stream: with the bee. Nothing in the beaver's head, reason floating free.

A seedling bending toward the window: reason floating free (light's good for it, nothing knows that).

A slime mold threading a maze to reach the oats: floating free, same as the bee.

But a river running downhill: no reason at all, and not even a free-floating one. It's just gravity. Same for a rock rolling down a slope.

And an RL agent that got good at some game just by climbing reward, where nobody ever told it what winning is: I genuinely can't decide. It feels different from the chess engine even though the outputs look the same, and I don't know if the difference is real or just that I can read one and not the other.

The case I keep going back and forth on is a chatbot giving me a helpful answer — there's plainly a reason for the tokens it spits out (they're the ones that get rewarded / reduce the loss), but does anything have that reason, or is it free-floating like the bee?

The story I'd like to tell is this: a reason is present whenever the behavour is explained by what it's for, i.e. whenever pointing at the outcome makes sense of why the thing does it. And the reason belongs to a mind only when the mind represents that outcome. So the engine has its reason because it represents winning; the bacterium's reason floats because nothing represents the sugar.

Does that actually get all of the above? In particular:

- The bacterium and the mousetrap are both mindlessly executing a design, yet I give the bacterium a free-floating reason and the mousetrap none. Is that defensible, or am I secretly just tracking whether the designer was a person? If it's the origin that matters, then a self-replicating trap that evolved would suddenly acquire a free-floating reason, which seems mad.
- Same for the plant vs the river — both just physics, but one has a free-floating reason and one doesn't. What exactly is the difference?
- What does my story predict for: a virus that evolves resistance to a drug, a self-driving car slamming the brakes, an ant colony's foraging pattern (as opposed to one ant), and a fungus network trading nutrients between trees?
- Is the engine / RL-agent split real, or is it the same free-floating reason in both and I'm just fooled by the engine's code being readable?

Tell me the cleanest principle that saves as many of these as possible, exactly which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and whether there's really a difference between there being a reason for X and X having a reason — or whether that's the whole trick.
