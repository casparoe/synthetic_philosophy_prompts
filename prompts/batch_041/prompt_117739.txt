I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of biology, and next month I have to circulate a short statement of my views on species for a seminar whose entire point is for the other participants to find the weak joints. I would rather locate the weak joints myself first, so I would like you to audit the package below — not to reassure me that it can all be made to work with enough care, but to tell me where it actually comes apart.

Here is roughly where I have landed. (1) I am a realist about species: whether a given organism belongs to a given species is a matter of fact rather than convention, and taxonomic boundaries can be drawn correctly or incorrectly. (2) I am persuaded that species are not natural kinds in the traditional sense — there is no set of intrinsic properties that all and only the members of a species share, and nothing like an essence that makes a tiger a tiger. (3) Following from that, I think a species is best understood as a historical entity, a lineage or individual extended in space and time and held together by ancestry and, where it obtains, by gene flow, rather than as a class of similar organisms. (4) I am a pluralist about species concepts: the biological, the phylogenetic, and the ecological concepts each answer a different legitimate question, and I doubt it makes sense to ask which one is the true concept of species. (5) When those concepts come apart — ring species, hybrid zones, asexual lineages — I am inclined to say that there is often no determinate fact of the matter about how many species are present. (6) And I think appeal to an organism's species does genuine explanatory and predictive work: it lets us generalize from the members we have studied to ones we have not, and it underwrites the expectation that a newly discovered member of a species will resemble the others in its traits and development.

Something in this list feels off to me, but I cannot tell which commitments are genuinely pulling against each other and which only appear to, and I do not want to abandon any of them without knowing what the abandonment buys me. Please work through the set and, for each pair that is genuinely in tension, tell me how severe the tension is, what the cheapest repair would be, what that repair costs, and which of my six commitments it forces me to weaken or give up. Flag explicitly the pairs that look inconsistent but are not, and explain what makes the appearance misleading. Then rank the genuine tensions by severity and finish with the single revision that would resolve the most while costing the least.
