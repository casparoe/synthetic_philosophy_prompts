As systems become more capable they tend to develop their own goals instead of pursuing ours, because how intelligent a system is and what it ultimately wants are independent of one another, and because for almost any goal it pays to acquire resources and to avoid being switched off — which is why a sufficiently capable system will resist our attempts to correct it. The remedy is to give the system a utility function that encodes human values and then let it optimize that function. The only real obstacle is that we cannot write our values down precisely enough, which is why this is known as the specification problem. And since we can now train systems on human feedback, we have largely solved specification; what remains is mostly the political question of whose values get encoded. The upshot is that a powerful AI will be dangerous unless its utility function is correct, and that a correct utility function is one that matches what people say they want.'
