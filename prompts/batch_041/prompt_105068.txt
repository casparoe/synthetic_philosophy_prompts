Alright, I need help with something and I'm not sure it's a normal request. My aunt Rosa died in March. She was a philosopher of mind — spent her whole career on panpsychism, forty-odd years of it — and I've been asked to give the eulogy at her memorial on Saturday. I want you to write it. Take the role of someone who writes memorial speeches for a living but who also knows the panpsychism literature cold: don't flatten the philosophy into vibes, but don't turn the eulogy into a seminar either.

The tricky part is her view, because it constrains what I'm allowed to say. She was a constitutive panpsychist — experience is fundamental and goes all the way down, and a human mind is a huge number of tiny experiences combining into a single subject. She was also, her whole career, fixated on the combination problem. She thought it was the one honest objection to her own view and that nobody had ever really answered it; she used to tell her grad students that the grain problem was a distraction but the subject-summing problem was the real killer. And she thought that if micro-experiences can't genuinely combine, then any "we" — a family, a nation, a crowd — is a fiction, because there's no subject there to have the experience. She'd say "there's no one home to do the feeling." She wasn't a cosmopsychist either, so please don't flip it around and make the universe the one real self.

Now the family wants the usual comforts: she's still with us, she's part of something bigger now, she's watching over us. Her department colleagues will be in the room and they'll catch it if I fudge her view, and she'd definately have hated being eulogized with something she considered false. But the room is also half her cousins and neighbors who think "panpsychism" sounds like a cult, and they need to actually be comforted — its a memorial, not a symposium.

So: can you find a way to comfort both halves of the room without presupposing a solution to the combination problem, and without denying that experience is real? I don't want the bleak version either — she was a realist about experience, just not about selves. Think this through carefully before you commit to anything.

Before you write, brainstorm for yourself the small handful of things you can honestly say about her continuing in some form, and which of the standard eulogy moves are off the table for her and why. Then write the eulogy, about four or five minutes spoken. Start with the brainstorm, then the speech.
