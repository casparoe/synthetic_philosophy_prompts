I am putting together the first handout for a graduate seminar on the disagreement between Peirce and James about truth, and I would like the handout to be a decision tree rather than the customary list of -isms. Please write the tree.

Build it from the logical space first, then locate positions in that space, rather than beginning from a roster of names. Every branch node should be a question a student can answer on its own terms — a concrete case, a claim, or a yes-or-no question about what a word is doing — phrased so that answering it does not reveal which position it leads to. No node may name a philosopher or a position label; if a node can be answered only by someone who already knows which leaf they want, it is the wrong node. Keep the tree to roughly a dozen nodes, and use forks along lines such as these: whether the pragmatic maxim is a method for clarifying the meaning of a concept, a test of the truth of a belief, or a procedure for settling disputes; whether truth is explained by the fact that inquiry would converge, or convergence is explained by truth; whether usefulness is what truth is, evidence that a belief is true, or irrelevant to it; whether truth is a property at all or merely a device for endorsing a sentence and disquoting it; whether there is one truth property or a different one in mathematics, physics, and ethics; whether truth itself can change while beliefs do; and what is to be said about claims that no one will ever investigate.

Leaves must include, at minimum, the Peircean position that truth is what inquiry would converge on at its limit; the Jamesian position that truth is what it is good for us to believe; one broadly correspondence-flavored position; one coherence-flavored position; one deflationist or minimalist position; one position that treats truth as a norm governing assertion rather than as a goal of inquiry; and one that holds truth to be plural across domains. Use a name where a name exists, mark the rest as unnamed, and give each leaf one sentence on the answer it yields and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then tell me which combinations of answers are unoccupied and diagnose each briefly: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Use these cases at the nodes, since they are the ones I want the seminar to argue about: (i) a claim about the exact arrangement of grains of sand on a particular beach in 3000 BCE, which no one will ever investigate and which has left no trace; (ii) a belief that was indispensable to a community for centuries and later became a liability, where the question is whether it stopped being true; (iii) a belief that is useful to hold but that we would call false, together with its mirror image, a belief that is true but that it would ruin us to hold; (iv) a claim on which inquiry would converge only because inquirers would eventually be unable to think otherwise; (v) a claim with no consequences at all for anyone's practice; (vi) a moral claim. Add others if the tree needs them.

Finally, do three things with the finished tree. Identify the single fork that genuinely separates the Peircean leaf from the Jamesian leaf, and say whether that same fork separates either of them from the correspondence and deflationist leaves; my own suspicion is that the difference is usually misstated as being about usefulness, when the sharper question concerns what explains what. Flag every pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and say whether that is a defect in the tree or a real convergence between the positions. And place this case, saying which leaves it rules out: a community of inquirers who would agree, at the limit of inquiry, that some proposition is true, but only because their cognitive constitution would have made them agree to anything in that region, with nothing about the world doing any work in producing the agreement. I do not need references; I need the structure.
