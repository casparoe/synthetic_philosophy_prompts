So I run this small pragmatism reading group — mix of phil grad students and a couple of people from the history of science dept — and every single time we do Peirce vs James on truth we spend two hours talking past each other. Everyone nods along at the idea that truth is what works and then quietly means something totally different by it. I want to hand out a one-page decision tree at the start of next session so people get sorted into actual positions before the shouting starts.

Can you build it? It should take someone from a pretty neutral starting point — basically "I think the pragmatists are onto something about truth but I couldn't tell you which pragmatist I am" — down to a named leaf. Phrase every branch as a question a person can actually answer without already knowing which label it leads to, and make each branch the claim that really seperates the two subtrees, not "are you a Jamesian or a Peircean?"

A few things I care about:

The spine should run through what the position thinks the truth-bearer is, and whether truth is a property at all. I want a branch that peels off the deflationist / Rorty-ish crowd early — the ones who say there's no property here, true is just an endorsement we hand each other — before the tree gets into the epistemic stuff.

For the people who do think it's a property, the next split should be whether the standard is indexed to an individual believer's needs and psychology or to a community of inquiry, and then whether the community standard demands convergence in the long run or only present warranted assertability. That should pull James apart from Peirce apart from Dewey without me having to name them anywhere in the nodes.

Please include at least these leaves: the Peircean limit-of-inquiry view; the Jamesian expediency view (truth as what it's good for someone to believe, not what's warranted); Deweyan warranted assertability; a Rorty-style social-endorsement position; Putnam-style idealized rational acceptability; and the option that pragmatism is a method or maxim for clarifying concepts and NOT a theory of truth at all (I suspect half my group is secretly sitting here and doesn't know it).

Use concrete cases at the decision nodes, not abstractions. I want the Newtonian-mechanics case (worked beautifully, got overturned), the comforting-false-belief case, an untestable past-tense claim nobody will ever check, and a maths case where inquiry in the empirical sense doesn't even get off the ground. If two leaves answer every case the same way, flag them as indistinguishable rather than fudging it — I'd rather the handout admit that than pretend.

Also, if there are combinations of answers that nobody actually holds, list them at the bottom and tell me whether you think that's because the view is incoherent, just unattractive, or genuinely unexplored.

Keep it to roughly 8-10 branch nodes, and label each leaf with the position name plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing a grad student would immediately poke at.
