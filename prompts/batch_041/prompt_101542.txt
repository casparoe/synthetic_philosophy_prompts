A friend and I spent most of a long dinner rehashing an argument we have had before. He insists that a model's false assumptions are like scaffolding around a building: you take the scaffolding down once the structure stands, and nobody afterwards asks whether the scaffolding was load-bearing. I kept insisting this cannot be the whole story, because some false assumptions are exactly what make a model's output come out right. I want to convert the argument into an assessment for the upper-level philosophy of science seminar I teach, in the unit on idealization and unrealistic assumptions, where we read Milton Friedman's essay on the methodology of positive economics and the standard critical responses to it.

Please write me one essay question for a 90-minute in-class final, together with a model answer of roughly 650 words and a marking scheme. The question should not be answerable by reciting the F-twist: a student who has memorized the slogan that a theory's assumptions matter less than its predictions should still have to work.

To keep the question in the weeds, I want it built around a distinction I intend to introduce to the class. It is my own, and I have not seen it stated in exactly this form, so please treat it as a fresh piece of apparatus rather than something to attribute to anyone. Say that an assumption is *inert* with respect to some output when perturbing it toward greater realism leaves that output essentially unchanged; *compensated* when the assumption is false in a way that would change the output on its own but is offset by another false assumption in the same model, so that the output is right for the wrong reasons; and *load-bearing* when the falsity propagates to the output without offset. The question should require students to (a) separate the two parts of Friedman's position — the part about predictions being the test of a theory, and the part about greater significance going with greater unrealism — and say which part can survive scrutiny; (b) use the inert/compensated/load-bearing distinction to state the conditions under which a false assumption is, and is not, excusable by predictive success; (c) apply this to a single case that I supply within the question itself, namely a macroeconomic model that assumes a single representative household with rational expectations; and (d) give a verdict on my friend's scaffolding metaphor.

The marking scheme should be built around must-hit points, with the marks for each adding to 20, and a one-line note on the mistake that typically costs students those marks. Please make the boundary between a strong answer and a merely competent one turn on the compensated case, since that is where the F-twist actually gets into trouble.

One thing I care about: in the model answer and the marking notes, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. Do not present a contested reading as the consensus — where the philosophy is divided, say so, and say what the disagreement is about.
