My friend Rosa and I got into a stupidly long argument last night after a seminar on welfare economics, and I'm still not sure if her argument is a trick or a real reductio. She calls it the menu-shuffling argument. Here it is: (1) A person's preferences are whatever their choices reveal; to prefer A to B just is to choose A over B when both are on offer. (2) If a person prefers A to B, then choosing A makes them better off than choosing B would. (3) So whenever someone chooses A over B, A makes them better off. (4) But choices flip when you change the default, the order, or the wording, even though nothing the person cares about changes—only the frame does. (5) So if we flip a default and she now chooses B, then by (3) B makes her better off. (6) Same person, same cares, same options: A makes her better off and B makes her better off, depending on which menu we hand her. (7) So we can make someone better off just by rearranging the menu. (8) But that's absurd—welfare can't be a function of our levers. (9) So one of (1)–(4) has to go.

I'd rather keep (1) and (4) than (8), because without (1) I don't know what preference is measuring, and without (4) the whole nudging literature is describing nothing. Rosa says the fix is to say the welfare-relevant preferences are the ones you'd still have after reflection—she calls them laundered preferences—but that seems like changing the subject. I'm taking a course on decision theory and we spent alot of time on this and I still cant tell. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I especially want to know if there's a step that's just invalid, or if it's valid and I have to pay somewhere. And if I keep (1) and (4), am I forced to give up (2) or (3), or is there a way to keep them by being more careful about what better off means?
