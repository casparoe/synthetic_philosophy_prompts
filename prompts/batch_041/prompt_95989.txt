I am a novelist by trade, midway through a book about a cardsharp, which may be why I cannot stop turning this over. For the past six months I have also been helping my nephew, who is twenty-two and has decided he wants to make his living as a tournament poker player, and what has surprised me is that the advice I give him keeps changing shape depending on which part of his game we are working on. Some things, I tell him, he can only learn by playing thousands of hands and letting his bad decisions cost him real money, because no amount of reading will put the sting in the right place. Others he drills with a training app and a small reward for getting them right, because repetition with feedback is the only thing that has ever moved them from knowing to doing. Others I tell him to work out at the kitchen table before he ever sits down, playing the hand forward in his head against an imagined opponent until the mistake dies there instead of at the table. And then there are the things I simply hand him ready-made: a chart, a mnemonic, a piece of jargon, a way of counting outs that some other player invented and that he can adopt wholesale without ever having to rediscover it. The trouble is that I cannot give him a principled account of how to divide his hours among these four ways of learning, and I have a nagging suspicion that they are not really four at all but a single ladder, so that time spent on the lower rungs is time stolen from the higher ones.

His coach tells him the chart is a crutch and he should throw it away and trust his instincts; his girlfriend, who is a nurse, tells him to treat the whole thing like clinical simulation and rehearse until he is bored. I would like you to tell me whether there is a way of thinking about these modes of learning that explains why each of them does something the others cannot, whether they really are arranged in a hierarchy of increasing power, and if they are, what the lower rungs still buy you once you have climbed above them. I am especially uneasy about the possibility that my nephew is using "I need to feel the pain myself" as an excuse to avoid the harder work of thinking the hand through in advance, and I would like your advice on what I should actually tell him to do with his time.
