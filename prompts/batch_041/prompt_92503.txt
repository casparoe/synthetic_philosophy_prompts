Right, I'm building a bank of philosophy-of-biology prompts and I need you to take on the role of a philosopher of biology who actually knows the function literature — the etiological / selected-effects camp (Wright, Millikan, Neander, Godfrey-Smith and the usual suspects) up against the causal-role / "systemic capacity" camp (Cummins and everyone who followed). Don't give me the textbook spiel. I want a case built to order. Before you write anything, spend a bit of time brainstorming a handful of candidate traits, bin the obvious ones, and only then commit to one — I'd much rather have a single carefully worked case than three hand-wavy ones.

The task: construct a thought experiment — one species, one trait, described in a single paragraph — engineered so that the etiological account and the causal-role account disagree about the *content* of the trait's function. That is, both accounts say the trait *has* a function, but they say it's a *different* function. The disagreement has to fall out of the theories themselves, not out of anything you stipulate about which answer is correct.

Constraints:
- Realistic biology. No aliens, no gods, no genetic engineers, no organisms that only exist because you said so — it has to be the sort of thing that could actually turn up in a real population.
- Don't reach for the two examples everyone uses: feathers-as-thermoregulation-turned-flight, and the panda's thumb. Find something fresher. Bonus points if it isn't a vertebrate at all — plants, fungi and microbes are criminally under-used here.
- The two accounts must genuinely disagree, not just describe the same function at two different grains of description, so watch for sneaky "same thing, different level" moves that dissolve the disagreement.
- Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph. The analysis afterwards can be as long as it needs.

Then, please:
1. State the trait, give each account's verdict, and lay out the exact reasoning each one uses to get there.
2. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work — the thing that, if you flipped it, would collapse the two verdicts into agreement. Then actually flip it and show me that they collapse.
3. Say what the case is *for* — what it's meant to establish — and be honest about what would count as cheating here (e.g. quietly baking the answer into the setup).
4. Give me the strongest reply from whichever side comes out worse: how a defender of that account would try to argue the case doesn't show what it seems to.

Think carefully and dont rush it.
