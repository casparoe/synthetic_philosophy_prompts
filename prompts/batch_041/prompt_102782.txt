I serve on the allocation board of a regional research foundation that each year divides a fixed $10M core-facility budget between two long-standing partner programs, A and B. Under the current arrangement each program draws a baseline of $2M, and the board tops them up from the remaining $6M; Program A can usefully absorb at most $8M in total and Program B at most $6M, so the feasible splits are any (a, b) with a + b ≤ 10, a ≤ 8, b ≤ 6. Two proposals are on the table. Our chair wants to keep dividing the surplus — the $6M above the baselines — in proportion to the baselines, i.e. half to each. A consultant wants us instead to adopt the Kalai–Smorodinsky rule, which draws the line from the disagreement point (the pair of baselines) to the ideal point (each program's best feasible payoff, here $8M and $6M) and picks the point where that line meets the Pareto frontier; equivalently, it divides the surplus so that the two programs realize the same fraction of their maximum possible gain. The consultant stresses that this rule is built on a monotonicity requirement — a program whose feasible options improve should not end up with a worse agreement — and that it deliberately gives up the independence of irrelevant alternatives that characterizes the Nash solution (the split maximizing the product of the two programs' gains over their baselines). I would like you to reason carefully through what each rule is actually trying to equalize before you commit to anything, because a complication has arisen that I think exposes the difference: Program A is opening a second laboratory line this year that raises its ceiling from $8M to $10M, even though the total pool is unchanged.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the board before the vote. Write as a decision theorist advising the board rather than as an advocate for either program. Open with your recommendation — a specific dollar split for this year, plus a standing rule to apply in future years — and then defend it. Work through what each candidate rule delivers here (the chair's proportional-to-baseline rule, a straight 50/50 split of the surplus, the Nash solution, and Kalai–Smorodinsky), showing the arithmetic, and be explicit about which of them respond to Program A's new ceiling and which do not. Then confront the hardest objection head-on: our finance director's view that A's internal expansion is irrelevant to how we divide the existing pool, and that it is simply unfair for B's share to shrink because A got bigger. Give that objection its strongest form and answer it honestly; if the answer requires conceding that Kalai–Smorodinsky imposes a real cost on B here, say so and say exactly what the cost is. Close with the operational steps and a date by which the board should adopt the rule. Keep the whole memo under 700 words, make it self-contained, and include only the finished document — no scratch work, no hedging about what you might have written instead.
