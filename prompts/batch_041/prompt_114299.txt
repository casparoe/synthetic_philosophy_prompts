I chair the investment committee of a mid-sized private foundation, and at our March meeting we must approve the next three-year asset allocation. Our models put two candidates at the same expected return: a duration-matched bond ladder whose return distribution we have estimated from sixty years of data, and an infrastructure fund whose returns depend on a regulatory regime that has existed for four years, where our consultants' models disagree by more than the entire expected return and no two of them share a prior. Several members have noticed that we drift to the first option whenever the numbers tie, and one of them now calls this our Ellsberg problem: she says we are the person refusing the ambiguous urn, and that whatever makes the paradox a paradox makes our reluctance a bias to be overridden rather than a judgment to be respected. Another member answers that the foundation's 4.5% payout rule and its inability to raise new gifts after a bad decade make a bad outcome for the second fund asymmetric with a good one, so treating the two as interchangeable in expectation is the real error.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete allocation with a time frame, take a clear position on whether our caution is a bias or a defensible response to ambiguity, and state honestly the strongest consideration against whichever side you come down on.
