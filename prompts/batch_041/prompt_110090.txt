After thirty-five years in experimental physics, mostly low-temperature work and far too much time spent repairing instrumentation, I retired last spring and have been working through the philosophy of mind that I always meant to read and never had the hours for. I have arrived at an impasse over epiphenomenalism, and what I want is a careful diagnosis of my reasoning rather than a verdict on the doctrine itself.

The position, as I understand it, is that the felt qualities of experience — the sting of a burn, the particular redness of red — are produced by neural events but produce nothing in turn; I have seen them compared to the steam whistle of a locomotive, which the engine makes and which does no work in moving the train. This is said to generate a paradox, and I take a paradox to be a contradiction, as in the paradoxes of set theory. The reasoning then seems plain enough: if my pain is causally inert, my belief that I am in pain, and my report that it hurts, were not caused by the pain. But I do know, reliably and not by luck, that I am in pain; my report is not a guess that happens to come out right. So the epiphenomenalist must say that I can never know what I am experiencing, and worse, that his own belief that experiences are inert is itself inert, cut off from the experiences it purports to describe and therefore unable to have moved him to write his book. A view that refutes itself in this fashion cannot be true, so I conclude that the felt qualities of experience must after all be causally efficacious, and hence that a complete physics cannot be causally closed but must leave room for consciousness to do work in the world.

A colleague in the department, more patient with me than I deserve, says this is too quick and offers a repair: the brain state that produces the pain can produce the belief and the report as well, so the report is reliable after all and nothing in the physical story needs disturbing. But that only relocates my difficulty. If the belief is caused by the brain state rather than by the pain, then surely the belief is about the brain state; it reports neural goings-on, not the hurt. And if a zombie twin, physically identical to me, would utter the same words with no hurt anywhere in him, then my words cannot be knowledge of the hurt either. I would be grateful to be told whether my conclusion is the right lesson to draw — that consciousness must push the physical world around — or whether I have simply misdescribed what the epiphenomenalist is committed to, and if the latter, exactly which of my steps is the bad one.
