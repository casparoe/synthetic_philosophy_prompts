I run the equity-review desk for a city council that has adopted a Rawlsian test for major policy proposals: a proposal may stand only if it can be justified to the residents it leaves worst off. We screen two to four proposals a month — tax changes, zoning, transit fares, school-funding formulas — I get about a week per proposal, the voting panel is made up of non-philosophers, and there is no budget for outside consultants. What I need is a decision procedure the panel can actually run, not an essay on the original position: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision back to the panel because the question is one of values rather than facts. Build it so that it survives the two parts of the theory that do the most work and are the easiest to get wrong — that "the least advantaged" are picked out by an index of primary social goods rather than by income alone, so the worst-off group can change depending on which good you weight, and that the difference principle is a requirement of reciprocity among citizens at the legislative stage, under a veil of ignorance, rather than a case-by-case maximin rule applied at the point of administration — and have it say explicitly which of the two lexically prior requirements (equal basic liberties, then fair equality of opportunity) is doing the work whenever the difference principle on its own would yield a different verdict. Then run the procedure on three proposals: (a) congestion pricing in the downtown core that would fund bus frequency but raise the cost of driving for low-income commuters who have no alternative; (b) replacing a uniform property tax with a land-value tax that every model we have says would raise the disposable income of the poorest decile while shrinking the tax base of the poorest school district; and (c) a proposal of your own choosing that the procedure rejects even though it helps the poor on average. State plainly what the procedure cannot settle, including at least one case where the panel must make a call the theory does not license, and say what a wrong call there would cost. Lead with the procedure's bottom line — the single ordering rule that does most of the work — in one sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of this request.
