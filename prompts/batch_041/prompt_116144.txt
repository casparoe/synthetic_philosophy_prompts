The question (10 marks): Dennett rejects the Cartesian theater and proposes the multiple drafts model in its place. (a) What is the Cartesian theater, and which views does Dennett think are committed to it? (b) What does the multiple drafts model claim, and how is it supposed to avoid the problems of the theater picture? (c) Set out the Orwellian and Stalinesque interpretations of filling-in phenomena, and say what Dennett thinks the choice between them shows.

The answer:

(a) The Cartesian theater is the picture of the mind on which everything happening in the brain converges on a single place and a single moment, and that is where you are — the audience watching the show. Dennett's name for the view is Cartesian materialism: the assumption that somewhere in the brain there is a finish line, and that the order in which things cross it is the order in which they show up in experience. The picture is really just Descartes minus the soul: for Descartes the pineal gland is the screen and the immaterial mind is the viewer, and for the Cartesian materialist some patch of cortex is the screen and a physical homunculus is doing the watching. So Dennett's real target is Descartes' dualism, and the theorists who are actually in trouble are the ones who still believe in an immaterial mind. He also thinks the picture generates an infinite regress, since whatever watches the screen would itself need something watching it.

(b) The multiple drafts model says there is no single stream of consciousness with a fixed order of arrival. Content fixation goes on in parallel in many places at once; there are many drafts of what is going on, being written and revised, and what we call the stream of consciousness is the product of a virtual machine that language installs in the brain, which Dennett calls the Joycean machine. Nothing becomes conscious by arriving somewhere. A content is conscious to the extent that it achieves fame in the brain — it manages to influence lots of other processes and leave traces that matter later. That is a matter of degree rather than a threshold at a location, so questions of the form "when exactly did you become conscious of X" often have no determinate answer. Dennett's method for studying this is heterophenomenology: treat what subjects say about their experience as data to be explained, rather than as an authoritative report of what is going on inside.

(c) Both interpretations concern cases where the brain seems to use information that wasn't available yet. Dennett's example is colour phi, where a light appears to change colour partway along a motion path it couldn't have travelled yet. On the Orwellian story, the subject's experience at the time really was missing something, and the memory of it gets rewritten afterwards, like the records being altered in Nineteen Eighty-Four. On the Stalinesque story, the revision happens before the content ever reaches experience, so the final, fully resolved version is the only one ever presented, like a show trial where the verdict is fixed in advance. Dennett's conclusion is that the Orwellian story is the right one: the brain does its filling in retrospectively, by revising the memory. This is exactly what the multiple drafts model predicts, since a later draft can overwrite an earlier one, and it explains why there is no single moment at which the content became conscious.
