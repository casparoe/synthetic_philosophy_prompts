Write a dialogue between two philosophers of language who disagree about the foundations of speaker meaning. The first defends an intention-based account: for a speaker to mean something by an utterance is for her to intend to produce a certain response in an audience by means of the audience's recognition of that very intention. The second defends a practice-based account: what an utterance means is fixed by communal regularities of use, and a speaker's private intentions matter only insofar as they align with those regularities.

The dialogue should center on two cases that pull the accounts apart. In the first, a speaker named Mira says, "The bridge is out," intending her hearer to believe that the bridge is out and intending him to form that belief by recognizing her intention to communicate it. Mira also knows, however, that her hearer would believe anything she says regardless of her intention, and in fact his belief is caused by a post-hypnotic suggestion rather than by any recognition of her intention. Does Mira mean that the bridge is out? In the second case, a visitor to a community makes a gesture that, by local convention, means yes. The visitor does not know the convention; he makes the gesture intending to convey yes by means of the host's recognition of his intention. The host, who knows the convention but not the visitor's intention, takes the gesture as yes. Does the visitor mean yes?

Both philosophers should be equally competent, and each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them. The dialogue should not end in a tidy resolution unless the arguments genuinely support one; if the cases expose a real impasse, let the impasse stand. Avoid citations, references to specific publications, and quotations from real texts.
