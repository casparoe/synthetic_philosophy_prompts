Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words. It should be a real work of fiction — a plot, characters, and at least one scene of genuine dramatic pressure — not an essay wearing a narrative costume.

The setting is a long-term field station built around the study of a single ant colony. The station's researchers have come to suspect, and a few of them have come to believe, that the colony's collective activity amounts to a mind. Take the following as given; these details are invented for this prompt, so do not substitute anything from the literature or try to source them:

- The colony has been under continuous observation for eleven years. Its behavior is not merely swarm-like. Over spans of days it appears to form something like a plan — abandoning a rich food source after a disturbance, redirecting its foragers, and returning to that same source a week later by a route it never previously used.

- The colony's cognitive processes run far slower than a human's. The team cannot converse with it in real time. They address it through a pheromone-writing apparatus laid across the nest entrance, and a full exchange of question and answer takes days. The station's "reader" — the one technician who has learned to parse the colony's replies — is the only person who claims to hold anything like a conversation with it.

- Two rival criteria are on the table, both of them invented here. The Continuity of Address test holds that a colony has a mind only if it sustains a stable self-model across the replacement of a majority of its workers. The Quorum Signature test holds that a mind requires an integrating signal that no individual ant instantiates and that exists only at the level of the whole.

- A funding renewal requires the station to demonstrate a result. The plan on the table is a gradual reset: over several weeks, workers will be removed one at a time and replaced with naive workers drawn from a cloned colony, while the station watches to see whether the colony's plan survives the turnover — and, if it does, for how long.

- A rival hypothesis haunts the station: that the apparent plan is an artifact of the interface itself, that the reader's own pheromone-writing has been quietly teaching the colony what to say. On this view the mind under study belongs to the observer, not the observed.

Constraints for the story:

Do not resolve the question of whether the colony has a mind by authorial fiat. The story may end without a verdict, and probably should.

It must contain a scene in which one of the two criteria is actually put to the test, and the result must be genuinely ambiguous — the reader should be able to argue either that the colony passed or that it failed.

Keep the colony from becoming cute, and keep the researchers from becoming villains or saints. The ants are ants.

Avoid the stock beats of the artificial-mind story: the laboratory that turns out to be the experiment, the machine that begs for its life, the scientist who is secretly the subject. Find the drama in the ordinary institutional textures of the station — a funding deadline, a logbook, a disagreement between colleagues who like each other — and in the specific strangeness of a mind that thinks at the speed of weather.

The story's central dramatic question should be whether a mind can be located in a pattern that outlives the replacement of its parts, and what it would mean — legally, morally, or merely practically — for the colony to be wronged by the reset. Let the ending leave the reader able to defend either answer.
