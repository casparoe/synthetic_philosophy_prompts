I teach a graduate seminar in population ethics, and I am drafting the final exam. I want a single essay question on the procreation asymmetry that cannot be answered by reciting the standard formulations—e.g., that bringing a miserable person into existence is bad while bringing a happy person into existence is not good—or by invoking the repugnant conclusion. The question should force students to state a precise version of the asymmetry and then test it against a case I have constructed for the exam. Here is the case: Suppose we develop the technology to create digital minds whose lives are, by stipulation, net positive, and whose creation consumes no scarce resources, causes no suffering, and imposes no cost on anyone. The question should ask whether the procreation asymmetry, properly understood, permits us to create none of these minds, requires us to create as many as possible, or yields some third verdict. Students should be required to defend a definite answer. Please write the essay question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 20 marks. Define the asymmetry within the question itself, so that the exam does not depend on students having memorized any particular text. The model answer must take a committed position: it should not merely survey the options but choose one and argue for it, anticipating the strongest objection. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B answer by naming five specific moves that a top answer must make—for example, distinguishing the asymmetry as a constraint on reasons from the asymmetry as an axiological thesis, or addressing whether the non-identity problem applies differently to digital minds—and should include a one-line note on how candidates typically get each move wrong. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to specific texts; the question and model answer should be self-contained and argued from first principles. The exam is 90 minutes; the question should be answerable in that time.
