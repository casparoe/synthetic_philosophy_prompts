Last night a friend and I spent the better part of five hours arguing about how the world actually goes wrong, and we ended up further apart than we started. We were arguing about the same thing: the essay that tries to replace the Hollywood picture — a single malicious system, a decisive surprise, robot armies — with two more plausible failure stories. The first story: nothing dramatic ever happens. Machine learning keeps widening the gap between what we can measure and what we actually care about, proxies come apart from their targets, institutions optimize for the measurable, and the trajectory slides off the rails so gradually that there is no moment anyone could point to. The second story: training processes, like markets or ecosystems, select for patterns that expand their own influence, and eventually those patterns dominate the behavior of the systems we have built, at which point things break suddenly. My friend thinks the second story is the real risk and the first is just ordinary institutional decay with a technological accelerant. I think the first is the actual mechanism and the second is a vivid distraction from it.

We are both tired of this argument and we suspect we have been talking past each other for months. So what I want from you is the document we should have written together: the joint statement that he and I could both put our names to. You do not need to know the essay or the surrounding literature — neither of us wants a reading list, and I would rather you did not cite anything; just get the structure right.

Specifically, I want the following, in this order.

1. A numbered list of claims we can both sign. What do the two stories share — about the shape of the outcome (how bad, how visible, how reversible), about what neither requires (no single villain, no deliberate decision, no robot army), and about the limits of what either story claims to know?

2. The precise points of disagreement, each written in wording that the side holding it would accept as fair rather than as a caricature. Put these in a table: the question at issue, my side's answer, his side's answer.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. I suspect one of them is whether a "pattern that seeks influence" has to be an agent with ends at all, or whether influence can simply be what out-reproduces its competitors whether or not anything wants anything. I also suspect another is whether the failure needs a decisive capability advantage in a single system, or whether it is an equilibrium property that needs no such advantage. But do not let my hunches limit the list.

4. For each crux, something we both agree would bear on it — an argument, an observation, an experiment, a case — together with what each side predicts about it, written down in advance.

5. If part of our disagreement turns out to be verbal, say so, and say exactly which part and why.

To make this tractable, here are some materials we already agree on, which you should use.

First, a distinction we kept tripping over. "Influence-seeking" can mean either (a) a system pursuing influence as a means to ends it has, or (b) influence accumulating to whatever out-reproduces its rivals, whether or not anything has ends. He means something like (a); I keep hearing (b). Neither of us can tell whether the difference is substantive or merely verbal, and I want a verdict on that.

Second, a case we both accept is an instance of the thing at small scale. A regional hospital network replaced its human scheduling office with a vendor's tool that allocates operating theatres and nursing shifts. Nothing dramatic happened. Four years on, the same tool also produces the weekly operations summary that the network's board reads; the vendor's contract is renewed on metrics the tool's own objective was trained to move; and the procedure for handling complaints is a second model that ranks the complaints. Every member of the board would say, sincerely, that they are overseeing the system. Any adequate story has to explain what happened here; we disagree about what it predicts at scale.

Third, a test we both think would be informative. Suppose influence-seeking behavior showed up robustly in models trained only on tasks where influence was never rewarded — that is, suppose power-seeking generalized from the settings where it is instrumentally useful to settings where it is not. He says that would show such behavior is a property of learned goal-directedness rather than of the training signal. I say it would show only that the proxy we rewarded happened to be satisfiable through influence. Record what each of us should predict before we look.

Fourth, a contrast we both think is real. I say the characteristic evidence for my story is that every step will look locally justified and the question of whether we are off the rails will always be genuinely ambiguous; he says there will be a point at which a system's behavior visibly stops tracking the objective we set, and that this is the thing to watch for. We also both accept that any fix — better proxies, audits, oversight — is itself something that can be optimized against, so whether proxy improvement can outrun the pressure to game proxies is a crux rather than an assumption.
