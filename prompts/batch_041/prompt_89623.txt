I teach a twelve-person upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind, and I have one seventy-five-minute session to spend on Dennett's case against the Cartesian theater and in favor of the multiple drafts picture. My difficulty is that the argument goes down too smoothly. The students read the prose, they agree that positing a single place where it all comes together for an inner audience is a mistake, and then ten minutes into discussion they are talking as though there were a moment, the moment a signal enters consciousness, and asking whether this or that content had made it in yet. They can recite the position and cannot stop presupposing the picture. I want a session in which the presupposition comes apart from the inside, under its own weight, rather than being diagnosed for them.

So design an in-class activity for twelve students in seventy-five minutes, in an ordinary seminar room, with a projector, laptops, index cards, and no lab equipment or precise timing apparatus. I can screen a short video if you specify the timing and the sequence, and I can hand out cards and run a metronome. The activity should put them in a position where they are obliged to answer a question of the form when did you become conscious of that? about their own experience, and where the only answers available to them seem to require an inner finish line, so that they feel the pull of the theater in their own case before anyone names it. Then something in the setup should make the finish line move: the judgments they have already committed to, out loud, in front of the others, should turn out to be revisable after the fact, so that the word when stops having a stable referent. I would rather the evidence be their own judgments and their disagreements about them than a demonstration I narrate; but if the design can exploit a phenomenon along those lines, apparent motion between flashes of different colors, illusory taps interpolated between real ones, a mask that seems to erase something already presented, use it, provided it runs with what I have in the room. Give me the rules, the roles or materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the wording of whatever I say to them at the moment the trap closes. Please don't hand me a lecture plan or a reading list; I want something they do.

Then the debrief, which should map particular moments in the activity onto the parts of the multiple drafts picture they are meant to be seeing: why the choice between an Orwellian and a Stalinesque revision of experience looks forced and why it may be a distinction without a difference, what fame in the brain is supposed to replace, and why asking when a content became conscious may be no better than asking when a country's center of gravity crossed its border, a comparison I half-remember from Dennett and would like either vindicated or replaced. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it; I expect at least one student to insist, sincerely, that they really did see the thing in the order they first reported, and I don't want to be caught without a decent answer to that. And be honest about which feature of the position the activity cannot reproduce at all and that I will simply have to assert, and about whether the whole thing works only because of some ambiguity peculiar to my setup that would not generalize.
