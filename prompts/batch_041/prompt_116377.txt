Ok so I keep running in to two competing stories about success and I want to actually test them instead of nodding along. Story one is the American one: the self-made man, the dream, we build the pedestal and put the winner on it. Story two is the Scandinavian/Australian one: the Law of Jante, tall poppy syndrome, the group mows down anyone who rises to far above everyone else. I more or less buy the contrast, but I want to run it through a real institution that I think neither story handles cleanly: Athenian ostracism.

Quick refresher, since you can't look things up: once a year the Athenian assembly could vote on whether to ostracize somebody, and if enough potsherds came in with the same man's name scratched on them, he was exiled for ten years — no charge, no trial, no appeal, not even an accusation of a crime. You could be the most useful person in the city and still get the boot simply for being too prominent. Themistocles, who basically saved Greece at Salamis, got ostracized. Aristides, nicknamed the Just, got ostracized too, and there's a story that an illiterate voter who'd never met him asked him to scratch Aristides onto his own shard, and when Aristides asked why, the man said he was just tired of hearing him called the Just. So here's a democracy that handed out crowns and statues to its heroes and simultaneously kept a standing, scheduled procedure for throwing them out.

What I want: take tall poppy syndrome / the Law of Jante as a theory of why a community cuts down its successful, and run ostracism through it. Do the mapping for real — who counts as the tall poppy, who's doing the cutting, what role envy is supposed to be playing, what the mechanism is, what the theory says is going on. Then be honest about what it explains and what it misses. Because it looks different in kind to me: this isn't spontaneous ressentiment, it's a formal majority-vote procedure on a calendar, and the stated target was the would-be tyrant, not the merely successful — the Athenians were scared of a man who could take the city, not of a man who'd done well. So does the tall-poppy lens actually fit, or does it flatten something the Athenians were doing on purpose? And then push it the other way — what does ostracism do to the theory? Does it show that cutting down the tall poppy can be rational institutional design rather than envy? Does it undercut the neat American-Dream-vs-Jante contrast, since here's a society that celebrated its winners and had a legal mechanism to banish them at the same time? And where is the tall-poppy idea just silent — is there a better lens for what Athens was actually up to, or does the tall-poppy frame still earn its keep?
