What I want to argue is that famines aren't natural disasters, they're political. Three cases I can talk about without notes. Bengal, 1943 — I'm fairly sure the rice that was around hadn't fallen off much from a couple of years earlier, but the price went through the roof and the landless couldn't buy it, while Calcutta and the war effort got fed first. Ireland in the 1840s — the blight was real, but food kept being shipped out of the country while relief came with conditions attached. And the Great Leap Forward — grain kept going abroad, and people who reported the real harvests got punished for it. I know you can't look any of this up: if one of my facts is off, say so and I'll cut that bit, and don't fill the gap with something you're guessing at. Also no death tolls in the speech — I can't defend a number and neither can you.

Here's what I actually want it to hang on, and I don't think I've seen it put this way: in all three, the people starving weren't only short of food, they had nobody they could ask.
