At a reading group that spans the philosophy and computer science departments here, I keep running into a conversational move that I find harder to assess than I would like, and I should be glad of your help in getting clear about it. The subject is the intentional stance and the propriety of attributing beliefs and desires to large language models; the move is a one-liner, delivered with the air of settling the matter, to the effect that such a system is only ever predicting the next token, that it therefore has no beliefs, and that any belief-talk about it is mere projection. I am not asking you to adjudicate whether my colleague is right. What I want is an inventory of the distinct things that sentence could be claiming, laid out as a menu rather than reduced to a single preferred reading, and a verdict on each entry: whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false; whether it could be established from the armchair, by inspecting the training objective, or only by empirical work on what the system's internal states in fact do; and, where an entry is true but harmless, a note to that effect, since I suspect that most of the true readings are compatible with a perfectly robust realism about belief attribution.

Two clarifications may help you to aim at the readings that actually matter to me. First, I have been trying to keep apart two things one might be doing when one credits such a system with a belief: a predictive use, on which the attribution is a device for forecasting the system's outputs across contexts, and an accountability use, on which the attribution registers a commitment to which the system can be held — we expect it to cohere over time, to revise when it meets counter-evidence, and not to assert a proposition and its negation in the same breath. Second, the case I have in mind is not a system that emits a single sentence: it is one that will, across a long exchange, assert some claim, later concede a counterexample, adjust its subsequent assertions accordingly, and resist instructions to contradict itself, while plainly doing none of this for reasons it could report. For each reading on your menu, tell me whether it bears on the predictive use, the accountability use, both, or neither; and tell me which reading my colleague must have in mind if his one-liner is to do the work he wants it to do. Another member of the group proposes that the better analogy is with the beliefs we ascribe to a character in a novel, and I should like to know whether that comparison strengthens my colleague's case or quietly abandons it. I am revising a lecture on the stance for a master's course and would like to use your inventory as the spine of a session, so a short concluding paragraph on which reading, if any, would license the curt dismissal would be very welcome.
