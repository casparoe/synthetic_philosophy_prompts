I am a second-year PhD student in philosophy, currently writing the opening chapter of a dissertation on causation and divine action, and I have been sitting in on a seminar in classical Islamic thought that has me re-reading the seventeenth discussion of al-Ghazālī's Incoherence of the Philosophers, where he denies that the connection between what we call a cause and what we call an effect is a matter of necessity and treats it instead as a settled pattern of divine custom, with the fire and the cotton as his stock example. My problem is not exegetical. It is that whenever the seminar tries to say what an account of causation of this kind is actually committed to, everyone offers a different criterion, and the disagreement turns out to be about the criteria rather than about the texts; my advisor's standing advice is to fix the specification before writing another line of commentary, and I have finally decided to take it.

So what I want from you is the desiderata: the full list of things an account of causation of this kind must deliver in order to count as a metaphysics of causation rather than a pious refusal to have one, with the case or consideration behind each requirement and an explicit marking of which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp. I am working with a rough list of my own, and I would like it corrected rather than merely supplemented. It seems to me that such an account must: (i) distinguish a cause from a mere occasion without circularity, which is what the old problem of accidental regularities forces — the night following the day cannot be a cause of it, yet a regularity that holds only under an unobserved condition, like a herb that works only when the moon is new, has to come out as causal or as something else, and the theory has to say which; (ii) say whether the unit of divine action is the individual event or the standing pattern, since a regularity that holds in nine hundred and ninety-nine cases out of a thousand with no discernible difference in the antecedent conditions will otherwise have no home; (iii) explain what individuates one divine act from another, given that the agent is one and simple, when a single occasion produces a compound effect or when two independent chains would each have produced the same result; (iv) ground whatever has to be true for the counterfactual that, had the fire touched the cotton, the cotton would have burned, since the practice of medicine, the techniques of agriculture, and the credibility of testimony all appear to require it; (v) tell us whether the difference between a change in custom and a miracle is available to a finite inquirer from the inside or only to God, and what follows for the epistemic status of reports of the former; (vi) either accommodate human action — by the familiar expedient of a power created in the agent for that specific act — or explain why the loss of genuine human causation is a cost worth paying; (vii) settle whether ordinary causal speech is true, elliptical, or systematically false, and thus whether the view is revisionary with an error theory or compatible with the surface grammar of our talk; and (viii) respect the difference between what the dialectical argument in the Incoherence actually establishes, namely that the philosophers have not proved necessity, and a positive metaphysics of divine action, which is a stronger claim than the argument supports. I would add one further requirement that seems to me to cut across the others: the pattern of divine action must not be arbitrary, on pain of losing any ground for the wisdom and goodness of the created order, and it must not be necessitated, on pain of losing divine freedom.

What I most want to know is which of these cannot be jointly satisfied. My own suspicion is that (iv) and the freedom requirement pull in opposite directions — custom can ground the counterfactual only if it is a fact about what God will do, and a fact about what God will do looks like a constraint on a will that is supposed to be under no necessity — and I would like your view on whether the arbitrariness-and-necessity pincer I just described is a second, independent deadlock or merely a restatement of the first. If the set is indeed unsatisfiable, tell me the least costly repair and what it gives up. Finally, and briefly, score the main contenders against the list — al-Ghazālī's own position as you would reconstruct it, the necessitarian picture he attacks, the concurrentist view on which creatures have real powers and God concurs in each exercise of them, and a plain regularity view of the Humean sort — and tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate the most candidates. I do not need citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I need the criteria, each attached to the case that motivates it, and a candid verdict on which of them are negotiable.
