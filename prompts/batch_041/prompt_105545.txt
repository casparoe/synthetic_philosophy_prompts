I'm giving a talk to our department's logic reading group in two weeks and I need my Q&A prep. Here's the room: about eight model theorists and proof theorists, plus two cognitive scientists who keep showing up because they work on analogy and metaphor. So basically nobody there studies self-reference for a living, but everyone can smell hand-waving from across the room, and the two cognitive people will pounce on anything that sounds like a metaphor being mistaken for a result.

What I'm defending: the inviolate level -- the level a self-referential system can't depict from inside itself, the hand that's drawing rather than the hand that's drawn -- is not a cute Escher trick or an artifact of whatever notation you happened to pick. It's ineliminable. Any attempt to fold the rules of such a system back inside the system itself either coughs up a fresh inviolate level or breaks into paradox. You can move the level around; you can't delete it.

The argument runs on three cases and I'd rather you treat the third as the load-bearing one. One: a camera pointed at the screen it's feeding, video feedback -- the level that isn't in the picture is the camera and the wire. Two: the usual self-referential sentence in a formal system, where the system's own truth predicate is exactly what can't appear inside it. Three, and this is the one I care about: a compiler that compiles itself. And the crux across all three is the regress -- point a second camera at the first camera and the second camera just becomes the new outside; add a metalanguage and the metalanguage is now the thing that can't speak about itself from within itself. My claim is that this regress is not a failure of ingenuity on our part, it's structural, and that the flat accounts people keep reaching for are all either the same fact renamed or quietly circular.

Give me the Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. I want the objections this specific crowd will actually raise, not the ones I'd get from a Hofstadter fan club -- ordered by how likely each is times how much damage it does, each phrased the way one of them would actually put it (tell me whether it's coming from the model theorists or the cognitive pair), each with a reply I could deliver in about two minutes and a blunt note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front so I don't look like I'm defending the indefensible. And tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
