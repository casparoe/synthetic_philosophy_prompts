I'm taking a third-year moral philosophy course, and our seminar has spent two weeks circling the question whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible. I want the document the two sides should have written, rather than another round of talking past each other. Take a committed dilemmist — someone who holds that there are situations in which an agent is genuinely required to do each of two things, cannot do both, and therefore does something wrong whichever way she turns — and a committed opponent who holds that every apparent dilemma is either resolvable (one option is, all things considered, what she ought to do) or merely epistemic (she cannot tell which). Draft the joint statement both could sign: the claims they agree on, including what a dilemma would have to be for the question to be substantive and what their dispute is not about; the precise remaining disagreements, each worded so that its own side would accept the formulation; the cruxes — I expect agglomeration, the status of 'ought implies can', and whether 'ought' is read all things considered or pro tanto to be among them; and, for each crux, the argument or case both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Two further demands. The statement must contain a minimal test case — the thinnest description of a situation the dilemmist will call a genuine dilemma and the opponent will not — together with each side's account of which extra features are doing the real work, with the agent having brought the dilemma on herself (a promise extracted under pressure, say) among the features tested. And it must say whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, for instance about how 'ought' or 'wrong' is being used, and if so which part, and whether settling that would leave a real disagreement standing. Close with what each side would accept as reason to abandon its position.
