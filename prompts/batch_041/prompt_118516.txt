You are a philosopher of causation and action theory, with a strong background in the metaphysics of omissions, causal selection, and the relation between causation and norms. I want you to act as a careful conceptual engineer. Do not give me a literature review, a bibliography, or citations. I have been trying to state what I think about causation by omission, and I keep running into trouble. I am interested in causation, not just moral blame, though I suspect the two will not come apart cleanly. In every case below, assume determinism, assume the agent knows the relevant facts, and assume the agent could act easily. My verdicts are these:

1. A child is drowning in a shallow wading pool. A stranger walking past could pull her out without risk or effort, but does nothing. She drowns. Verdict: the stranger did not cause her death. He is morally monstrous, but the water and her inability to swim caused the death.

2. A lifeguard employed to watch that same pool is texting instead of watching. She could pull the child out easily, but does not. The child drowns. Verdict: the lifeguard caused the death.

3. A hiker accidentally kicks a rock loose above a trail. The rock rolls toward another hiker. The first hiker could shout a warning, but does not, because he is embarrassed. The second hiker is injured. Verdict: the first hiker caused the injury.

4. A bystander sees a rock already rolling from a natural rockfall toward a hiker. The bystander could shout a warning, but does not. The hiker is injured. Verdict: the bystander did not cause the injury.

5. A doctor fails to prescribe a cheap, effective antibiotic. The patient dies of the infection. Verdict: the doctor caused the death.

6. A doctor fails to prescribe a drug that would not have worked. The patient dies of the infection. Verdict: the doctor did not cause the death, though the doctor may be negligent for other reasons.

7. Two strangers are standing near a public pool. A child is about to be trapped by a malfunctioning drain. Each stranger could hit a clearly labeled kill switch, and each knows the other could too. Neither has any special role or duty. Neither hits it. The child dies. Verdict: neither stranger caused the death.

8. Two lifeguards are on duty at that pool. Each independently could hit the kill switch. Each is required by the job to hit it. Neither hits it. The child dies. Verdict: neither lifeguard individually caused the death; the pair's failure caused it, but not either one alone.

9. One lifeguard is on duty at that pool, and is the only person who could hit the kill switch. The lifeguard does not hit it. The child dies. Verdict: the lifeguard caused the death.

10. A village of one hundred people could each donate one dollar to buy medicine that would save a child's life. No one donates. The child dies. Verdict: no individual villager caused the death; the village's collective omission did.

11. A company programs a self-driving car not to brake for a certain pedestrian edge case. The car does not brake, and a pedestrian dies. Verdict: the company and its programmers caused the death; the car's failure did not cause it, because the car is not an agent.

12. A poisons B's tea. C sees the poisoned cup and could easily swap it for a clean one, but does not. B drinks the tea and dies. Verdict: A caused B's death; C did not.

13. A camper starts a campfire, then falls asleep. The fire spreads. He could have stamped it out when he woke briefly, but did not. Verdict: the camper caused the fire damage.

14. A parent fails to feed an infant. The infant dies. Verdict: the parent caused the death.

15. A neighbor of that parent could easily feed the infant, but does not. The infant dies. Verdict: the neighbor did not cause the death.

The pair I cannot reconcile is (2) and (8). In both, a person with a role-based duty could easily have prevented a death, and the death would not have happened if they had acted. In (2) I say the omission caused the death; in (8) I say neither omission caused it. The only difference is that in (8) there is another person who also could have acted. That difference feels morally important, but I cannot see why it should be metaphysically decisive. I also cannot reconcile (10) with (7): in both, no individual seems to be a cause, yet I want to say the village's collective omission caused the death, while I do not want to say the two strangers' collective omission caused the death. And (3) versus (4) makes me worry that creating the risk is doing all the work, while (5) versus (6) makes me worry that counterfactual dependence is doing all the work.

Here is a tentative principle I have been trying out: an omission causes an outcome just in case (i) the outcome counterfactually depends on the omission against the baseline of what the agent was required or expected to do, and (ii) the omission is a departure from that baseline that is either a breach of a role-specific duty, a failure to mitigate a risk the agent created, or part of a plan to bring about the outcome. I already see that this principle makes causation depend on norms, and that it seems to say each of the two lifeguards in (8) caused the death, which I deny. It also seems to handle (10) only by treating the village as an agent, which I am not sure I want. And it may say the bystander in (4) did not cause the injury only because he had no duty, while the hiker in (3) did cause it because he created the risk; I am not sure that asymmetry is stable.
