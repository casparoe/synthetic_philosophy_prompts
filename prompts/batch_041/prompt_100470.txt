Write a dialogue between two philosophers of biology about free-floating rationales -- the idea that evolution can be described as acting for reasons even though nothing in the lineage ever has a reason. One is a defender: she thinks these rationales are real, discoverable facts about a lineage, which is why you can predict what a trait will do before you've measured it, and why two unrelated lineages can converge on the same solution. The other is a deflationist: he thinks "the rationale" is something we project onto selection, which only ever tracks who left more offspring, and that calling that a reason smuggles in a reasoner. Have them argue over a concrete made-up case: a moth that insists on laying its eggs on one particular host plant that's rare and, in its new range, actually worse for the larvae than a common alternative -- so the "reason" behind the behaviour ("give the larvae the right food") seems both present and out of date. Push both sides to their limits: does convergence between two lineages give you one rationale or two? Can a rationale be there without selection ever having tracked it? Does the defender's view just collapse into the deflationist's once we notice the thermostat also "tracks" temperature? Neither should be a strawman -- the deflationist needs a positive account of why rationale-talk works so well, and the defender should give ground somewere. Keep it a real back-and-forth, not two monologues, and have each of them actually respond to the other's last point.
