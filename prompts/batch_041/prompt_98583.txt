I've been reporting on a fight in my state over civil commitment, and the thing that keeps bugging me is this: we lock someone up against their will when a psychiatrist says they're mentally ill and a danger to themselves, but if that same person commits a crime, the law only excuses them if they couldn't tell right from wrong at the time. So the diagnosis that's enough to hold you isnt enough to let you off. Whats the theory of mental illness sitting under that? It only seems to make sense if there's a real disease in there that can override a person's own judgment about their own life. And the weirdest part is that saying you're not ill gets read as itself a symptom — the less insight you have, the more justified the hold, which feels like it could go in a circle. Meanwhile half the clinicians I talk to will say off the record that a lot of what they're treating isn't any brain disease at all, just misery and bad circumstances, which is basically Szasz's old line that mental illness is a myth and these are really problems in living. So: does the commitment system actually need the disease view, or could it run on Szasz's view with some paternalism bolted on? And if it could, what would have to change about who gets held and on what grounds?
