I'm a third-year PhD student (decision theory, philosophy of econ, depends who's asking) and I've fallen down a hole on the absent-minded driver problem. Tell me what principle, if any, they add up to, and which of the verdicts has to go.

The base case, spelled out so we're on the same page: he's driving, there are two exits. First exit = 0 (nowhere he wants to be), second exit = 4 (that's where he's going), and if he sails past both he ends up somewhere ok-ish = 1. He's absent-minded, which here just means: when he's at an exit he can't tell whether it's the first or the second — his memory of the drive so far is the same at both moments, so whatever he decides to do he does at both. He can still carry a die and roll it, he just can't tell the exits apart.

1) Base case: he should pick the plan that maximises expected payoff, and I make that out as exiting with prob 1/3 at each exit, which gets him 4/3. I'm pretty confident here, and I don't think it's close — 1/3 beats always-continue's 1 and always-exit's 0. What messes with me is that 1/3 is not the best thing to do at either exit. At the second, exiting is 4 vs continuing for 1; at the first, continuing is clearly better than exiting for 0. So the best plan prescribes the worse act at every single node. I think that's fine, not a contradiction, but I can't explain why in a way that satisfies me.

2) When he's actually standing at an exit, though, my gut says exit. Like if you grabbed me and said 'quick, what should he do right now', I'd say exit. I know this is in tension with (1), and I know that if he exits every time he gets 0, so I'm not defending it. I just notice that's what I want to say.

3) Now make the second exit worth 2 instead of 4, keep 0 for the first and 1 for sailing past both. My verdict: he should just keep driving and take the 1. I checked it — exiting with prob p gets you (1-p)(1+p) = 1 - p², maxed at p = 0, so never exit, expected 1. But I hate this one. He's giving up on a 2 that's sitting right there.

4) Base case again, but the consolation for sailing past both goes up from 1 to 3. Verdict: also just keep driving. (1-p)(3+p) = 3 - 2p - p², decreasing, so p = 0 and he gets 3. This one feels right — if the fallback is nearly as good as the prize, trying only risks landing him on the 0.

5) If someone offers to cure his absent-mindedness — a GPS that tells him which exit he's at, nothing else changed — I say he should pay up to 8/3 for it, since with it he gets 4 and without it he gets 4/3. I think that's the tell that the whole problem is about recall and not about rationality.

6) Someone who always exits and ends up with 0: I'd say he did something dumb, and I'd say it even though at every moment of choosing he did the locally best-looking thing. So I do evaluate the plan rather than the act. Which is exactly what I said in (2), except (2) says the opposite.

So what's the principle? I want something like 'evaluate a strategy by its ex ante expected payoff, and don't demand that the strategy be optimal at each node' — but then (3) and (4) make me nervous, because that same principle says 'never even try' in cases where trying looks obviously right, and it tells me my gut in (2) is just wrong. And if the fix is 'he should be sequentially rational', I seem to get 0 or something incoherent, so that can't be it either.

And if you can say what work 'plan' vs 'act' is doing here without it turning into a debate about free will and personal identity, please do, because my reading group will definately go there and I'd like alot of advance warning.
