I'm in the second year of a PhD program, and for the past few months a handful of us have been meeting after the Thursday decision-theory seminar at a pub near campus to keep arguing about the things the seminar didn't settle. Last week the conversation turned to infinite worlds, and two of my friends — Ravi, who works on formal epistemology, and Elena, who works on population ethics — went at it for the better part of an hour while the rest of us listened and occasionally refilled our glasses. I've been asked to write the exchange up for our reading group, and I'd like you to help me referee it.

Ravi started. Suppose, he said, that the universe is spatially infinite and roughly homogeneous, so that any local configuration with nonzero probability in a finite region is realized in infinitely many regions. Then there are infinitely many people in agony and infinitely many people flourishing, and the total welfare of the world comes out infinite on any way of counting. But then "maximize total welfare" says nothing at all: the totals are the same size either way, the sums don't converge, and adding or subtracting finitely many happy lives makes no difference to anything. So consequentialism, at least in its totalist form, cannot even rank the worlds it exists to rank; the whole apparatus is undefined. Elena said his mistake was to reach for cardinality in the first place. Cardinality is not a measure of how much of something there is, she said; we already accept as much in mathematics, where the even numbers are no fewer than the naturals and yet plainly half of them. What we should compare infinite worlds by is density — the limit of the good-to-bad ratio taken over larger and larger finite regions, the way one defines the natural density of a set of integers. If that ratio settles down, the world with the better ratio is better, full stop. Ravi answered that the limit is a fact about the order in which you enumerate the regions, not about the world: enumerate them one way and the running proportion of good to bad creeps upward, enumerate them another way and it creeps downward, and nothing in the universe privileges one enumeration over another. And for many worlds the proportion simply never converges, so the density view delivers no verdict precisely where a verdict seems to be needed. Elena replied that we are not free to choose the enumeration: the physical measure over regions, or the limiting frequencies generated by the world's own dynamics, supply a privileged order, and where the ratio genuinely fails to converge the two worlds are incomparable — which is a real answer rather than a failure to answer, and far better than the alternative, which is to say that an infinite world teeming with both joy and suffering is exactly as good as an empty one plus a single contented person. Ravi said that conceding incomparability loses the game rather than winning it. If infinite worlds can be incomparable, then what matters for any choice I actually make is whether it improves the world I am in; and if all my finite acts leave both the density and the total unchanged — as they must, since they touch finitely many lives in an infinite sea — then consequentialism declares every one of my choices equally good and equally bad. That, he said, is the real problem: not that we cannot rank infinite worlds, but that inside one, nothing we do matters.

Please go through the exchange step by step and adjudicate it: for each distinct claim the two of them make, say who has the better of it and why, separating the claims that are clearly right from those that are clearly mistaken and those that are defensible but overstated. I am less interested in a verdict on who won the argument overall than in a map of where each of them is correct, where each is trading on an ambiguity, and what both of them are leaving out — in particular, any assumption the two of them share that is doing far more work than either of them notices. Write it as a set of numbered rulings I can read aloud at the group.
