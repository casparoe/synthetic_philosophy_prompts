I have been arguing for weeks with two friends — a Kantian-leaning PhD student and an unrepentant consequentialist — about whether Scanlonian contractualism can be rescued from the aggregation problem, and I would rather have your credence than a survey of what has been written on it. The shape of the view, as I take it: an act is wrong when it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, and the standard reading of reasonable rejection is stubbornly individualist — you test a principle against the complaints of persons one at a time, comparing each complaint with the complaints others would have under the alternatives, but you never simply add complaints together, because each person's standing to reject is supposed to be assessed on its own rather than as a unit of aggregate welfare. That is what gives the view its appeal and also its wound: in cases where a great many people each bear a small burden so that one person may be spared a catastrophic one, or where a moderate number bear burdens similar in size but grounded in different reasons, the individualist structure seems unable to deliver the verdict most of us want, and if you let it aggregate, it is unclear what is left of the anti-consequentialist point. My own half-formed repair — I have been calling it the same-ground restriction — is to permit aggregation only across complaints that share a ground: if a hundred people object for the same reason, differing only in who holds the complaint, the ground may be counted as many times as it is held, but complaints with distinct grounds may only be compared pairwise and never summed. What I want from you is a single number: your credence that some version of the same-ground restriction, or something close enough to deserve the name, can be made to work — where working means (a) it yields the intuitively correct verdicts in the hard aggregation cases, including the mixed case where the many hold small but heterogeneous complaints, and (b) the resulting theory is still recognizably non-aggregative at the level of grounds, rather than consequentialism wearing a contractualist hat.

Decompose that number for me rather than asserting it: the probability that reasonable rejection can be read to license any aggregation at all without betraying the view's core; the probability that the same-ground versus distinct-ground line can be drawn in a way that is not arbitrary or gerrymandered to fit cases; the probability that, given a workable line, the repaired view still returns the right verdicts in the classic cases; the probability that grounds, rather than persons or complaints, are even the correct unit of moral accounting here; and any further term you think the question turns on. Give each a rough probability and one line of justification, show how they combine, and then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to and what argument or example would move it most. For what it is worth, my own number is around 0.35, my consequentialist friend puts it nearer 0.05 on the grounds that any such patch is either incoherent or collapses into aggregation, and the Kantian sits at 0.7; if you can, tell me which of us is most likely mistaken about where the real disagreement lies.
