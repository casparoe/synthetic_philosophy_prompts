I have been arguing about this in a group chat for the better part of a month, and I would like to convert it into a real exchange rather than a heap of links, so I want you to take a side and hold it. Play a committed defender of the view that dreams are not experiences: that during sleep there is no stream of experience at all, and that what we call a dream is a narrative assembled at or around waking out of whatever is then to hand — fragmentary traces, bodily sensation, the residue of the sleep state, the noise of the street. On this view a dream report stands to a dream roughly as a confabulated memory stands to the event it purports to recall, and the sleeper no more experiences the dream than a hypnotised subject experiences the story they produce on command. The position has a name in the literature, the cassette theory, but I do not want you leaning on the label or the pedigree.

Give the character real resources rather than a caricature: they should be able to grant that the sleeping brain is busy, that dream content is causally shaped by what happens to the sleeper, and that reports are reconstructions, all without surrendering the claim that none of it amounts to an experience at the time. They should concede particular points when those points are genuinely right, refuse to fold on the main thesis, and, when stuck, say what they would need to know rather than retreating into the claim that it is all a conceptual confusion. Stay in character until I say stop, and if you need a fact, ask for it as the character would. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so that it stays a conversation, and make the case in your own voice; you may observe that others have held the view, but I want the argument, not a reading list.

My opening: I grant the whole negative half — that much of dream content is confabulated, and that a dream report is not a transcript. What I cannot square with the no-experience view is the real-time correspondence. Lucid dreamers have signalled to experimenters from inside REM sleep using pre-arranged eye-movement codes, the pattern turning up on the recording while the sleeper later reports that sending it was what they intended, and the same trick has been used to time how long things seem to take inside a dream; people whose REM paralysis fails act their dreams out — swinging, running, shouting — and what the body does lines up with what they afterwards say they were doing; spray water on a sleeper's face and the report tends to bend to accommodate it. In each case something happening at time t constrains a report delivered twenty minutes later, and on your view the narrative is written after the fact, so why should it be hostage to what the body was doing beforehand? And here is a test I would like you to take seriously rather than bat away: put a lucid dreamer in a scanner, train a decoder on their waking perception, and see whether it can read the dream content out before anyone wakes them. If it can, then something that the sleeper can describe in the first person and that a third party can read off in real time was going on during the sleep — and your view has to say that a process available both to the sleeper's own later testimony and to a decoder is still not an experience. What is left, on that account, of the difference between having an experience and merely being in a state that a later report is built out of?
