I sit on the investment committee of a small endowment, and a few times a month we are asked to join — or to bet against — positions that a great many other institutions already hold: a crowded currency trade, a sector everyone has piled into, a settled view of where rates are going. We have to decide within a day, only the three of us sign off, and none of us is a specialist in the underlying markets, so I need a procedure I can actually run rather than an essay on herd behaviour. Please build me one, in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a decision, and where it hands the judgment back to us because the question is about our own risk tolerance rather than about the world.

The distinction I care about most is between three things that look identical from the outside. First, a crowd in which each member independently saw something — many private signals, correlated only because we are all reading the same public data. Second, a crowd in which the later arrivals are simply acting on what the earlier ones did, so that real private information is being discarded; the theory says such a crowd should be fragile, reversible by a surprisingly small amount of new public evidence. Third, a crowd that is imitating in order to avoid looking foolish, which carries no information at all. The procedure has to separate these three, and to specify what evidence would actually distinguish them — in particular, whether the earliest movers had strong private reasons, and whether a small, publicly checkable piece of contrary evidence would make the crowd reverse.

Then run it on three cases: a crowded consensus that a particular government's bonds are safe; a consensus that a company's earnings will keep growing, where the first few analysts demonstrably had real private information; and our own largest current position, which the committee loves. At least one verdict should be one I will not like. Where the procedure reaches a verdict, state what single observation would overturn it, and be explicit about the steps where the reasoning is most likely to fail or where two of the tests disagree and the procedure cannot settle the case.

Give me the procedure in prose, then a JSON object with one entry per case, using these fields: 'case'; 'diagnosis', one of independent-signals, information-cascade, reputational-imitation, or undetermined; 'fragility', high, medium, or low; 'action', one of join, fade, wait, or gather-more-private-signal; 'key_uncertainty'; and 'what_would_flip_this'.
