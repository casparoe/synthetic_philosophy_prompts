I belong to a reading group on nineteenth-century critiques of the press, and we keep circling Kierkegaard's account of the public: the thought that the public is a phantom, an abstraction that is everything and nothing at once, that nobody is it and yet anyone can be addressed as it, and that the press is at once its instrument and its maker. I would like to stop arguing about whether the account is fair or rhetorically overheated and instead ask what an adequate theory of the public, in this vein, would actually have to deliver. Please write me the desiderata. Give me the phenomena such a theory must explain — the public's unreality alongside its real effects on individuals; leveling that proceeds without any leveller; the difference between an assembly, a crowd, a congregation, a people, and a public that never meets; the way anonymity and print generate a voice that belongs to no one and yet can praise or destroy a particular person. Give me the cases it must get right: the individual who is edified or ruined by something that is not a person; the polemicist who denounces publicity through the very channels of publicity; the reader who is a member of the public without ever having joined anything. And give me the distinctions it cannot collapse — between the public as a social formation and the mere sum of its readers, between leveling as a dated event and leveling as a standing condition, between being part of the public and being the single individual whom the public addresses. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp within this tradition; where you think a demand reflects Kierkegaard's own polemical commitments rather than anything the phenomena require, say so plainly.

I am most interested in the places where the list turns against itself, so flag any pair of requirements you think cannot be jointly satisfied — in particular, whether a public that is genuinely unreal can also be a genuine cause, and whether the press can be both the creature and the creator of the public without circularity. Then tell me which single requirement, if I could keep only one, does the most work in excluding otherwise plausible theories: one that treats the public as a large group of people, one that treats it as a piece of ideology with no distinctive structure of its own, one that treats it as a mere by-product of a particular media technology. You may close by saying briefly which of those three survives your list, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves. Please take a position rather than surveying the options: tell me what you think a theory of the public has to be, which of the demands are spurious, and where the account as it stands cannot be made consistent. And do not send me to particular editions or secondary literature — I want the requirements worked out, not a report on who has said what.
