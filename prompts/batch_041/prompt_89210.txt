I am a science journalist at work on a feature about the vocabulary we use for machines that appear to think, and my touchstone case is the 1997 rematch between Garry Kasparov and IBM's Deep Blue. Kasparov lost the match 3.5 to 2.5; the hinge was a game in which the machine produced a move he read as too subtle, too human, to have come from a computer, and in the aftermath he suggested publicly that a grandmaster had been brought in to help the machine. IBM denied it, declined a rematch, and retired Deep Blue. I want to treat the episode as a test case for Dennett's intentional stance — the proposal that we predict and explain a system by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs and desires, a stance we may adopt or abandon according to whether it earns its predictive keep.

Give me the mapping first. What is the system whose behavior is being predicted, what beliefs and desires does the stance attribute to it, and what was Kasparov actually doing — at the board, and then in front of the press — when he reasoned about it? Then tell me where the stance earns its keep and where it misfires. I have a particular question I cannot settle: was the accusation of a hidden human a failure of the intentional stance or its logical completion? Does adopting the stance commit you to a mind behind the behavior, or does it explicitly refuse that commitment? Dennett sometimes writes as though the stance is a predictive instrument that says nothing about what is really there, and elsewhere appeals to the notion of real patterns; tell me which reading the episode supports, whether the two can be held together, and what work the distinction is doing.

Then run the analysis in reverse. Deep Blue was a closed, hand-tuned, rule-and-search system; the systems that occupy us now are trained rather than programmed, and people attribute beliefs to them far more readily and with far less discomfort. Does the Kasparov episode suggest that the readiness to attribute mentality belongs to the attributor rather than the system, or does it suggest the stance is tracking a difference that is genuinely there between the two kinds of machine? A philosopher I have been interviewing proposes what she calls the leverage test: attribute a belief to a system only when you can state the counterfactual predictions the attribution licenses and name the observations that would make you withdraw it. Run Deep Blue and a current language model through that test and tell me what it does and does not separate. Finally, give me the honest limit — what the intentional stance cannot in principle tell us about a system whose behavior is, in fact, fully explained by its design.
