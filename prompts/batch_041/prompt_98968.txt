I am a novelist, and I serve on the governing council of the Meridian Society of Authors, an international membership organization that speaks publicly on behalf of imprisoned and persecuted writers. On Thursday the council votes on two connected matters, and I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand. I would like you to draft that opinion.

The first matter is a statement we intend to issue about our member, the novelist Vera Lindqvist, who has been held for eleven months without charge in a country I will not name here — call it simply the detaining state. Two drafts are on the table. The first names the state, uses active verbs and short sentences, applies the words hostage and punishment, and demands her release by a stated date. The second expresses deep concern, urges all parties to respect due process, notes with regret the length of her detention, and names no one. The drafters of the second say the first would be quoted by prosecutors as proof that she is an agent of foreign interests, and would close the consular channel through which we have so far obtained her medication; Lindqvist's partner has privately asked us not to use the word hostage for exactly that reason. The drafters of the first say the second is the kind of prose that lets a government hold a novelist for eleven months while everyone involved feels they have said something.

The second matter is a standing rule proposed by one of our members: that every political statement the Society issues be rewritten until no plainer version exists, that the passive voice and abstract nouns be banned outright, and that the rule be justified by appeal to Orwell's essay on politics and the English language. The proposal has gathered support because it sounds like integrity. I am uneasy about it and want the memo honest about why. That essay runs two claims together — that vague, euphemistic prose causes political dishonesty, and that it merely registers it — and I suspect only the weaker claim survives inspection, in which case a rule against abstraction cannot do the work its sponsors want. A translator on the council has also proposed a test she calls the re-translation test: a sentence is evasive if you can render it into another language and back and recover everything except a commitment. I think that test is more useful than the rule, but I want you to judge.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. Recommend a concrete course of action — which draft we should issue, with what specific amendments; whether we should adopt the standing rule in whole, in part, or not at all; and by when. Engage the strongest argument on the side you come out against, on its own terms: take seriously both the claim that plain speech here may cost Lindqvist her medication, and the claim that a society of writers unable to say what it means has no reason to exist. Do not dissolve the tension by assuming that clarity and effectiveness always coincide.

Begin with your recommendation in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; no preamble, no restating of the question.
