I am preparing a seminar on theory change and I want to pin down what I actually believe about when a superseded scientific theory deserves credit for having got something right. My verdicts are these. Fresnel's wave optics, built around an elastic ether, was on the right track: its equations survive in Maxwell's theory, and it yielded the striking prediction of a bright spot at the centre of the shadow of a small opaque disk. Maxwell's ether was not a discovery but a mistake, and I am content to say the equations were retained while the medium was simply abandoned. Bohr's model of the atom was not on the right track, even though the energy levels it assigned to hydrogen are the levels quantum mechanics assigns; I will not say Bohr was approximately right about electrons in orbits. Newtonian mechanics was approximately true, and for me that means it is recovered as a limit of relativity, not merely that it predicted well within its domain. Carnot's theory of heat engines was framed in terms of caloric, and I take his results to be true, but I hesitate to call the caloric theory itself approximately true. The phlogiston theory of the eighteenth century, by contrast, I want to reject outright — and yet its central quantity can be mapped onto negative oxygen, so that the old scheme tracks the new one almost step for step.

That last case is what defeats every principle I have tried. If the criterion is whether the old formalism reappears as a special or limiting case of the successor, phlogiston seems to pass as cleanly as Fresnel's equations, and I do not accept that. If the criterion is whether the old theory's central theoretical entities survive as entities in the successor, then the ether fails and Fresnel's optics fails with it, which I equally do not accept. Give me a principle, or a small set of rival principles, that reproduces as many of my verdicts as possible; for each clause, the case that forces it; what it says about a case I have not listed, such as the retention of Ptolemaic epicyclic machinery in Copernicus or the caloric reasoning behind Carnot's cycle; and an honest account of which of my verdicts the best version of it cannot accommodate. Argue from the cases — I do not want a reading list.
