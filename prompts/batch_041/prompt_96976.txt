After thirty-five years in comparative physiology I retired, and I have spent the past two years teaching myself early modern philosophy from the ground up. The position I keep circling back to is Descartes's on animal minds, and not the version that gets repeated — the vivisection caricature — but the actual structure of the argument, which I find far stranger and far better than its reputation. As I read him, he grants animals life and even sensation in a bodily sense: their cries and struggles are genuine events, the movements of a real and intricately built body. What he withholds is thought. He supports the withholding with two considerations that pull against each other. The first is that if you built a machine with the outward shape and the internal organs of a monkey, you would have no way of knowing it was not a monkey; with a beast, the outside is all we ever have. The second is that no machine, however well made, could use words or signs the way we do — flexibly, across novel situations, to declare its thoughts to others. Parrots and magpies can produce the sounds but cannot speak, and he takes the decisive point to be that a person born deaf and mute will invent signs for himself, so the failure cannot be in the organs. He is also fond of the clock: a mechanism of wheels and springs can keep time better than we can judge time, so excellence at a narrow task is no evidence of a mind.

Write me a poem of twenty-four to thirty lines in American English, free verse, no rhyme, spoken in the voice of someone who has watched a single animal closely for a long time and kept notes on it. The poem should turn on the fact that everything in those notes is consistent with two readings — a creature that is answering, and a body that is only continuing — and it should not settle the question.

Here is what I want the form to do. Build the poem as a sequence of paired observations. In each pair, the same behavior is described twice, once under each reading. Each pair of lines must share exactly three words, and the shared words should be the plain descriptive ones; the interpretation should be carried entirely by what differs. By the end of the poem the second reading should be the more natural one to read, even though I do not believe it. The word "because" should appear exactly once, near the middle, and a reader should not be able to tell whether it introduces the animal's reason or the observer's.

Do not name Descartes. Do not use the words soul, machine, automaton, or instinct. Do not end on a verdict, a consolation, or a joke. If you have the position right, someone who knows this material should be able to say which of the two considerations each reading answers to, and why the observer's notebook cannot separate them; if the poem is any good, someone who has never read a page of it should feel the same difficulty without being told what it is.
