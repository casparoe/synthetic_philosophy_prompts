I'm teaching a seminar on punishment and modern literature, and I'm pairing Camus's novel The Stranger with his later abolitionist writing on the death penalty. The pairing is obvious enough—Meursault is condemned to death, and Camus spent years arguing that the guillotine is a uniquely barbaric institution—but the more I sit with it, the less the two pieces seem to line up, and I'd like a reading that takes the mismatch seriously rather than smoothing it over. Camus's case against capital punishment, as I understand it, rests on a few interlocking claims: that execution is irreversible in a way that makes judicial error monstrous; that it is a premeditated, ritualized killing by the state rather than a spontaneous act of passion; that it fails as a deterrent; and that it corrupts the society that authorizes it by turning citizens into spectators and accomplices. He also insists that the horror of the death penalty is not mitigated by the guilt of the condemned—that even a guilty man should not be killed by the state. The Stranger, though, gives us a protagonist who is guilty, a trial that is a mockery of justice, and an ending in which Meursault seems almost to consent to his execution, opening himself to the indifferent universe and hoping for a crowd to greet him with cries of hatred. That is not obviously an abolitionist novel. So I want you to read The Stranger through the lens of Camus's views on the death penalty and tell me: where does the novel dramatize or strengthen the abolitionist case, and where does it complicate, resist, or even undermine it? Pay particular attention to the trial, where the prosecution seems to care more about Meursault's failure to weep at his mother's funeral than about the killing itself, and to the final pages, where Meursault's defiance and reconciliation might be read as a kind of aestheticization of execution. Does the novel show capital punishment as a social ritual that requires the condemned to perform a role—remorse, conversion, recognition of the law—and then kills him anyway? Or does it risk making the execution seem like a natural fact, one more indifferent event in an absurd cosmos, and so drain it of the political horror that Camus's essay wants to attach to it? I'd also like you to say whether Meursault's guilt matters for the argument. Camus opposed the death penalty even for the guilty, but the novel's reader may feel that the injustice is not the execution as such but the fact that Meursault is condemned for his character rather than his crime. Is that a confusion the novel invites, or is it the point—that the death penalty always turns a trial into a judgment of the soul? I don't need a bibliography, and you shouldn't quote the text; I know it well. What I want is a philosophically serious reading that does justice both to the novel and to Camus's abolitionism, and that ends by saying what The Stranger adds to the death penalty debate that the essay alone does not.
