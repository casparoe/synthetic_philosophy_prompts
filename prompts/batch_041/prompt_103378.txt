Having spent forty years in the physical sciences and only recently turned to decision theory, I find myself stuck on the two-envelope paradox. Before I take up any proposed resolutions, I want the specification itself: what must an adequate resolution deliver? Give me the desiderata — the intuitions it has to preserve (the apparent force of the expected-value argument for switching), the symmetry it has to respect (that before opening, the two envelopes are on a par, so the argument cannot license switching from whichever one you happen to hold), the cases it has to get right (a setup in which the distribution is known and switching is genuinely favourable; one in which the amounts are fixed in advance and switching is genuinely indifferent), the distinctions it must not collapse (conditional versus unconditional expectation; an improper or non-normalizable prior versus a genuine probability distribution; expected value versus expected utility), and any consistency condition it must satisfy — in particular that the reasoning cannot be run to demand an endless sequence of swaps, nor to make each envelope simultaneously the better choice. For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates it, mark which are non-negotiable and which are merely optional, and flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied; that last part is what I most want to see. Finally, tell me which single requirement, if enforced, would rule out the greatest number of would-be resolutions.
