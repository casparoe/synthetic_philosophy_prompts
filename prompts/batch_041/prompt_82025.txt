I have been reading rationality and decision theory blogs for the better part of a decade, and I keep running into the Pasadena game as a one-line refutation of expected value maximization. The game, as I understand it, is this: a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time; if that happens on the nth toss, you receive a payoff of \((-1)^{n-1} \cdot 2^n / n\). The expected value is the alternating harmonic series, which converges to a finite number if you sum it in the natural order, but is only conditionally convergent, so by the Riemann rearrangement theorem its terms can be rearranged to yield any real number you like. So the expectation is not infinite; it is undefined. The Altadena game is the same except the payoffs are all positive, which makes the expectation diverge to infinity. And the St. Petersburg game, where the payoff is \(2^n\) with no alternating sign and no division by \(n\), has an expectation that diverges to infinity as well.

I want the specification, not another blog post. Write the desiderata any adequate theory of rational choice must meet to handle decision problems in which expected value is undefined or infinite, with the case behind each requirement. I am especially interested in the requirements that come from the mathematics rather than from intuition: the theory cannot contradict the fact that a conditionally convergent series has no unique sum, and it cannot deliver verdicts that depend on an arbitrary ordering of the terms. But I also want the requirements that come from cases: which choices the theory must get right, which distinctions it must respect (for example, between an expectation that is undefined and one that is infinite; between a game you must play and one you may play; between a single play and a repeated play), and which results from decision theory or probability it must be consistent with.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you think it is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since that is the part I most want to see. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, say which and why. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the most popular theories in this area, and briefly score two or three contenders against the list — for example, plain expected value maximization, expected utility with a bounded utility function, and the response that the Pasadena game is simply ill-posed and no verdict is required.

Say how confident you are in each part of your answer, and single out the point you are least sure of.
