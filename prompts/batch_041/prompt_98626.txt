I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been having a long, circular argument with a colleague about whether the categories our team lives by are real or just things we made up. She keeps saying that if a category is socially constructed then it's arbitrary, and I keep feeling that this can't be right, but I don't have the vocabulary to say why. I'd like you to build me a thought experiment rather than explain the issue in general terms, because I want a case I can actually walk her through. The raw material I have in mind is the kind of category a software team invents and then can't do without — the obvious one being the flaky test, a test that passes when you rerun it, so that membership is decided by the team's own rerun-and-threshold practice rather than by any property nature hands you. Feel free to invent the specifics of the team, the service, and the category yourself, including a category unlike the flaky test if you think a different one isolates the point better; I'd rather the details be invented than borrowed, so that neither of us can reply with a canned answer.

Here are the specifications the case has to meet, and I need them met jointly, not one at a time. First, the category must be socially constructed in the strong sense: its boundary is fixed by the team's practice, and another equally competent team could draw the boundary elsewhere without either team being mistaken. Second, for any particular item in the story, there must nonetheless be a fact of the matter about whether it belongs, a fact that does not depend on what anyone believes or says about it. Third, the category must be causally efficacious — being in it has to change what people do, so that it can't be waved away as a mere label. Fourth, the case must not collapse into either of the two boring readings: it must not be mere convention, where the rule is arbitrary but fully explicit and everyone could just as well have chosen otherwise, and it must not be a natural kind, where nature simply hands you the boundary. After the scenario, which should be no more than a paragraph, walk me through the demonstration: identify the item in the story that sits on the borderline and say why, name the single feature of the case that is doing the work of separating constructed from arbitrary on one side and real from natural on the other, and then hold the underlying population fixed while varying only the team's practice, so I can see exactly what moves and what refuses to move. Finally, tell me what happens if the classifying practice is deleted outright — does the kind vanish, does the population change, or does something else take over — and what a debunker who concludes that the category is therefore not real but merely made up has gotten wrong. The purpose of all this is argumentative, so the case has to generate its verdicts from the setup rather than stipulate them; if the scenario only works because you've decreed who belongs to what, it's useless to me. I'd also like one closing paragraph on how far the analogy to categories like race, gender, or money extends and where it breaks down, but please keep that brief and don't let it turn into a survey of those debates.
