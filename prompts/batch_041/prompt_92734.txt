ok so I want a parody piece, and I want it to be a specific genre rather than a general joke. Set it up as the FAQ / investor prospectus page for a company that sells civilization-collapse risk scores to insurers, sovereign wealth funds and maybe a reinsurance firm — basically a credit rating agency but for whole societies. Deadpan corporate voice all the way through, no winking at the reader, no fourth wall. Around 600-700 words, formatted as Q&A entries, and if you can work in a little "risk factors" box at the bottom like an SEC filing, even better.

The comedy has to come from the actual method, not from generic "predictions are hard" material. So the product should be recognizably the kind of thing where you fold wage stagnation, elite overproduction (too many credentialed people competing for too few elite positions), and state fiscal distress into one composite number — call it a political stress index — and then read collapse risk off that. They should talk about secular cycles as if the period is just a parameter you tune in the backtest. They should mention a giant database where past societies get coded into variables by research assistants. They should have a "solidarity" term they borrowed from Ibn Khaldun (asabiya) which they cheerfully admit is "hard to operationalize" and include anyway. And a question near the top, "isn't this just cliodynamics with a sales department," which they answer with something defensive about being the ones who actually ship.

Let the real objections surface without anyone ever stating them. What exactly counts as an elite, and how do you count them the same way across three thousand years. How "collapse" gets defined — post hoc, so the model always looks prescient. That the index is calibrated on events everyone already knows about and can't be tested out of sample because each civilization only happens once. That the cases aren't independent of each other. That the score is a probability of something that hasn't happened yet, so it can be revised forever and never be wrong. That if enough clients act on the forecast, the forecast changes — they should have a whole chipper little question about this and completely fail to notice the problem. And the awkward fact that the 2010s backtest "predicted" unrest that had already happened by the time they ran it.

A specialist in quantitative macrohistory should both laugh and wince; someone who's never heard of any of this should still be able to follow it. Don't name real people or real papers. Couple of typos are fine, I'm not submitting this anywhere.
