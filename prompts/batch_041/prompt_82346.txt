For fifteen years or so I have read rationalist blogs and forums, and no slogan I have encountered there does more work with less argument than "rational agents cannot agree to disagree." The episode that finally made me want this spelled out: a long thread about a contested empirical question ended when one participant announced that, since we had both seen the same evidence and still differed, one of us must be failing at rationality — the theorem, he said, guarantees that two rational agents cannot genuinely disagree about a fact, so the disagreement itself was evidence of a defect in one of us. I think there is a real result somewhere in the vicinity, and I don't want to throw it out, but I don't believe it says that.

Give me a menu rather than a single verdict: the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be making, arranged from the nearly vacuous to the very strong. For each, state what it requires and what it rules out. The dimensions I care about are these. What counts as agreement — identical credences about a proposition, each merely knowing the other's credence, or convergence over time rather than equality now? What must be true of the agents — a common prior over the relevant space, common knowledge of one another's rationality, common knowledge of one another's evidence or information partitions, perfect Bayesian updating, full and honest disclosure of credences? Whether the disagreement itself has to be common knowledge, and how much work that single condition is doing; I suspect it is doing almost all of it. Whether the claim concerns what is possible given the assumptions, what rational agents will in fact do, or what it is rational for them to do. And whether any version survives when the disagreement is about values, about what to do, or about what the evidence is, rather than about the probability of a fixed event.

Then mark each reading: platitude, substantive and defensible, true but irrelevant to actual arguing, or false. Say which reading, if any, actually licenses my thread-mate's move from "we still disagree" to "one of us is irrational" — and if none does, what the honest lesson of the result is for arguers who are not ideal Bayesians sharing a common prior, since that is the case I actually inhabit. Finish by saying which reading you think people who repeat the slogan usually have in mind. No citations or reading list; I want the logic, not the literature.

Hard limit: 700 words.
