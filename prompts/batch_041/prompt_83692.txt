I'm a novelist about two thirds of the way through a book whose protagonist is a philosopher, and I've gotten myself tangled in something I can't untangle alone. He's a sort of fictionalist about lots of things, and in one chapter he lays out an argument he claims shows that nothing is ever really one thing. I built it out of stuff I half-remember from a seminar on early analytic philosophy, so it's probably mangled, but here it is, cleaned up: (1) Whenever some things make up a single thing — a proposition, a fact, a state of affairs, an object with parts — there has to be something that does the making-up, i.e. a relation, tie, link, whatever you want to call it, that connects the constituents. (2) But a relation that connects things had better itself be connected to the things it connects. If it just sat there next to them, it wouldn't be doing any connecting — you'd have the constituents plus a relation, which is still a list, not a whole. (3) So there must be a further relation tying the first relation to its relata, and then a further relation tying that one to what it ties, and so on. (4) Nothing ever gets finished, so there's no level at which the constituents actually get unified. (5) But a proposition that isn't unified isn't a proposition at all, it's just a heap of constituents, and heaps aren't true or false. (6) So there are no propositions — and the same reasoning runs for facts, states of affairs, complexes, wholes, everything dissolves into a heap of simples. (7) But obviously there are propositions and facts and wholes, so one of the steps above is wrong.

He's thrilled about this, because it's the climax of the book and it makes him insufferable at parties, but I think the conclusion is absurd and I need to know exactly which step is the bad one — and I'd rather not sacrifice (7). What bugs me is that (2) seems so innocent; I can't see how you keep it and still stop the regress, so maybe the flaw is somewhere I'm not looking. I also want to know whether the same argument, if it works, kills sets and numbers and properties too, or whether there's something about those that blocks it, and whether the regress is actually vicious or whether an endless chain of ties is perfectly fine and the argument only seems to bite because infinite chains feel like cheating. My protagonist keeps insisting the only honest response is to admit that unity is a brute fact with no explanation — is that a real position or just a dignified name for giving up?

Take this on as someone who genuinely works on relations and the unity of the proposition — think it through and lay out the live options before you commit to one — then tell me which step to give up and what each alternative costs. Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who know the literature and which are still genuinely contested, and don't hand me one school's preferred resolution as if it were the consensus. I've heard the names Bradley and Russell and Frege thrown around and I honestly don't know whats owed to whom, so if the diagnosis turns on who said what, spell it out rather than assuming I know.
