After forty years in a chemistry laboratory I have retired into philosophy, and I have joined a small reading group that meets monthly. Last week we read al-Ghazālī on causation, and two of the more philosophically experienced members fell into an argument that I could not settle.

Al-Ghazālī, as the group understood him, denies that any created thing is genuinely a cause: what we call fire burning cotton is God's habit of producing the burning whenever cotton meets fire, and all we ever observe is one event following another, never a bond of necessity. Ruth took this to be the sober account of our situation rather than a sceptical extravagance. The necessity, she said, was always our own contribution; and the position has a real payoff, since a departure from the customary order — cotton that does not burn — would be no violation of any law, only an interruption of a custom, so nothing a laboratory could discover could ever exclude it in advance. Owen answered that the argument proves too much. The very same reasoning that shows we never observe fire producing burning shows that we never observe God's will producing anything either, and al-Ghazālī simply exempts the one unobserved cause he favours. He added that if God's habit is the only tie between events, the habit explains nothing we did not already know, and leaves induction without a ground, since a free will could pair things differently tomorrow; a necessity in things, even one we never observe, would at least give us reason to expect regularity.

Ruth replied that an unobserved necessity gives no better ground for induction than an observed regularity does — the necessitarian is in exactly the same position, and nobody has ever derived a guarantee that the future will resemble the past from a bond between two events. She also suggested that the symmetry objection lands only against a reading she rejects: perhaps al-Ghazālī denies merely that we perceive necessity, and not that created things have any efficacy whatever, in which case there is no special exemption to complain about. Owen said the milder reading cannot survive the rest of the view, and that if created things really had powers, al-Ghazālī's insistence on the world's total, moment-by-moment dependence on God would collapse.

I sat between them with nothing to contribute. Please go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. Tell me who is right about which specific point, whether any of these arguments is stronger or weaker than it looks, and what both of them are missing. I would also like to know what the answer implies for someone who spent his working life saying that one thing caused another. Please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text or tables; a letter is the right model.
