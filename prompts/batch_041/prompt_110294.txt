I'm writing something about the wrong kind of reasons problem -- the puzzle that a threat ("admire me or I'll burn your house down") gives you a reason to admire someone, but not the kind of reason that counts in favor of admiring them the way their actually being admirable would. I've got the four usual moves on my list and I want them tierd, best to worst:

1. "reason to admire" is ambiguous between a reason that bears on whether admiring is fitting and a reason that bears on whether it's good for you to admire.
2. the threat is really a reason to want to admire, or to try to, never a reason to admire.
3. right-kind reasons just are the ones that bear on fittingness, so the puzzle is definitional and there's nothing to solve.
4. there's no such thing as a wrong kind of reason -- any reason to have an attitude is a reason for it, the demon included.

But rank them by how they handle a twist I keep running into: the threat is aimed at making the attitude self-fulfilling. Someone offers you a fortune to admire them, and you're admiration is itself what makes them admirable -- it spurs them on, they become worth admiring. Now the payoff looks like a right-kind reason, because taking up the attitude is part of what makes it fit. Tier the four by how well they cope with that, one sentence each, then name the one you'd keep. Say if you think the twist is rigged. 300 words max.
