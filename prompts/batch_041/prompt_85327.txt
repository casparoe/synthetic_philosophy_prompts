Next month I'm on a panel about whether the fall of the Roman Republic is a useful analogy for what's happening to modern democracies, and I want to walk in ready for the Q&A. My line is this: the Republic didn't die of "democratic decay" and it didn't die of Caesar. It died of a design flaw I've been calling the *deadlock deficit* — the constitution had no cheap, peaceful way to break a deadlock between a tribune's veto and the senate's grip on money and command. When the only way to settle a fight is the last resort, the whole system runs on an equilibrium in which nobody ever uses the last resort; once one side does, every later escalation is cheaper than the one before. So the Gracchi, Sulla, the proscriptions, the street gangs, the consuls marching on Rome — that's a ratchet, not a rot. And the lesson for modern analogies is that we're mostly doing tropes, not structure: we invoke Rome to signal seriousness and then quietly borrow its fatalism. The useful comparison, I'll argue, is about missing deadlock-breakers, not about virtue or decline.

The audience is the problem, because it's mixed. Three late-Republic historians, who will hate that I'm treating the sources as transparent — Appian, Plutarch, Dio, and Cicero's speeches are all late and all have axes to grind; they'll also say "democracy" is just the wrong word for a timocratic republic run by a noble-dominated senate. A political theorist who works on emergency powers and will say the Roman dictatorship *was* supposed to be the deadlock-breaker, temporary and constrained, and it worked for centuries — so my "deficit" is refuted by the very thing it's supposed to explain. Two comparative politics people who'll want the outcome variable defined, and who'll either say Rome isn't a case of democratic backsliding at all or that a single case proves nothing. An economic historian who thinks the fiscal-military story — public land, the Italian allies' revolt over citizenship, the grain supply, armies that became clients of their own generals — is doing all the work and I've buried it. And a journalist who wants one sentence and will ask me on mic whether I'm saying the US is Rome.

What I need is my Q&A prep sheet. Sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

**The pitch, three sentences** (~60 words) — what I say if the moderator gives me two minutes.

**The room** (~100 words) — who's there, what each of them actually cares about, who's quietly on my side.

**The objections, worst first** (~900 words) — eight of them, ordered by how much damage they do, each headed by who raises it and phrased the way that person would phrase it (a historian's objection should sound like source criticism, the theorist's like a conceptual point about emergency powers, the economist's like a complaint about mechanism), then a 90-second reply in my voice, then one line on where the reply is soft. Don't pad the list with things nobody in this room would actually say.

**Concede up front** (~100 words) — the two or three things I should give away before anyone asks, since conceding them late looks like I got caught.

**Hold** (~100 words) — the claims I should refuse to soften, and how to refuse without sounding evasive.

**The reply that has to land** (~80 words) — the single exchange that decides whether the panel goes my way, and the exact shape of the answer.

**The question I'm hoping nobody asks** (~120 words) — plus my best improvised answer, flagged as improvised.

**One sentence for the journalist** (~30 words) — not a dodge, just short enough to be quoted whole.
