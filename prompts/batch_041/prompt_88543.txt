A friend and I argued until two in the morning about a way of deciding that she calls rule-worship and that I can neither defend nor abandon. When a decision matters, I settle it by asking which rule I would have wanted to have adopted before I knew which of several situations I would turn out to be in; then, once I know which one I am in, I act on the rule anyway, even where the reasons plainly favour something else. Nothing binds me: I keep the information, and I could change my mind at any moment. I refuse to pay off a blackmailer when paying would cost me less; I keep a promise made by a much younger self who had none of my present information; and in one-off dealings I behave as though my conduct were the sample from which strangers forecast how people like me will behave. She says this is precommitment with the commitment left out, and so either confused or self-serving. I suspect it belongs with precommitment, and with the manoeuvre of choosing as if I did not know who I would be, but I want the carving rather than the label. Does the thing I am doing have an established name? What makes it rational to follow a rule I know to be worse for the case I am actually in? And where does it come apart from precommitment, from the veil-of-ignorance manoeuvre, and from plain stubbornness? If nothing established fits, coin a name and mark its edges. Please answer in British English.
