I am trying to pin down how much work Aumann's agreement theorem actually does, and I want your credence in a specific claim rather than a survey of the debate. Here is the claim, stated so that it has a truth value:

(P) The theorem can never bear on whether an actual disagreement is rational, because it is caught between two cases. If its conditions fail — no common prior, no shared space of states, no common knowledge of one another's posterior credences — then it is silent about the disagreement before it. If its conditions are met, then its conclusion (the posteriors coincide) holds, and there is no disagreement left for it to explain. Either way it describes only situations in which the phenomenon of interest is absent, and it constrains nothing real.

Give me your credence in (P) as a number, then the decomposition: the sub-questions the number rests on, a rough probability for each, how you combine them, and which term the final number is most sensitive to.

I want the decomposition to take up at least the following, and to add whatever else you think belongs.

1. The common prior. Is a shared prior over a shared space a defensible idealization for two agents with different histories, or does it assume away the disagreement it is meant to illuminate? Note that the agents in the cases that matter to me are not strangers: they are two people with the same training, reading the same evidence, who have argued with each other for years.

2. Common knowledge. The condition is of infinite order: each knows the other's credence, each knows that the other knows, and so on. Is this ever approximated between real agents, or is it a limit that only ideal agents reach? If it is never approximated, say whether you think that makes the theorem vacuous or merely normative.

3. The shared space, which is the part I care about most. Take this case and treat it as the test. Two Bayesians share a prior over every proposition they can both state and agree on every such proposition; their credences are common knowledge and identical, so nothing in the theorem is violated. But one of them distinguishes two possible origins for an outbreak and assigns 0.8 to one of them, while the other does not merely lack the information: she regards the distinction as ill-formed and has no credence to offer, only a refusal to entertain the question. She will not be moved by evidence about a proposition she does not recognize. I want your probability that disagreements of this kind — disagreements about which distinctions exist, rather than about how probable something is — are the ordinary case in real disputes; and I want your verdict on whether the theorem has anything to say about them, or whether calling them disagreements is already a mistake.

4. The idealization defence. Is (P)'s dilemma a false dichotomy? One might hold that the theorem is a normative constraint on ideally rational agents, in the way a frictionless-plane result is normative for mechanics, and that our failure to satisfy its conditions is a fact about us rather than a defect in the theorem. If you take that line, say what the theorem is supposed to tell a real disputant who cannot know which condition she and her interlocutor have failed.

Then, in a few lines: which single sub-question carries most of your number; what argument or piece of evidence would move it most; and what would have to be true for the theorem to do genuine explanatory work about a disagreement you have yourself witnessed or read about.

For calibration, I put (P) at about 0.7. A colleague who works on epistemic logic puts it near 0.25, on the ground that the theorem is normative and that our inability to attain common knowledge is our problem rather than the theorem's. Tell me where you land, and which of the two of us, if either, is making the more interesting mistake.
