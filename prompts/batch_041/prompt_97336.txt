My two flatmates got into it last night over a rule in our halls: you can eat as much as you want in the dining hall, but nothing leaves — not even an apple. Nobody knows why the rule is there. It's been around forever.

Jamal wants it gone. He says it's petty and arbitrary and "the rule exists" isn't a reason for anything. Priya said that's the Chesterton's fence mistake — you don't pull up a fence until you know why someone put it there, and neither of us knows. Jamal said fine, then let's find out, and if the reason turns out to be dumb we tear it down. Priya said the problem is that some rules like this exist *precisely* because the harm they prevent only shows up rarely, or only over years, so "we looked and couldn't find a reason" isn't the same as "there is no reason," and an invisible function is exactly what you'd expect when the function is subtle. Jamal said that proves too much — by that logic no inherited rule can ever go, because its defenders can always say the function is invisible. Priya said no, it just puts the burden on whoever's removing it, and Jamal's the one flipping the burden.

Then Jamal said something that actually made Priya pause. He said the *shape* of the rule is evidence: a rule against carrying food out is a strange way to stop, say, waste or theft, so whatever it was for was probably something else, and probably something that doesn't apply to us now. Priya said he's just guessing at the fence's purpose from its position. Jamal came back with: even if I find out why it was built, that doesn't tell me whether it's doing anything useful *now* — a fence can outlive its purpose or quietly start doing a different job. Priya said then you've just moved the question up a level, because "what is it doing now" has the exact same epistemic problem, and at least the original reason might be recoverable from records while the current function might not be. Jamal said that's just Burke with extra steps.

They've asked me to referee and I honestly can't tell who's winning. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right about which specific point — not just who wins overall? I especially want to know whether "we couldn't find the reason" can ever justify scrapping a rule, and whether the shape of a rule can actually tell you anything about its function. And tell me what both of them are missing.
