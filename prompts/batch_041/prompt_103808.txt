I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, and I keep circling a puzzle about what our everyday taxonomic practice commits us to. What I want from you is not a survey of species concepts, but a reconstruction of the implicit metaphysics of a cluster of nomenclatural conventions — what view of kinds they only make sense under, which rival views they quietly rule out, and how much of the package is strictly presupposed as opposed to merely well-suited to the practice.

Here is the package. The codes governing the naming of animals, plants, fungi, and bacteria require that every scientific name at the species level be permanently attached to a type — ordinarily a single preserved specimen, though an illustration, a culture, or, under the bacteriological code, a published description may stand in its place. The codes describe the type as name-bearing only: it need not be a typical member of the species, and it may be a juvenile, an aberrant individual, or a deformity. The codes also insist that they govern names and not taxa, leaving the delimitation of species to the taxonomist's judgment. When a species is subsequently divided, the original name stays with whichever lineage contains the type, even if that lineage is the smaller, less familiar, or less representative one, and the other lineage must take a new name. Priority of publication decides which of two competing names is correct, but a commission can vote to set priority aside in order to conserve a familiar name for the thing long called by it — as when it ruled that the familiar name for the wildcat be kept even though an older name based on the domestic cat has priority. And in current work, names are increasingly applied by matching short DNA sequences against reference libraries, while occasional new species are proposed from photographs or sequences alone, with no voucher specimen preserved anywhere.

My question is what conception of a kind this cluster presupposes. Does anchoring a name to one individual presuppose that species have an essence which that specimen fixes, or does it presuppose that the specimen is a mere handle whose tie to the rest of the species is conventional? I am drawn to the second reading, but then I cannot see why the identity of the type should have such sweeping classificatory consequences when a taxon is split, or why a committee's vote should be able to override them. Can the codes' disclaimer — types are not definitions, names are not taxa — be squared with the work the type actually does? Which accounts of species (sets of organisms united by characters, historical individuals or lineage segments, purely pragmatic groupings for convenient reference) does the practice rule out, and which does it merely fail to require? Where is it underdetermined, in the sense that several incompatible views would license exactly these conventions? And if we took seriously the idea that a species is a particular historical entity with no essence, and rewrote the code so that names were fixed by ancestry rather than by a specimen, what would have to be abandoned, and what would survive because it was never doing metaphysics in the first place?

Please be explicit about the line between what the practice strictly requires and what it has merely come to look as though it requires. My suspicion is that the type convention is more than nominalism but considerably less than essentialism, and I would like to know whether that middle position can actually be stated.
