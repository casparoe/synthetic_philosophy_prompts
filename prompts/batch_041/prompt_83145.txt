Ive somehow ended up as the person who owns the taxonomy and data side of a fairly big biodiversity and invasive-species project (my background is ecology, I never took a philosophy course, so bear with me), and I keep slamming into the same wall. Half our fights are about whether a category is real and the other half are about whether it's useful, and nobody seems to notice those are two different questions. The cases we argue about: clusters of gene sequences lumped together at an arbitrary similarity cutoff that everyone admits is arbitrary and everyone uses anyway; 'invasive', which seems to be doing normative work and changes depending on where you draw the map; 'species' in bacteria, where the concept really does creak; and then the boring load-bearing ones like ecosystem types and soil classes, which we can't do without and can't quite justify.

My hunch, and I'd rather you test it than assume it: the practical fights are never really realism versus convention, they're about which of three jobs a category is being asked to do -- support predictions, support explanations and interventions, or just file things so a regulator is happy. If that's roughly right, it should change which questions are worth asking.

I've got one day a week for the next year, plus a small budget to put three people in a room for a workshop, and I want to spend it on questions that are actually worth the time rather than the famous ones. So: what are the live open questions about natural kinds right now, and which should I prioritize? I want the reasoning more than the list -- which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for someone with my background and one day a week, which ones sound deep but wouldn't change a single decision in our database, and what would move a question up or down your ranking. Dont send me a reading list, I'll find the papers once I know what I'm looking for.
