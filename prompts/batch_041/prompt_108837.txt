I've been reading about Jain disputation and a modern adaptation of it in some conflict-resolution workshops, and I want to understand what the practice is implicitly committed to. In the traditional seven-fold scheme, a claim is asserted only with a qualifier meaning in some respect, and one may affirm a thing's existence, deny it, affirm both, call the matter inexpressible, and combine these; the workshop version adds a procedural rule that you must first restate your opponent's position in terms they accept, and then present your own view as one standpoint among others rather than as the final word. What theory of truth and predication does this practice presuppose? Does it require the metaphysical thesis that reality has infinitely many aspects, or only the epistemic thesis that our concepts are partial? Does it commit to a non-classical logic, or can it be reconstructed within classical logic by relativizing predicates to standpoints? Is the practice consistent with the same tradition's commitment to non-harm in speech, or does the requirement to affirm both a claim and its negation in different respects make it impossible to call anything simply wrong? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by a straightforwardly two-valued semantics.
