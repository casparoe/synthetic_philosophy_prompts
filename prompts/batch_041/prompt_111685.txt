I have a colleague in my department, a specialist in South Asian philosophy, who has argued for years that karma is a genuine theory of moral desert: when someone suffers, the suffering is not merely caused by their past, it is owed to them. She and I are to discuss a new case at a workshop next month, and I would like to predict her verdict before we do.

Her verdicts on earlier cases, as I have heard her give them:

- A man who deliberately ruins his business partner, and who in a later life is ruined in turn, gets exactly what he is owed.
- A person who intends to kill but is stopped before anyone is harmed has incurred the entire karmic debt; the deed is complete in the intention.
- A careful driver who nonetheless kills a child incurs nothing.
- A woman born with a painful lifelong illness, whose previous life was spent tormenting animals, is receiving her due.
- Someone who has done nothing wrong and whose life goes well is not being rewarded; karma is a matter of debts, not of prizes.

The new case is a dog that is beaten and starved by its owner. My colleague accepts that the dog's previous life was that of a man who beat and starved animals, and she also holds that the owner is incurring fresh debt by what he does. Predict her verdict on whether the dog's suffering is deserved. The verdict itself matters less to me than the rule she is running, the other rules that fit her record just as well, what each of those predicts here, and which further case would tell them apart; if the record does not force a choice, tell me what she would be committing herself to on either side.
