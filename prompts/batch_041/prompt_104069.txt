My SF book club is doing Asimov's Foundation next month and I want to run it against the historiography of Rome's fall instead of the usual psychohistory-is-cool chat. The Empire is transparently a Rome pastiche, and Asimov builds in a real theory of collapse: decline is statistical and lawlike, individual actors are just noise, and the Foundation's whole job is to sit tight and shorten the interregnum. So read it through the argument about whether Rome fell at all -- Gibbon's moralized decline and fall, the barbarian and fiscal and climate and lead-pipe stories, and the revisionist line that nothing actually fell, there was just a transformation into something else. Which of those does the novel really dramatize, and which does it just inherit because it's a pastiche? Where does it cheat?

Two things I especially want you to take seriously. First, the Mule: the premise is that one man can't matter, and then one man matters enormously. Does the book refute its own theory of history, or does the Second Foundation rescue the theory in a way that's philosophically a cop-out? Second, the moral texture. Foundation leans hard on decay versus frontier vigor, soft capital versus hard work on Terminus, and that's the civic-virtue story that most people working on late antiquity now treat as a projection onto the evidence. Does the book's picture of why empires go commit it to a moral theory of history whether it wants one or not?

And then be honest about the analogy's limits -- Rome had rivals, a surviving eastern half, and no Seldon; Trantor's end is a single catastrophe, not a slow transformation into successor kingdoms. What, if anything, does the novel show about collapse that the historians' debate can't get at? Point me to specific beats I can bring to the group (Hardin's religion gambit, the sack of Trantor, the Mule's takeover), not just themes.
