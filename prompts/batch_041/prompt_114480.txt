I've been having a long-running argument with a colleague about Bayesian vs frequentist statistics and it has completly stalled, and I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is rather than a verdict on who's right. The shape of it: he says the replication crisis is basically a p-value problem — NHST plus optional stopping plus forking paths generates findings that don't replicate, so fields should move to Bayesian methods. I say fine, but Bayesian analyses have their own degrees of freedom (priors, and the exact same garden of forking paths), and a p of .05 usually only buys you a likelihood ratio of about 3, which does nothing for you if the prior odds were low to begin with, so switching frameworks doesn't fix anything, it just relocates where the judgement call lives. We agree on the math, we agree preregistration helps, we agree lots of published findings had low prior odds of being true — and yet we cannot agree on what a field should default to, and neither of us has moved in a year.

My hypothesis is that the camp labels are misleading: the real fault line is between treating inference as decision theory (losses, error rates, what to do) and treating it as a logic of evidence (what the data support), and both camps contain both kinds of people — frequentist testing is nakedly decision-theoretic, and there's a whole Jeffreys-style Bayesian tradition of inference-as-logic — so the names don't line up with the split. Is that right, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath (maybe about which counterfactual repetitions define "warrant", or about whether the frequentist's long-run guarantee is even the kind of thing that can warrant a single conclusion)? Or is the whole thing a hidden empirical question about how scientists actually behave that neither of us can settle from the armchair? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a theorem, an experiment, or nothing?
