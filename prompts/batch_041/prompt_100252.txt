I'm a philosophy PhD student finishing a chapter on the twentieth-century revival of the ontological argument, and I want you to pressure-test a thesis I've come to hold. Gödel's modal version strikes me as changing the argument's status altogether. Unlike Anselm's or Descartes's versions, it is a formal derivation whose conclusion is a theorem of S5, so it does not appear to trade on the intuition that God is the greatest conceivable being — the very intuition that Kant's claim that existence is not a real predicate was meant to puncture. Its axioms look like modest modal principles about positivity and essence that even a sceptic can write down without embarrassment. So it seems the old objection simply does not reach it: granted the validity of the proof, the atheist's only remaining move is to reject an axiom, and if the axioms are individually plausible, then the existence of a necessary being is at least as credible as the weakest among them. Is it right, then, that the formalization is doing the real work, and that Kant's objection is beside the point for the Gödelian argument? I'd like you to separate what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested here, and not to let a contested reading of the proof pass as the consensus.
