I cover philosophy and the mind sciences for a general readership, and I have been unable to shake a rejoinder I heard at a workshop on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. The illusionist holds that experiences have no phenomenal properties — no felt qualities — and that our confidence otherwise is a systematic introspective illusion. A realist in the audience offered this reply, and I cannot tell whether it is decisive or cheap:

(1) For an experience to be phenomenal is for there to be something it is like for the subject to undergo it.
(2) Illusionism denies that any state is phenomenal in this sense: when we take an experience to have felt quality, we are misrepresenting a purely functional state as though it had intrinsic qualitative character.
(3) But an illusion is not nothing. For something to seem to have qualitative character, the subject must be in some state that presents it that way.
(4) A state that presents things that way, considered as it is in itself, has the very qualitative character the illusionist says no state has.
(5) So illusionism must posit the phenomenal properties it set out to deny.
(6) So illusionism is self-undermining.

My worry is that the argument proves too much. Its shape — the state that makes it seem as though F is present must itself have F — would equally demolish the eliminativist about beliefs, whose denial is said to be a belief, and, I suspect, any error theory that explains an apparent property by positing a misrepresentation; yet those charges are usually waved away as cheap. So either this one is cheap too, or illusionism is in a special bind its neighbors are not. Which step should the illusionist give up, and what would giving it up cost? If I could keep only (1) through (3), what would I be buying, and at what price?
