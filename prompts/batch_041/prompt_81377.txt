I'm in a seminar on Victorian literature and philosophy, and we've been reading George Eliot's translation of Feuerbach alongside her fiction. I think I grasp the broad thrust of the projection thesis—that the predicates ascribed to God are human predicates, alienated and then contemplated as though they belonged to another being—but I keep getting stuck on two claims in the preface to the second edition, as Eliot renders it. Feuerbach writes: "I do not generate the object from the thought, but the thought from the object; and I hold that alone to be an object which has an existence beyond one's own brain." A little further on he adds: "I am an idealist only in the region of practical philosophy, that is, I do not regard the limits of the past and present as the limits of humanity, of the future; on the contrary, I firmly believe that many things—yes, many things—which with the short-sighted, pusillanimous practical men of to-day, pass for flights of imagination, for ideas never to be realised, for mere chimeras, will to-morrow, i.e., in the next century,—centuries in individual life are days in the life of humanity,—exist in full reality."

What I cannot fit together is how these claims are meant to cohere. The first sounds like a fairly austere empiricism: thought is answerable to an object that exists outside the thinker's head, and presumably nothing else counts as an object. Yet the second has him calling himself an idealist precisely in the region where he affirms a future that is not yet actual—and he seems to treat that future as something more than a wish. Is the "Idea" here being construed as an object in his own demanding sense, something with an existence beyond one's brain, albeit not yet realized? Or is he quietly reinstating a Hegelian teleology he claims to have repudiated, and if so, does that infect the projection thesis itself? I would like the passage explained on its own terms before any application to Eliot.

The second thing I want to understand is whether Eliot's own religious position is best described as a straightforward inheritance of Feuerbach's inversion—theology dissolved into anthropology, the emotional and ethical content of Christianity retained while its object is relocated to humanity—or whether she alters the structure, in particular by abandoning his confidence that history will eventually realize the Idea. My impression is that her novels are far more chastened about whether human beings will ever collectively embody their own projected ideals: that the gap between aspiration and attainment is permanent, and that the ethical life consists in laboring without assurance of completion. But I may be reading a modern skepticism back into her, and I'd rather be corrected than flattered.

Please give me an account of the passage and of the Eliot question, and be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail: which alternative reading of Feuerbach's two claims, or which consideration about Eliot's essays, letters, or fiction, would move you to a different verdict on whether she is a Feuerbachian in any strict sense or something else again.
