I write a monthly column on philosophy of science for a general but intellectually serious readership, and I'm at work on a piece about the long-running dispute over the nature of laws of nature. On one side stands the Humean view, according to which a law is nothing over and above the most economical and powerful summary of the patterns in what actually happens — the best-system tradition, whose most influential modern statement is David Lewis's. On the other side stand the anti-Humean views, according to which laws are something more than summaries: primitive modal facts, or the dispositions and powers of the things themselves, supposed to govern or produce the regularities rather than merely record them.

I don't want the stock tour of analogies. I'd like the comparison to earn its keep by being tested against three cases I have devised myself, and I'd like the result laid out as a table I can work from while I draft. The cases are these:

1. The One-Particle Cosmos: a universe containing exactly one particle, which travels in a straight line at constant velocity forever. Nothing else ever happens in it.
2. The Single-Shot Cosmos: a universe containing exactly two particles, which approach one another, interact exactly once, and then separate forever without ever interacting again, so that whatever principle describes the interaction is instantiated exactly once.
3. The Vacuous Cosmos: a universe whose history is rich in regularities, but in which the conditions that would trigger one candidate law — say, a principle governing the decay of a certain kind of particle — never arise, so that the law is never instantiated.

Please produce a table with exactly these six columns:

- Case: the name of the cosmos.
- Humean verdict: does this world contain any law at all, and what would the best systematization of it look like? Two or three sentences.
- Anti-Humean verdict: the same two questions answered for the governing-laws view.
- Genuine disagreement or verbal dispute?: whether the two camps actually conflict about this case, or are describing the same facts in different vocabularies, with a one-sentence justification.
- Bearing on the governing metaphor: what the case shows about whether laws can be said to govern or produce anything.
- Confidence and defeater: high, medium, or low confidence in the row, plus the single consideration that would most change your mind.

Then, in a closing paragraph of no more than 150 words, say which of the two camps handles the three cases more convincingly overall, and what that verdict implies for how scientists and journalists should talk about laws.
