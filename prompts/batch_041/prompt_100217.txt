I am not a game theorist, but I have been working through some strategic puzzles on my own, and I keep running into a pattern I cannot name. In a game with several choices, I can sometimes rule out one of my options because, although it is not worse than any single other option in every circumstance, a lottery between two or more of my other options would have done better than it no matter what the other players did. I then treat that option as eliminated and look at the smaller game. In the smaller game, another option may become removable in the same way—again, not because some single remaining option beats it everywhere, but because a mixture of remaining options beats it everywhere. I understand the ordinary idea of an option being simply worse than another no matter what; this seems different, because the thing doing the beating is a probability mixture, and because the process can, in principle, keep going until nothing is left, even though I still have to choose something. Is there an established name for this kind of elimination, or for the specific oddity that it can empty the set of options? If there is no name, I would like one that marks the difference from the ordinary case where one option is simply worse than another and from simply choosing the best available option. More importantly, I want to understand what exactly is going on: is the conclusion that the game has no rational choice, or that the method of elimination has broken down, or something else? I would also like to know how this relates to the usual advice to keep eliminating options that are never the best thing to do, and what the boundary cases are—when does the process stop, and when can it consume everything?
