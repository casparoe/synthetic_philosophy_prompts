hey — I'm writing a short piece for a reading group on the ethics of pest control, and I want someone to go at the draft below hard. It's the gist, not the final prose.

The standard way of carving up pesticide ethics is a split between anthropocentric arguments (yields, farmworker exposure, residues, resistance) and non-anthropocentric ones (insect mortality, birds, soil fauna, whole ecosystems). My claim is that this split hides a third question that neither side can answer on its own: "pest" isn't a biological category, it's an economic one. Nothing in the biology of a locust makes it a pest. What makes it a pest is that its feeding crosses a threshold of loss that somebody has decided is intolerable, and that threshold is fixed by prices, crop values, property rights and how much risk a grower can absorb. So the same organism can be a pest in one field and a protected species in the field next door; a bee is a valued pollinator in an orchard and an unwelcome guest at a picnic. Even the invasive-species case, which looks like it has nothing to do with money, turns out to depend on a baseline somebody chose — which prior state of the ecosystem counts as the one we're defending.

Worse, the threshold isn't stable even relative to biology. It's normally calculated against the cost of the control measure itself, so when the spray gets cheaper the threshold drops, more killing counts as warranted, and nobody has re-opened the ethical question. The ethics tracks the price of the insecticide.

The corollary I want to draw is that the ethical decision gets made before the ethics is discussed. Whoever sets the threshold — the extension service, the agronomist, the market — has already decided how much death is acceptable, and everyone downstream argues about methods. I've been calling this "threshold laundering": a moral question (how much harm to these creatures is too much?) gets converted into a technical one (what's the action threshold for this field?), and it never gets converted back.

So the thesis is roughly: the interesting question in pest-control ethics isn't whether insects matter, it's who sets the loss threshold, on what authority, and who eats the loss when it's set wrong — and that's a question about markets and property as much as about moral standing.

What I want from you: comments on accuracy, and on whether this third question is really a third question or just a relabeling of the old anthropocentric/ecocentric split. Then tell me the single objection you'd press hardest. Things I'm already worried about:

- am I overstating it? Disease vectors, termites, total crop loss — dont these make pesthood independent of price?
- does the laundering point just collapse into the standard fact/value gap, or into the old technology-is-politics line? If so, what's left that's mine?
- if pest status is price-dependent, why doesn't the same argument swallow every harm category and become trivial?
- what does a biocentrist say back — someone who thinks the threshold is beside the point because the killing is what matters?
- seperate question: is my framing secretly just the old idea that a weed is a plant in the wrong place, with economics bolted on?

And if the strong version doesn't survive, give me the strongest version I can still defend. No reading lists please, I want the argument itself picked apart.
