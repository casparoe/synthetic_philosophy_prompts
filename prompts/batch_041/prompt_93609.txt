My closest friend and I spent most of a long evening arguing about al-Ghazālī, and we reached an impasse that I would like help breaking. He has read a fair amount of Islamic philosophy in translation and in popular accounts, and he put his position roughly as follows.

According to him, the Incoherence of the Philosophers attacks the Aristotelian doctrine that causes bring about their effects by necessity. Ghazālī's central argument, he says, is that we never perceive any necessary connection between a cause and its effect: we observe that fire is followed by burning, never that fire must burn the cotton. The connection is therefore nothing but God's habit of arranging events in a regular sequence, and since God is free to depart from his habit, miracles are possible. Because every event is produced directly by God and no created thing has any power of its own, my friend says, Ghazālī holds that a miracle is in principle no more surprising than the ordinary course of nature, and that we have no grounds for expecting the sun to rise tomorrow beyond the fact that it always has. He calls this essentially Hume's point about causation, which is why, he says, Ghazālī is sometimes nicknamed the Islamic Hume. He adds that the book made occasionalism the official theology of Sunni Islam and is the main reason philosophy and the sciences declined in the Islamic world, and he notes that the philosophers Ghazālī attacked — Ibn Sīnā and al-Fārābī — were answered later by Averroes. His conclusion is that Ghazālī is the great anti-rationalist of the tradition, and that any religious defense of science has to begin by refuting him.

I suspect this is too tidy, but I could not say precisely where it goes wrong, and I know too little about the text to press him. What I would like is an audit of his summary, claim by claim: which parts a specialist in Islamic philosophy would accept as stated, which are common simplifications that need qualification, and which are outright mistakes. Where the summary misrepresents Ghazālī, tell me what the text actually commits him to and what a sympathetic reader of him would object to; in particular, I am unsure whether the argument in the Incoherence is presented as Ghazālī's own settled metaphysical position or as something else. I would also like to know how far the comparison with Hume holds and where exactly it breaks down. Finally, tell me whether my friend's conclusion — that Ghazālī is the tradition's great anti-rationalist and that a religious defense of science must first refute him — survives once the errors in his summary are corrected, or whether it depends on the very parts that need correcting.

Please write in American English, and organize the answer around the sentences of the summary rather than as a general essay on Ghazālī.
