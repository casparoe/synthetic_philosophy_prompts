Bit of an odd request, this one. I've somehow ended up running a creative-writing option for physics postgrads — long story — and the brief I've been handed is to make one of the genuinely unsettling bits of cosmology read like fiction rather than a bullet point. I've picked the Boltzmann brain thing, but not the version everyone's already seen (you wake up in a void, is anything real, cue the twist). What I'm after is the version people actually argue about: not 'am I a brain in a vat', but the self-undermining move — if your cosmological model, together with the way you count observers, says that brief fluctuations popping into existence with a head full of false memories vastly outnumber orderly observers like us, then the model has handed you a reason to distrust the very reasoning you used to arrive at it. And then the counter-punch, which I find at least as interesting: that move looks question-begging, because it treats our own orderliness as evidence at the exact point where the counting rule is what's at stake, and if the rule really does make disordered observers typical then the probabilistic inference to that conclusion is in the same boat as everything else. There's also the awkward bit that the answer depends entirely on which measure or cutoff you use, and nobody can justify that choice independently — so the whole thing hangs on a number, which is definately the part I want to land.

So could you write me a short story, 1,500–2,500 words or thereabouts, that dramatises that rather than name-checking it? Setting I had in mind (take it or leave it): a mostly automated observatory somewhere high and dry, one person on site for a fortnight, and her actual job is babysitting a Monte Carlo that spits out, for each model she feeds it, the fraction of observers it predicts are the cognizable sort — ones with a long orderly history, who could have evolved, kept records, done a sum — as opposed to the flicker-into-being sort. Say there's a standing departmental rule, of the kind institutions do invent, that nothing gets written up if it's fluctuation fraction comes out above a threshold, on the grounds that a model which makes us atypical isn't a model we can rationally hold. The story should turn on her own number coming back above that line, and on why she can't simply shrug it off: rejecting the result means trusting the very calculation that produced it, and trusting the calculation means accepting a verdict she can't live with.

I'd like a properly argued voice on the other side too — maybe a retired colleague on a bad video link who reckons the whole instability argument begs the question, and who should get to make that case at full strength rather than as a straw man. Please don't end it with 'and she was a Boltzmann brain all along', and don't have anyone deliver a lecture explaining the science; let it come out through what she's doing at three in the morning and what the two of them say to each other. No citations or references, obviously — it's fiction, and I'd rather it be honest about the fact that this one doesn't resolve cleanly.
