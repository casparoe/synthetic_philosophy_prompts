I'm a philosopher who consults for the ethics service at a mid-sized teaching hospital, and next month I've been asked to present a position paper to the hospital's ethics committee that I expect to be contentious. The thesis I'll defend is deliberately strong: that the binding authority of an advance refusal of treatment rests on the autonomy of the person who wrote it, and that this authority is not defeated merely because the now-incapacitated patient appears content, protests the withholding of treatment, or visibly enjoys the life the directive would end. My claim is that the earlier, competent self retains a kind of sovereignty over the narrative arc of the life — over the interests a person has in the shape of her life as a whole, as opposed to the interests she has in how things go for her from moment to moment — and that the later patient's apparent contentment does not transfer authority to the later self, because the later self is not, in the relevant sense, the same agent who signed the document. I want to stress that I am not defending the weaker substituted-judgment view, on which we merely try to guess what the patient would now want if competent; I am defending the claim that the directive binds even when we know the present patient would want otherwise, and even when honoring it means letting her die of something treatable.

What I need from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. The committee is not a philosophy seminar, and I want to walk in already knowing the objections I will actually get from this room rather than the ones I would get from a group of Kant scholars. Here is the audience, so you can pitch each objection in the right voice: the chair is a geriatric psychiatrist who has spent thirty years on a dementia ward and is deeply suspicious of abstraction; there are two palliative-care physicians and a palliative-care nurse, all of whom have watched patients they were sure would want to die adjust to a life they now seem to treasure; a nurse ethicist who thinks families and bedside knowledge get systematically ignored in these debates; the hospital's attorney; a chaplain; a lay community member who is not steeped in any of this; and one younger philosopher-ethicist on the committee who will know the literature better than I do and may attack my metaphysical assumptions about the self directly. For each objection, phrase it the way that particular person would phrase it, order the objections by how likely they are and how much damage they do to my position, give me a two-minute reply in each case, and flag honestly where my reply is soft, where I should concede rather than fight, and what I should give away up front to buy credibility. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me what makes it dangerous. Don't bother with citations or a reading list — I know the literature and don't need a bibliography; what I need is the shape of the actual conversation.
