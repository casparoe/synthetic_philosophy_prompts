I have been keeping a private casebook of my own reactions about survival, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they amount to. In every case I am the person in question; the question is whether I survive it, and where I say I do not, what precisely has gone wrong.

1. The Relay. I am anaesthetised. A scanner reads my brain down to the last connection, my body is destroyed, and a new body is assembled elsewhere from the scan and wakes up. My verdict: I died on the table. Whatever opens its eyes on the far side is a stranger who has my memories; my attitude toward it is the attitude I would have toward a flawless impersonator, plus a certain queasiness.

2. The Long Sleep. I am anaesthetised for eighteen hours and wake in the same body. My verdict: I survive, and nothing about this is even faintly troubling.

3. The Slow Swap. Over two years, one neuron at a time, my neurons are replaced by silicon units that do exactly what the neurons did. I am awake throughout and notice nothing. My verdict: I survive. There is no particular chip whose installation was my death.

4. The Overnight Swap. The same replacement, done in one night while I am unconscious, in place — no scanning, no rebuilding, just parts exchanged in a sleeping brain. My verdict: my head says this is case 3 with the tempo changed, so I survive; my gut says it is much closer to case 1. This is one of the two I keep going back and forth on.

5. The Checkpoint. My brain state is read while I am awake and well; nothing is done to me; a new body is built from the reading and wakes up. My verdict: I survive — I am still here. The other one is a different person who happens to remember walking into the room.

6. The Fork. Two new bodies are built from the same reading and both wake up; my original body is then destroyed. My verdict: I do not survive. But neither of them died either, and both will grieve for me while insisting they are me, and I cannot say I died twice.

7. The Rewrite. Over two years, again one neuron at a time and again with me awake, my memories and character are not swapped for functional equivalents but overwritten with those of a stranger, until nothing of my history or temperament remains. The process never halts for an instant. My verdict: I do not survive. I am being erased in place, and the fact that the machinery keeps humming throughout is no comfort at all.

8. The Stutterer. I am duplicated, and for ninety seconds there are two of me, both awake, both remembering walking into the room; then one of the two is dissolved, chosen at random. My verdict: I cannot settle this one, and it is the second case I keep going back and forth on. It feels like Russian roulette, but the two outcomes are indistinguishable from the inside, so I cannot even locate what I am supposed to be afraid of.

9. The Fusion. Two people, each with a full life behind them, are read together and a single person wakes up carrying both sets of memories, or a blend of them. My verdict: neither of us survived; the being that wakes is a new person. Yet it remembers being both of us and grieves for both of us, and I find it hard to say that nothing of either of us continues.

The tentative principle I have been trying out is this: what matters is not the preservation of the pattern but the continuity of the ongoing process — a process halted and restarted from a stored state is not the same process, even when the state is bit-for-bit identical, whereas a process that keeps running while its parts are exchanged underneath it is the same process throughout. That seems to give me cases 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6 for free. Case 7 appears to refute it outright, since the process in case 7 never stops and yet I am plainly gone.

What I want from you: a precise statement of the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best fits these verdicts; for each candidate, which of my verdicts it forces me to give up; what each candidate predicts about the Stutterer and the Fusion, where my own reactions give it nothing to work with; and an honest verdict on whether the tentative principle can be repaired — say, by conjoining process-continuity with pattern-continuity — or whether it has to be abandoned, and if abandoned, what should replace it.
