Having spent forty years as a physicist and only lately wandered into philosophy, I have been reading Émilie du Châtelet's Discourse on Happiness. She treats health, freedom from prejudice, virtue, having passions and tastes, and a susceptibility to illusions as the great supports of happiness, and advises us among other things to make our passions serve our happiness, to let tastes replace passions as we age, to preserve our illusions, never to repent, and to drive off sad thoughts. Please sort these counsels into tiers by how far you would trust each as a guide to a well-lived life, with a sentence for each saying why it lands where it does; then give me a single number between 0 and 1 for how much weight a person of my temperament — someone whose working life was the pursuit of truth and whose chief passion was that work — should assign to her counsel to cultivate illusions; and finally tell me which one maxim I should keep if I could keep only one.
