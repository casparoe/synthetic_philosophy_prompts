I am a postdoctoral fellow in moral philosophy, and in three weeks I am giving a work-in-progress paper to my department's ethics reading group. The paper defends a claim I expect to be unpopular in that room: that the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, as it is pressed by the Rawls–Nozick tradition, is not an independent argument against aggregative consequentialism at all, but a restatement of a Kantian premise about the source of moral authority, and so begs the question when it is offered as a refutation rather than as a declaration of an alternative commitment. The argument turns on a distinction I have drawn between two readings of separateness. On the first, which I call the distinctness reading, the objection is simply the observation that pains and pleasures occur in different people and that there is no super-person who undergoes the aggregate. On the second, which I call the authority reading, the objection is the claim that each person's claims carry a weight that cannot be traded off against another's without that person's consent. I argue that the distinctness reading is true but entirely harmless — a utilitarian can grant it without surrendering anything — and that the authority reading, which is what actually does the work in the objection, is not a datum available to both sides but a substantive commitment inherited from Kant, which the utilitarian is entitled to reject. I also claim, more provocatively, that most of the rhetorical force of the objection comes from sliding between the two readings mid-argument.

The reading group is small and I know exactly who will be there, which is why I want to prepare properly rather than improvise. There are two Kantians: one works on the Formula of Humanity and will want to know why I think the authority reading is an inheritance rather than a deliverance of practical reason itself; the other is a constructivist about practical reason and will press me on whether my distinction presupposes a realist picture of moral weight that she rejects anyway. There are two Rawlsians, both of whom work on political liberalism rather than on the foundations of the original position, but who will reach for the difference principle and the strains of commitment the moment aggregation is mentioned. There is a Parfitian who works on personal identity and reductionism and who will want to know how my distinction survives the reductionist thesis — whether, if the boundaries between persons are less deep than we suppose, the authority reading has anything left to stand on. There is a historian of nineteenth-century ethics who will ask what I think Mill and Sidgwick were actually doing, and whether my reconstruction is a reading of them or a replacement for them. There is a colleague from legal philosophy who will insist that the authority reading is Lockean rather than Kantian and that my genealogy therefore misses its target. And there are two sympathetic consequentialists who will want me to say precisely what I mean by 'cannot be traded off' and will be disappointed if I say it is a primitive.

Please draft my Q&A preparation sheet for the hour after the paper. I want the objections this particular group will actually raise, ordered by how likely each is to come up and how much damage it would do if I have no good answer, and each phrased the way the person who raises it would phrase it — the Kantians in the vocabulary of maxims, ends in themselves and the conditions of possible consent, the Rawlsians in the vocabulary of the original position and the strains of commitment, the Parfitian in the vocabulary of reductionism and the boundaries of persons, the historian in the vocabulary of what Mill and Sidgwick actually wrote. For each, give me a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is thinnest and which follow-up would expose it. Tell me which one or two points I should concede at the outset rather than fight over, which single objection I ought to be most afraid of, and which one reply absolutely has to land for the paper to be worth publishing. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody in the room asks, and what I should say if it comes anyway. I know the literature; what I need from you is the room.
