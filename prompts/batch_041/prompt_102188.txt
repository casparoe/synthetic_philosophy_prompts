I am a journalist who covers science and ideas and also writes a monthly humor column for a small magazine that sits at the intersection of philosophy and the social sciences—the sort of publication where a joke about population ethics can appear two pages after a serious essay on moral aggregation. For the next issue I want a short joke, perhaps in the form of a mock news brief or a two-person dialogue, built around the torture-versus-dust-specks thought experiment. The joke should be comprehensible to a reader who has never encountered the case, yet it should be funny to someone who knows it well: the punchline ought to turn on the actual structure of the debate—the aggregationist claim that enough tiny harms can outweigh one enormous harm, the anti-aggregationist insistence that some harms are not commensurable, and the way the case forces a choice at a threshold instead of allowing us to remain comfortably agnostic. Please avoid the lazy version of the joke whose only point is that utilitarians are cold or that philosophers are strange.

I would also like you to seed the joke with a new wrinkle: imagine that the dust specks have organized themselves into a tiny civil service, complete with a union, a grievance procedure, and a forms office, and tell the joke from the perspective of one speck who has just learned that its entire existence is being used as a unit of moral currency in a debate it was never invited to join. The humor should come from the collision between the speck's bureaucratic language and the cosmic scale of the calculation, and it should leave the reader with the uncomfortable sense that the thought experiment's real cost is not the torture or the specks but the exchange rate itself. Keep the finished joke to a few hundred words.
