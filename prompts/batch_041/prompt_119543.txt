You are a philosopher of science with a firm grasp of the realism debates and of the physics and chemistry of the last two centuries, and I want your help predicting how a colleague will rule on a case she has not yet considered.

R. is a selective realist who takes the pessimistic meta-induction seriously without accepting it, and I have been keeping a record of her verdicts on particular posits. Phlogiston: not real. Caloric: not real, though she concedes that Carnot's results survived its demise. The luminiferous ether: not real. Newtonian absolute space: not real. The electromagnetic field: real. Dalton's atom: real, and she insists that its later subdivision retracts nothing. The neutrino: real, and in her view already real well before anyone detected one, since it had been postulated only to keep energy conservation in beta decay intact. The Mendelian gene: real, even though molecular biology reconceived it almost beyond recognition. Neptune: real, having been predicted from perturbations and then found. Vulcan: not real, though it was predicted from Mercury's perihelion by the very same method. Quarks: real, even though they can never be isolated. Supersymmetric partners of the known particles: she will not call them real, and holds that the elegance of the theory buys them nothing. The Higgs boson: real, and she says it was real well before its confirmation in 2012.

The case now before her is the inflaton. It is a field postulated to solve the horizon, flatness, and monopole problems by driving a burst of exponential expansion in the first instant of the universe. Its framework predicted a specific pattern in the cosmic microwave background — a nearly scale-invariant spectrum of fluctuations with a slight tilt, together with a series of acoustic peaks — and those predictions were confirmed in detail. The field itself is not something we can intervene on or detect directly; our only access to it is through its supposed effects, and pinning it down requires extrapolating known physics to energies some twelve orders of magnitude beyond the LHC's reach. Some cosmologists now treat the inflaton as a placeholder for whatever produced the observed fluctuations, and rival frameworks such as bouncing or cyclic cosmologies would dispense with it altogether.

Work through this carefully. Begin by brainstorming the candidate rules that would jointly fit her record, and say what each predicts about the inflaton. Then give me your best guess at her verdict, with your reasons for preferring the rule you attribute to her, flagging explicitly any place where one rule fits her record better than another. Finally, name the case that would best discriminate the surviving rules — the case whose verdict would tell us which rule she is really running, and what each candidate verdict would reveal. Close with a line of the form \answer{...} containing only your predicted verdict (real, not real, or not yet decidable) and the name of the rule you take her to be running.
