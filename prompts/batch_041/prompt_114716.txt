I am an early-career philosopher with one semester of protected time and a doctoral student working with me for a year, and I want to make Plato's treatment of drunkenness the centre of a book chapter. I am not asking for a survey of the secondary literature or a reading list. I want a defended decision about which of the open questions in this material we should actually take up, and in what order.

So that you can work from the material rather than from whatever summary you happen to have absorbed, here is the shape of it as I understand it. In the Laws, the Athenian stranger argues against the Spartan and Cretan assumption that wine should simply be banned, and treats the supervised drinking party as a serious institution of the city. Wine produces a temporary fearlessness and loosens a man's tongue, so that his real disposition shows itself; the party is therefore an instrument of inspection that the lawgiver can stage at will. The Athenian also treats drinking as practice and not merely as diagnosis: a man who has learned to hold himself together while drunk has trained under conditions closer to the ones he will meet in earnest than the man who has only ever trained sober. The discussion sits alongside the image of the human being as a puppet of the gods, pulled this way and that by cords of pleasure, pain, fear and confidence, with reason, or law, as the one golden cord we are to hold on to. The Athenian goes on to set different rules for different ages, gives wine a place in the chorus of the elderly, and insists that a drinking party be presided over by a sober officer. Elsewhere the picture is not the same: in the Symposium, Socrates is the one man who can drink all night and remain himself, while Alcibiades arrives already drunk.

The reading I am drawn to, and want to know the cost of, is that the defence of wine in the Laws is really a defence of a certain picture of legislation. On that picture the lawgiver does not make anyone virtuous; he arranges conditions under which virtue is exercised, tested and made visible. Wine is the clearest case of such a condition, and the supervision, the age-graded rules and the sober president are doing more of the philosophical work than the drink is.

Given that, which open questions in this material should we take up, and in what order? Please prioritise rather than survey. For each item, say why it is upstream of the others, how far it can be settled by attention to the text alone, and how much of the rest of the project depends on getting it right. If you think the questions that matter lie not in the exegesis but in what the reading implies, for instance for how we should think about the reach of legislation or about whether character can be tested at all, put those on the list instead. Tell me what would move an item up or down the ranking, such as a feature of the text I have overlooked, a distinction that collapses, or an implication of the reading that turns out to be unacceptable, and name the one or two questions you would leave alone, with reasons.

Lead with the ranking, then give the reasoning. Please write in British English and keep the whole answer to 700 words at most.
