I'm a PhD student in the philosophy of science — my own work is on confirmation and evidence — and I'd like a careful second opinion on a characterization of the accuracy-first programme in epistemology that a colleague gave me. My colleague is a Bayesian statistician, well read in the philosophical literature but not working in it, and over coffee he summarized the position roughly as follows. Accuracy-first epistemology, he said, is the view that truth is the only thing that matters epistemically; a credence is essentially a bet on the world, and its accuracy is a matter of how close it comes to the proposition's truth value, one for true and zero for false, as measured by some scoring rule. The programme's central claim, on his telling, is that a rational agent holds exactly those credences that maximize expected accuracy, and its central result is the theorem that only probabilistic credence functions maximize expected accuracy — which is why accuracy-firsters take probabilism to be rationally required. He described the underlying argument as a dominance argument: any non-probabilistic credence function is accuracy-dominated by some probabilistic one, so probabilism follows. Beneath the formalism, he said, the view is really reliabilism in disguise, because it evaluates a state of belief by how well it tracks the truth rather than by the evidence the agent actually possesses. And since accuracy is the only thing that matters, he concluded, the view implies that there is nothing epistemically wrong with a true belief that is unjustified, provided only that it is accurate.

I suspect he is closer to right than wrong on the broad picture, but that several of these claims are doing more work than the programme licenses — the last sentence in particular strikes me as too quick, and the reliabilism remark seems to me to trade on a distinction the programme needn't collapse. What I want is an audit of his summary, sentence by sentence: for each claim, say what an accuracy-first epistemologist would accept as fair, what they would wince at as a simplification that obscures something important, and what they would deny outright as a misrepresentation, being explicit about where the boundary between the second and the third category falls. I'd especially like you to separate the mathematical results from the normative conclusions drawn from them, and to say which of his closing conclusions — the reduction to reliabilism, and the alleged permissiveness about unjustified true beliefs — survive once the summary is corrected and which do not. Please write this as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; I would rather read it the way a colleague would set it out in a long email.
