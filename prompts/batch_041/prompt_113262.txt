I'm preparing a handout for a graduate reading group on paradoxes of the infinite (about ten participants), and I need a decision tree that walks a reader from a single starting commitment to a named position on the lamp puzzle — the case where a switch is flicked at the 1/2-minute mark, again at 1/4, again at 1/8, and so on, so that infinitely many flicks are completed before the one-minute mark, after which we ask whether the lamp is on or off.

The reader's entry point should be: "I accept that a completed infinite sequence of actions is at least conceivable." From there, every branch node must be a single assumption the verdicts turn on, phrased as a question the reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. I want at least these to appear as branch points, though you should add others if the logical space demands them: whether the completed sequence itself counts as having a final flick; whether the lamp's state at the limit time is fixed by a rule that is continuous there, so a limit exists, or only by a discontinuous toggle, so none does; whether the claim that the lamp is on at the limit time has a truth-value when no convergent rule fixes it; and whether the verdict should be constrained by what is physically realizable.

Please thread two variants of my own through the tree and say, for each, which leaves survive and which go silent. Variant A replaces the on/off switch with a dimmer whose brightness is a continuous function of the flick index, arranged so the sequence converges to a definite brightness at the limit: ask which positions still have anything to say. Variant B keeps the toggle but stipulates that the lamp's state at the limit is whatever it was after the first flick: ask whether stipulating an answer this way is a genuine position or an evasion, and where it belongs on the tree.

Leaves should carry a name where one exists — for instance, the view that the lamp is determinately on (or off) because some convergence condition settles it; the indeterminacy view that there is no fact of the matter; the view that supertasks are impossible, so the question never arises; and the view that the puzzle is ill-posed because it equivocates about completed infinities — plus an explicit unnamed leaf or two for coherent combinations nobody has bothered to defend.

Build the tree from the logical space first: enumerate the combinations of branch answers that are coherent, and only then locate the actual positions within that space. Mark the combinations no one occupies and give a one-line diagnosis of why — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two positions your tree cannot separate, rather than pretending a branch distinguishes them.

Keep the whole thing to about a page: no more than 700 words, tree and annotations included.
