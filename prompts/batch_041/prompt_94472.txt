Me and a few friends have been argueing about updateless decision theory — the idea that instead of picking the best action at each point with the info you have then, you choose one whole policy up front, before you know which decision you'll actually be facing, and then you just carry it out even once you find out you're somewhere a spot re-optimizer would sell out its earlier self (so you don't defect, you don't take the money and run, you keep the promise now that keeping it hurts). We get why that's meant to be the right way to think about your own decisions, and the part where your choice stands in for whatever anyone reasoning like you would do. What I want is the practical fallout. What are the most important implications of actually running your life updatelessly for commitments in ordinary life — promises to friends, New Years resolutions, the apps you delete so you can't re-decide later, the standing donation you said you'd keep making, and whatever it is you do when nobody's watching? Rank them by how much they'd really change what I do next week, and don't just tell me precommitment is powerful, I know that, I want the places where it tells me to do something other than what ordinary self-help wisdom would. Tell me which implications hold no matter how anyone else behaves and which ones only hold if the people around me are running something like the same policy, and flag any implication that would make me act like a sucker if I'm wrong about that. Skip the Newcomb and one-boxing material and the desert-island hitchhiker, I've seen them a hundred times, and nothing that needs a perfect predictor or a copy of me.
