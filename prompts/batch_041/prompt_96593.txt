For about fifteen years an international working group of geologists worked to establish the Anthropocene as a formal epoch of the geological time scale, eventually proposing a mid-twentieth-century boundary marked by plutonium fallout from nuclear weapons tests, with the sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario as the reference section. In 2024 the proposal was voted down by the relevant stratigraphic bodies, and yet the argument among geologists, archaeologists, historians, and social theorists has not slowed at all. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is rather than a verdict on the date.

My hypothesis is that three questions are tangled together and only one of them is stratigraphic: whether a globally synchronous and preservable signal exists that could serve as a boundary marker; whether the Anthropocene is the kind of thing that can be a chronostratigraphic unit at all, given that such units are defined by a single boundary point in a single reference section, whereas the human reshaping of the Earth looks more like a diachronous geological event — an extinction, an oxygenation — spread out in time and space; and who has standing to fix a term that historians, archaeologists, and the public already use for their own purposes, and what the definition is meant to do.

Assess that hypothesis: which of the three is doing the real work, is the choice of start date a genuine empirical matter or a choice relative to a purpose, and should the 2024 rejection be read as settling the geological question, as foreclosing it by institutional authority, or as evidence that the question as posed was ill-formed? Then say what would count as progress here — a stratigraphic discovery, a conceptual distinction, an argument that no single date can be right — or whether no progress should be expected.
