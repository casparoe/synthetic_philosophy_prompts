I'm a retired biochemist — forty years on enzyme kinetics, mostly — and since I retired I've been reading a lot of moral philosophy, especially the medical ethics stuff, and there's a pattern in how hospitals and courts actually operate that I can't square with itself.

Take the ICU. As I understand it, the law and the official ethics line is that stopping a treatment and never starting it are the same thing morally: if you're allowed to decline the ventilator, you're allowed to have it taken away, and if the patient is going to die either way the disease is what kills them, not the doctor. Fine. But on the ground those two things are treated as seperate animals. Stopping the ventilator or the dialysis or the feeding tube triggers ethics consults, second opinions, waiting periods, family meetings with a chaplain in the room; not starting it usually doesn't. Nurses who'll happily follow an order not to escalate will hesitate over an order to withdraw. Meanwhile the same act done at home by a spouse — pulling a tube, or just not calling anyone — looks like a homicide to a prosecutor, or might. And then there's the transplant side, which seems to assume the opposite of the official line: in the donation-after-circulatory-death protocols, the whole thing is timed around the withdrawl. They pull the vent, watch the clock, and the number of minutes they're willing to wait for the heart to stop — and the extra minutes of no-touch afterward before anyone touches the body — only makes sense if everyone in the room agrees that the withdrawl is what's killing the patient. If the heart doesn't stop in the window they wheel them back and start treating again. And yet the death certificate says the underlying illness, not "treatment withdrawn." (The palliative sedation case confuses me too — they'll sedate someone deep and stop the feeding, and someone tells me the double effect thing covers it, but I'm not sure the sedation and the stopping are even seperate acts by then.)

So: what is this whole bundle of practices assuming about the difference between doing and allowing? Is it the causal claim that omissions aren't really causes — and if so, how does the donation protocol get away with the opposite assumption? Or is the distinction really a normative one about duties and what the patient has a right to refuse, in which case what's actually doing the work in the paperwork asymmetry, and why would a right to refuse a treatment not be equally a right to have it stopped? Which rival accounts does the practice quietly rule out — the flat view that there's no moral difference at all, the view that it's all about the agent's intention, the view that it's about who's causally salient to an observer? Does the practice need one consistent theory underneath it, or can it limp along on several incompatible ones as long as nobody looks too hard? And if you swapped in the flat view — withdrawing and withholding are identical, full stop — what would actually have to change on the ward: which forms, which consults, which parts of the donation protocol, which death certificates?

What I'd really like is for you to tell me which bits are load-bearing and which are just institutional habit and inertia, because from where I'm sitting those two things look identical.
