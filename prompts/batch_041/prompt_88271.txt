For most of my working life I was a field ecologist, and since retiring I have been trying to educate myself in moral philosophy — with, I suspect, the usual autodidact's mixture of enthusiasm and blind spots. I have just finished a popular book on the moral status of wild animals, which I shall leave unnamed, since my question concerns the argument rather than the author. Before I repeat its central claim to anyone, I would like you to audit my understanding of it.

The argument, as I have reconstructed it, runs roughly as follows. The wilderness is not a sanctuary but a killing floor: for every animal that survives to reproduce, many more die young and die badly — taken by predators, starved, diseased, or frozen. A hectare of mature forest or grassland therefore sustains a standing population of creatures whose lives are, on the whole, more misery than flourishing. And because those losses are continually made good by reproduction, the quantity of suffering in a given habitat is stable over time rather than declining; the book calls this the habitat's "suffering carrying capacity." Now consider that same hectare converted to cropland or pasture. Farmland supports far fewer animals, and the animals it does support are not, for the most part, living wild lives. The book concludes that the humane course is to clear wild land: fewer animals, and hence less suffering. On this view, the conservationist who fights to save a rainforest is unwittingly fighting to keep a vast engine of misery running.

I would like to know how much of this I can trust. Please work through the argument step by step, separating (i) what a careful defender of the position would readily endorse, (ii) what is a popularizer's simplification, and (iii) what is simply wrong or unsupported. Three points interest me in particular. First, the inference from "wild animals live lives that are, on balance, not worth living" to "clearing their habitat is humane": does that follow, and if so under what assumptions — in particular, does it depend on a total-welfare rather than an average-welfare view, and does it survive if the premise about wild animal welfare is merely plausible rather than established? Second, the phrase "suffering carrying capacity": as someone trained on the ecological notion of carrying capacity, I find the coinage suspicious, but I cannot tell whether the book has merely chosen a misleading metaphor or has genuinely run together two ideas that ought to be kept apart. Third, whether the conclusion survives contact with the facts of habitat destruction itself — what becomes of the animals when land is cleared, and whether fewer animals really does mean less suffering. I am a beginner in this literature and would rather have the joints of the argument exposed than be handed a verdict.
