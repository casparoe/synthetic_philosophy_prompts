ok so I keep going back and forth on the Browning/Goldhagen fight over that Hamburg reserve police battalion — you know the one: middle-aged reservists, not fanatics, cops not soldiers, sent to Poland, and at the first mass shooting they were ordered to do, the commander made the offer that anyone who didn't feel up to it could step out, no punishment, and basically nobody took him up on it. I don't want a summary of the two books, and don't give me citations or reading lists, I want the thing actually argued out, because I think both sides get sloppy with the words situation and character and I want to know where.

First the mapping. Who counts as an agent here, what is the situation, what are the actual options, and what would count as evidence for a dispositional explanation versus a situational one. Be honest that the explanatory target is a months-long pattern of behaviour across a whole unit and not one decision on one day — I suspect a lot of this debate is people answering different questions and not noticing.

Then the test. The situationist stuff — the social psych literature on character and situations, the finding that cross-situational consistency in behaviour is weak, that behaviour tracks how strong the situation is far more than it tracks traits — comes almost entirely from lab studies of small, cheap, low-stakes behaviour. Minutes long, no cost, nobody watching you afterwards. Does any of that survive the trip to mass murder? What does it predict about who refused and who didn't, and how would we even check that without going in a circle, given that a situation is partly constituted by how the person reads it?

Then run it the other way. Goldhagen's move is basically that the situation that mattered wasn't the immediate one, it was a whole culture of antisemitism that had already made the killing thinkable. Is that a situational explanation at a bigger grain, or a dispositional one in disguise — a whole people with a trait? And what does the episode do to the theory: confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never anticipated?

Then the part I actually care about. If character isn't doing the explanatory work, what happens to responsibility? Can you blame someone for what they did if what they did is explained by the situation rather than by them? Is the descriptive claim (traits are weak predictors) even in tension with the normative one (they should have refused)? I think it isn't, but make the case properly rather than just asserting it.

And say where both theories are just silent. Dont force them to speak.
