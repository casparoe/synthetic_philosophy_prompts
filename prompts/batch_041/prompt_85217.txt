I recently agreed to serve as a trustee of my family's small foundation, which means I now sign off on grants that spend real money in the name of doing good. I expected the difficulty to lie in working out which organizations actually help. What has unsettled me instead is different: the evidence, even where it is good, never seems to reach the thing I am being asked to decide, and I have begun to suspect that the problem is not the evidence. Write me a short story that takes this seriously.

Center it on one character who makes a single irreversible decision intended to do good — a grant, a signature, a vote, a hire, a donation of something that cannot be given back. Setting, period, and register are yours. The story's engine should be this, dramatized rather than explained: a complete causal account of what a well-intentioned act sets off downstream still does not add up to a verdict on the act, and the shortfall is not our ignorance of the future. Two things should do real work. First, give the character some device, archive, ledger, or procedure that reports the act's actual downstream consequences in detail, and let them find that the report does not close the question: the effects land on different people at different times, and there is no non-arbitrary way to sum them into a single verdict, so that the completeness of the account makes the verdict harder rather than easier. Second, let the character notice that "the difference my act made" is not well-defined on its own, since it depends on which later choices one holds fixed when asking what would otherwise have happened; the same act can be credited with a rescue or charged with a disaster under different conventions, and no one ever chose the convention.

Two craft constraints. Do not write the well-worn parable in which a good deed backfires — the strongest version of this story is one in which nothing goes wrong. And do not stage a philosopher or a scientist explaining the conceit in dialogue; keep the exposition inside action and image. Somewhere between 1,200 and 2,000 words. I do not want a reading list or citations; the story is what I am after.

After the story, add a short note, two paragraphs at most. Tell me which reading of the underlying idea you committed to in the dramatization, and where the story takes liberties with it or leans on a contested claim — in particular, whether you assumed the consequences are in principle knowable or genuinely open, and whether you treated the summing-up problem as merely practical or as a defect in the question itself. Then tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would have pushed you to write a materially different story. Finally, say where in your own reasoning you think this is most likely to fail.
