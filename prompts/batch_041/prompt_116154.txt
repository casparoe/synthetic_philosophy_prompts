For a session of my school's philosophy club I would rather present a case than summarize the literature, so please build one to my specifications. I need an ordinary, realistic situation — something that could plausibly happen to a high school student, with no experts, no contrived brain experiments, and no information the characters could not have had — in which two people who are peers with respect to some question, neither better placed to be right and neither having reasoned more carefully, disagree about it, and in which conciliationism says the disagreement requires at least one of them to weaken or drop their belief while steadfastness says the belief may be kept. Two constraints matter most. First, the question must be one that cannot be settled by looking it up, running an experiment, or waiting to see; otherwise the case dissolves. Second, the steadfast verdict must not rest on any asymmetry between the two — no extra evidence on one side, no better track record, no superior insight — and the case must be symmetric, so that if the two disputants swapped places the description would read exactly the same. The verdicts have to follow from the two views rather than be built into the setup. Keep the scenario to a single paragraph. Then walk me through what each view says and why, name the structural feature that is doing the work, tell me what becomes of the case if that feature is removed, and give me your honest judgment on the obvious objection: that in a perfectly symmetric disagreement steadfastness licenses both parties to keep their conflicting beliefs, which looks like licensing arbitrariness. If the case cannot survive that objection, say so and say which premise has to give.
