At a recent meeting of a local history society, I watched two of its members, Adela and Bernard, spend most of an evening arguing about how far one may argue from the silence of the sources. I have no stake in the outcome and would like an outside adjudication of their exchange, move by move, rather than a general essay on historical method. The occasion was an illustrative case they had posed to each other: a hypothetical claim that a small ancient city once enfranchised a contingent of foreign mercenaries in a single act. Neither of them thinks much hangs on that particular case; it was a peg for a methodological dispute. What follows is my reconstruction of what they said, in order.

(1) Adela: The literary sources for the period say nothing about any such grant. An act of that kind — a whole body of foreigners admitted to the citizen body at once — would have been conspicuous, and conspicuous things get recorded. Their silence is therefore evidence, and strong evidence, against the claim.

(2) Bernard: Silence is evidence only when you have reason to think the sources would have spoken. The surviving texts for that region and century are a few fragments, most of them written elsewhere by authors with no interest in the place. Their silence tells us nothing about what happened there; an absence of evidence is not an evidence of absence.

(3) Adela: That slogan is false as it stands, and you know it — the absence of your keys from your pocket is evidence that they are not in your pocket. An argument from silence is an ordinary inference; the only real question is whether the sample is informative. And here it is. What survives is not a random sample of what was written; it is filtered toward the notable. A notable event therefore had a better chance of being both mentioned and preserved than a mundane one. Silence about a notable event is more telling than silence about a mundane one, and that is the case we have.

(4) Bernard: I will grant the direction of your point. But see what it demands. To convert silence into anything like a probability, you must specify how likely a mention was — which means a model of what moves authors to write things down and of how texts come to survive. Neither of us has such a model; you are asserting a likelihood, not computing one. And the premise doing all your work — that the grant was notable — is the very thing in dispute. To the authors in question, a border town enlisting a few hundred soldiers may have been beneath notice altogether. You cannot establish the notability that gives your silence its force without already assuming the conclusion.

(5) Bernard: In any case, we are not confined to silence. There is the inscription fragment — a list of names under a formula that resembles enfranchisement grants. It is a physical artifact, independent of the literary tradition, and on its face it records exactly what the claim asserts.

(6) Adela: On its face is doing a great deal of work there. The fragment is broken; the formula is abbreviated and turns up in other contexts as well, some of them private dedications or lists of donors. You are treating the most convenient reading of an ambiguous object as the default. And even if some later author does mention such a grant, we would still have to ask whether he knew anything himself or was repeating a single lost source. Apparent multiple attestation is often one witness in several copies.

(7) Bernard: I accept that. But notice the asymmetry: you hold the positive evidence — the artifact — to a standard you exempt your own negative evidence from. The fragment must be unambiguous to count for me, while a silence whose force depends on an unstated model of notability counts for you. If we are to be skeptics, let us be skeptics about both.
