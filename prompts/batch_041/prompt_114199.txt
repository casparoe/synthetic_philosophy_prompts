Last night a friend and I spent most of the evening arguing about the framework that cause prioritizers use — importance, tractability, neglectedness — and I lost. Her case was that the three criteria cannot be made independent. Importance, she said, has to be assessed at the margin, as the value of one more person or one more dollar devoted to the cause, and at that margin the number of people already at work is already counted; so neglectedness either repeats the same consideration under a new name or does no work at all. Tractability, on her account, is marginal value a third time. Her conclusion was that the framework is not a decision procedure but a set of prompts that lets the user's existing preferences come out looking like the output of an analysis. I could not refute it, and I have not stopped turning it over.

Which is awkward, because I have agreed to give the commencement address at the small college I graduated from twelve years ago. I work in global health, which is how I met the framework in the first place. The room will be six hundred people: students, their families, and a faculty contingent that includes a few philosophers and a few economists who will notice if I cheat. I am not interested in delivering the usual address. I want one that actually uses importance, tractability, and neglectedness to structure the advice — the criteria should shape what I tell them about choosing what to do with a life, and should constrain what I am able to claim, rather than being mentioned in the second paragraph and abandoned by the third.

Write that speech. Roughly eight minutes, in a register the parents in the back row can follow the whole way through; decide for yourself whether the occasion can bear my naming the movement the framework comes from. It has to take my friend's objection seriously in the body of the speech — if the framework is a checklist rather than a calculator, I want to say so in terms a graduating senior and an economics professor can both accept — but it must not turn into a seminar or a defense of the framework. Let it cost me something to say. Do not resolve the argument; I have not resolved it. And it must be a speech, not an essay with "you" sprinkled through it: it needs an opening that earns the room and a closing that does not pretend to settle what it hasn't.

End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing one sentence: the one sentence the speech deliberately never says.
