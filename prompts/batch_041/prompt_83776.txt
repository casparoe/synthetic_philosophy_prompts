I teach an undergraduate seminar on Wittgenstein and religion and want to use The Brothers Karamazov in it without reducing the novel to an illustration of the lectures. Read it through Wittgenstein's account of religious belief — that such belief is not a hypothesis about what is or will be the case, that evidence would not shore it up but dissolve it, and, as he presses against Frazer, that religious practice is not a botched attempt at empirical explanation, so that whoever calls it bad science has already misdescribed it. The episode I keep returning to is Alyosha's collapse when Zosima's body begins to stink: the faithful expected incorruption, the miracle does not come, and his faith breaks. On Wittgenstein's view that is a category mistake on Alyosha's part rather than a refutation; does the novel see it that way? Set that against Ivan's Grand Inquisitor, who holds that men cannot bear a God who asks for free belief and must be given miracle instead — the Inquisitor's psychology and Wittgenstein's grammar agree oddly well, and I would like that tension taken seriously rather than dissolved. Then say where the reading fails. I suspect the novel's machinery of vindicating and withheld miracles, and its treatment of the soul's immortality as a proposition to be accepted or rejected, presupposes precisely the evidential logic Wittgenstein denies, so that a Wittgensteinian reading may have to convict Dostoevsky of superstition in Wittgenstein's own sense; tell me whether that charge is fair or an imposition, and what the novel shows about belief that the philosophical discussion of belief misses. Say how confident you are in your claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
