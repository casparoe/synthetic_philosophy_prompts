Advance directives as fallback config

The usual framing, as far as I can tell, is a fight between two pictures. On one side the directive is like a will: the earlier self is the author of the life and gets to say how it ends, so if the earlier self wrote "no ventilation, no feeding tube, no heroics", that settles it even after the person has changed their mind, because the one who changed their mind isn't really the author any more. On the other side, only the present self's preferences count, because the earlier self is gone and you can't let a ghost vote.

Both pictures treat the earlier self and the later self as two parties, like a contract between two companies. I think that's the mistake, and it's the same mistake as treating an old config file as a contract with the current version of the service. A config file isn't a counterparty. It's a fallback. It was written by an earlier commit of the same thing, for the case where the thing can't work out its own settings.

So my first move: an advance directive is authoritative to the extent that the later self can't run its own preference-computation, and not otherwise. Call this the fallback view. If the later self is fully in the game, the directive is just a default it can override, same as any other default. If the later self is not in the game at all, the directive is the last known good configuration and we run it.

The problem is what "in the game" means, and I think the whole debate has been sloppy here. There are two seperate questions hiding in it: (1) is the later self still a preference-holder, can it form and hold preferences at all? and (2) if so, are those preferences continuous with the earlier self's?

The standard positions collapse these. The will picture answers (1) yes and then ignores it, because it only cares about authorship. The present-self picture answers (2) yes and then ignores it, because it only cares about who's speaking now. I want to keep them apart, because the interesting cases are the ones where they come apart.

Case A, preference-holder and continuous: directive is a weak default, override freely. Case B, preference-holder but discontinuous, i.e. a conversion or a value change: this is the hard one. Case C, not a preference-holder but continuous, can't express anything though the values are intact underneath: the directive binds hard. Case D, neither: the directive binds hard too, because there's no competing voice, but we should be honest that we're now choosing for a stranger.

Case B is where the usual arguments fail. The usual move is: if the new values are caused by the disease, they don't count as the person's values. But that proves too much. Every value any of us has is caused by something, upbringing, a book, a head injury, a slow afternoon. If being caused disqualifies a preference, nothing qualifies. So the disease-causation move, as stated, is empty.

I want a narrower test. My proposal: the later self's contrary preference beats the directive only if two keys turn at once. Key one: the preference is stable and self-endorsed, it survives a bit of time and the person owns it as theirs rather than treating it as a mood or a symptom they'd disavow. Key two: the person still owns their past self, they recognise the earlier self as themselves, they can say that was me and this is me now, even if they no longer agree with it.

The twist, which is the bit I actually care about: key two is doing something key one can't. It isn't asking whether the new values match the old ones. It's asking whether there's still a single someone spanning the change. If there is, the change is a change of mind and the earlier self should loose. If there isn't, if the later self has no grip on the earlier self at all so that the preference is floating free of any history, then I don't think there's a someone there to override anything, and the directive stands. So the authority of the earlier self isn't grounded in authorship and it isn't grounded in the earlier self's rights. It's grounded in being the only thing that keeps the life a single life.

But I can see the objection coming. This makes the directive almost unrevisable in exactly the case where we're most worried about it, because the people who lose their grip on their past self are the ones with advanced dementia, and those are the ones whose present preferences I was trying to protect. So the two-key rule might be a ratchet dressed up as a compromise. And I can't tell whether the ownership-of-the-past-self idea is a real criterion or whether I've just smuggled the conclusion in, since I define the override so it only fires when the person is still the sort of person who'd have written the directive, which is circular.
