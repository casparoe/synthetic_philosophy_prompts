I've been reading a popular science book on behavioural genetics — the kind written by a science journalist rather than by a specialist — and it offers the following summary of heritability, which it presents as the standard, uncontroversial picture. I'd like a careful audit of it before I lean on it in an essay I'm writing.

The book's summary runs roughly as follows. "Heritability is the proportion of a trait that a person owes to their genes. When researchers say the heritability of height is around 0.8, they mean that about 80% of a person's height comes from their DNA and about 20% from the environment — diet, childhood illness, and so on. Estimates like this are about populations, so they don't describe any one individual; you can't say that a particular child's height is 80% genetic. What a high heritability does tell us is that a trait is relatively insensitive to environmental change: a trait that is 80% genetic leaves only about 20% of the variation for schools, diets, or parenting to work on. The main tool is the twin study. Because identical twins share all their genes while fraternal twins share half of theirs, a trait on which identical twins are more alike than fraternal twins must be genetic, and the size of the gap gives you the heritability. Heritability also varies between populations — a trait might be 0.8 heritable in one country and 0.4 in another — and where that happens, the trait is more strongly genetic in the first population."

Please go through this claim by claim. Which parts would a working quantitative or behavioural geneticist endorse as stated, which would they merely wince at as loose talk, and which are straightforwardly false? I'm especially unsure about the inference from the twin gap to a genetic conclusion, and about the closing sentence. I'd also like to know whether the intervention claim — that only the non-genetic share is "left" for schools and diets to work on — follows from anything true in the summary, or whether it is a separate mistake. Two further things I can't sort out on my own: what exactly follows from heritability being a property of a population rather than of a trait, when it comes to transporting an estimate from one country or generation to another; and whether heritability estimates for a trait could rise or fall as a population's environmental variation is reduced, and why.
