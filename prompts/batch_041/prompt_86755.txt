I write about science and ideas for a living, which mostly means I get to sit in on other peoples arguments, and last week I sat in on one that I can't get out of my head. A friend of mine who's finishing a PhD on perception — vision science on one side, philosophy on the other — walked me through what he claims is the cleanest argument in his field, and the conclusion is that none of us ever actually sees anything. Not in the sense that our eyes are bad. In the sense that the whole idea of being directly in touch with the world is a mistake, and that all we ever get is our own inner states plus a guess about what's causing them. He thinks this is a great result. I think it can't be right, and I can't find the bad step, so I'm hoping you can.

Here it is the way he put it. (1) Whatever state your visual system is in when you look at a tomato — retina firing, cortex doing its thing — that same state can be produced with no tomato anywhere around: a dream, a knock on the optic nerve, an electrode in the right spot. He says the mechanism doesn't matter, only that the inner state is producible in the absence of the thing. (2) In that case you aren't aware of a tomato, because there isn't one, so whatever you're aware of has to be something on your side of the fence — a state of you. (3) The two cases are subjectively indistinguishable: no amount of careful attention, no improvement in your position as a subject, tells you which one you're in. (4) If two experiences are indistinguishable in that way, then they're the same kind of thing, since a difference you couldn't in principle detect isn't a difference in the experience. (5) So seeing the tomato and the electrode case are the same kind of experience. (6) Therefore in the good case you're also only aware of your own state, with a tomato sitting offstage as a cause. (7) So perception is really inference: we don't see the world, we see our own inner states and infer. I can't accept (7). And the part that really gets me is the downstream damage — it means the belief I have right now that there's a screan in front of me is an inference from my own private cinema, and so is every belief I have about anything outside my own head.

So which step goes? I'd rather not give up (3); I'm not going to pretend I can introspect my way out of a hallucination, and I want to keep (1), because it sounds like the sort of thing vision science actually says. A friend of a friend says the answer is something called disjunctivism, which as far as I can tell means the two cases simply aren't the same kind of state at all — that sounds like a dodge, but I gather it's respectable, so if that's the move, tell me exactly what I'm buying. What happens to the fact that they feel identical from the inside? Can someone who's hallucinating still be reasonable in believing what they believe, or does that come out false too? And if I refuse the move and insist that a hallucination can justify a belief just as well as a perception does, do I escape (7), or do I just arrive there by a different road? No need to send me to the literature, I just want the shape of the options and what each one costs.
