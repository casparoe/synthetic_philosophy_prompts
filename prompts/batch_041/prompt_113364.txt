I am a retired invertebrate palaeontologist — forty years spent deciding, specimen by specimen, whether a shell fell inside a named species or outside it — and since retiring I have been attending a seminar on constitutional theory at the local university. We have been working through the conventions of constitutional monarchy, and in a spirit of self-examination I have written down my considered verdicts on a run of cases, most of them invented, so that someone can tell me what principle they express. I record them as I actually hold them, not as I would tidy them up for publication. (i) A bill has passed both houses in the ordinary way, with a clear electoral mandate behind it; the sovereign thinks it is a wicked bill. She must assent. (ii) The same bill, except that it was forced through by a caretaker government that lost a confidence vote a fortnight earlier, with an election three weeks away. Here I think she may properly refuse assent for the time being, or at least insist on delay — and I notice how awkwardly that sits with (i), since the bill is the same bill. (iii) After an inconclusive election the incumbent prime minister, plainly lacking a majority, declines to resign and proposes to meet parliament and see what happens. She should send for the leader who can plausibly command confidence. (iv) Two rival leaders each say they can command confidence and neither can show it. Here she must not choose; she should wait, and if necessary let the house settle the matter. (v) A prime minister who has just lost a confidence vote asks for a dissolution. I think she may refuse, at least where an alternative government is in view — which again cuts against (i). (vi) A prime minister advises prorogation for the avowed purpose of preventing a debate he expects to lose. Refusal seems to me permitted, though I could not say whether it is required. (vii) A bill would abolish the monarchy. She must assent; I feel the pull of the contrary view and reject it. (viii) A bill touches the crown's own revenues. Assent, as in any other case. (ix) At her weekly audience she tells the prime minister, frankly and at length, that his policy is a disaster — and she is right. Entirely proper. (x) She says the same thing on television. Entirely improper. (xi) Her private secretary, unprompted, lets it be known which way she leans on a hung parliament. Improper, even though the lean is sound. And the case that troubles me most: (xii) a prime minister advises a declaration of war that she believes to be a catastrophic folly; I think she must sign, since on matters of substance she has no discretion — yet in (vi) I let her refuse an advice that is also, at bottom, about substance, since what is really at stake there is whether a debate is allowed to happen at all. I cannot see how both can be right.

What principle, or small set of principles, captures the bulk of this? I have a tentative one and would like it tested rather than endorsed: that the sovereign has no discretion over the substance of legislation or policy but retains a discretion over process — over who is to form a government, and whether the conditions for responsible government are met — and that this is why (i), (vii), (viii) and (xii) run one way while (ii), (iii), (v) and (vi) run the other. Is that a real line or merely a description of what has lately been tolerated? If it is unstable, I would rather be told which of my verdicts has to go than have the principle patched until it saves all of them; and I would like to know what the repaired principle predicts for two or three cases I have not listed — for instance, a sovereign asked to assent to a bill enlarging her own powers, or one whose government proposes to fill the senior courts on openly partisan grounds.

Finally, a request about the manner of the answer. I would like it as committed as the material allows: not a survey of positions, but a principle stated precisely, defended, and applied. Tell me where the reasoning is likeliest to fail and what would move you off it — which consideration, if it turned out to be true, would lead you to a different verdict, and which of my verdicts you would abandon if you had to. I am a beginner at this and would rather be told plainly that my framing is confused than be flattered.
