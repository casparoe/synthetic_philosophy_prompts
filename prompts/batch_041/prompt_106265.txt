I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, but I spend most of my time with the econ and policy crowd, and I'm on the board of this tiny student-run grant fund — we hand out about $40k a year and I'm the one who actually writes up the recommendations. So I've been ranking applicants with the whole importance / tractability / neglectedness thing, and my gut keeps disagreeing with my spreadsheet. I cant tell if my gut is just being dumb or if the framework is.

Roughly what my gut says, case by case:

1. There's a cause that's enormous by any measure — millions of beings, decades of impact — but basicly nobody works on it *because* nobody has any idea how to make progress. The spreadsheet loves it (importance high, neglectedness high). Gut says no, and my reason is that "nobody works on it" and "we don't know how" are the same fact wearing two hats, so I'm scoring it twice.

2. Another one is all-or-nothing: nothing happens at all until we hit roughly $200k, and then it either works or it doesn't. Below that, marginal dollars do nothing. The spreadsheet gives me a nice smooth score. Gut says a dollar here is worth about zero today and the two-hundred-thousandth dollar is worth a fortune, and I don't know how to put that into a product of three numbers.

3. One cause is important, tractable, and neglected by the usual measures — except I happen to know a big foundation has all but committed to funding it next year if we don't. Gut says our money is worth far less than the score suggests. Flip it: another cause where we're plausibly the only funder for a decade, and gut says our dollars are worth far more.

4. One affects beings whose moral status I'm honestly unsure about — minds we might build, or insects-ish things. "Importance" there is doing at least three jobs at once (how many, how bad, how long, plus how likely they count at all) and the framework squashes them into a single number. Gut says fund it, but not because of the product.

5. On anything catastrophe-shaped my gut goes weirdly non-consequentialist: I'd spend the whole budget shaving a tiny risk of something unrecoverable before I'd spend it on a much bigger pile of expected suffering. I suspect that's just me being a coward about variance.

6. The neglectedness number swings by a factor of ten depending on where I draw the boundary of the "cause." Is the cause the whole field, or is it this one subproblem? Gut says neglectedness should be about the specific marginal thing I'm buying, not the category.

7. Research-y and meta stuff scores terribly (low tractability, the good is diffuse) and gut says fund it anyway, because what it produces makes every later decision better, including decisions we won't be making.

So is there a principle behind these verdicts or am I rationalizing? My own guess is "expected marginal value per dollar, counterfactually," which is fine as far as it goes, but it seems to make the three factors redundant — they're just pieces of that one product — and it doesn't obviously handle 2, 5, or 7. And 3 and 7 pull against each other, which is part of why I'm asking. If you had to state the best version of what I'm doing, with the case that forces each clause, which of my verdicts would you tell me to give up? Then what does it predict for a case I haven't listed: funding something that scores well on all three, but where I can't tell whether the good would have happened anyway?

American English please — my advisor's American and flags every extra "u" I write.
