Take the role of a philosopher who also writes speeches for a living, and think carefully before you draft anything.

I direct a small independent museum housed in a converted Victorian chapel. Two years ago the building burned down. We have rebuilt it around the surviving east wall, relaid the original floorboards in the new reading room, and rehung the collection, which came through only because it was in a vault. The reopening is in a fortnight and I have to give the address: about seven minutes, spoken aloud, to a mixed room — the donors who paid for the rebuild, the volunteers who salvaged fabric from the rubble, the founder's family, and two reporters who will quote me.

Everything I try to write runs into the Ship of Theseus problem, and it is not decoration; it dictates what I can and cannot say. I cannot claim that the museum is back, or that nothing was lost, or thank the donors for "restoring" what burned, without settling a question about identity that a seven-minute speech has no business settling. Three ideas from colleagues should govern the address. First, our registrar's working rule that a museum object is constituted by its accession record — number, provenance file, condition reports — so that the material thing is, in the end, an illustration of the file. Second, the conservator's insistence that patina and original fabric carry a value that honest repair cannot manufacture, and that restoration is defensible only while it stops short of invention. Third, the unglamorous fact that the charity never ceased to exist, so the only thing in the building with unbroken identity is the minute book.

Write the address. It should be warm, concrete and free of museum jargon. Begin with a few lines telling me which identity claims the speech can and cannot make, and why; then give the speech itself; then list the points at which you had to make a judgement call that another writer would have made differently.
