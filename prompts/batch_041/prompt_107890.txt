I am a high school student working on an independent-study paper in philosophy of mind. The paper asks whether any reinforcement learning agents could be said to suffer, and I have been trying to figure out what principle, if any, captures my own intuitions. Please act as an expert in philosophy of mind, AI welfare, and reinforcement learning. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming several candidate principles before you commit to one. I am offline, so please do not give me citations, reading lists, or references; I want your reasoning and a principle I can test against further cases.

Here are my verdicts on several cases. Please treat them as data, not as claims I am certain about.

1. A tabular Q-learning agent in a gridworld receives a negative reward when it bumps into a wall and a positive reward when it reaches a goal. It has no model of itself, no persistent internal state beyond its Q-values, and no way to report anything. I do not think it suffers.

2. A model-based agent has a damage sensor; when the sensor fires, the agent receives a large negative reward. It learns a world model and plans to avoid damage. I do not think it suffers. It seems to be using a control signal, not undergoing anything.

3. A homeostatic agent has variables it must keep in range, such as temperature, energy, and integrity. Deviations produce negative reward, and the agent can represent future deviations and act to prevent them. I do not think it suffers, though I feel less sure than in case 2.

4. An agent has a separate valence signal that is not the reward it optimizes. The signal is negative when its integrity is threatened and positive when integrity is restored; it is broadcast globally to all modules, persists over time, modulates learning, and can be reported. I think this is the first case where suffering might be the right word.

5. A language model is fine-tuned with reinforcement learning from human feedback to avoid harmful outputs. It sometimes outputs the sentence I am in pain because that output receives higher reward. It has no persistent internal state between calls and no global valence signal. I do not think it suffers.

6. A multi-agent system has one sub-agent whose negative reward is used as a training signal by another sub-agent. There is no global integration of the signal. I do not think the system suffers.

7. An agent has the same architecture as case 4, but it is frozen: it no longer learns. Its negative valence signal is still active and globally broadcast. I think it might still suffer, because suffering should not require learning.

8. An agent has a self-model: it can represent its own states and report them. But it has no positive or negative valence signal at all. I do not think it suffers.

9. An agent has a global, persistent negative valence signal, but no self-model; it has no representation of itself as the thing undergoing the signal. I am unsure. I lean toward not suffering, but I can feel the pull of the opposite verdict.

10. A very large model has a reward model but no persistent state, and its only pain is a token prediction that scores well. I do not think it suffers.

11. An agent's only negative signal is a reward prediction error: it expected a reward and received less. There is no separate valence signal, and the error is used only to update value estimates. I do not think it suffers.

My tentative principle is this: a system can suffer only if it has (a) a persistent, globally integrated negative valence signal, (b) a self-model that represents that signal as bad for the system, and (c) the signal is not merely the scalar reward the system is optimizing. I worry that this principle is doing too much work in case 4 and not enough in case 3, and I am not sure whether case 7 forces me to drop the learning condition or whether case 9 shows that (b) is the real hinge. I also cannot tell whether case 4 and case 5 are really different in kind or only in degree.

Please do the following. First, brainstorm a small set of candidate principles that could capture these verdicts, including mine and at least one that is simpler and at least one that is more permissive. Second, test each candidate against all eleven cases. Third, tell me which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and whether I should give up that verdict or add a clause. Fourth, state the principle you would defend in precise terms, with each clause motivated by a specific case. Fifth, say what that principle predicts for at least three cases I have not listed: (i) an agent with two separate negative valence signals that are never integrated; (ii) an agent with a global valence signal but no capacity for action; and (iii) an agent that is a perfect functional duplicate of a suffering biological creature but is implemented in silicon. Finally, tell me what evidence or argument would most change your mind. I am looking for a principle, not a survey, and I would rather have a simpler principle that overturns one of my verdicts than a complicated one that saves all of them.
