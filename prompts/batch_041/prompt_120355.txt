I'm giving a talk in two weeks at the strategy/org-behaviour seminar at the b-school my partner teaches at — basically a favor gig, fifteen people in a room, mostly empirical management researchers who study firms, two game theorists who do contract theory, and one guy who runs lab experiments on bargaining. Working title: "Escalation isn't the bug: the dollar auction and where commitment actually goes wrong." Here's the thesis, and I want you to figure out what this particular room is going to throw at me.

The dollar auction (you auction off a dollar, highest bidder pays what they bid and gets the dollar, second-highest bidder also pays what they bid and gets nothing) gets taught as a parable about escalation of commitment: watch, people keep bidding past a dollar, the money just burns, sunk cost fallacy in a bottle. I think that's backwards twice over. First, once you and a rival are both in, "keep bidding" is often locally optimal given the structure — the escalation phase is just what auctions do, and the real puzzle is entry: why anyone takes the first step into a game whose logic is a race to the bottom. Second, and this is the part I actually care about: the standard telling assumes the prize is fixed at a dollar. It isn't. If part of what you're buying is being the person who didn't back down — not being the sucker — then the prize inflates as the bidding goes on, and "why would you pay $1.10 for a dollar" is just the wrong question, because by then the thing on the table isn't the dollar anymore. Which means the escalation is less irrational than advertised, and the pathology sits at entry: you buy a ticket into a game that manufactures its own escalating prize.
