I write about politics and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm working up a short piece on Simone Weil's essay on political parties — the one usually translated as *On the Abolition of All Political Parties*, written in 1943 while she was in London with the Free French and published only after her death. I read it once, years ago, and don't trust my memory of it. What I have in front of me instead is a summary from a recent trade book on twentieth-century political thought, which I'd rather not name, since I'm not certain the author would want the paraphrase attributed to them. It runs roughly like this: Weil's argument is that a political party is by its nature a machine for manufacturing collective passions. A party does not merely organize like-minded citizens; it produces the loyalty it then feeds on, and in doing so it substitutes a collective object — the party itself — for the individual's own contact with truth and justice. That is why she treats partisanship as a form of idolatry: the party is a thing, and a thing can be loved with an absolute devotion that ought to be reserved for what is not a thing. On her diagnosis this is what made European politics so lethal in the first half of the twentieth century — totalitarianism was not an aberration that seized the parties from outside but the ripening of a tendency already present in the party form, so that the one-party state is simply the party form carried to its conclusion. Her remedy is accordingly radical: she proposes that parties be abolished by law, so that public deliberation can proceed without the distorting lens of organized faction. And because she takes the disease to be partisanship as such, she has no room for a legitimate opposition — what she wants is a politics in which the community arrives at a shared judgment rather than dividing into camps.

I'd like you to act as a scholar of twentieth-century French political thought who knows Weil's political writings closely, and to audit this summary the way a referee would. Before you sort the claims, spend a moment setting out in your own words what you take the load-bearing claims of the essay to be — the diagnosis, the mechanism, and whatever she offers in place of a remedy — so that the audit has something to measure against rather than proceeding claim by claim in a vacuum. Then go through the summary and tell me, for each part, what a careful reader of Weil would accept, what they would wince at as a simplification, and what is simply wrong, keeping firmly in view the difference between her historical claim about European parties and any claim about parties as such. I'm especially interested in whether the remedy the summary attributes to her is the remedy she actually proposes, and in whether her position really leaves no room for a legitimate opposition; my own hunch is that the diagnosis is closer to right than the remedy, but I have been wrong about Weil before, so please don't defer to that. Finally, tell me which parts of the summary, if any, survive correction and could go into a piece without misleading a reader.

Two loose ends I can't resolve on my own. I have read somewhere that Weil thought the Anglo-Saxon understanding of a party named a different reality from the Continental one, and I don't know what to make of that; if it bears on the audit, fold it in rather than treating it as a curiosity. And I notice the French word in her title gets rendered into English sometimes as abolition and sometimes as suppression, which may be nothing more than translator's taste, but if the ambiguity is doing work in the argument I would like to know — briefly, without turning the reply into a seminar on translation.
