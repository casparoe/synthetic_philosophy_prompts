I write long-form essays on politics, science and ideas for a British weekly, and I am at the early stage of a piece about why anyone bothers to vote at all. The peg is a municipal by-election in a ward where turnout scarcely cleared a fifth of the electorate, and I want the essay to be more than the customary lament about apathy: I am after the genuinely unsettled theoretical questions that sit underneath the complaint, because the lament has been written a thousand times and my editor will not take it again. I know the rough shape of the debate well enough — the expected-utility reckoning that makes a single ballot look worthless, the various rescues in terms of expressive or ethical voting, the older social-choice worry about cyclical majorities and agenda control, the thought that rationality might not be a matter of maximising expected utility at all — but what I cannot work out is which of the live questions here deserves the bulk of my word count, and why. I am also aware that the phrase 'the paradox of voting' gets used for at least two quite different things: the puzzle about why an individual turns out when their chance of being decisive is vanishingly small, and the puzzle about how a group's preferences can cycle however scrupulously each member ranks the options. Part of what I need from you is a judgement about whether these are one family of problems or two, and which is the more fertile seam for an essayist who wants to say something that has not already been said.

So please treat this as a prioritisation exercise rather than a survey. Rank the main open questions in this area, in the order you would have me spend my time on them, and defend the ordering explicitly: tell me which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for someone reasoning at a desk rather than running experiments, which are neglected precisely because they look settled, and which would not much change anything downstream even if resolved. I have a half-formed suspicion I would like you to test rather than flatter: a colleague suggested that the whole turnout puzzle only looks hard because we insist on asking whether an individual vote makes a difference, when the real question is whether voting is a practice we have reason to sustain together — but I cannot tell whether that is a solution or merely a change of subject, and I would like you to say which, and to weigh it against the rival reconstructions honestly. I am also curious where the ethics of complicity and collective responsibility fits in: if a great many people staying home elects someone dreadful, what, if anything, does that do to the calculation of the person who stayed home, and is that a question for decision theory, for moral philosophy, or for neither?

Give me the reasoning in full — for each question, why it matters, what depends on it, and what evidence or argument over the next year or two would move it up or down my list — and finish by telling me the two or three I should actually build the piece around, together with the one I am likely to be tempted by and should resist. Please write in British English, and do not give me a reading list or citations; I want your defended agenda, in your own words, not pointers to other people's.
