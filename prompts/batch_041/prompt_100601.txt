Consider a constitutional arrangement in which a court that finds a statute unconstitutional does not thereby remove it from the books. The legislature may re-enact the identical statute; it takes effect; the court may strike it down again — but only after the ordinary litigation cycle has run, so that each round consumes a legislative term and a judicial decade, and the loop may continue indefinitely. Suppose this arrangement is then formalized: a struck-down statute revives automatically if the legislature re-enacts it by supermajority within a fixed window, and it then stands for a fixed term before lapsing unless re-enacted again. Call the resulting institution dialogue by attrition.

What conception of constitutional authority does dialogue by attrition presuppose? Does it commit its defenders to the view that judicial review is advisory rather than binding, that constitutional rights are considerations to be weighed by the political branches rather than trumps against them, that the court's proper function is to force public justification rather than to settle outcomes, or to some fourth thing? Which rival conceptions — judicial supremacy, departmentalism, weak-form review, popular constitutionalism — does the arrangement strictly rule out, and which does it merely happen to fit? Is it internally coherent, or does the same court's simultaneous claim to be enforcing a supreme and entrenched constitution contradict what the practice assumes? Finally, say what would have to change about the institution if we rejected the presupposed view and put in its place either a straightforwardly majoritarian conception of constitutional authority or a straightforwardly legalist one.
