A competent adult signs an advance refusal of treatment — a living will, a do-not-resuscitate order, a written refusal of blood products or of artificial nutrition. The law then gives that refusal the force of a decision made in the present: it binds after capacity is gone, and it binds even when the later, incapacitated patient is placid, cooperative, and would accept the very treatment that was refused. The earlier self's refusal governs the later self. Now the mirror case. A competent adult's written authorization of a harm to be inflicted once they can no longer decide — consent to be killed, to be maimed, to be used as the subject of a serious non-therapeutic experiment — has no comparable standing. Consent to one's own killing is not, by itself, a defense to murder; the handful of jurisdictions that recognize a written advance request for euthanasia treat it as exceptional, contested, and hedged with safeguards, in sharp contrast to the routine advance refusal of life-sustaining treatment. The earlier self, it appears, may veto but may not license.

Treat that asymmetry as the datum, and assume it is not merely an artifact of how the statutes happen to be drafted. I want to know what conception of a person's persistence through time it presupposes, and whether it presupposes one at all. Say whether the asymmetry sits more naturally with endurantism, on which a person is wholly present at each moment and is one and the same thing throughout, or with perdurantism, on which a person is a four-dimensional whole whose earlier and later stages are numerically distinct parts; say what the practice strictly requires as against what it merely accommodates; say whether the two halves of the pattern presuppose the same thing or pull in opposite directions; consider whether the real load is borne by something other than persistence — the inalienability of certain rights, the revocability of consent, the discount we apply to the wishes of the impaired, or the difference between invading and declining to invade — and if so, whether any thesis about identity is doing work at all; and say what would have to change in the practice if we adopted the rival view of persistence, and whether that change would show up in the law itself or only in its rationale.

Answer with exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
