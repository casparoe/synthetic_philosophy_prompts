You are a political theorist with a solid working knowledge of Simone Weil's writing on political parties and of the empirical literature on party systems, and I need you to prepare me for the question period after a talk.

My talk defends a revised Weil. I open by presenting her critique in its strongest form: a party is not primarily a vehicle for this or that policy but an organization whose first commitment is to its own unlimited growth, one that holds its members together by manufacturing a collective passion and exerting pressure on their minds until agreement with the line becomes the test of truth. I then break with her. What she identified, I argue, is not a property of parties as a legal form but a functional property — call it party spirit — that can attach to any body whose members have something to gain from presenting a united front: NGOs, movements, caucuses, editorial boards and online communities can be more party-spirited than the formal parties they despise. If that is right, abolishing parties would be cosmetic. The real task is constitutional design that keeps decision-relevant bodies party-spirit-proof, and I defend three devices: a second chamber chosen by lot, secret and non-transferable voting on questions of conscience, and mandatory rotation for any body that sets the agenda.

The audience is a political theory workshop of six people: two deliberative democrats in the Habermasian mould who take parties to be the main site of public reason; one comparative politics scholar who works on party systems, clientelism and accountability; a guest organizer from a left party who thinks parties are the only instrument the powerless have ever had; a sortition advocate who will find my proposal far too timid; and one Weil specialist. Nobody but the specialist has reread her essay recently, and the room distrusts arguments that lean on a single author's authority.

Prepare my Q&A prep sheet. Begin with a few lines of working-out for yourself — what each of the six is committed to, and what each thinks politics is for — then give me these sections in this order and roughly these lengths: "Where I am exposed" (about 100 words, naming the weakest joint in my thesis); "The objections, ranked" (eight to ten, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, each stated in one sentence in the objector's own vocabulary and attributed to the right person, then a two-minute reply and a line on where that reply is soft — roughly 120 words each); "What I concede before I am asked" (bullets, about 120 words); "The replies that have to land" (the two or three that decide the session, with why they matter, about 200 words); and "The question I am hoping nobody asks" (one question, with the honest answer, about 150 words). Think carefully about the difference between an objection that would be decisive in print and one that will actually be voiced in the room, and give me no generic seminar objections that any audience could have raised — each one should be an objection only this person would make.
