An argument I assembled while working through the charity constraint in radical interpretation, and which I now can't decide whether to keep or abandon. (1) You cannot interpret a speaker's utterances without assigning meanings to her words and beliefs to her in the same breath; there is no way to fix the meaning of a word first and then ask separately what she believes. (2) Because meaning and belief are fixed together, interpretation must credit the speaker with beliefs that are mostly true and reasoning that is mostly sound; a speaker we could not so credit would be a speaker we could not interpret at all. (3) So the beliefs of anyone we can interpret are mostly true. (4) So someone whose beliefs were mostly false could not be interpreted. (5) So a community that appears to be systematically mistaken about some subject matter is not: either we have misidentified what its words mean, or we have misdescribed what it believes. (6) So where two interpretable communities disagree wholesale about a subject matter, there is no fact of the matter about which of them is right — only a difference in what their words mean. (7) But there is a fact of the matter. Those who explained combustion by phlogiston, or illness by possession, were wrong; and wrong in what they believed, not merely speaking a different language. (8) Therefore the charity constraint, taken as a constitutive requirement on interpretation rather than as a useful heuristic, has to be rejected.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would rather keep (1) and (2) — I think interpretation is charitable through and through, not merely charitable when convenient — and I cannot accept (6). Tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis, and single out the point in it you are least sure of.
