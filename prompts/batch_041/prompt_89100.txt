Analyze the European and American practice of dueling — the code of honor under which a gentleman who was insulted issued or accepted a challenge, met his opponent under agreed terms, and staked his life to vindicate his standing — as a case of costly signaling under the handicap principle. Make the mapping explicit: what the willingness to fight is meant to advertise, to whom, and why the real risk of death rather than a cheap display of outrage does the advertising work. Then apply the condition the theory actually imposes, which is easy to state and easy to overlook: a handicap must be differentially costly, cheaper for those who genuinely have the quality being advertised and dearer for those who do not, so that faking does not pay. Say whether the duel meets that condition or whether the risk is roughly the same for everyone, in which case the practice may be a convention that only resembles a handicap; address the common observation that many duels ended without a death. Finally, tell me what the case shows about the theory itself — a confirmation, a strain, or an anomaly it never anticipated — assuming the historical facts are as commonly reported rather than resting on any single account.
