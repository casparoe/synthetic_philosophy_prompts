Question. Set out the Dutch book argument for probabilism, say what it does and does not establish about the rationality of our degrees of belief, and identify a standard objection to it and assess it.

Student's answer. The Dutch book argument begins from the idea, associated with Ramsey and de Finetti, that an agent's degrees of belief are revealed by the odds at which she is prepared to bet. If her degrees of belief violate the axioms of probability, a bookmaker can offer her a collection of bets, each of which is fair by her own lights, whose combined result is a loss for her whatever happens. That is a Dutch book. Since a rational agent would not accept a collection of bets that guarantees her a loss, her degrees of belief must satisfy the axioms; probabilism is therefore a requirement of rationality. Note that the bookmaker need not know anything the agent does not, since the bets are built from her own odds, and no deception is needed. The sure loss is a matter of logic rather than chance, because the payoffs are arranged to cancel out in every state of the world. What the argument exposes is that the agent's credences are internally contradictory: she is committed to betting on both sides of a proposition at once. The usual objection is the depragmatic objection, namely that people do not in fact spend their lives making bets, so the fact that an incoherent agent would lose money in a betting situation says nothing about whether her beliefs are rational. This objection seems to me to fail. The argument is not a prediction that you will be exploited; it is a demonstration that your credences violate a consistency requirement, and consistency is a requirement of rationality whether or not anyone ever bets.
