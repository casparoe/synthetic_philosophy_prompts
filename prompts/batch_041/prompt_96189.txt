I'm writing up my position on dispositions for a reading group this week and I want a consistency check on it before I open my mouth. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) Fragility is intrinsic. Two glasses alike in every intrinsic respect are equally fragile, even if one is wrapped in foam and the other is bare on the bench.

(2) What makes "this glass is fragile" true is a fact about what would happen: it would break if you struck it, absent interference.

(3) On the cases: a foam-wrapped glass is still fragile even though it wouldn't break if struck. A glass that a sorcerer would un-fragile the instant you struck it is still fragile, and still wouldn't break. A dud that isn't fragile but would turn fragile on impact, and so shatter, is not fragile, even though it would break if struck. And I don't think it matters whether the interfering thing is inside the object or outside it — a glass whose own microstructure would anneal on impact seems just as possible as the sorcerer version.

(4) I don't buy the usual patch of adding "and it stays fragile" to the antecedent of the counterfactual. That's circular: it assumes the very disposition it's supposed to be analysing.

(5) So dispositions are irreducible. They're real properties of things, not reducible to counterfactual facts or to categorical ones.

(6) But they're not ungrounded: fragility is grounded in the glass's microstructure together with the laws, and if you knew both you'd know the glass was fragile.

Do these hang together? I know some of them are pulling in different directions but I can't tell which.
