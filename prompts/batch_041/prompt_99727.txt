I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month I am leading a forty-five-minute salon at a literary and ideas festival on how conventions harden into norms — and why they so often outlive the coordination problems that first made them useful. The audience will be educated non-specialists: a few will have read some philosophy, most will not, and a handful will be policy people who care about norm change. I want you to plan the explanation for me, not deliver it. I am not asking for a reading list, citations, or a survey of the literature; I need a teaching sequence.

The intellectual spine I want to build on is the account of convention associated with David Lewis: a regularity in behavior that solves a recurrent coordination problem, where everyone prefers to conform given that others conform, and where alternative regularities would have worked just as well. But I want the talk to end somewhere Lewis does not quite take it. A researcher I have been interviewing describes what she calls normative lag: a regularity that began as a Lewis-style solution to a coordination problem can persist after the original problem has vanished, and can then acquire the feel of an independent obligation — people follow it, expect others to follow it, and resent deviation, even when no one's interests are being coordinated by it anymore. I want the audience to understand both the original mechanism and this drift from convention to norm.

Please give me a plan with the following elements. First, the order in which the ideas must be introduced, with an explicit account of what depends on what; tell me where the audience will be tempted to jump ahead and why that jump would break the argument. Second, the single carrying example that should do most of the work. I am tempted by the old standbys — driving on one side of the road, or standing on the right of an escalator — but if one of those is the wrong choice, say so and tell me what should replace it, what features the replacement must have, and what it must avoid. Third, the misconceptions you expect this audience to bring or form: for instance, that a Lewis convention is just a shared habit, that it requires an explicit agreement or a promise, that common knowledge is merely mutual belief, that norms are simply conventions plus punishment, or that normative lag is a story about biological evolution. Tell me where in the sequence each misconception is most likely to appear and exactly how I should head it off. Fourth, one question I can put to the room near the end that would show whether they have grasped the difference between a coordination equilibrium and a norm with independent normative force, rather than merely memorized the vocabulary. Fifth, what I should deliberately leave out at this level — the formal apparatus, the infinite hierarchy of common knowledge, the debates about whether Lewis's account is reductive — and how I should signal that omission without making the audience feel cheated.

I would like the plan to be concrete enough that I can rehearse from it, and honest about the places where the audience is likely to resist. Where a term is unavoidable but potentially misleading, tell me whether to keep it, replace it, or define it on the spot.
