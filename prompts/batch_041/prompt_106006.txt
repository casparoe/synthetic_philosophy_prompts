I'm running a book club session on Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day and I want to read it through Nietzsche's On the Use and Abuse of History for Life, the 1874 essay where he splits up monumental, antiquarian and critical history and worries about what too much history does to a person who still has to live. My hunch is that Stevens is a walking case of the first two and a complete failure at the third: he reveres his father and the whole idea of the great butler as a monumental image to live up to, he polishes Darlington Hall and its standards as something to be conserved and handed down even after the man himself is ruined, and the one move he can never make is to sit in judgement on Lord Darlington or on his own wasted years, which is exactly what the critical mode is for. But please don't just slot him into three boxes and call it a day. Nietzsche also thinks an excess of history can paralyse you, make you feel like a latecomer with nothing left of your own to do, and he clearly thinks a certain capacity for forgetting is what lets anyone act at all, so Stevens deciding on the seafront bench to stop dwelling on the past and work on his bantering with Mr Farraday can be read either as choosing life over history or as one more piece of self-deception, and I want a real answer on which reading the book supports. Also the essay is about a whole culture and its health, not one man's soul, so tell me honestly where the analogy breaks down — he's a butler, not a nation, and Nietzsche's remedies for a sick historical culture aren't obviously available to him. And I'd like to know where the novel does something the essay can't: the 1923 conference and the secret meeting, his father dying upstairs while he keeps serving, the evening Miss Kenton tries to tell him about the proposal, the two housemaids Lord Darlington has sacked, the lawsuit that finishes him off. Give me two or three sharpest points of contact plus the one place where the reading has to give up, in a form I can actually use as a twenty-minute discussion opener — and no, I don't need you to quote anything, I just want the argument.
