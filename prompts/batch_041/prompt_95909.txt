I have been arguing about this with two friends all term — one a programmer whose side projects keep getting cloned, the other a lawyer who thinks the very idea of owning an idea is a category mistake — and I have finally written down where my gut lands on a pile of cases, in the hope that you can tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. Two mathematicians prove the same theorem in the same year; the one who publishes second, having never seen the first paper, still deserves full credit for it, and saying so takes nothing away from the first. A comedian retells a rival's joke with a two-word change and no nod to the source: that is theft, even though nobody supposes comedians own jokes. A chef recreates a rival restaurant's signature dish: fine, and I feel no tug at all, though I notice it is the same move as the comedian's. A firm isolates the active compound from a plant that a community has used medicinally for generations and patents it: the patent is an injustice, yet if the same firm engineers a new molecule merely inspired by that compound, the patent is fine. A student drops a paragraph from a published paper into her draft without a citation: not fine. A company buys a rival's unpatented device, takes it apart, and sells a cheaper copy: fine, and again this is the chef's move. Someone describes a startup idea to a friend over drinks; the friend builds it: not fine, and this one strikes me as worse than the joke, even though less seems to have been taken. Fan fiction posted for free: fine. The same fan fiction sold as a paperback: I want to say not fine, but I cannot get that verdict to sit still next to the chef. Reimplementing an algorithm from a published paper without asking: fine. Copying a decade of someone's painstakingly assembled factual database: not fine, though I cannot say what is owned there, since no individual fact in it belongs to anyone.

The tentative line I have been defending is that you have a claim on an idea just in case you are the one who made it costly for others to have — the labour view, the sweat-of-the-brow idea — but that plainly gets the joke, the friend's startup, and the reverse-engineered device wrong, and in different directions. So perhaps there are two principles rather than one: one governing who gets credit, another governing who may use. If so, say which of my cases fall under which, and what fixes the boundary between them.

I would also like to know what your principle, or principles, predict about a case I cannot place at all: a large model trained on a living author's published novels which then writes a novel in her manner and sells it, where nothing is copied sentence by sentence and no reader is misled about who wrote it. And if the honest answer is that no single principle saves all of the verdicts above, tell me which verdicts I should abandon rather than which clause to bolt on. Keep the whole reply under 700 words, and lead with the principle or principles in two or three sentences before you go case by case.
