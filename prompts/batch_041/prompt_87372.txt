I keep losing arguments to a single sentence: you cannot repeal the law of supply and demand. It arrives at the end of a discussion and functions as a full stop, and I have never once seen anyone spell out what it is supposed to be asserting. I would like to know what it can assert.

Two recent occasions, because the design details seem to matter to whether the sentence even applies. The first was a tenants' association meeting where a member opposed a proposed rent stabilization ordinance on these grounds. The ordinance would cap annual rent increases by an index tied to a regional inflation measure, but would include vacancy decontrol (a unit drops out of the cap when it turns over), an exemption for buildings first permitted after the ordinance takes effect, and a hardship waiver for owners below a certain portfolio size. The second was a city council comment period on a minimum wage ordinance that phases in over four years, permits a tip credit, and exempts firms below a headcount threshold.

What I want is a menu, not a lecture. Set out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the nearly empty to the nearly cosmological: for instance, a claim about how people behave when a price is held away from its clearing level; a claim about what we have defined a demand curve to be; a claim about the direction of adjustment as opposed to its magnitude, timing, or incidence; a claim that no institutional design can blunt the adjustment; and a claim that the adjustment is itself a reason the policy ought not to be enacted. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive claim worth defending, or simply false, and say which reading you think the people who use the sentence usually mean.

Then tell me which readings actually bear on the three design features of each ordinance I described above, and which readings would have to be true for the sentence to show that either ordinance is self-defeating. I am equally interested in the reverse: which readings are entirely compatible with the ordinances doing what their sponsors say they will do, so that the sentence, on that reading, is not an argument against them at all.

Please argue from mechanism and from what would have to be true, rather than from the empirical literature: I will be reading this offline and cannot check named studies or authors, so do not lean on them.

Structure the response as follows, with these headings in this order:
- "What the sentence was doing" — roughly 100 words, reconstructing the role the sentence played in each of the two arguments.
- "Readings, trivial to radical" — the menu, roughly 600 words, numbered, each entry a one-sentence formulation followed by your verdict label.
- "Which readings carry the arguments" — roughly 250 words, mapping readings onto the specific ordinance features and saying plainly whether either conclusion survives.
- "Where this is most likely to be wrong" — roughly 150 words, identifying the point in your reasoning that is weakest and stating what evidence or argument would change your verdict on whether the sentence settles either case.
