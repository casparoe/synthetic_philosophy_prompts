So my reading group has been going round in circles for about four months on how you're even supposed to study consciousness, and I want to force the two loudest people to write something together. Here's the standoff. One of them is a heterophenomenologist in the Dennett mould: all you ever get is what subjects say, write and do, and the intellectually honest move is to take those reports as raw material for a "heterophenomenological world" -- a bit like the world of a novel, which you can study in as much detail as you like without ever having to decide whether the subject's experience really was the way they describe it. The other is a first-person person: reports, if the reporter is trained and you cross-check against physiology and behaviour, are data about experience itself; and she says the heterophenomenologist is quietly smuggling in a deflationary theory of the subject -- no inner theatre, self as a useful fiction -- while claiming to be methodologically neutral.

Write the joint statement the two of them could actually put their names to. Ground rules: no bibliography, no reading list, no "as X argued in Y" -- neither of us can check that and you can't either, so every claim has to stand on its own argument. And I definately don't want you to just say "both methods have a role" and call it a day; I want to see exactly where the two positions collide, in wording each side would actually sign.

One thing I want you to build on, because its the only way we've got anywhere in the group. Split what's going on in any experiment into four seperate things: (i) the experience, if there is one; (ii) the subject's belief about it; (iii) the subject's report of it; (iv) the experimenter's model of the subject. Then for each link between them, say whether both camps think it's reliable, and how you'd find out. Use that scaffold throughout, and make the two sides say something concrete about the links they disagree on.

Sections, in this order, with rough lengths:

1. "What we both think the method is for" -- 150-200 words, no hedging, no history of the field.
2. "Agreed claims" -- 8 to 12 numbered claims, each one sentence, each one something both sign. At least two should be substantive rather than platitudes (about what a report is evidence of, say, or about whether neutrality is achievable, or about what training does to a reporter).
3. "Where we part" -- a table, one row per disagreement, columns: The question / Our wording (side A) / Our wording (side B) / What actually turns on it. Every wording has to be one that side would sign, so no strawmen and no "some might say".
4. "Cruxes" -- 4 to 6 numbered cruxes. For each: the sub-question; why settling it would move at least one side; the case, experiment or argument both sides agree bears on it; side A's prediction, written so it could turn out false; side B's prediction, same. At least two cruxes should be things we could in principle run. If a crux can't be tested, say so and say why it still matters.
5. "Word games" -- which of the disagreements are really about terminology, and what the disambiguated wording is. If none are, say so and say why.
6. "What would move us" -- each side names the one result or argument that would change its mind, and the one it would just explain away.
7. "Unsigned" -- the one or two things neither will sign, and what it would take to get there.

Aim for 1200-1600 words total. Write it in the first person plural wherever both can say it, and switch to "we, the first-person side..." / "we, the heterophenomenology side..." only in sections 3, 4 and 6.
