I run HR at a company of about 300 people, and a few times a month I have to figure out whether someone actually did a thing with their words. Not the legal side -- our lawyers handle what's enforceable, and I'm not asking you for employment law. I mean the prior question: did the act even get performed? A guy stands up in a team meeting, says "I'm done, I'm out," walks off, and is back at his desk Monday morning. Did he resign? A manager tells someone "we'll make this right" about a bonus that never comes. Was that a promise? Someone says "if you schedule me for another Saturday I'm gonna lose it." Threat, warning, or just venting? And the one that drives me up the wall: "I'm sorry if anyone felt uncomfortable." Is that an apology or not, and does it change anything whether she meant it?

My instinct is that some of these get settled by the words and the setup -- who's in a position to do this, whether the form was followed, whether anyone was there to hear it -- and some get settled by what was going on in the person's head. But I keep getting those two backwards. The "I'm out" guy obviously meant it and I still don't think he resigned. The fake-sounding apology was insincere as hell and I still think she apologized. Meanwhile I've noticed you can say "I warn you that this will happen" but not "I threaten you that this will happen," which seems like it should definately matter and I can't say why.

So: write me a decision procedure I can actually run, in order, when a manager brings me one of these. What do I ask first, what counts as a real answer at each step, where does it stop with a hard no, and where do I stop pretending and hand it to a human? It has to fit in about twenty minutes, with no philosophy degree, and be something I can defend to my boss and to a lawyer if it comes to that. I dont care who said what about it -- no reading list, just the thing itself.

Then run it on the four cases above plus one more you pick that's likely to break it. And tell me which steps are real distinctions and which are me dressing up a gut call, and what the whole procedure can't do.
