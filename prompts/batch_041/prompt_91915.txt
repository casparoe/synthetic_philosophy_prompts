So I'm covering for a friend who teaches the ethics unit in our MFA program — long story — and I've got a stack of take-home answers to grade against this question she set. I'm a novelist, not a philosphy person, and I honestly can't tell whether this one is good or just sounds good. The question was:

"We said in class that a compromise between two people who value different things can be worth making in the way a trade is worth making: each gives up something they care about less in exchange for something they care about more, so both can end up better off than if they'd simply refused to deal. A student then asked whether that picture secretly assumes the two sets of values can be measured on a common scale. Explain what the 'trade' picture of compromise actually requires, and say whether a compromise between people whose values are genuinely incommensurable can still be a gain for both. Illustrate with the publisher and the author fighting over a novel's ending — the author wants it bleak, the publisher wants it hopeful."

Here's what the student turned in:

"The trade picture doesn't need a common scale. All it needs is that each party can rank whole packages of what they might end up with — bleak ending, hopeful ending, or some mixture — and that at least one package be better for each of them than the no-deal outcome. That's an ordinal ranking, not a measurement, and each ranking can be private to whoever is doing it. So incommensurability is no obstacle: the author doesn't need to know how many units of artistic integrity a happy ending costs, she only needs to prefer one package to another. Incommensurable values can't be added up, but they can still be traded. On that reading the fight is a classic bargaining problem — the deal is a Pareto improvement over the stalemate, so both have reason to take it, and where they land between the two ideal endings is just a question of leverage.

What this also shows is that the two of them share a common currency after all: if there were really no common measure between artistic integrity and commercial viability, there'd be nothing for them to trade, so the trade only works because the values turn out to be commensurable underneath. And since the compromise is better for both than no deal, it's the outcome they should both prefer, which means the author has no grounds for complaint if she ends up with the hopeful ending, seeing as she's still better off than in the stalemate. The only real obstacle here is irrationality — people who can't rank their own options, or who let pride get in the way."
