A gamble can have no expected value at all: when its probability-weighted payoffs are not absolutely summable, their sum depends on the order in which they are added, and one and the same set of outcome-probability pairs can be made to add up to different amounts. I want a compact case of exactly this kind, built to order rather than recalled, for a unit on whether rational choice requires a ranking of gambles whose possible payoffs never run out.

Design a gamble specified by an explicit procedure — a fair coin tossed until it first lands heads, with the dollar payoff fixed by the toss on which it lands, terminating with probability 1 — together with one sure payoff M, meeting all of the following. (a) Every finite truncation of the gamble, taking only the first N outcomes, has an ordinary expected value, and those truncation values converge to a finite limit L different from M. (b) Summing the probability-weighted payoffs in the order in which the tosses would actually resolve also yields L, so a theory that values a gamble by that limit, and likewise anyone who merely approximates the gamble by long finite truncations, ranks the gamble strictly against M. (c) The very same probability-weighted payoffs admit other orderings whose sums run to any real number, and to plus and minus infinity, so a theory holding that a gamble's value is fixed by its outcome-probability pairs rather than by the order of summation must deny that the gamble has a value and call it incomparable to M. (d) Nothing is smuggled in: every outcome has positive probability, no payoff is infinite, all the events are measurable, and the trouble comes only from the size of the payoffs, not from a nonzero probability of never stopping.

Then do the bookkeeping. State each theory's verdict on the pair and the exact step at which they part; name the single structural feature doing the work; say what the case collapses to once that feature is removed by making the series absolutely summable, and why the disagreement then vanishes; add a third rule that values the gamble by the supremum of what its probability-weighted payoffs can be made to sum to, and say what it delivers and whether it helps or hurts the limit theory; and finish with how a defender of the incomparability verdict would attack the case and how a defender of the limit verdict would reply. Define each theory by its rule, and do not cite any literature.
