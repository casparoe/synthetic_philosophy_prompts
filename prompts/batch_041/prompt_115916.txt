I'm building a session for an undergraduate seminar on cities and political philosophy — the week on exclusion — and I've decided to teach Lorraine Hansberry's A Raisin in the Sun alongside Bruce Norris's Clybourne Park, the later play that returns to the same Chicago house, first in 1959 and then in 2009. What I want from you is a reading of the pair through the idea of a right to the city: Henri Lefebvre's late-1960s thought that inhabitants hold a collective claim to appropriate and shape urban space, as against the conception of a city as a set of parcels held by owners who may decide who joins them. My students will know the plot of Raisin well enough — the insurance money from Lena's husband's death, the house in Clybourne Park, Karl Lindner's offer, on behalf of the neighborhood association, to buy it back, the family's refusal, the move at the end — and some of them will know that Hansberry's own family bought into a white Chicago neighborhood and ended up in a restrictive-covenant case that reached the Supreme Court. What I don't want is another essay on racism in housing. I want the philosophical structure of the situation.

Work through a tension I keep running into, and which a colleague in the law school tells me is the sign that the whole right-to-the-city tradition is confused. Every version of the claim I find persuasive turns out to be, at bottom, a right to stay put: against displacement, against being priced out of a place that is yours because you live there. But Lindner's case is also, in its own idiom, a claim to stay put — he is not threatening the Youngers, he is offering them money so that the neighborhood can remain what it has been — while the Youngers' claim is a claim to move in. So one premise appears to generate both the exclusion and the objection to it, and everything hangs on who counts as an inhabitant with standing and on how that boundary got drawn, given that every such boundary was at some earlier moment somebody's act of exclusion. I want to know whether the two plays dramatize this symmetry knowingly, and whether there is a way out of it that is more than a decision about whose claim wins. Things I'd like brought in: the difference between housing as a place to live and housing as an asset whose value depends on keeping the wrong people out, so that a neighbor's equity is partly a claim on the exclusion; the fact that Lindner's constituents stand to lose something real rather than merely imagined, which is what makes him a more interesting antagonist than a bigot would be; and the notion, from Charles Tilly, of opportunity hoarding — advantage secured not by overt discrimination but by monopolizing access to a valuable position, here a block, a school catchment, a zoning variance. Note, too, that Raisin is not obviously a right-to-the-city play at all: its aspiration is a house of one's own, bought with a windfall, and its resolution is one family's ascent rather than any collective claim on the city, which may mean the play quietly accepts the property logic that produced Clybourne Park in the first place. Norris's second act, where the neighborhood has become Black and a white couple's plans to enlarge the house are resisted by the residents who were once the newcomers, is the obvious place to test whether the inversion exposes the symmetry or merely swaps the parties.

Write this as criticism rather than as an argument for a thesis: stay close to scenes and characters, tell me where the lens genuinely illuminates something — I expect the ending, and Lena's decisions about the money, to look different once the house is treated as a claim on a place rather than as a piece of property — and tell me where the plays resist the reading and I should stop pushing. I am not asking for a verdict on whether Hansberry was a good urbanist, and I don't need sources, a reading list, or citations; I know the secondary literature exists and would rather have the reading itself. What I want to end with is what these two plays together see about who has a right to a city that the philosophical literature tends to miss, and what the literature sees that the plays cannot show.
