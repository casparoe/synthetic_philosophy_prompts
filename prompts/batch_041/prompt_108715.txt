I've been trying to understand a puzzle about overwork that keeps splitting into three different pictures of what work is for. In France, the working week is capped at thirty-five hours for most employees, and there is a legal right not to answer work messages outside working hours; the state seems to treat leisure as something a person is owed and the employer as the party who must be restrained. In Japan, the state pays compensation to the families of people who have died from overwork, once the overtime has passed a rough threshold that is treated as establishing the causal link between the job and the death; there the harm is real enough to be compensated, and the employer is the one who pays for it. But in the United States, the same law that guarantees overtime pay to most hourly workers exempts salaried employees whose duties involve discretion and independent judgment — managers, professionals, and the like — so that an employer may lawfully require an exempt worker to put in eighty or ninety hours a week. That exemption seems to rest on the idea that a person who directs his own work does not need to be protected from it, whereas the karoshi rules seem to rest on the opposite idea, since many of the dead were salaried and self-directed.

I would like you to reconstruct the view of work, freedom, and the person that these arrangements presuppose. Why does the law protect some workers from their own willingness to overwork and not others? Does it assume that the harm of overwork is unfreedom rather than exhaustion or death, so that a freely chosen eighty-hour week is no harm at all? Or does it assume something about who is competent to judge his own interests, so that the professional's choice is treated as authoritative and the factory worker's is not? And how should the Japanese practice be read: if a worker voluntarily, even proudly, works himself to death, does compensating the family presuppose that the work was not truly voluntary after all, or only that the employer had a duty to stop it? I have also been reading about the old idea that work is a calling, which makes diligence a virtue and idleness a vice, and I cannot tell whether that idea supports these rules, undercuts them, or is simply irrelevant to them.

I am not a lawyer or a philosopher — I manage a small team and have been talking this over with a friend in Tokyo and another who is a nurse in Ohio — so I would like the implicit theory laid out plainly. Please tell me which commitments these arrangements strictly require, which they merely sit comfortably with, and where they are underdetermined, so that several quite different pictures of work and leisure would license exactly the same rules. And if the law dropped the autonomy test and simply treated hours as hours, what would have to change in how we think about the meaning of work, the value of leisure, and the difference between a calling and a job?
