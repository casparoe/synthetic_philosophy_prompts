'Newton's bucket is the experiment that settles the big debate about space. Spin a bucket of water: at first the bucket turns and the water just sits there; a bit later the water is turning with the bucket and its surface has gone concave, climbing up the sides. Since the water climbs even when it isn't moving relative to the bucket, the climb can't be a relation between the water and the bucket -- it has to be motion relative to space itself. So space must be a real thing, not just a way of talking about where bodies sit relative to one another. Leibniz had said space is nothing over and above the relations between things, and Mach later said the same about motion, adding that the water is really rotating relative to the distant stars; but Newton's point is that you can imagine the bucket all alone in an otherwise empty universe and the climb still happens, so there's nothing for it to be relative to except absolute space. Which is why Einstein's general relativity is standardly taken to vindicate Leibniz and Mach after all: in GR there is no absolute space, just the metric field describing relations among events, and the bucket's behaviour falls out of the water's rotation relative to the surrounding matter.'
