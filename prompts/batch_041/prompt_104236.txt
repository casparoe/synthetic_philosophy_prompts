I am a philosophy professor preparing the final examination for a cross-listed upper-level seminar on value, comparison, and rational choice. I need one long essay question on the transitivity of betterness and the spectrum arguments that have been pressed against it, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. I have drafted the question myself and built the difficult cases into it, so that students cannot pass by reciting the stock population-ethics examples; keep my cases and do not substitute famous ones. Finalize the wording of the question, then write the model answer and the marking scheme.

DRAFT QUESTION (75 minutes; 30 marks)

Throughout, treat better than as meaning better all things considered, and treat indifferent as meaning neither better nor worse.

Transitivity is usually taken to be constitutive of betterness: if A is better than B, and B is better than C, then A is better than C. Spectrum arguments are supposed to show that we cannot keep transitivity together with certain other judgments we will not give up. Consider two sequences.

Case I: The Long Conversation. C0 is a one-minute conversation with a kind but tedious relative; it is mildly pleasant. Each Ck+1 lasts one minute longer than Ck, and the added minute is only imperceptibly more tedious than the minute before it, so that, judged on its own, Ck+1 seems neither better nor worse than Ck. C600, ten hours long, is clearly much worse than C0.

Case II: The Escalator. O0 is a life of extraordinary richness — deep relationships, absorbing work, aesthetic pleasure — marred by one hour of discomfort each day. Each Ok+1 trades a small amount of richness for a small reduction in discomfort, in a proportion that, judged on its own, makes the life slightly better overall; you would accept each trade. By On, the life is bland but comfortable: almost no richness, no discomfort. You judge O0 clearly better than On.

(a) Set out the general form of a spectrum argument and identify the judgments that cannot all be true. For each case, say what a defender of transitivity must give up, and what a critic of transitivity must give up instead. (8 marks)

(b) The two cases are said to fail transitivity in different ways. Distinguish intransitive indifference — the strict betterness relation remains transitive while indifference does not, as with just-noticeable differences, a structure sometimes called a semiorder — from intransitive strict betterness, in which betterness itself cycles. Say which case threatens which, and explain the formal consequence: which of the two failures is compatible with representing the ranking by a real-valued measure of value, or by an interval-valued one (a numerical score paired with a threshold, so that items whose scores fall within the threshold of each other count as indifferent), and which is not. (8 marks)

(c) Assess the principal responses. Consider at least: denying the stepwise judgments; accepting that betterness is intransitive; locating the trouble in vagueness or in incommensurability rather than in intransitivity; positing a fourth comparative relation, parity, holding between items that are neither better, nor worse, nor equal; and holding that the cycle in Case II is an artifact of aggregating several dimensions of value — as when majority voting over dimensions yields a cycle — rather than a feature of overall betterness. (10 marks)

(d) Take a position. State which response you would adopt, what it costs you, and whether an agent whose betterness relation cycles can be exploited by a sequence of trades returning her to her starting point minus a fee, and whether that possibility is decisive against your response. (4 marks)

REQUIREMENTS

Model answer: roughly 900 words, written as a strong student would write it, in continuous prose organized by parts (a) through (d). It should demonstrate command of the material, not survey it.

Marking scheme: for each part, the specific moves a top answer must make, with the marks each is worth, summing to exactly 30; then a short list of misunderstandings that should cost marks, each attached to a part; then two or three sentences on what separates an A answer from a B answer. State each must-hit point as a checkable claim, so that a grader who is not a specialist can apply it.

CONSTRAINTS

The examination is closed-book and the students have no internet access. Nothing in the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme may depend on a citation, a title, a date, or a named author; do not name philosophers, describe the positions instead. No bibliographies or reading lists. Do not quote any text; the only quotation marks should be around terms of art. Gloss any technical notion inside the question itself, as I have done with semiorder, parity, and the interval-valued measure, so that the exam is self-contained. Write everything in American English.
