I convene a graduate seminar in metanormative theory, and this year the final is a single essay question. The unit that consistently gives my students the most trouble is the family of indirect views — those that locate the standard of rightness neither in the agent's actual attitudes nor in a list of first-order duties, but in what an idealized version of the agent would endorse. My students can recite the stock positions fluently, which is exactly the problem: I want a question that tests whether they can work a version of the view that appears in no textbook and locate its seams. So I have constructed a view for the exam, the Corrected-Concern View (CCV), and I would like the question built around it. CCV holds that an action is right for an agent just in case that agent's ideal counterpart would endorse it, where the counterpart (i) possesses all empirical information relevant to the agent's situation, (ii) deliberates without any time limit, and (iii) is perfectly coherent, in the sense that her attitudes satisfy the usual constraints of logical and means-end consistency — but where (iv) her fundamental concerns are held fixed, being simply the agent's actual fundamental concerns, whatever those happen to be. CCV is thus a deliberately thin idealization on the conative side and a robust one on the epistemic side.

Please draft the exam question itself, together with a model answer of roughly 600 to 700 words and a marking scheme out of 20. The question should require candidates to determine whether CCV escapes what I have been calling the dilemma of idealization: the worry that an indirect view either idealizes too little, so that the ideal counterpart's endorsement is merely a prediction of what would satisfy the agent's actual concerns and the idealization does no independent normative work — the view collapsing into a form of informed-desire subjectivism — or idealizes too much, so that the counterpart is no longer recognizably the agent and her endorsement lacks authority over him, which is the alienation horn. Candidates should have to say which horn CCV in fact falls on and why, propose the minimal modification, if any, that would move it toward the space between the horns, and defend a verdict on whether that space can be occupied at all. I also want the question to force them to confront the self-effacing character of indirect views: if CCV is correct, its own criterion may be one that the ideal counterpart would endorse the agent's *not* deliberating by, and candidates should have to say whether that is a defect or a consequence to be embraced.

For the marking scheme, I would like five must-hit points, four marks each, each accompanied by a one-line note on how candidates typically get that move wrong — and please separate a genuinely top answer from a merely competent one by naming the specific moves the former must contain. Constraints to state for the candidates: sixty minutes, closed book, and no citations required or expected.
