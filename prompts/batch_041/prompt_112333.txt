Next month I am presenting a paper at our formal metaphysics and philosophical logic reading group, and what I need from you is not a defence of the paper but a preparation sheet for the question period.

The paper argues that Gödel's modal ontological argument is caught in a dilemma. On the first horn, the primitive notion of a positive property is left unconstrained: the derivation runs, but the axioms are strong enough to license the modal collapse — the result that nothing is contingent, so that every truth is necessary — and strong enough to generate the familiar parody proofs, so the machinery proves too much to prove anything. On the second horn, positivity is constrained enough to block those results, which requires giving up the axiom that for every property, exactly one of it and its negation is positive; but then the possibility of a God-like being can no longer be derived from the axioms alone and has to be assumed, and it is an assumption that only someone already persuaded has reason to grant. I then offer a repair of my own, which I expect to be the main target: I distinguish a formal notion of positivity, on which a property counts as positive when it is not the privation of some more basic property and when positivity is closed under entailment, from an axiological notion, on which a property counts as positive when its instantiation makes its bearer better than the lack of it would. My claim is that the derivation needs the formal notion at the steps that carry the proof and the axiological notion only where positivity is tied to value, and that both the collapse and the parodies come from running the two together. I add that even if the repair works, the conclusion is the necessary existence of a maximally valuable something, and that nothing in the repaired derivation secures that it is a person, an agent, or a knower, since the positivity of personhood is just the kind of axiological premise the repair gives up.

The audience is specific and I have no allies in it. There are two modal logicians: one works on quantified modal logic and holds that S5 is simply not the logic of metaphysical modality and that accessibility must not be treated as an equivalence relation; the other is a proof theorist who will ask which steps survive in a free logic and whether anything depends on the domain of quantification being non-empty. There is a philosopher of religion who is an atheist and has spent years on parody arguments and on the charge that the axioms are ad hoc. There is a Christian philosophical theologian committed to divine simplicity, who thinks a being assembled out of a maximal collection of positive properties is a category mistake, and who for his own reasons resists the collapse objection, since the same objection is used against simplicity. Finally there is a philosopher of mathematics who would rather recast the whole thing in terms of definable classes than treat properties as modal primitives.

Give me the prep sheet. First, the objections I will actually get from this room, ordered by how likely each is combined with how much damage it does, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it — the logicians in proof-theoretic terms, the theologian in the vocabulary of classical theism — and not in generic philosophy-of-religion terms. For each, a reply I could actually deliver in two minutes, written as prose I can say aloud rather than as bullet points, followed by a note on where that reply is soft and what the objector will press next, and a judgement on whether I should concede, hold, or reframe. Second, the concessions I should make in my opening remarks, before anyone has to extract them from me, and what each concession costs me. Third, the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Keep it to eight objections at most; I have twenty-five minutes.

I want a committed answer rather than a survey of positions. Tell me which of these objections genuinely damages the argument and which of my replies is a rationalisation; if you think the formal and axiological notions of positivity cannot be held apart without equivocation, say so plainly and tell me which horn I should retreat to; and if you think the dilemma is false because the two horns are not exhaustive, identify the third option and defend it. I do not need citations, names, or a reading list — the room knows the literature, and I want your reasoning, not a bibliography.
