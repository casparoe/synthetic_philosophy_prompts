Ok, this one's a bit personal so bear with me. I've just been put on our town's heritage and planning advisory committee (dont ask) and in three meetings I've now watched "that's Chesterton's fence, surely" kill the discussion twice, and once get used to defend a leftover bylaw that turned out to be strangling the only bus route to the hospital — nobody, including the guy who originally drafted it, could tell us what it was for. So I've become weirdly obsessed with when that move is a real argument and when it's just a way of not having the argument.

I teach the upper-level seminar on conservatism and its critics, and the midterm is a single 60-minute essay worth 25 marks. Please write me that essay question, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme. Take the role of someone who's taught Burke for fifteen years and has also read enough decision theory to be annoying about it. Think carefully before you commit: brainstorm two or three candidate cruxes for the question first, then pick the one that best seperates students who've actually understood the argument from students who've just memorised the parable, and build the question around that.

Two things I need from the question itself. It has to be unanswerable by retelling the fence story and tacking on "so we should be humble." And it has to push students into territory they wont have prepped, which means the new material goes in the question stem rather than being assumed. Specifically, I want the stem to introduce (i) the idea that an institution might not be a tool with a purpose at all but an equilibrium that holds because everyone expects everyone else to hold to it, and (ii) the idea that some practices might be ones whose point depends on people not asking what their point is — so that "go away and think about what it's for, then come back and tell me" isn't something you can actually do without destroying the thing you went away to understand. Then the question should ask what those two ideas do to the argument, and what's left standing. I'd also like one part forcing them to say whether the fence argument is distinctively conservative in the first place, or just a general point about acting under uncertainty that applies equally well to tearing something down.

Structure I want: the question first (four parts, marks shown, total 25), then the model answer at about 650 words, then the marking scheme as five must-hit points, each with the marks it's worth and a one-line note on how students typically blow that point. No citations or reading lists anywhere in the model answer — the students get it as a study sheet and I dont want them off chasing references. Same for quotations: if you can't be sure of the exact wording of something, paraphrase it rather than putting it in quotation marks.
