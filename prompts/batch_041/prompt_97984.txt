Last weekend I spent a long drive arguing with a friend — a philosopher of science — about whether a model's assumptions have to be realistic. Her stated view is the F-twist: assumptions may be as false as you like so long as the predictions come out right, and asking after their truth is a category mistake. But her verdicts on particular cases were more discriminating than that slogan, and I can no longer tell which rule she is actually running. Her track record:

Frictionless planes and ideal pendulums, used to derive equations of motion: legitimate. A frictionless surface, she says, is not a falsehood about the world but a statement about a limit, and we know how the equations deform when friction is switched back on.
The ideal gas law applied to a dilute gas: legitimate.
An enzyme-kinetics model that posits a constant steady-state concentration of an intermediate complex to obtain a simple rate law, though no molecule literally holds that concentration fixed: legitimate.
A model of a large stirred reactor that assumes perfect spatial mixing, used to predict bulk yields: legitimate.
A model that assumes an unlimited resource, derives indefinite exponential growth, and is then used to forecast a real fishery's catch: not legitimate.
A model that assumes an infinite, unstructured host population to conclude that a pathogen cannot invade, applied to a small structured population: not legitimate; she says the assumption is doing the work.
A model whose false assumption is neither a limit of any more detailed treatment nor removable, and whose predictions are contradicted in the domain of use: not legitimate.

The new case: a macroeconomist models an entire economy as one immortal representative household with stable preferences, solves for the equilibrium path, and predicts how aggregate consumption responds to a tax change. The predictions track aggregate data well enough that a finance ministry uses them. But the representative household is not the limit of any finer-grained model she will accept — there is no knob you can turn down to get from a heterogeneous economy to a representative one — and the result is load-bearing: spread the same preferences across households with idiosyncratic shocks and the predicted response changes materially, reversing sign over part of the relevant range.

Tell me how she will rule. Set out the two or three most plausible rules her record could be tracking, say what each predicts about the representative-agent case, give your best guess at the one she is running and why, and name the single further case that would best separate the rules that agree on the new case. She will not accept an answer that says it depends on the purpose.

Start with a one-sentence verdict, then a compact table of the candidate rules against the new case, then the argument. No more than 650 words, and no reading list.
