I'm a novelist by trade — five books, zero philosophy degrees — and through a string of questionable decisions I've ended up co-teaching an upper-level seminar with the philosophy department, cross-listed, called "Fate, Choice, and the Novel." We spent the last three weeks on the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP, the idea that you're only morally responsible for an action if you could have done otherwise) and on the Frankfurt-style cases that are supposed to blow it up: you know the shape of them, some meddling intervener — a neuroscientist, a demon, whatever — is standing by ready to force the agent to do the thing, but the agent goes ahead and does it on his own, so the intervener never has to lift a finger, and yet the agent seems plainly responsible. The students can recite all that in their sleep, which is exactly the problem.

I'm writing the final and I want one essay question that they can't answer by parroting the case back at me. The crux I want them to hit is the part everyone skips: why the "he would have intervened only if the agent wavered" version is doing all the work, and why the standard objection is that this either leaves the agent with *some* sliver of a choice (a last-minute reason to hesitate, a flicker) or else secretly assumes the whole thing was already determined and so begs the question. Then I want them to push past the textbook: have them invent a Frankfurt-style case built around an *omission* instead of an action — an agent who fails to do something, where the intervener would have blocked him from doing it anyway — and say whether it lands. And finally, the twist I actually care about, because I think it's where the real fight is: does a *merely probabilistic* intervener work, one who'd have stepped in with, say, 90% likelihood rather than certainty? Let them decide, but make them say what their answer does to the claim that these cases only work if determinism is true.

So: give me the question, a model answer of roughly 600–700 words, and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points at 4 marks each (20 total), where each point names the specific move that separates an A from a B, plus a one-line note on how students typically botch it. Keep the whole thing in American English. And please don't let the model answer just wave at "the flicker strategy" or "the dilemma defense" by name — I want the substance spelled out in plain language, because half my students are English majors and I'll be the one grading this thing at 2 a.m. with a glass of wine.
