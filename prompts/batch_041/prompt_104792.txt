I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar and we've spent the last couple weeks on epiphenomenalism — the view that conscious experiences are caused by what's going on in the brain but don't themselves cause anything downstream, they just ride along like a shadow or a hum. For my term paper I want to do a case study instead of yet another abstract back-and-forth about zombies, and I've picked the Libet-style experiments on the timing of voluntary action: subjects are asked to flex their wrist whenever they feel like it while a clock lets them report when they first became aware of the urge to move, and the headline finding is that a readiness potential in the motor areas builds up roughly half a second before the movement, while the reported awareness of the urge shows up only a couple hundred milliseconds before it. So the neural cause seems to come first and the conscious intention trails behind it, and people have used this to argue that conscious will is not what actually initiates the action. I know the experiment has a million methodological complaints (the clock, the averaging, what the readiness potential even represents), so I don't need a rehash of those — take the paradigm at face value for the sake of argument and treat it as the real episode.

Here's what I actually want from you. First, map the experiment onto epiphenomenalism as carefully as you can: who or what is the agent here, what exactly counts as the evidence, and what does epiphenomenalism say the subjects were doing and experiencing at each stage — does the readiness potential cause the movement and the urge, or the movement and separately the urge, or what? Then bring in the objection I keep hearing called the paradox of phenomenal judgment (I think it's Chalmers's, but don't worry about the attribution): if my conscious states are causally inert, then my beliefs and my verbal reports about those states can't be caused by the states themselves — they'd be caused by whatever neural machinery runs independently — which seems to make my testimony about my own experience a lucky coincidence, and that in turn seems to pull the rug out from under the only evidence I could ever have for epiphenomenalism in the first place. So I want you to run the analysis in both directions. Does Libet's case actually support epiphenomenalism, or does the paradox of phenomenal judgment show that the paradigm's own data (the subjects' reports of when they felt the urge) can't be taken at face value by an epiphenomenalist — and if so, what's left of the finding? And does the case test the theory in some way it never anticipated, or does it just bounce off? Be honest about where epiphenomenalism is simply silent rather than making it say something to fit the experiment, and if you think some nearby position (say, a deflationary or eliminativist one, or the idea that the "veto" is where the action is) handles the episode better, tell me and say why.
