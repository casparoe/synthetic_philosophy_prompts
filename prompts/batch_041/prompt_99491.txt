I retired from physiology a few years ago (forty years in the lab, mostly renal stuff) and since then I've been doing a philosophy degree for fun, and somehow I've talked my way into a seminar slot next term. My thesis is that two things keep getting run together that shouldn't be: akrasia, and the preference reversals you get out of hyperbolic discounting. My line is that a preference reversal isn't a failure of will at all. At every moment the person is doing what they most want right then; there's no all-things-considered judgement that they ought to be doing otherwise, and without that judgement you don't have akrasia — you've just got someone whose valuation of the future isn't exponential. So hyperbolic discounting is a fact about how we weight the future, not a breakdown of the will, and I want to argue that conflating them has muddled both the action-theory side and the econ side. Coming out of physiology I can't help seeing it as two different control problems wearing the same coat.

The audience is a joint session — the behavioural econ people who model discounting for a living and think time-inconsistency just *is* the phenomenon, plus the philosophy of action crowd, a couple of whom are Aristotle scholars and will care about judgement and the practical syllogism. Prep me for the Q&A: the objections I'll actually get from each camp, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. And the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
