I am in a seminar on determinism and free will this term, and I share a house with someone who runs regional weather simulations, so the butterfly effect has stopped being popular-science decoration for me and become a premise in arguments I have to answer. My housemate deploys it twice in a single evening. Against determinism: if a butterfly's wings in Brazil can set off a tornado in Texas, then the future is not fixed in advance and the demon who computes everything from the initial state is a fantasy. Against the point of individual action on climate: the atmosphere is chaotic, my emissions are a far smaller perturbation than the butterfly's wingbeat, and whatever I do is either amplified into something unforeseeable or drowned in noise, so nothing is attributable to me and I am not answerable for it. The same slogan is being made to carry opposite conclusions, and I suspect at least one of the arguments is drawing on something the metaphor supplies rather than on anything chaos theory delivers. I have read the standard history — Lorenz's rerun in which he typed 0.506 instead of the full-precision 0.506127, and the difference doubling roughly every four days until all resemblance to the original run was gone, with the seagull replaced by a butterfly for a 1972 talk — but the history is not what I need.

What I want is an audit of the analogy rather than a general essay on chaos. Begin with the correspondence table: in the picture as Lorenz posed it, what plays the role of the butterfly, of the wingbeat, of the tornado, of the laws, of the initial state, and of the amplification — and which of those items is doing real work as against being supplied by the phrasing. Then the disanalogies, sorted by whether they matter for the two uses above. My own suspicions, which I would like confirmed or corrected: that the systems in question are deterministic by construction, so the anti-determinism reading is self-undermining; that singling out one flap is arbitrary, since every other micro-feature of the state differs between the two runs just as much; that the divergence cannot grow without limit, since the state space is bounded and trajectories are confined to an attractor; and that shrinking your initial error buys only a modest, roughly fixed extension of the forecast horizon rather than a proportional one. I would also like to know what the tornado case would have to look like for the second argument to go through, and whether it survives once trajectory-level prediction is separated from statistical prediction — since the effect of a perturbation on the distribution of outcomes seems perfectly well defined even when its effect on any particular day's weather is not. Finish with a verdict on each of my housemate's conclusions, and, if the metaphor cannot be repaired for the work he wants it to do, with a better one that conveys sensitive dependence without importing agency, cause-selection, or a guarantee that small things matter.
