I'm taking a seminar this term on suffering-focused ethics, and the instructor has been working through a sequence of cases with us, giving her own verdict on each one before opening the floor. What I'd like is to work out what she would say about a case she hasn't yet put to us, and what rule she is actually running. I'm not asking for a survey of the literature, so please don't bother with a reading list or citations; the point is to reconstruct her position from her own verdicts.

Here is what she has said so far. First, asked to divide a fixed budget between two programs, one of which would spare a single person twenty years of severe pain and the other of which would give ten thousand people twenty years of mild contentment, she chose the pain relief immediately and treated it as obvious. Second, on a research-ethics question — whether to approve a study inflicting a week of moderate pain on one consenting adult in order to develop a treatment that would make a hundred thousand people considerably happier for a week — she said no, and again as though the answer were not close. Third, and this is the one that surprised the room, when the suffering on offer was only an hour of mild but distinct discomfort for one person, weighed against an hour of the most intense pleasure available to human beings for a hundred million people, she still chose to spare the discomfort. Fourth, when the comparison was suffering against suffering rather than suffering against pleasure — one person's week of moderate pain against a hundred people's month of mild pain — she chose to prevent the hundred milder pains, so she is evidently not simply deferring to the single worst instance in every case.

The case I want predicted is this. A funder must choose between two programs. The first would spare one person a few seconds of faint discomfort, the sort of thing you would not register unless you were attending to it; that person is, in every other respect, the most fortunate party in the scenario. The second would give a billion people, whose lives are hard though not painful, one full day of the most intense pleasure they will ever experience, and the faint discomfort would be an unavoidable side effect of that program rather than anything anyone seeks.

Predict her verdict. Set out the rules her four earlier verdicts are jointly consistent with, say what each of them predicts here, give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would most efficiently tell the remaining candidates apart — including what would have to be true of her for the other verdict to be the right prediction. Please answer in continuous prose: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, just paragraphs, the way you would write this in an email.
