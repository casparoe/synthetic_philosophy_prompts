Okay, I've been chewing on this for months and I think I've landed on a view, so I want you to try to knock it down. My view: what someone wants just is what they choose. There's no extra fact about their "real" preferences sitting behind their choices waiting to be discovered. My brother keeps ordering the thing that makes him sick, so he wants it, full stop, and when I say he doesn't really want it I'm just saying I'd have chosen different. Same with the guy on the slots, same with advertising, same with the kid who'd rather play games than study. And utility isn't a feeling or a hidden amount of something, it's just the ranking you can read off the choices. Which makes me wonder what the word is even doing when people use it to mean happiness or wellbeing.

So: what would count against this? Actually against, not just "interesting." I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are, including things that have arguably already happened and that I should already know about.

Things I'm half-worried about already, so tell me if they're real defeaters or not. (a) I'm told you can have someone whose choices at every set of options are perfectly consistent and yet still can't be read off any single ranking of the options, because what they pick depends on whats on the menu and not on the items themselves. If thats right I've lost track of what utility is supposed to be. (b) The addict, or the person ground down by a bad situation until their wants have bent to fit it. My view says their choices are authoritative, so I've lost the ability to say anything is bad for them, and I notice I don't want to give that up. (c) Everyone's escape hatch is "what they'd choose if they knew more and were thinking straight" -- but thats not a choice anymore, so is that just sneaking in a standard of whats actually good for people, i.e. quietly giving up the view? (d) Whole classes of people can't choose at all -- kids, my grandmother with dementia, animals, people not born yet. My view seems to have nothing to say about them, which is a big hole for something that's supposed to tell us what's good for people.

And the big one: can the view even be wrong? If utility just means whatever gets chosen, then "people maximize utility" is a tautology and there's nothing to test. But economists clearly think there is something to test, something about the consistency of choices. Which is it? And if the consistency part is the real content, then are the framing effects and preference reversals that keep turning up genuine hits against the view, or does the view just absorb them by saying the frame is part of what they're choosing? At some point that move has to run out or the whole thing is unfalsifiable, and I want to know where that point is.

Last thing: what would someone who actually holds this view admit counts against it -- not what critics say. And where do you come out? Is it well insulated, and if so is that a bug, or just what you get from something that's half a definition and half a theory?
