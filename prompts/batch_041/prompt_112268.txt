I am a senior software engineer who was recently promoted to manage a group of about twenty engineers, and I have no formal training in philosophy. The part of the job I am worst at is deciding how to respond when someone's behavior or a team's habits need to change. My instincts oscillate between two stories. One story says people are basically decent and want to do good work, so when someone behaves badly it is because something in the environment is starving or distorting that decency—unclear expectations, a bad example set by a senior person, incentives that reward the wrong thing—and my job is to remove the obstruction and give the good tendency room. The other story says people are self-interested and will drift toward whatever is easiest unless the structure makes the right behavior easier than the wrong one, so my job is to install rituals, checklists, and consequences that reshape the default. I recently heard a discussion of Mencius and Xunzi that made me think these are not just moods but two developed positions, and I want to stop flip-flopping. These decisions come up weekly, sometimes daily, and the stakes are moderate but cumulative: attrition, whether people trust each other, whether the team can absorb a bad quarter. Anything that touches someone’s employment ultimately has to go through me and HR.

Please turn the Mencius–Xunzi disagreement into a decision procedure that I and my tech leads can actually run on a recurring problem—something we can do in about twenty minutes without reading the primary texts, and that preserves the parts of each view that make them different rather than collapsing them into nurture versus nature. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to me or to HR because the remaining question is about values or about someone’s employment rather than about diagnosis. In particular, keep whatever is distinctive in Mencius’s idea that the moral sprouts need to be extended and can be lost through neglect or bad company, and in Xunzi’s idea that goodness is deliberate artifice, that ritual and propriety do not merely suppress desire but nourish and channel it, and that the right structure can make a person’s own approval of the ritual the thing that makes it stick. The procedure should tell us when to remove an influence, when to install a ritual, and when neither will work.

Then run it on three cases we actually face: a talented senior engineer whose code review comments are consistently dismissive and who says he is just being direct; a team that reliably misses documentation and handoff commitments even after two rounds of gentle reminders; and a case where the procedure tells me the problem is my own incentives and that I am the one who has to change, which I will not enjoy hearing. For each case, show the questions in order, the answers, the recommended intervention, and what would count as evidence after a month that the diagnosis was wrong. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot decide and where I am likely to mistake a Xunzian problem for a Mencian one or the reverse.
