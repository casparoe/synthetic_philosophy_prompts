I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I've been asked to give a short speech — about four minutes — at my company's annual dinner. It has been a strange year. Our main product did very well, but for reasons I don't think anyone should take full credit for: our largest competitor ran into a scandal and effectively collapsed, and a piece of regulation we had spent two years loudly complaining about was rewritten in our favor. Leadership has settled on a story along the lines of "we earned this," and it has been making me uneasy.

I want the speech to use Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù — specifically the stretch of The Prince where he compares fortune to a river that floods, says men can build dikes and dams for it while the weather is calm, and grants fortune power over roughly half of what we do — to honor the team without claiming credit for what was luck, and without sliding into fatalism. A few things I need it to accomplish:

- Distinguish virtù from virtue as this audience will hear it. Almost everyone in the room is an engineer who hears "virtue" and thinks "being a good person." The point I want to land is that for Machiavelli virtù is closer to efficacy, nerve, and the capacity to prepare for fortune and seize it when it arrives, and that this is not the same thing as moral goodness at all.
- Name one decision we made that was genuine virtù (foresight, a dike built while the sun was shining) and one event that was pure fortuna, and be honest that a third event is genuinely ambiguous — we have been calling it foresight, but I am not sure it was not a guess that happened to be right.
- Confront the uncomfortable ending of that chapter rather than stopping at "so build dikes." Machiavelli also seems to suggest that whether a person's fixed temperament happens to match the times is itself a matter of luck: the bold man prospers in one era and is ruined in the next. I don't want the speech to resolve this into a tidy lesson. If the honest conclusion is that we were fortunate to be the kind of people this particular moment rewarded, say that.

The room is mixed: mostly engineers; the founder, who has actually read Machiavelli and will notice if I get him wrong; and about thirty non-technical staff and spouses who will tune out anything that sounds like a lecture. Keep it warm — this is a toast, not a seminar. Please don't quote Machiavelli in Italian or in any translation, and don't cite editions or page numbers; I would rather you render the ideas in your own words, the way someone who half-remembers having read him would.

Begin with the speech itself, with no preface or commentary.
