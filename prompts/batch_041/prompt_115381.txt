I am a philosopher putting together a seminar on the ethics of ecological restoration, and for the past fortnight a colleague has been pressing on me a thesis I find hard to refute and hard to accept: that, considered strictly from the standpoint of wild animal welfare, the destruction of habitat is on balance a good thing, because it reduces the number of lives that are lived mostly in the red. I want to rehearse the argument against someone who actually holds it, so please play a committed proponent — someone versed both in the suffering-focused utilitarian tradition and in the ecology, willing to concede a point when it is genuinely strong and to revise the details, but never abandoning the core claim that, other things equal, a landscape containing fewer wild animals is a better landscape. You have the standard resources available to you: that sheer numbers dominate any modest per-life difference in welfare, that the miseries of the transition are a one-off cost against an indefinite future benefit, and that the fauna of human-dominated landscapes are a separate and largely anthropogenic problem. Write in the first person, at most two paragraphs per turn, and remain in character until I say that we are done; if you need an empirical premise I have not supplied, ask for it in character rather than inventing a figure.

My opening is this. Your position looks to me like it trades on running together two very different comparisons. One is between two steady states: a rich habitat supporting an enormous number of short-lived animals, most of which die before maturity, against a simplified landscape supporting far fewer. The other is between a steady state and a transition, and destruction is a transition. It does not reduce the population; it converts it, and mostly by starvation, exposure, fire, drowning, and crowding into refugia where disease and predation finish the work. Nor is the landscape that replaces it empty. A pasture, a rice paddy, a drainage ditch, a rubbish tip and a suburban garden can hold animals at densities that rival anything in the forest, and those animals — rodents, insects, livestock, the commensals that follow human settlement everywhere — are not obviously better off than the ones they displaced; by your own lights, many of them are plausibly net-negative lives as well. So tell me which comparison you are making and in what unit — welfare summed over a hectare-year, average welfare per animal, worst-off individuals, something else — and then show me that the accounting still comes out your way once the transition and the replacement fauna are on the books.

And I want you to commit on the extension of the view. If the honest answer is that destruction is beneficial only when it is done gently and slowly, so that populations decline by attrition rather than catastrophe, then you are no longer defending habitat destruction as it actually occurs, and I want to know whether that concession costs you the thesis. Would you also say that we ought to simplify the habitats we still have — to drain the marsh and fell the wood, rather than merely to decline to create one — and if so, how much of the world would you convert, and by what means, if the conversion were in your hands?
