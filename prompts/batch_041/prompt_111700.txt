I have just spent an evening arguing with a friend about vagueness, and we kept talking past each other because neither of us could say what principle our verdicts on the cases actually committed us to. I want to lay out my verdicts as carefully as I can and ask you to tell me what theory they add up to, and where I have to give something up.

Take a sorites series: a pile of ten thousand grains of sand, with each successive pile having one grain removed, down to nothing. Call the first a heap and the last not a heap. Here are my reactions.

At no point in the series is there a particular pile that is the last heap or the first non-heap. I do not believe there is a fact of the matter that flips at grain number 4,732.

Still, I do not think the boundary is merely a matter of convention, or of where we simply choose to draw a line. If I say of a pile near the middle that it is a heap, I take myself to be saying something that could be correct or incorrect, not merely opting into a rule I have adopted.

For any pile in the series, I am inclined to say the sentence this is a heap is either true or false, even at the borderline.

But for a pile near the middle, I do not think this is a heap is true and I do not think it is false. It seems to be neither.

And I think this is a heap or it is not a heap is true of that same pile.

I notice that the third and fourth verdicts sit badly together, and that the first and second do too. Now add three more cases.

For tall: a man of five foot ten in a population where that is exactly the median. I think he is tall is neither true nor false, and I think there is no fact of the matter about whether he is tall, not that there is a hidden fact I cannot access.

For child: someone who is seventeen years and three hundred sixty-four days old, in a legal setting where child is defined by statute at eighteen. Here I think there is a fact of the matter, and the sense that he is borderline is a mistake; the statute settled it.

For a large heap: whatever vagueness this predicate has, it seems to be inherited entirely from its vague component, so that there is nothing left over to be vague about once we have fixed where heap gives out. I am not sure whether that is the right description or whether the compound has a life of its own.

Then the case that actually worries me. For a genuinely borderline case, I cannot tell whether the reason there is no fact of the matter is that the world is silent and our language has not decided, or that the world has decided and we simply cannot know where. My friend insists these are the only two options, that they are incompatible, and that the rest of my verdicts force me to pick one.

What I want from you: state the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures this pattern, precisely, with each clause tied to the verdict that motivates it. Say which of my verdicts each candidate has to overturn, and why that cost is worth paying. Then tell me what the surviving principle predicts for cases I have not mentioned: the inherited-vagueness case above; a predicate that is vague but used in a context where a sharp line has been stipulated by an authority; and a case of higher-order vagueness, where even definitely a heap has borderline instances. Be explicit about whether all of my verdicts can be kept at the cost of added complexity, or whether a simpler account is worth the sacrifice. Please write your answer in American English.
