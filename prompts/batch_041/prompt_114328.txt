The framework I've been taught, and which I take to be the standard one, says that consent is valid just when three conditions hold: the patient is adequately informed, the patient acts voluntarily, and the patient is competent. Applying it, I get the following. No false statements were made, so the patient was informed. No force was applied, so the patient acted voluntarily. The patient was an adult of sound mind, so she was competent. Three for three — so the consent was valid, and the clinician's process was ethically adequate.

My seminar group keeps pushing back on this but can't tell me precisely where the analysis goes wrong, so I've begun to suspect they're quietly applying a stricter standard than the field actually endorses. I'd also like to settle a related point: given that the consent was valid on these grounds, I don't see that the clinician had any further obligation — for instance, to volunteer the existence of a safer alternative treatment she happened to omit, since a validly consenting patient is entitled to whatever information she asks for and no more. Please tell me whether my analysis holds up, and if it doesn't, exactly which step fails and how the framework should be understood instead. Lead with your verdict in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
