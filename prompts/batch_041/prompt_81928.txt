Next month I'm giving a workshop talk in a comparative-politics department and I need you to write me a Q&A prep sheet, because this audience is going to be rough and I'd much rather find out now than in the room.

Here's the arguement I'm making. The whole democratic-backsliding literature, and the whole fight over whether this or that country has actually slid, is confused because everyone reads off the severity of the latest outrage instead of the structural feature that actually matters. What matters, I claim, is what I've been calling the ratchet: whether a given move raises the cost of putting things back the way they were. A move can be perfectly legal, genuinely popular, and reversible on paper and still be part of backsliding, if it makes the next reversal harder. That's the ratchet. Run the usual suspects through that lens and the sorting gets scrambled. Some things people loudly call backsliding don't ratchet at all, they're just bad policy or bad vibes that a normal election could undo; and some things people wave away as minor do ratchet, quietly. I also want to claim this explains why the term reads as partisan: people are using severity, which is deeply contestable, as a stand-in for the ratchet, which is at least less contestable.

The audience, as best I can tell: about half are the quantitative measurement types, the ones who build and argue over cross-national indices, and they care above all about whether a concept is operationalizable, so they'll want to know what exactly my ratchet is a measurement of and whether it's separable from the outcome it's supposed to predict. Then a couple of area specialists, one who knows Hungary and Poland inside out and one who knows Turkey, both of whom know the cases far better than I do and will have concrete counterexamples ready. Then one constitutional lawyer who thinks the only interesting question is always the legal form of the move, and one democratic theorist who's skeptical that democracy has a determinate enough core for any of this to be more than a power grab dressed up as method.

What I want from you: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each one phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, each with a short reply I can give in a minute or two and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front rather than get dragged into conceding. And tell me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Write it as continuous prose, please, the way you'd write a long email to a friend who owes you a favor. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. And don't bother with citations or reading lists, I've got those.
