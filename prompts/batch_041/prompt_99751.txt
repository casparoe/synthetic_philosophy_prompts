I do incident investigations for a regional rail safety body — not the big national one, we cover a couple of smaller networks and a depot or two — and for the past few years I've been the one who drafts the findings where we say what somebody knew. That word does a lot of work in our reports, because the panel then has to call it one of three things: 'knew', 'should have known', or 'couldn't reasonably have known'. The first one can end a career and twice now has ended up with the police taking an interest, the second is usually a training note, and the third is basically a line for the file. I've been going round in circles on it. A mate lent me a philosophy book and there was a chapter on an account of knowledge where your belief only counts if it's true and it would have tracked the truth — you wouldn't have believed it if it had been false, and you would have believed it if it had been true — and apparently the bloke who came up with this gave up on the rule that if you know one thing and you know it implies a second thing, then you know the second thing. I think it's Nozick but don't hold me to that, I read it on a train.

What I want is a procedure my team can actually run on a case in about an hour, sitting in a room with the report, the interview transcripts and the maintenance logs — no experiments, no going back to the person, no asking them what they would have done, and no me in the room making the final call, because the panel chair signs off and I only write the draft. Something like a list of questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, when we're entitled to stop and write 'couldn't have known', and where the thing runs out and it's a values question the panel has to settle on its own. Three snags it has to handle, because I keep hitting them. First, quite often the person never formed any belief about the thing at all — it didn't cross their mind either way — and I can't tell whether that's a fail or just an empty case, since a question like 'would they have believed it if it had been false' seems to come out true for someone who doesn't believe it in the first place, which can't be right. Second, a lot of our findings are about the company rather than a person — 'the depot knew the brake blocks were overdue' — and I've no idea whose belief that's supposed to be or how you'd test it, and yet those findings are the ones that get the most attention. Third, the obvious one: a fitter has two seperate facts, one from the job card and one from the radio log, and between them they mean the track isn't safe, but he never joins them up, and our draft ends up saying he knew the track wasn't safe. That feels wrong to me and I think it's the very thing the book said the theory gives up on, but I need a rule I can defend in a meeting, not a feeling.

So: please give me the procedure, and then run it on three cases so I can see it working. (a) A signaller who was told twice about a possession, wrote it on his pad, and then cleared a train into the section anyway. (b) The planner who had the two facts in two systems and never put them together. (c) One where I'd bet the honest answer will annoy people here: a driver who genuinely believed the right thing about a failed axle counter and acted on it, but only because he'd mixed up two diagrams and would have believed the same thing if the counter had been fine — I think our panel would still want to write 'knew' and I want to see what your procedure does with him. And tell me straight where the procedure can't decide, because 'should have known' doesn't sound like knowledge at all to me, it sounds more like a standard of care, and I don't want a checklist pretending to settle that bit.
