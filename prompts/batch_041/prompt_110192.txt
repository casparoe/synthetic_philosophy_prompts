I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, and I'm applying to a small interdisciplinary workshop that funds six-month projects. I want to pitch a philosophy-of-action project on joint action, and I'd like you to write the proposal.

My half-formed idea comes out of distributed systems. Whenever I reason about a collection of processes, I find I need to keep apart two things that philosophical talk about shared intention seems to run together: a coordination layer (whatever lets the parts align their behaviour so the joint outcome actually happens) and a commitment layer (whatever binds the parts, so that one can demand something of another and be answerable to the group). My suspicion is that the standing dispute between reductionists (a group's shared intention is nothing over and above the members' individual intentions plus their common knowledge) and non-reductionists (it is a genuinely plural attitude had by the group) partly persists because the contested cases slide between these two layers, and that once the layers are held apart, part of the disagreement turns out to be about words while a narrower and harder question remains.

Write the proposal so that the central question turns on a case where the two layers come apart: a group whose members each follow a private rule and reliably produce a coordinated joint outcome that none of them represents as a joint goal. State the question precisely enough to have a definite answer; explain why the two standard camps leave it open; set out what I would actually do over six months given that I can only reason and read, not run experiments; say what a result would look like, what would count as the project failing, and where I'm most likely to fool myself; and keep it to roughly a page and a half.
