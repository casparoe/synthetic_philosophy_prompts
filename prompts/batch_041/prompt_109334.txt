Here's the deal. A friend keeps invoking something he calls the unilateralist's curse, and honestly I think he's onto something. The setup: twenty of us on some committee, and each of us is independently able to unilaterally trigger one and the same intervention — a trial, a release, whatever. It's common knowledge that the intervention is worth doing iff one underlying fact C holds (it really works, the side effects really are mild). Each of us has our own private, noisy read on whether C holds; nobody sees anybody else's read. The intervention goes ahead if *any* of us triggers it. It only doesn't go ahead if all twenty of us hold back.

His argument, as I understand it:

(1) Each of us should trigger it iff our own private evidence makes the expected value positive.
(2) Since it goes ahead if anyone triggers it, the person who actually triggers it will tend to be whoever's private evidence most strongly favoured it — i.e., the most misleading sample, when C is in fact false.
(3) So conditional on it going ahead, C is less likely than the triggerer's own evidence makes it out to be. There's a selection effect, and it worsens as the group gets bigger.
(4) Therefore each of us should demand more evidence before triggering than our private read alone justifies, and the more of us there are who could act, the higher that bar should be.
(5) Extend it: as the number of possible actors grows, the bar keeps climbing, and in the limit nobody should ever trigger anything on private evidence. All unilateral initiative — founding the company, publishing the result that bucks the consensus, blowing the whistle — is someone being the tail of a noisy sample, so it's all suspect.

I can't accept (5). If nobody ever acted on private evidence then nobody would ever have founded anything or overturned a consensus, and that's definately worse, not better. So something in there is off.

But here's the part that's actually got me stuck. I tried to run the whole thing in reverse and it seemed to go through just as cleanly. Look at *not* triggering. The intervention fails to happen only if every one of us holds back. So the people who hold back are selected for the most restraint-leaning evidence — again the tail of a noisy sample, just at the other end. By parity, conditional on the intervention *not* going ahead, C is *more* likely than the hold-backers' evidence suggests, so each of us should *lower* our bar for acting, not raise it. Identical setup, opposite prescription.

That can't be right either, and now I've got 'never act' out of one pass and 'always act' out of the mirror pass. I don't believe the curse reasoning is just incoherent, so one of these passes has to be cheating somewhere. Which step actually fails? Is it the same step in both directions, or is the whole point that the two directions aren't symmetric at all? I'd most like to keep (2) and (3) — the selection effect feels like the real thing — but tell me what I'm paying if I have to drop something. And if the fix is 'defer to the group's pooled evidence', say precisely why the pooled number is the thing to defer to, and whether the mirror argument has a pooled number of its own that I've been ignoring.
