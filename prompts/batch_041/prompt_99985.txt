My friend and I spent last night arguing about whether the last financial crisis was predictable. He says central banks and regulators already treat uncertainty as quantifiable: central banks publish fan charts that put probability bands around future inflation and growth, and regulators make banks hold capital against estimated worst-case losses at a given confidence level. I say those practices smuggle in a philosophy of probability that Keynes would have resisted. What exactly do such practices presuppose about uncertainty? Do they presuppose that every economically relevant uncertainty can be assigned a precise numerical probability, that probabilities are frequencies or subjective degrees of belief, and that rational policy is expected-value maximization? Can they accommodate Keynes's distinction between risk and uncertainty, or his idea that the weight of evidence is independent of probability? Which of these commitments are strictly required by the practice, which are merely convenient, and where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? Finally, if a central bank took the Keynesian view seriously, what would it have to change about how it forecasts and communicates?
