You are an expert in the philosophy of biology and in the methodology of quantitative genetics, with a reputation for drafting documents that rival camps can actually put their names to. I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar, and I want you to produce one self-contained deliverable: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two positions that have been talking past each other for years.

The two positions. The first is the working behavior geneticist, who holds that the heritability coefficient is a well-defined and genuinely informative parameter — that it answers a real question about the sources of variation in a population, and that the standard criticisms of it are either already answered or beside the point. The second is the critic, who holds that the coefficient is at best a fragile descriptive statistic, and that treating it as an explanation of why individuals differ, or as evidence about what could be changed, is a category error. Both are competent and neither is a caricature. I want the document they could co-sign.

Approach. Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you generate candidate agreed claims and candidate cruxes, then discard the ones that are vacuous, merely definitional, or that one side would visibly reject. Only then write the statement itself. You may assume the reader knows the standard literature; no citations or reading lists are needed.

The statement should contain, in this order:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered. It should cover, at minimum: what a heritability coefficient is a property of (a population, a time, a particular range of environments — or none of these); the sense in which it is relative to the environmental variation present; whether and in what sense it is causal; what it does not license — in particular about the development of any given individual, about differences between groups, and about the likely size of the effect of an environmental intervention; and how the technical term relates to the ordinary-language word inherited.

2. A disagreements table, one row per live disagreement, with columns for: (a) the disagreement stated in neutral terms; (b) the wording the behavior geneticist would sign for her own view; (c) the wording the critic would sign for his; (d) the crux the row turns on; and (e) the argument, experiment, or case that both sides agree would bear on that crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Every formulation in columns (b) and (c) must be one that its own side would accept, so that neither is a strawman and neither is a disguised concession.

3. An explicit verdict on which parts of the quarrel, if any, are verbal rather than substantive, and why.

4. A short closing section on what neither side will assert — the inferences both agree are unwarranted — since that is where the agreement does the most work.

Test the draft against two constructed cases, neither drawn from any actual study. First: in population A an environmental input varies widely and the heritability of trait T is high; in population B that same input has been equalized across the population and the heritability of T is near zero. One colleague says T is genetically caused in A but not in B; the other says that is incoherent. State exactly what each side must say about this case, and whether it exposes a genuine disagreement or a terminological one. Second: consider the proposal to retire heritability talk in favor of an explicit intervention-and-response formulation, namely the effect on the trait of shifting the distribution of some specified cause. Is that a clarification of the same question, a replacement of it, or a change of subject? Say what each side should say.

Finally, be self-critical about your own drafting. Identify where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and state what argument, result, or piece of evidence would lead you to move a claim from the agreed section into the disagreements table (or the reverse), or to revise the cruxes you proposed.

Write in American English, in the register of a formal departmental document.
