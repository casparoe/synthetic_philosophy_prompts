I've been reading a lot of the rationalist blogosphere's material on consciousness and moral circle expansion, and I've ended up with a position about fish and decapod crustaceans in aquaculture that I'd like to stress-test. The position is a conjunction. First, the morally relevant capacity is phenomenal pain, and I understand that as requiring two things: a global representation of a noxious event as bad for the organism itself, and the capacity to use that representation flexibly — to trade it off against other goods, to learn from it in non-reflexive ways, to avoid it in novel contexts. Second, the evidence from decapods and at least some teleost fish supports attributing both. Third, if that is right, then routine aquaculture practices — boiling crabs and lobsters alive, chilling shrimp to death, crowding fish into chronic stress — are morally on a par with doing the same to a mammal, and differences in neural architecture are no more exculpatory than the differences between a bird and a mammal. What would count against this? I want the actual defeaters, not the general skeptical gesture.

Specifically: which empirical findings would undermine the inference from nociception to pain — evidence that the avoidance behaviors are reflex arcs, that the apparent trade-offs are confounded by simple associative learning without any felt state, or that the animals lack the neural integration needed for a unified representation of harm? Which conceptual moves would count against the position — the claim that pain requires a narrative self over time, that the concept is being stretched past the point where it does explanatory work, or that the right response to uncertainty is not precaution but a refusal to extend the moral circle on the basis of a capacity we cannot confirm? What does the position predict that its rivals do not, and which of those predictions are already testable? I'd also like to know what a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against them, not only what critics say. And I want an honest assessment of whether the position is insulated by the precautionary principle in a way that makes it unfalsifiable, and if so, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.

I was arguing about this with a friend who works in a wet lab and thinks the whole thing is anthropomorphic projection, and I found myself unsure whether I was using the precautionary principle as a genuine tie-breaker or as a way of avoiding the possibility that the answer is simply unknown. I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with some indication of which ones a defender would dismiss as not really counting and which ones would force a genuine revision. If the position turns out to be well insulated, I would rather know that than have it patched.
