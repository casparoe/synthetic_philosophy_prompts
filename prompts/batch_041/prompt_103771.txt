I spent my working life as a chemist and only since retiring have I been working through the philosophical canon in any sustained way, so I come to this with more enthusiasm than expertise. A book club I have joined is reading The Merchant of Venice next month, and I have been struck by how thoroughly the play turns on money: Shylock's lending at interest, the bond of a pound of flesh, Bassanio's borrowing in order to court Portia, the caskets that require the suitor to hazard everything he has. I would like a reading of the play through the lens of Aristotle's account of money and exchange. My difficulty is that the commentary I have sampled tends to flatten Aristotle into a proto-theorist of the just price, or to flatten the play into a simple morality tale about greed, and I suspect both moves lose what is genuinely interesting. So give me a sustained reading that takes both the play and the philosophy seriously: the contrast in the Politics between household management and the unlimited art of wealth-getting, the treatment in the Nicomachean Ethics of money as a conventional measure that exists to make proportionate exchange possible between things that are not naturally commensurable, and the claim that usury is the most unnatural form of acquisition because money, unlike a flock or a field, does not by itself bring forth anything. I am especially interested in whether the play dramatizes a tension between money as a mere token or convention and money as something that appears to breed, and whether Shylock's insistence on the letter of the bond is best understood as a refusal of the conventional, symbolic character of money or rather as an uncomfortably honest embrace of the logic of contract on which the Venetians themselves depend.

I would like the reading to be faithful in both directions. Where Aristotle genuinely illuminates a scene or a character's decision — the trial, the caskets, the elopement, the business with the rings — say so concretely and show your work; where the play resists or exceeds the Aristotelian frame, and the honest critic has to concede that his categories do not fit, say that as well. Please close by saying what the play adds to this corner of the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot. I want you to be careful to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material — whether Aristotle's account of exchange rests on need or on some measure of labor, and whether the so-called just price is even his doctrine, are matters on which specialists disagree, and I do not want a contested interpretation presented as the consensus. Since I will be discussing this with people who know the play well but the philosophy hardly at all, and since I have no way to check citations, keep any references to Aristotle's texts at the level of the argument rather than the page. Write it as continuous prose, please — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables; the way you would write a long letter to a friend who is curious about all this.
