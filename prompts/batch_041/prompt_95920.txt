For several years I have served on committees that resolve a single question by taking majority votes on each of its components separately — a hiring committee that votes on whether a candidate clears the research bar, then the teaching bar, then the funding bar, and only afterwards announces a decision to hire or not — and only recently did it strike me that such a procedure can produce a collective decision that no member of the committee actually holds, and that contradicts our own component-by-component majorities. I now have to explain this to a reading group of colleagues from law, medicine, and engineering, none of them philosophers, in a single ninety-minute session, and what I want from you is a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself. Set out the order in which the ideas must be introduced for the puzzle to feel like something we discovered rather than a conjuring trick: what has to be in place before the inconsistency even registers as a problem, and why each piece depends on the one before it. Identify the one example that should carry the whole session, state what it must contain — several issues that are logically connected, so that issue-by-issue majority voting can yield a majority for each premise and a majority against the conclusion those premises entail — and tell me whether the well-worn three-judge contract case is still the right vehicle or has gone stale. Then list the misconceptions they will bring or form — that this is merely a restatement of Arrow's theorem, that it is really about insincere or strategic voting, that the remedy is simply to vote on the conclusion instead of the premises, that it shows collective decision-making to be impossible — and mark the precise point in the sequence where each should be headed off. Close with a single question I can put to the room that would show whether they have grasped where the trouble comes from rather than merely followed the arithmetic, and tell me what I should explicitly refuse to go into at this level.
