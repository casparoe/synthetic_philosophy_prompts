I think Ive basically talked myself into the view that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem does most of the work in the social choice debate. Setup as I understand it: a group of people who each have coherent preferences over uncertain prospects, a social preference that's coherent in the same way, and a Pareto-ish requirement that if everyone prefers one option to another then so does society. The result is that the social ranking has to be a weighted sum of individual utilities. If that's right, being anti-utilitarian is a complaint about the axioms rather than the conclusion -- and the axioms look like pure bookkeeping. Correct me if I've garbled the theorem, but then tell me where the reading goes wrong.

I want the defeaters, ranked by how much they should actually move me:

- which axiom is really carrying the weight, and what would have to be true for it to be the wrong constraint? I care about the difference between dropping it and still getting something sensible, versus dropping it and getting mush.

- the weights. I get handed a weighted sum but no weights, and I can rescale anyone's utility function by a positive constant without changing a single thing about their preferences, so the utilitarianism feels thinner than advertised -- is the real content just hidden in how you normalize? Fatal, or is that how any cardinal framework works? Related: I also lean on the veil-of-ignorance story to get equal weights, and I suspect that's doing more work than the theorem is.

- a case I keep getting stuck on: two people who disagree about the probability of some event but agree completely about what's at stake. To even write down the social lottery I seem to need one probability distribution over the states, and neither person's is privileged. What does the theorem say there, and does it need a common prior to get off the ground?

- the fairness objection, where the before-the-fact principle forces society to prefer a coin flip between two fates over a guaranteed outcome both people would rather have. How much does that cost? Would a defender just say the axiom is right and my reaction is the mistake?

- how much does all this depend on people being expected-utility maximizers with real-valued, comparable utilities? If preferences are incomplete, or only ordinal, does the theorem simply not apply -- and is that a defeater or an admission that it was never about real people?

Then: which of these would the theorem's defenders themselves concede as a cost, which would they wave off, and is there anything the view commits me to that a Rawlsian or a prioritarian wouldn't? Also tell me honestly whether the position is even falsifiable, or whether it just collects caveats forever. I'd definately rather know if its unfalsifiable than get a fake list of testable predictions.
