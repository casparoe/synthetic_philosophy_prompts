So my friends Nadia and Rob got into it at a party and I ended up as the referee, which I'm bad at, because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. It was about perception — the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist reply to it. Roughly how it went:

Nadia: 'You know the stick in water thing. I flinched at a coat on the back of a chair last week because it read as a person. In the illusory case you're having an experience as of something being a certain way and nothing is that way; in the veridical case you're having an experience as of something being that way and something is. And from the inside you can't tell the two apart — that's the whole point of the example. The obvious explanation is that the two cases share something, some inner state, and the only difference is whether it lines up with the world. Your disjunctivist says no, they're different kinds of thing, a perception versus a mere seeming, nothing shared. But then you owe me a story about why I can never tell them apart, and any story you tell is going to appeal to what they share. So either you're just stipulating the difference or you've got a view with less explanatory power than the boring one. It's a cheat.'

Rob: 'You're assuming that "can't tell them apart" has to be explained by a shared property. Those come apart. There's a difference between "these two states have something in common" and "the subject is in no position to discriminate between them." I'm saying the second. That you can't tell is a fact about your epistemic position, not about some common inner thing. And it buys a lot: the common-factor view puts something between you and the world, an inner state you're really acquainted with, and then it's a mystery how you ever get out to the stick. I'll take your mystery about discrimination over a veil.'

Nadia: 'Two problems. One, if the difference between perceiving and merely seeming is never detectable from the inside, what makes it a difference in the experience, rather than a label you've stuck on from outside? You're positing a distinction that can't show up for the only person it's supposed to matter to. Two, you still haven't explained anything. I can say why I can't tell: because the same thing is going on in both cases. You have to say "you can't tell, and that's just a fact about your epistemic position," which is a shrug. Explanations that predict the data beat shrugs.'

Rob: 'Take the vat. If you were a brain in a vat your experience would be just like this one, but that doesn't mean this experience and the vat experience share an inner core. It means you're not in a position to rule it out. Same structure. And look — the hallucination doesn't have to be a positive state with its own character that happens to resemble a perception. On the view I like, it's the case where no perception is happening at all. It's a lack, characterized only negatively, by what it isn't. Nothing to share.'

Nadia: 'Now you've made it worse. If the hallucination is just an absence, then "it looks to her as if there's a person there" isn't true of her in virtue of anything about her. But she can describe exactly what she seemed to see. You've made the appearance evaporate, which is absurd — the appearance is the one thing we're sure of. And you never answered the vat thing: in the vat case it's stipulated that the inner facts are the same and the outer facts differ, so it's a case where inner sameness is the premise. You can't use it to show that inner sameness is optional.'
