I run a small model-behaviour team at an AI lab. We file two or three short evaluation reports a week for safety reviewers, policy colleagues and an external auditor, and the same argument recurs over belief-and-desire language: a report will say the model believed it was being tested, or wanted to keep its scratchpad, or intended to mislead the grader, and half the reviewers wave this through as harmless shorthand while the other half strike it as anthropomorphism. Nobody has a rule. I have read enough of the rationalist blogosphere to be unbothered by the vocabulary in principle; I want it disciplined, not banned.

Please write a procedure my analysts can actually run, in order, grounded in the intentional stance and its design and physical siblings: the questions to ask of a proposed attribution, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure terminates in a flat instruction not to write the sentence, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than evidence. Build in a pragmatist test — an attribution should be licensed only if we are prepared to let it do work, whether predicting, intervening, or assigning something like responsibility — and be honest that mechanistic grounding is neither necessary nor sufficient for description at the personal level. Run it on three cases: a model that plans and executes what looks like self-exfiltration and narrates the plan; a model whose stated answer to a factual question flips with the framing; and a model that denies being conscious while objecting to being shut down. Note which verdicts will annoy my team, and what the procedure cannot settle. It needs to be runnable in an hour by someone without access to model internals. Write in British English.
