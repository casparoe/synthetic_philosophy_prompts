I teach in the philosophy department at a mid-sized university and am currently grading the midterm for a third-year course on metaethics, the first unit of which is devoted to Hume's moral psychology and to the cluster of issues usually gathered under the headings of the fact/value distinction and the naturalistic fallacy. The final question on the exam asked students to explain what Hume takes to be at issue in the observation that writers on morals pass from premises concerning what is the case to conclusions concerning what ought to be, and then to assess whether that observation, on its own, establishes that no ought-statement can be derived from is-statements. I have reproduced one student's answer below. What I need from you is a grade and, more usefully, the specific comments that justify it, since I will be returning these to the student rather than writing a fresh critique myself.

The student wrote: "Hume's point is that when we read moral philosophy we find the author arguing in the ordinary way, describing human nature and the passions and the constitution of society, and then all at once, instead of propositions about what is or is not the case, we meet with propositions about what ought or ought not to be done. Hume notes that this change is made imperceptibly, and that it is not obvious how the new propositions can be inferred from the old ones. His observation is that the transition is illegitimate, since no set of purely factual premises can ever entail a normative conclusion; the gap between is and ought is a logical gap, and any argument that crosses it commits the naturalistic fallacy. It is easy to see why Hume is led to this. He has argued that reason is inert: it discovers truth and falsehood, but it can never by itself move us to act. Morality, by contrast, plainly does move us; we are deterred from actions by the thought that they are unjust. Since reason cannot produce action, moral distinctions cannot be discovered by reason alone, and so the inference from factual premises to moral conclusions cannot be a deduction. This is why Hume turns to sentiment. On his view, to call an action vicious is not to report a fact about it but to express a feeling of disapprobation that a spectator has on contemplating it; moral distinctions are felt rather than discovered by the understanding. The strength of the position is that it explains the motivational force of morality, which the rationalist systems of his contemporaries could not. Its weakness is that it makes moral disagreement look like a clash of tastes rather than a disagreement about anything real, and Hume seems content to accept this. Still, the observation about is and ought is a permanent contribution, and it is essentially the same point that Moore later makes with his open-question argument."
