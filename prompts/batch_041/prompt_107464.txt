I host a small interview series on moral psychology, and the episode I am preparing has acquired an unexpected personal weight. Over the past year I have been deliberately trying to become more patient and more generous, and I have been struck by how poorly the effort transfers across contexts: I am patient with my family and brittle the moment I am behind schedule; I give freely when rested and guard my time when anxious. For a while I read this as evidence that I lack the very traits I thought I was cultivating. More recently it has made me suspicious of the idea that anyone possesses stable character traits at all, which I understand is roughly the situationist challenge to virtue ethics.

My guest for this episode is a philosopher who does not believe the empirical findings refute virtue ethics but does believe they force a redefinition of it. Her position, as I understand it, is that a virtue is not a property of a person considered in isolation but of a person-plus-environment system: to call someone honest is shorthand for saying that, within the settings she actually inhabits, she reliably responds honestly. On this account the unit of moral assessment is the person-in-her-niche, and the practical task is not merely self-improvement but the deliberate arrangement of one's surroundings — routines, defaults, company, prior commitments — so that the virtue has something to operate on.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that will let her develop this position rather than merely restate it. I want at least one question pressing on whether relocating virtue into the person-environment system quietly converts virtue into a species of prudence or environmental engineering, since the person who is good at arranging her surroundings may simply be someone skilled at managing her own behavior. I want at least one question on what happens to responsibility and to praise and blame if the reliable response belongs to the system rather than to the agent. And I want at least one question on whether virtue is possible in a setting that offers no support for it — the case of someone who must act well against her surroundings rather than with them. For each of these, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and let the sequence build so that her opening formulations become progressively harder to sustain. Please avoid questions that only ask her to rehearse the famous experiments, and avoid anything that could be answered by reciting a textbook summary of the debate; I want the questions to turn on the internal pressure points of her own view.
