I'm a postdoc in a computational cognitive science program, and in three weeks I have to run a ninety-minute session on the Gödelian anti-mechanist arguments — the Lucas–Penrose line — for a room of PhD students who work on machine learning and cognitive modelling. They are fluent in computability in the loose, working sense: they know the halting problem cold and they have all heard the word 'diagonalisation'. They have essentially no proof theory. And they arrive already convinced that the whole anti-mechanist argument is a howler — they just cannot say precisely where the howler is, which is why the argument keeps returning to haunt them. My aim is deliberately the reverse of the usual one: I want them to feel the argument at its strongest before I let them take it apart, so that their eventual rejection is earned rather than reflexive.

Don't explain the argument to me; I know it. Plan the explanation. I need the dependency structure — which ideas have to be in place before which, and why the order cannot be permuted — not a summary of the content.

I want the whole session carried by one device. My candidate is a two-player extension game: the mechanist is never left holding a single fixed machine. After each apparent refutation, the mechanist is permitted to add the offending sentence to the machine's axiom set and play again, and the anti-mechanist must win against the resulting sequence of machines rather than against one machine. My reason for wanting this is that it forces the audience to distinguish 'for every machine there is a sentence it cannot prove' from 'there is a sentence that no machine can prove', and I suspect that quantifier order is where the argument actually slips. I am not confident that is the real crux. If you think the crux is instead something about soundness, or about whether we could ever know that we are consistent, or about whether we could know which machine we are, then say so and re-plan the sequence around that instead. If you think my game is a poor carrying example, propose a better one and defend it explicitly against mine — and in that case tell me why the obvious candidate, a single machine with a single Gödel sentence, fails as the thing that carries a whole session.

I also want the misconception map. My guesses, in the order I expect them to surface: that Gödel's theorem shows no machine can be as intelligent as a human; that the argument is refuted by the plain fact that we make mistakes; that the Gödel sentence is merely a self-referential parlor trick; and that the reply about not knowing which machine we are is a technicality that leaves the argument standing. For each: when it forms, where in the sequence I should head it off, and how, plus which of these I should not try to kill outright because it is doing useful work at that moment.

Then the check for understanding — one question I can put at the end, what a good answer to it contains, and what the most likely wrong answer reveals.

Finally, what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say so: I have ninety minutes and no proof theory, and I would rather name the omissions out loud than let the audience suspect I am bluffing.

No citations, no reading suggestions, no pointers to who said what — I just need the plan.

Return it as a single JSON object with exactly these keys, in this shape:

- "session_frame": an object with "audience_assumptions" (array of strings — what I may take as given, and what I may not) and "target_takeaway" (a single sentence that is the one thing they should be able to write down at the end).
- "prerequisite_chain": an array of objects, each with "order" (integer), "idea" (string), "depends_on" (array of integers), "why_here" (string), and "what_breaks_if_moved_earlier" (string). Cover the whole arc from what must come first to what must come last.
- "carrying_example": an object with "description" (string), "what_it_must_have" (array of strings), "what_it_must_lack" (array of strings), "enters_at_step" (integer, matching an "order" above), and "why_the_obvious_alternative_fails" (string).
