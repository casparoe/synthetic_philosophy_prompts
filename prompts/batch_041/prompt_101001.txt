I spent most of last night in a long argument with a friend about free-floating rationales, and I came away with a half-formed idea that I think might actually be a project. I'm applying for a small grant — six months, roughly one day a week — and I need a short proposal, about three pages. I'd like you to write it.

The idea, such as it is. The standard way of introducing free-floating rationales makes a simple point: a trait or a bit of behavior can be assessed as a solution to a problem, and can exist because it solves that problem, even though nothing anywhere represents either the problem or the solution. A plant that orients its leaves toward the light, a bacterium that swims up a sugar gradient, a bird that feigns a broken wing to draw a predator from its nest. Nobody has to think any of this for the assessment to be correct, or for the behavior to be explained by its correctness. My friend's line was that this is all just a fancy way of redescribing a selection history, that nothing is genuinely there, and that talk of a rationale is a courtesy we extend to causal processes that happen to look purposeful. My line was that he was throwing away something real, but I couldn't say precisely what, and by the end I wasn't sure whether I was defending a fact about the world or a fact about our explanatory habits.

Here is what I think the real question is. We need to distinguish a rationale that is genuinely present in a system — one that the system's behavior is answerable to, in the sense that if the problem changed in the relevant way the behavior would have to change or fail — from a rationale that we merely project onto the system because we find the description flattering or useful. The deflationist says there is no such distinction to draw, or that if there is, it is only the distinction between explanations we like and explanations we don't. The inflationary view says the rationales are really out there, mind-independent, part of the furniture of nature. Both seem to me to leave something open: the deflationist cannot explain why some redescriptions strike us as having normative force and others do not, and the inflationary view has a hard time saying what a rationale is made of or how we could ever be wrong about one.

My half-formed proposal is this. Stop asking whether a rationale floats free of all minds and start asking how much grip it has. Index any candidate rationale to a design space — the space of variations on the problem and on the system — and say that a rationale has grip to the extent that the system's behavior is counterfactually sensitive to the features of the problem in the way the rationale requires: intervene on the problem, and the behavior tracks it; change the problem so the rationale no longer applies, and the behavior degrades in the way the rationale predicts. Grip, on this picture, is a matter of degree, and it is a fact about the system and its history rather than about our preferences. Then the interesting question becomes whether free-floating rationale should be defined as a threshold on grip, where that threshold lies, and whether the threshold can be specified without smuggling a reasoner back in through the side door — for instance by requiring that someone could in principle recognize the rationale.

I want the proposal built around that, but I want it tested against cases rather than asserted, and there are three I keep coming back to. The first is the control case: a trait that can be described as solving a problem but that is there for an unrelated reason — a byproduct, a structural consequence, something that happens to be useful but was never maintained for being useful. Does grip separate this from the plant and the bacterium, and does it separate them for the right reason? The second is the co-opted trait: something selected for one problem that now solves a different one and is now maintained for solving the new one. The third is the one that actually started the argument. A network trained by gradient descent can internalize a heuristic that no one specified, that improves its performance on a task, and that it cannot articulate or inspect. Is that a free-floating rationale, a represented rationale, or a third thing? My suspicion — and I'd like the proposal to treat it as a suspicion rather than a conclusion — is that it forces a distinction between a rationale being tracked by a system and a rationale being available to it, and that the free-floating cases are precisely the tracked-but-unavailable ones. If that distinction does the work, the whole debate looks different, because the deflationist and the inflationist are each answering a question about availability while pretending to answer one about tracking.

So: state the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than a mood. Explain why the existing positions leave it open — and be honest about whether the deflationary and inflationary views are really as stuck as I think they are, or whether one of them already has the resources and I have just misdescribed it. Lay out the method as something I would actually do with one day a week for six months, month by month: which cases I would work through, what a grip-based criterion would have to get right for each, and where the criterion is most likely to break. Say what a result would look like — what it would mean for the threshold to be defensible, and what a publishable outcome would be as opposed to merely a clarification. Say what would count as the project failing, and whether failure here means the criterion collapses or merely that it turns out to be the deflationary view in new clothes. And give me an honest assessment of scope: is six months and one day a week enough for the framing I've described, or should the proposal cut the third case and keep only the control and the co-opted trait?

Do not bother with citations, a bibliography, or a literature review. Refer to positions and arguments as positions and arguments and I will add the references myself. I would rather the proposal argue that the question is worth asking and that it can be answered than demonstrate that I have read the right things. My friend will read the draft, so write it to survive someone who thinks the whole notion is a redescription, and who will notice immediately if I have quietly assumed my conclusion.
