I'm a doctoral candidate in philosophy, and in three weeks I have to defend, at a departmental workshop, a thesis about causation by omission that I've been developing for the better part of a year. My claim is that omissions can be genuine causes, but only derivatively: an omission causes an outcome when the agent's failure to act makes a difference to that outcome relative to a baseline fixed not by what the agent could have done but by the norm that applies to the situation — legal, moral, or merely conventional — so that where no norm is in play, talk of an omission's "causing" something is at best pragmatic shorthand. I take this to explain why we hold the lifeguard responsible for the drowning but not the stranger watching from the beach, and why the same non-action counts as a cause in one context and a mere condition in another. I should say up front that my supervisor suspects the normative-anchor move is ad hoc, a patch rather than a theory, and I am not entirely sure she is wrong; I would like the prep sheet to show me where the real pressure lies.

The audience is the awkward part. The workshop is attached to a large interdisciplinary centre, so the room will hold roughly a dozen analytic metaphysicians of causation who cut their teeth on difference-making and pre-emption puzzles and who will want to know how my account handles redundant omission and causation by double prevention; three or four epidemiologists and biostatisticians who think of causes in terms of potential outcomes and interventions and will keep translating everything I say into a treatment-versus-control frame; one tort lawyer who cares only about whether the account yields determinate verdicts a court could apply; and a handful of graduate students who will ask the broad "what does this actually buy us" question. Nobody in the room works primarily on omissions, and nobody will have read my paper closely.

Please act as a philosopher of causation who is also a seasoned workshop survivor, and prepare me for the question period. Begin by brainstorming: think carefully about what each constituency in this room will seize on, and about where their vocabularies will collide with mine. Then give me a prep sheet: the eight to ten objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it — the metaphysician's version, the biostatistician's version, the lawyer's version — with a two-minute reply for each and an honest note on where that reply is soft or where I am bluffing. Tell me what I should concede at the outset to buy goodwill, what I must hold firm on for the thesis to survive, and finish with the one question I am hoping nobody asks and how to answer it if it comes. Please write in British English, and keep the whole thing to something I can realistically rehearse in an afternoon.
