For most of my working life I counted insects; I am now retired and, rather to my surprise, spending my mornings on moral philosophy. One argument in particular has me stuck, and what I want is a piece of conceptual cartography rather than a reading list.

The argument runs roughly as follows: we cannot rule out that insects have morally significant experiences; there are a very great many insects; therefore the expected quantity of morally significant experience in the insect world may dwarf that of the vertebrates, and a low probability does not rescue us, because the numbers are so large. I should like to see the logical space of responses laid out, rather than defended.

Please build me a decision tree, for a session of the philosophy of biology group I have joined, that begins where a reader grants that insects may be sentient with some non-negligible probability, and ends at a named position on what follows. Work from the distinctions rather than from a list of authors, and let the tree carry between eight and twelve leaves. I want the branches to include at least these:

- whether the reader treats the claim that insects are sentient as a determinate matter of fact about which we are merely ignorant, as an indeterminate matter on which there is no fact of the matter, or as a normative placeholder;
- whether the probability in question is a credence about a binary property or an expectation over a graded one;
- how expected value is aggregated — plainly linear, damped by a precautionary or worst-case rule, or gated by a threshold below which the possibility is simply not counted;
- what the unit of moral accounting is — individuals, lives, life-years, or moments of experience — and whether future insects count;
- whether the argument needs the further premise that insect lives go badly on balance, or is neutral as to the sign of an insect's welfare;
- whether the reader accepts a stopping rule that prevents the same reasoning from reaching mites, nematodes and eventually creatures we are confident are not sentient, or accepts the extension;
- whether the correct response to the uncertainty is to act now or to buy information first.

Give each leaf a short descriptive label — a standard name where one exists, but no attributions, dates or reading list; I want the shape of the space, not a bibliography — together with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment. Include one worked toy case, clearly hypothetical, with numbers chosen so that the linear rule and the threshold rule return opposite verdicts. Phrase every node as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to, flag any leaves your tree cannot separate, and note which combinations of answers no one occupies, saying in each case whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Please write in British English.
