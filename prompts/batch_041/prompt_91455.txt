For the past six years, since retiring from a career at the bench, I have been reading my way into epistemology, and I have arrived at a conclusion I now want to defend at a departmental colloquium. I would like you to prepare me for the objections I will actually face there.

My thesis is this. The cost of our growing dependence on AI systems for cognitive labor is usually framed as a loss of skill, a loss of employment, or a fit of nostalgia. I want to argue that it is an epistemic harm in its own right — one that the frameworks my audience will bring are built to miss. I distinguish two things that are routinely conflated. The first is the capacity to arrive at a correct result; call it performance. The second is what I call reconstructive grip: the ability to regenerate the reasoning from a different starting point, to say which perturbations would break the result and which would leave it standing, and to situate the result within the web of dependencies that make it what it is. My claim is that AI dependence can leave performance entirely intact — and can even leave a thin sense of understanding intact — while degrading reconstructive grip, and that this degradation is a genuine loss of epistemic standing rather than a mere reallocation of labor.

The audience will be a philosophy department colloquium: mostly epistemologists and philosophers of science, two or three cognitive scientists who work on distributed and extended cognition, and one machine-learning researcher. They are not hostile, but they are exacting, and they will press on whether my central notion is even well defined. The epistemologists will want to know whether reconstructive grip is factive, whether it is a species of knowledge or a distinct epistemic good, and whether I am quietly assuming that the solitary knower is the proper unit of analysis. The cognitive scientists will regard the scaffolding of thought as the normal human condition rather than a recent affliction. The ML researcher will treat opacity as a transient engineering problem that better interpretability tools will retire.

Prepare me a Q&A sheet for the question period: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience — not the ones a technology critic or a luddite would raise — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, each phrased the way that particular audience would put it, with a two-minute reply and an explicit note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, before anyone forces it from me, and which single objection I must have a real answer to if the thesis is to survive the hour. Include, separately, the question I am hoping nobody asks. I would rather be shown where I am wrong now than discover it in the room. I have no interest in citations or reading lists; give me the arguments themselves, and if a reply depends on an empirical premise I cannot establish, say so plainly.
