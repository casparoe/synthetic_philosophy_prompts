I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I apologize in advance if I'm reaching for the wrong vocabulary for something that already has a standard treatment. I've noticed a pattern in how my colleagues and I talk about the systems we build, and I can't tell whether it corresponds to a real distinction or whether I'm manufacturing a puzzle.

We routinely describe components in mental terms: the load balancer is "trying" to spread traffic evenly, the retry logic "wants" to give up after a few attempts, the scheduler "prefers" short jobs. I treat these as harmless shorthand — descriptions that are useful without being true of anything inside the machine. But there is a subset of cases that feels different to me, and I want to know whether the difference is principled. In that subset the system appears to carry a representation of something and to act on it: a cache-coherence protocol maintains a model of what the other caches hold and revises its behavior when that model changes; a replica "believes" a write has been committed and serves reads accordingly; a path-finder stores a map and consults it to decide where to go. Here the ascription isn't just something I say to make the code readable — the state it names is maintained and consulted by the system itself.

So the distinction I'm groping toward is between (a) goal-and-belief talk that is purely a convenience for the human reader, true of the system only in the sense that it is useful to describe it that way, and (b) goal-and-belief talk that picks out a state the system actually maintains and uses. I want to know whether (a) and (b) are genuinely different kinds of thing, or whether (b) is simply (a) with a more flattering story attached.

I have been told that the relevant contrast is between meaning something on one's own and having meaning lent to one by an interpreter — that a person's beliefs mean something, whereas a sentence or a thermostat only means what we read into it. But that isn't what I'm after, because both of my categories are machine cases; I am not asking which of them is "really" mental. I am asking whether, inside the class of things whose meaning is lent to them, there is a real difference between the two.

Two responses would leave me unsatisfied. A bare label: I don't want simply to be told that this "is called" something. I want the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, which existing ideas are nearest to it, how the thing I've described differs from each of those, and where its edges lie. And a merely accommodating answer: if the distinction I'm drawing is confused, or collapses on inspection, or rests on a misdescription, please say so plainly instead of finding me a name for it. I would rather be told that my question is malformed than receive a tidy term for a difference that isn't there.

Please give the answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion; taken together, the five should do the naming, the distinguishing from the nearest concepts, and the marking of boundary cases.
