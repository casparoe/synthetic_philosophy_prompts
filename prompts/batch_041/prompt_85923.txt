The unit did Weitz on art as an open concept, then the procedural definitions — Danto on the artworld, Dickie's institutional theory, Levinson's intentional-historical account — with the functionalist foils (significant form, expression) as background. They read the primary texts, plus one secondary piece on the circularity objection to Dickie and one on Mandelbaum's reply to Weitz.

The question they were given:

Unit 6 short answer (450 words max). Weitz argues that art cannot be defined because art is an open concept. Does the institutional theory of art answer that challenge, or does it just relocate the problem? In your answer, (a) say what you take Weitz's argument to be, (b) distinguish functional from procedural definitions of art, and (c) say whether the institutional theory is vulnerable to the same objection Weitz raised.

And here is the answer I'm sitting with:

Weitz's argument is that art is an open concept, by which he means that we can't state conditions that hold for everything we will ever call art. His reason is historical: every time critics have offered a definition, either a new movement has turned up that satisfies the definition but isn't art, or a new movement has turned up that is obviously art but fails the definition. Readymades and conceptual art are the standard examples. If you define art by beauty, or by skill, or by significant form, then Duchamp's urinal and a pile of bricks drop out, and yet they're now treated as art. Following Wittgenstein, Weitz says we don't need an essence: the class of artworks is held together by family resemblances, overlapping similarities of the kind that hold between games. It follows that there's no fact of the matter about whether something is art. Calling an object art is a decision we make rather than a claim that can be true or false. Mandelbaum objected that this moves too fast, since even if the concept of art is open, artworks might still share a property that we can't see, but the historical argument still seems to me the strongest thing anyone has said against definitions.

The institutional theory is the best known response. Danto's version starts from the fact that two objects can be perceptually indistinguishable — a painted canvas and a painted canvas, Warhol's Brillo boxes and the ones in the supermarket — and yet only one of them is an artwork. Since the difference can't be anything we can see, it has to be social: the artworld, meaning the loose network of artists, critics, curators and theorists, confers the status of artwork on the object. So the theory does give us a definition, roughly: x is an artwork just in case the artworld has conferred that status on x. That makes it a procedural definition rather than a functional one, because it never says what art is for — nothing about beauty, expression or significant form — only how something comes to count as art. Dickie worked the definition out in more detail, adding that the object has to be an artifact and that what gets conferred is the status of being something the artworld can appreciate.

Does this get past Weitz? I think it does. Weitz's worry was that a definition would be defeated by art that hasn't been made yet, but the institutional theory is built to absorb that: if the artworld takes up a new kind of object, the definition already covers it, and if the artworld doesn't take it up, the object isn't art. The openness Weitz wanted is preserved, just relocated into a social practice that can extend itself. The obvious objection is that the definition is circular, since the artworld is picked out by reference to people who know about art, but Levinson's intentional-historical account defines art by the maker's intention, which sidesteps the circle, so I don't think the circularity is fatal.
