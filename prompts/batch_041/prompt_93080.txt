I have been trying to defend a deflationary account of akrasia in a seminar on practical rationality, and I want to know how much exposure the account actually has. The account runs roughly as follows. What we call weakness of will is, in the philosophically central cases, not a contest between an agent's all-things-considered judgment and a distinct faculty of will. It is a contest between preference orderings indexed to different times, and the pattern is generated by hyperbolic discounting: when both options lie in the future, the agent prefers the larger-later good; as the smaller-sooner good approaches, the discount curve steepens and the agent prefers it now. The long-run self is not defeated by weakness; it is outvoted by a temporally closer self. On this view, the interesting normative work is not strengthening willpower but designing commitments, precommitment devices, and choice architecture that bind the later self, and the traditional puzzle of akrasia is either dissolved or relocated to an engineering problem of self-binding. My friends and I have been using a crude test, which we call the frozen-preference test: if we could freeze the agent's discount curve at the moment of judgment and hold it fixed through the moment of action, would the akratic choice disappear? If it would, the deflationary account gains; if it would not, the account loses. I am not asking for a bibliography, reading list, or citations. I want a map of the account's exposure.

Tell me which observations, arguments, intuitions, formal results, or empirical patterns would genuinely count against this account, and which would be merely awkward. Rank them from potentially fatal to mildly embarrassing. I am especially interested in three pressure points: first, synchronic akrasia, where an agent at a single time judges that A is best all things considered and yet chooses B, with no change in the discount curve between judgment and action; second, cases in which the agent does not experience the later preference as a preference at all, but as an alien urge, compulsion, or intrusion; third, cases in which the agent's long-run preference is not a stable given but is itself constructed or revised by the act of choosing. I also want to know what a committed defender of the deflationary account should concede as counting against it, as opposed to what its critics would say, and whether the account can be qualified until it becomes unfalsifiable. If it can, is that a defect for a view of this kind, or is the account better understood as a research program whose immunity is a feature rather than a bug? Finally, since I begin sympathetic to the account, tell me what I should find most persuasive on the other side.
