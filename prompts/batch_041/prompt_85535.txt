I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy, and I have to file a dissertation prospectus before the end of the term. The prospectus must include a detailed plan for the first paper — the one my advisor calls the safe chapter, meaning something I can plausibly finish in about nine months — and I want that plan to be about Nozick's tracking theory of knowledge. My interest is not the usual one. The theory gets attacked from two directions, and I have come to think the two attacks are one attack seen from two sides. The first is closure: because the tracking conditions are counterfactual and get evaluated case by case, the theory lets me know that I have hands while denying that I know I am not a handless brain in a vat, and nearly everyone treats that result as either a reductio or a cost to be paid in embarrassment. The second is what I have been calling the method problem: those counterfactuals have to be evaluated relative to some way of arriving at the belief — seeing the barn, reading the letter, doing the sum in my head — and the theory, as it is usually written, carries a clause fixing that method without giving any principled account of how coarse or fine methods are, so a single case can be made to come out either way depending on whether we describe the subject as perceiving or as perceiving a barn-shaped expanse. Whether that clause belongs to the theory's official statement or is a repair that got quietly folded into it is itself something I would like the proposal to settle, or at least to be written so that it does not matter.

What I have been chewing on since a seminar last month is that these are not two problems. Closure fails precisely because the tracking conditions are evaluated along a route from the subject to the fact rather than at the level of the proposition believed, and the method problem is just the question of how finely we carve those routes. If that is right, then the standard repairs — bolting a closure principle onto the theory, or restricting the counterfactuals to whichever method the subject actually used — are treating a structural feature as a defect. What I want to explore instead is a deliberately route-first reconstruction, on which knowledge is primarily a property of a subject's way of getting at the fact and only derivatively a property of the belief formed. I do not yet know whether that reconstruction is a genuine third option or a relabeling of one of the existing ones, and that uncertainty is exactly what the proposal is meant to resolve.

So what I need from you is a short research proposal, roughly 1,500 words, written in my voice and aimed at my committee, that turns this into a project someone could actually evaluate. Please do not include a bibliography, and do not lean on names and dates: talk about positions and arguments, and I will add the references myself. Use the following sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths. First, "The question" (about 150 words): state the question so that it has a determinate answer, something a reader could in principle mark as settled one way or the other rather than a topic announcement. Second, "What is already settled, and what is not" (about 300 words): say why the existing options leave my question open, and include the possibility that the route-first reconstruction collapses into a position already on the table, with a statement of what would have to be true for it not to. Third, "The angle" (about 300 words): the reconstruction stated as a thesis, what it predicts that its rivals do not, and why this approach beats the obvious alternatives — patching closure, dropping the counterfactuals in favor of a safety condition, or keeping the theory and simply biting the bullet on closure. Fourth, "Plan of work" (about 400 words): what I would actually do, month by month across the nine months, including what counts as a test case, which cases I would run the reconstruction against, and how I would tell whether it survives them. Fifth, "What a result would look like" (about 200 words): the deliverable, and what success amounts to at three grades — a modest result, a good result, and the best result I could reasonably hope for. Sixth, "How it fails, and how big it is" (about 150 words): what would count as the project failing, and an honest assessment of whether this fits in nine months or needs to be cut down. My advisor will also want to know what happens if the method problem turns out to be unsolvable in the general case — if there is no principled way to individuate routes and every individuation is a pragmatic choice — so please give that a real answer rather than a promissory note.
