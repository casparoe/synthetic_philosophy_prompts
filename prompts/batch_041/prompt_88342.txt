I'm a software engineer by trade — twelve years in machine learning infrastructure, no formal training in philosophy beyond a lot of reading on my own — and I've been asked to lead the session at a monthly discussion group I've been attending for the past year. The group meets out of a local university's physics outreach office, and it draws a mixed crowd of about twenty: a retired cosmologist who worked on inflationary models and thinks the multiverse is an unfalsifiable dodge; a Bayesian statistician from the statistics department who is scrupulous about what a probability claim actually requires; a philosopher of religion who teaches at a nearby seminary and uses the fine-tuning argument in his apologetics course; and a rotating handful of engineers, physicians, and curious retirees who mostly want to know whether any of this is real. I have thirty minutes to present and then a long question period, and it is the question period I am worried about — the last session ended with two people talking over each other for twenty minutes, which is part of why I want to be prepared.

My position, which I'll sketch so you know what the objections are aiming at: I think the fine-tuning argument fails, but not for the usual reasons. I am not going to argue that the constants could have been different, or that a multiverse is more probable than God. My claim is that the premise of the argument — that the life-permitting region of parameter space is "small" — is not a well-posed quantity at all. I have spent my working life tuning hyperparameters, and one thing you learn quickly is that how much of a space works depends entirely on how you parameterize it; any smooth change of variables stretches and squeezes volumes, so a region that is a sliver in one coordinate system is a fat slab in another. When someone tells me the cosmological constant is tuned to one part in some astronomically large number, my first question is: in what coordinates, and who decided those were the coordinates? As far as I can tell, the argument's persuasive force comes from a parameterization nobody has ever justified, and once you notice that, the inference from "small region" to "cries out for explanation" has nothing to stand on. I will be honest about where this leaves me: I suspect the multiverse is still the least bad option, though I can see that my own objection, if it works, removes the main reason anyone had for taking the multiverse seriously — so I may be sawing off the branch I'm sitting on.

What I need from you is a prep sheet for the question period, not a defense of the thesis. Assume the role of a philosopher of physics who has sat through a hundred of these and knows exactly how this particular room will react; before you start writing, work out for yourself what each person there believes and what they would need to hear, then build the sheet on that. Go through the audience member by member and tell me the objections I will actually get — phrased the way each of them would phrase it, in their own vocabulary, not translated into neutral philosophical prose. Order them by how much damage they would do if I fumble them rather than by how likely they are, though flag the ones that are both. For each objection, give me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a reply that fits in two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest — I would much rather find out now than in the room. Tell me which single objection, if it lands, kills the whole talk, and which ones I should simply accept and move past without a fight. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Be committed: make definite calls about which objections have real force and which are bluster, and commit to a verdict on my thesis instead of handing me a menu of options — if you think the parameterization point is a genuine problem for the argument, say so and say why; if you think it is a clever-sounding move that a competent cosmologist will dismantle in thirty seconds, say that instead. Write in American English, and skip the citations and reading lists; I won't be able to check them, and I would rather have your reasoning than a bibliography.
