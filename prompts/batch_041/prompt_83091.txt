I have been arguing with a friend in a cognitive science reading group about the four grades of creature usually credited to Dennett: the Darwinian creature, which is simply born with a design fixed by natural selection; the Skinnerian creature, which can vary its behaviour and keep whatever gets reinforced; the Popperian creature, which can run a model of the world internally and so let its hypotheses, rather than its body, be the thing that dies; and the Gregorian creature, whose design is stored partly outside it, in information taken from other minds. I have ended up holding something more specific than the bare taxonomy, and I want to know how much it is exposed. My claim is that the four grades are not rungs on one scale of intelligence or computational power but a succession of relocations of the locus of trial and error — genome, then the individual's own behaviour and its consequences, then the individual's internal model, then a public and transmissible artifact or informational niche — and that each later grade replaces the earlier one as the primary selector rather than merely supplementing it. The corollary I care about, and suspect is where the view is most fragile, is that no creature has a grade simpliciter: grades are modes that can coexist within one animal and are relative to a task domain, so the taxonomy is really a taxonomy of capacities rather than of creatures.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure, sorted from the defeaters that would be decisive to those that would be merely awkward, with an honest separation of the two, and an account of what the world would have to look like for the view to come out false. In particular, which findings that may already exist bear on it, and how heavily — one-trial, long-delay taste-aversion learning and the misbehaviour-of-organisms literature, which look awkward for a picture of the second grade as contiguity-driven reinforcement; the apparently model-based planning of corvids and apes, which threatens to dissolve the line between the second and third grades; observational social learning in insects and cumulative cultural transmission in non-human animals, which would make the fourth grade a difference of degree rather than of kind; and the possibility that the capacity for culture is itself an ordinary biological adaptation, in which case the fourth grade collapses back into the first and my succession is gene–culture coevolution wearing a ladder costume. I would also like to know which of these a defender of the view should concede as genuine counterevidence and which they are entitled to dismiss as the same facts redescribed at a different grain, and what my position predicts that its rivals do not — a single continuous dimension of model depth, or a plain gradualism that denies any joints here at all — since I would be most convinced by a counterexample that is not merely awkward for my labels but leaves a rival with the better explanation of a specific case. Finally, tell me whether the view can be falsified at all, or whether the replacement-not-supplement claim has been stated so loosely that any finding can be absorbed by saying the earlier grade merely persists in the background; and if it is that insulated, whether that is a fatal defect in a taxonomy or an acceptable feature of a scheme whose job is description rather than prediction. For what it is worth, I would take most seriously an argument that the ladder is doing no explanatory work that a careful inventory of mechanisms would not do better, and least seriously a complaint that the grades are fuzzy at the edges.
