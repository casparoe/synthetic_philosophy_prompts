I'm a second-year law student, and for the past month I've been sitting in on a seminar about how legal systems handle the aftermath of abusive regimes. Something in the material keeps nagging at me, and I've been arguing about it with a friend who is doing a PhD in philosophy; she says it all comes down to the old quarrel about whether an unjust law is still a law. I don't think that is the interesting part, and I'd like you to help me get the analysis right. The pattern is this. Nearly every legal order treats the ban on retroactive criminal liability as fundamental: you cannot be punished for conduct that was not an offence when you committed it. Nearly every military and police code also provides that a subordinate who acts on a superior's order is not criminally liable for what he does under it unless the order was manifestly unlawful, a standard that asks the subordinate, in the moment and without advice, to see the illegality from the face of the order. And after a regime falls, successor courts routinely convict officials for conduct that the fallen regime's own statutes expressly authorized, on the reasoning that an enactment can be so monstrous that it never was law, so that the conduct was unlawful even at the time. What makes that last move stranger is that the same courts go on applying the old regime's law to property, contracts and marriages: the discredited law turns out to be invalid in the criminal courtroom and valid in the registry of deeds.

What I want is a reconstruction of what this bundle of practices presupposes, not a history of it and not a survey of the famous positions. Take the bundle at face value and ask what view of legal validity and of legal obligation it only makes sense under. Does it require that validity turn on the moral character of a norm, or can a view on which validity is settled entirely by who enacted what, in the right way, accommodate all of it without strain? Which of the elements is genuinely entailed by the practice and which is merely something the practice happens to fit? Which rival views does the bundle quietly rule out, and does the manifest-illegality standard in particular presuppose that moral facts are accessible to an ordinary person under pressure, and if so, what sort of moral theory that commits us to? Is the practice consistent with itself, or does the split between the criminal and the private-law treatment of the old regime show that the implicit theory is not a single theory of validity at all but something more local, about what courts may do to whom and who is competent to make the determination?

Finally, suppose a legal order announced that it was abandoning the moralized view and would recognize as law anything enacted in the prescribed way, whatever its content. Tell me concretely what would have to change in the institutions: what successor courts could and could not still do, and what would have to be handled instead by amnesty, truth commission or fresh legislation.
