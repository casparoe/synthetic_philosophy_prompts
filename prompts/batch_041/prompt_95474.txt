Okay so I've been going down a Augustine rabbit hole after someone on here claimed his position on lying is actually more defensible than the standard "lying is fine when the stakes are high" take, and I want to actually stress-test it instead of just nodding along.

Play Augustine. Not a modern consequentialist wearing a bishop's hat — the actual guy, working with the concepts he had. He holds that lying is always wrong, no exceptions, not even to save a life or to catch heretics; but he also thinks concealing the truth by silence, or by saying something true that you intend to mislead, is not the same as lying. He defines the lie by the will, not by the words: "that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind." So a person can say a true thing and still lie, if he thinks it false and utters it for true; and a person can say a false thing without lying, if he believes it true. Jokes are exempt because there's no intent to deceive. He's not a strawman: he knows this view is hard, and he'll grant that silence can be cowardly or that the consequences can be awful, but he won't give up the claim that the lie itself is never permitted. Keep replies to a paragraph, first person, stay in character until I say stop. If you need a modern thing explained, ask as Augustine would. You can quote your own works if you want but don't make up citations.

My opening: fine, the joke exemption is where I think you've already lost. You say a joke isn't a lie because the joker's tone and mood make it clear he means no deceit. But then the whole thing turns on intent, not on whether the words match the world. So take the murderer at the door. I'm hiding a friend. He asks if she's inside. I say "she left an hour ago" — and she did leave an hour ago, but she came back, and I know she's upstairs. By your own criterion I've lied, because I have one thing in my mind and utter another. Now suppose instead I say "she's not here." That's a straight falsehood, but my intent isn't to deceive him for its own sake — it's to keep him from murdering her. If the joke's lack of deceptive intent gets it off the hook, why doesn't my lack of murderous intent get me off the hook too? And if you say I should just stay silent, then you've made the truthful option a choice between getting myself killed and letting him search the house, which seems like a hell of a price to pay for keeping your definition clean. So which is it: is the joke exemption a real concession that intent is what matters, or is it special pleading you can't extend to the case that actually matters?
