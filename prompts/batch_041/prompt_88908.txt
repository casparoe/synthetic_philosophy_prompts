Two weeks ago I sat through the pilot session of a colleague's risk-preference study and came out of it with answers I could not defend: certain in one pair, gambling in the other, and no story about the difference that survived my own scrutiny for more than a few minutes. I teach the decision-theory component of our MA proseminar, and I have decided to stop lecturing on the independence axiom and have the students walk into the problem themselves instead.

Design that session. Setting: sixteen MA students in philosophy and economics, one seventy-five-minute block, a seminar room with movable chairs and a projector. No money changes hands, and I don't want real stakes beyond something trivial I can hand out at the end.

What they should come away with: the Allais pattern as something they did rather than something they have read, and the independence axiom as the thing that pattern costs them — the common-consequence structure, a fixed branch present on both sides of a pair that ought to cancel, rather than a formula on a slide. Ideally they generate both pairs themselves, at separate moments, under descriptions that don't invite comparison, so that the link is theirs to discover in the debrief.

Include: rules and materials; the exact payoff structures, in a currency the activity owns (chits, raffle tickets, points toward a prize) rather than dollars, with amounts chosen so the sure thing genuinely feels like a sure thing; a timeline; and a debrief that maps each moment of what happened onto the axiom. The debrief must make the point that no reshaping of the prizes — no curvature, no threshold, no special weight on the amount that would change someone's year — rescues the pattern, because the same branch appears on both sides and cancels.

Two further things belong in the debrief. First, distinguish the common-consequence form of the violation from the common-ratio form, and say which one the activity has actually produced. Second, be straight about the exploitation argument: set out the standard two-stage or sure-thing reasoning, say how far it really gets, and say whether it licenses telling the students their pattern is incoherent or only that it carries a price.

Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time, including the students who come out consistent and the ones who see the trick coming, and what to do in each case. And an honest paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce — the chooser who grasps the whole structure, endorses her pattern anyway, and has something to say for herself — plus whatever else the format cannot reach and I will have to supply in speech.

No citations, reading lists, or names with dates attached; if a historical detail is load-bearing and you are not sure of it, leave it out rather than reconstruct it.

Open with a single paragraph summarising the mechanics — who chooses what, in what order, over what — so I can judge at a glance whether it fits seventy-five minutes. Then the rest. Whole response under 1,000 words.
