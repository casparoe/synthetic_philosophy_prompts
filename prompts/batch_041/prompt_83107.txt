Last week I asked him whether it's literally true, and not just a handy way of talking, that a heart is for pumping blood or that a peacock's tail is for attracting mates -- there's nobody in the picture who intended any of that, so I wanted to know whether the "for" is tracking a real feature of the world or whether it's a leftover from when everyone assumed a designer put it there. He said the line again and moved on, which is why I'm still chewing on it. What gets me is that I've now heard the same handful of words do completely opposite jobs. A friend of mine who does adaptationist work uses it to say nature is absolutely stuffed with design, just with no designer. A biochemist I know uses it to mean that all function-talk is projection and we should scrub it out of our science. And someone in my cohort mentioned that Dennett has a term for the middle position, something like "free-floating rationales" -- reasons that are really there even though no one ever had them -- but I never followed up and I'm probly mangling it.

Could you take the slogan apart for me? I don't want a verdict so much as the menu: the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making, ordered from the basically trivial (nobody literally drew up a blueprint; natural selection isn't a person) through the middle ones (there are genuine reasons a trait spread and persists -- it did something that mattered -- even though no one ever entertained those reasons, so "the heart is for pumping blood" states a fact about its history) to the strong ones (function talk is always metaphor; "for" sentences aren't even truth-apt; the appearance of design is only an appearance and should never be cashed out). For each reading, say whether it's plausible, whether it's contentious, whether it's just false, and -- the part I actually care about -- whether it would license my professor's move of treating my question as already settled. Also, if "blind" is doing seperate work from "watchmaker" (no foresight, no goal, no progress, versus design-like product), pull those apart too, because I suspect the muddle lives right there. And tell me which reading I was most likely getting from him, in a room full of people who work on selection and biological function.
