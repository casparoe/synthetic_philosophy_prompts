I'm a distributed-systems engineer — I build and tune cluster schedulers, so I spend my days with Lagrangian duals, shadow prices, auctions, and feedback loops that are only sort of gradient descent. I know convex duality and dual decomposition cold, I've read enough RL to be dangerous, and I took exactly one econ course in college, so I know the words supply and demand and not much else. I keep hearing that Hayek and Mises had an arguement with Lange back in the 30s about whether a socialist economy could allocate resources rationally, and that it all comes down to prices being information. That sounds like a slogan to me and I want to know how far it actually goes.

So: take the three positions separately — Mises (no prices, so no rational calculation), Hayek (the knowledge is dispersed and the price system is how it gets used), Lange (a central planning board can set prices by trial and error until things clear, and that's good enough) — and restate each one as precisely as you can in the vocabulary I actually use. I mean the real vocabulary, not a gesture at it: constrained optimization and its dual, multipliers as prices, Dantzig-Wolfe-style decomposition where a coordinator publishes prices and the subproblems respond, ADMM, auction and market-based scheduling, congestion pricing on a shared link, dual ascent where queue lengths stand in for excess demand, spot prices, price of anarchy. If a claim in the debate is about convergence, say what it's a convergence claim about. If it's about information, name the information-theoretic object. If it's about incentives, say which mechanism it's a claim about.

Here's a concrete thing to map onto, because that's how I'll know whether the translation is honest. Our scheduler has a central allocator that hands each team's job manager a budget in fake currency; they bid for capacity; the allocator nudges prices up and down based on queue lengths until demand roughly matches supply. It works fine. Twice now, a team has wanted a resource that didn't exist yet — a new accelerator with a weird memory hierarchy — and there was no price for it, no bids, no queue, until somebody built the hardware and wrote a shim, after which it got priced like everything else. And our feedback loop assumes the demand curve doesn't move because we changed the prices, which is obviously false: teams notice something got cheap and go build things that use it.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. For each of the three positions: what carries over cleanly, what only carries over if you distort it, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Name the mismatches specifically instead of hand-waving. A few things I'm suspicious about, and I want these addressed head on:

Whether "the market computes the optimum" is even the right shape of claim. My instinct is that a competitive equilibrium isn't the argmax of any single objective, it's a fixed point of everyone's best responses, and that this matters for whether decentralized optimization is the right analogy or a misleading one. If I'm right, say what breaks in my picture.

Convexity. My duality intuitions assume no gap. Increasing returns, nonconvexities, things that only pay off at scale — is Lange's trial-and-error procedure a dual ascent that can just fail to converge, and did the economists care about this or stipulate it away?

Time. My loop is online, with stale information, and the agents react to my prices and don't wait politely for the coordinator to finish an iteration. Tatonnement, as I understand it, is closer to a batch method doing repeated passes over a fixed dataset. Is the out-of-equilibrium trading that Hayek cares about expressible in my terms at all, or is that one of the things that just doesn't survive?

New coordinates. Everything I do assumes the action space is fixed and I'm tuning within it. The new-accelerator story above looks like the real content of Hayek's point, and in my terms it looks like architecture search rather than hyperparameter tuning — the thing being searched over is the variable set of the objective. Does that translate, or am I overreading?

Tacit knowledge. When I run dual decomposition, the multiplier really does carry information no individual subproblem has, and the coordinator can't articulate it either — it's just a number that makes the subproblems behave. That feels like a genuine translation of Hayek's claim and I want you to say so if it is. But I suspect it translates too well, in the sense that it makes his point sound like a banal fact about distributed algorithms, and the residue might be in what he thought the number could and couldn't do.

Finally, the verdict: for each thing that didn't survive, is that a hole in my vocabulary — some structure I don't have a name for — or evidence that the original claim was doing less work than it sounded like? I'm happy to be told that Mises's version collapses into a stipulation the moment you let me define a scalar objective, and that Hayek is the one with actual content, but only if the translation shows that rather than me wanting it to be true. Don't be nice about it.
