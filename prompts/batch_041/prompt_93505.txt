Question (10 points): Epiphenomenalism is the view that experiences are caused by brain states but themselves cause nothing at all. There's a long-standing objection to it: if that's true, then your judgment that you're in pain is not caused by your pain. So either it's a mystery why your judgments about your experiences line up with those experiences, or those judgments aren't really about the experiences in the first place. Set out the objection, give the strongest reply available to the epiphenomenalist, and say whether the reply succeeds. Be careful to keep apart two things the objection tends to run together: why the judgments are reliable, and why they have the content they do.

Student's answer: Start with the objection. Epiphenomenalists say experiences have no effects. But a judgment like "I'm in pain" is an effect of something in the head, so if the pain itself causes nothing, the judgment can't have been caused by the pain. It must be caused by whatever brain state the pain goes with. The objection then presses: that leaves it a coincidence that our judgments about our experiences are accurate. If the pain plays no part in producing the judgment, then the machinery that produces it isn't sensitive to whether there's any pain there, and it would be a lucky accident that we ever get it right, which is hard to believe. And if you insist the judgment is still about the pain, you owe an account of how a state that causes nothing gets itself represented in a state that does.

The best reply open to the epiphenomenalist is to deny that any coincidence is needed. The brain state that causes the experience also causes the judgment. So the judgment isn't caused by the experience, but it's reliably correlated with it, because both are effects of one common cause. No miracle and no pre-established harmony is required: the neural state does all the work and the experience rides along. This is why the view isn't self-contradictory, and it's also why we shouldn't say the judgment is unconnected to the experience, since the neural state just is the experience as described from the outside, the judgment does trace back to the experience after all, via the physical description of it.

I think this reply works, at least against the objection as stated. It handles the reliability half of the problem completely, and it handles the content half too: because the judgment is reliably triggered if and only if the experience occurs, the judgment is a state that represents the experience, which is exactly what being about something amounts to. So the paradox dissolves and epiphenomenalism survives. The only thing the reply doesn't do is explain why the brain state gives rise to an experience in the first place, but that's the general problem of consciousness, not a special problem for epiphenomenalism.
