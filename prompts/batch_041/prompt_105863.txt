The question (worth 20):

"Lemkin coined the word genocide, but the legal category that word now names is narrower than his original idea. (i) What did Lemkin mean by genocide, and how does the 1948 Convention's definition differ from it? (ii) Someone says: 'If it isn't legally genocide, it isn't morally genocide either -- the lawyers already drew the line.' Assess that claim. (iii) Why is the question of what we call an atrocity politically loaded?"

The student's answer:

"Lemkin came up with the word in the middle of the war, to name what the Nazis were doing to the Jews and other peoples. He made it out of the Greek genos, meaning a group or a tribe, and the Latin caedere, to kill, so it literally means killing a group. The real innovation was that the victim was the group as such, not just the individual people one at a time: the crime aimed at wiping out a people, and it could be carried out by attacking a culture and a language just as much as by shooting people. He wanted a name for a genuinely new kind of crime so the law would have something to charge.

The 1948 Convention turned the idea into a legal offence, but narrowed it, and Lemkin resisted some of that narrowing. The definition needs an intent to destroy a protected group, and the groups it protects are national, ethnic, racial and religious ones. Lemkin had actually wanted political and social groups in there too, but it was Lemkin himself who insisted on leaving them out, so the definition would stay tight and actually be enforceable. The Convention also cut the cultural side of things, keeping only acts aimed at physical destruction, which is why cultural genocide isn't a crime under the treaty. So because the definition is so narrow, plenty of atrocities people call genocide don't legally count.

On the second bit: I basically think the lawyers have already made the moral call for us. They fought over every word of that definition, so if a case doesn't fit, that's a decent sign it isn't genocide in the moral sense either, and calling it genocide anyway is mostly rhetoric. So the claim is more or less right.

On the third: naming is political because whoever gets to slap the label on controls how everyone reacts. Saying genocide triggers legal duties and international attention, and refusing the label lets a state dodge all of that. So the fight over the word is really a fight over whether anything gets done."
