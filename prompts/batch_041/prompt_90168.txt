Consider two agents who disagree across a set of independent questions — for instance, how to divide a fixed budget between animal welfare and biomedical research, how much civil liberty to trade away for security, and how strictly to enforce a contested norm of public speech — and suppose each has a complete and consistent ranking of the possible settlements. Neither can persuade the other to adopt its ranking wholesale. The proposal on the table is that they can nonetheless reach a settlement both prefer to continued disagreement by logrolling: each concedes on the questions it ranks lowest in order to prevail on the questions it ranks highest.

Make the case that such a settlement is a genuine instance of gains from trade — that it constitutes a Pareto improvement judged by each agent's own values — and then meet the strongest objection you can construct, namely that the analogy to economic exchange breaks down because the relevant goods are neither transferable nor divisible: what one agent gains is not something the other can hand over, so there may be nothing to trade. In developing the argument, address at least the following: (i) whether logrolling is well-defined when an agent's priorities are lexicographic or partly incommensurable, and whether conceding on an incommensurable value is even coherent; (ii) whether an agent who concedes on a low-priority question has sold out or merely traded, and whether the distinction between changing what one values and accepting a settlement one values less does any real work; and (iii) whether the Pareto claim survives the possibility that the two agents disagree not only about rankings but about which questions are legitimately on the table.
