I write about science and ideas for a newsletter, and last week I sat in on a philosophy of language reading group because I'm doing a piece on how we decide where vague words stop. I came out with a headache and an arguement I can't shake. A grad student put it on the whiteboard and called it "the reason supervaluationism eats itself." I want to know if she's right, and if so where the body's buried. I've got no signal in this building, so please work it out from what's below.

Here's the background she gave us first.

A word like 'bald' is vague because it has borderline cases -- men who are neither clearly bald nor clearly not. The supervaluationist says: stop asking which hair-count is the last bald one, there is no such number. Instead take every admissible way of drawing a sharp line for 'bald' (she called each one a 'precisification'), and say a sentence is true if it comes out true on all of them, false if false on all of them, and indeterminate if the ways disagree. So "there is a sharp line between bald and not-bald" comes out FALSE, even though every individual precisification has a line -- because no single line is the line on all of them. Same trick for 'heap,' 'tall,' 'red.'

Now the argument, the way she wrote it on the board:

(1) Let 'D' abbreviate 'definitely,' so 'DA' is true exactly when A is true on every admissible precisification.
(2) Which precisifications count as admissible is fixed in advance and the same for every sentence we evaluate -- it's just the collection of admissible sharpenings of the language.
(3) So whether A is true on all of them is a single, determinate fact. There's no wiggle room in it: either A holds on every admissible precisification or it doesn't.
(4) Therefore for any A, 'DA' is either true or false, never indeterminate.
(5) 'A is borderline' means, roughly, that A is neither definitely true nor definitely false.
(6) By (4), the claim 'A is borderline' is never itself borderline -- you can't be on the fence about whether you're on the fence.
(7) So there's no such thing as second-order (or third-, or fourth-) vagueness. Vagueness is only ever one level deep.
(8) But higher-order vagueness is real. It can genuinely be borderline whether a shade is borderline-red, or whether a man is borderline-bald. The phenomenon of vagueness has no bottom.
(9) So supervaluationism proves too much -- it flattens a genuinely endless hierarchy into one flat level -- and should be rejected.

Part of me thinks the sneaky bit is (2), that "fixed in advance" line. But I also can't see how (6) is supposed to follow from (4), so maybe it's (4) that's doing too much work. I genuinely can't tell.

Two things I need priced out, not just named. First: if the fix is "the admissible precisifications are themselves vague," does the supervaluationist just get to say that and carry on, or does it break the whole machine? Second: the rival camp she mentioned says vague words DO have a real sharp line, we just can never know where it is -- if she has to give up (2) or (3), does that just hand them the win?

So: which step should she abandon, why did it look innocent, and what does abandoning it cost her? If you think the argument is actually fine and she should just swallow (7), say that instead and defend it.

Finish with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing just the number of the step she should give up (or 'none' if you think it stands).
