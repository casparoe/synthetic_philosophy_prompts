I produce a small interview series for graduate students in a philosophy of education reading group, and next month's guest is a scholar whose work I find genuinely odd in a productive way. Her line is that Whitehead's educational essays have been read far too sentimentally, as a soft paean to interest and enthusiasm, when the real content of the three-phase rhythm she attributes to him — romance, then precision, then generalisation — is structural rather than motivational. On her reading the rhythm is not a developmental stage theory at all: it is scalar or self-similar, recurring within a single seminar hour, within a term, within a degree, within a research career, and the pedagogical art lies in knowing which timescale you are operating on. Her second claim is the one I most want to interrogate. She thinks romance is not a preliminary that precision later supersedes, but the condition under which any precision is worth having, so that the standard institutional sequence — inspire them briefly, then drill them hard, then hope for synthesis — inverts the actual logic. And she has a third, more provocative claim that she tends to skate past: that Whitehead's educational writing sits in real tension with his later metaphysics, because the metaphysics treats becoming as relational all the way down and never cleanly phased, while the pedagogy seems to require discrete phases that a student can be said to have completed or to have been cheated out of.

I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions, ordered so that the interview has an arc rather than a list, and for each question a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The audience will already know the three phases, so no question should give her room merely to restate them. I need at least one question on the bootstrapping problem for romance — what romance is supposed to be available to a student confronting a subject for which she has no antecedent interest and no prior competence, where the standard reply is that the teacher must find the point of contact, and I want the follow-up to press on whether that reply makes romance a fact about the teacher's rhetoric rather than about the learner. I need at least one question on assessment: whether a rhythm whose middle phase is precision can be evaluated at all without the instruments of evaluation freezing the student in that phase, and whether she is willing to accept that her view implies most current grading is not merely imperfect but structurally hostile to the thing being taught. I need at least one question that goes directly at the tension with the metaphysics, phrased so that she cannot resolve it by saying the essays are practical and the metaphysics theoretical. And I want a closing question about whether any of this survives contact with machine tutoring — whether a system can be said to have romance, or to afford it, and what her view predicts about the difference between a student taught by a person and a student taught by something that can generate unlimited precision on demand.

Two constraints. First, press where the objections actually are: the charge that the rhythm is unfalsifiable because any successful learning can be redescribed as some ordering of the three phases, the charge that generalisation is the vaguest of the three and does most of the work while doing none of it, and the charge that the whole scheme is a rationalisation of a particular elite tutorial tradition rather than a claim about learning. Second, do not ask her what to read or whom to read; if a question needs to invoke a position, invoke the position and let her supply the names.
