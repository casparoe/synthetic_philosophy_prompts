I am a high-school student taking an IB Theory of Knowledge course, and my supervisor has allowed me to write my extended essay on whether psychiatric categories are discovered or invented. The handout she gave me sets out the two positions in the philosophy of medicine that I am meant to use. The first is naturalism, which in Boorse's biostatistical version holds that health is the statistically normal efficiency of biological functions and disease is a deviation from species-typical functional design, with no values built into the concepts at all. The second is normativism, which holds that calling something a disease is always already a value judgment about what is harmful, undesirable, or deviant, so that the concepts are evaluative through and through. The case I have settled on is the history of the American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual. It listed homosexuality as a disorder from its first edition in 1952 and removed it in 1973, though it kept a residual diagnosis for people who were distressed by their own orientation and dropped even that only in 1987; more recently, in 2013, the same manual replaced gender identity disorder with gender dysphoria, and the stated reason for the renaming was that the term disorder was stigmatizing, while the diagnosis itself was retained in part so that patients would not lose insurance coverage for treatment. Two further ideas came up in our seminar and I would like them used rather than merely mentioned. The first is what Ian Hacking calls the looping effect, the thought that classifying people can change the people classified, so that the statistical norms a naturalist appeals to may themselves be partly produced by the classification. The second is Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis, which tries to split the difference by saying that a disorder is a biological dysfunction together with the harm that the dysfunction causes. My teacher says the 1973 decision is the classic proof that normativism is right and that naturalism cannot account for psychiatric classification at all, and I suspect that is too quick, but I cannot yet say why.

What I want is a case analysis that runs in both directions, written in British English, with no citations, references, or reading list of any kind, since I will be working from memory when I write it up. Please begin by mapping the episode onto each position. For the naturalist, say what exactly is supposed to be malfunctioning in the 1952 classification, what the relevant reference class would have been, and whether the 1973 removal and the 2013 renaming come out as corrections of earlier mistakes or as evidence that these categories were never natural kinds in the first place; for the normativist, say what the operative values were, whose values they were, and whether anything in the episode is left over that the position cannot explain. Then tell me which features of the episode each position explains well and which it distorts or must explain away, and in particular whether the naturalist has a reply to the 1973 case that amounts to more than a retreat into saying the profession had simply got the biology wrong. After that, work through the two extra ideas: does the looping effect undermine the naturalist's reliance on statistical normality, or can he absorb it, and does the harmful-dysfunction analysis come out stable here, or does it collapse into normativism once the harm component is doing all the work and the dysfunction component cannot be specified independently of it? Finish with a verdict on what the episode shows about the two positions, whether it is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case neither of them anticipated, and where they are simply silent about what the case raises — institutional pressures, insurance, professional self-interest, the stigma the renaming was meant to address — please say so rather than making the theory speak.
