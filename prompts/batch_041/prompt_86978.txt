I'm a novelist and next month I'm giving a paper at a small workshop on partiality — the room is mostly Kantians, a couple of utilitarians, and one moral psychologist who studies how people actually justify what they do to the people closest to them. My line, roughly: Williams's case where a man can save only one of two people, his wife or a stranger, and saves his wife, has been read wrong. The complaint was never that impartial morality hands you a reason; it's that it wants the reason occurrent at the moment you act, and what gets lost then isn't integrity in the abstract, it's a relation — the person in front of you becomes an instance of a category rather than this person. So I claim the same propositional content is harmless when it's merely standing (something you could produce afterwards if someone asked) and corrosive only when it's what you actually deliberate from. I'll test it on a scene from my own novel.

Prepair me for the Q&A: what this room will actually throw at me, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each does, a two-minute reply to each, where each reply is soft, and the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Also tell me which one, if it lands, should actually change my mind rather than make me dig in. 800 words max.
