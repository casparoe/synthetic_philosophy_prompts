Ok so a friend of mine who's a bit of a rationalist-blog guy sent me his write-up of the Condorcet jury theorem and I want to know how much of it I should swallow. His version, roughly:

Condorcet proved that if every voter is more likely than not to get a yes/no question right, and the voters make up their minds independantly, then the more of them you have, the more likely the majority is to be right, and with enough voters the chance of the majority being wrong goes to zero. That's the deep reason democracy works: individuals are mostly noise, but the errors cancel out in a big enough crowd. It's also why majority rule beats rule by experts — a big group of mediocre voters will beat a small panel of smart ones, since the panel is small enough to be dragged off course by a couple of bad calls. Condorcet's other famous result, the one about voting cycles, is basically the flip side of the same coin: once you have more than two options the majority becomes incoherent, but with only two it's practically infallible. The obvious catch is independence, which never really holds, since we all read the same feeds and talk to the same people — so in the end the theorem is really a statement about an ideal world rather than about politics as it is.

I don't know enough to argue with him. Can you go through it and tell me what a theorist would sign off on, what's just a loose way of putting a true thing, and what's actually wrong or a stretch? In particular I'm not sure about the expert-panel claim and about how much the independence thing really damages the result — it sounds like the kind of caveat that either matters a lot or not at all, and I can't tell which. And does his last sentence follow, even if everything before it is fine?
