Imagine that David Hume has been revived for a one-day seminar on contemporary philosophy of induction. The seminar packet contains no historical commentary; it presents a series of modern claims and distinctions that arose long after his death. Your task is to reconstruct, as rigorously as the evidence permits, what Hume would say about each. Do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations; reason from his known commitments and from the structure of the issues.

1. The no-free-lunch results in optimization and learning theory. When performance is averaged uniformly over all possible target functions or problem instances, no algorithm outperforms any other. Some participants treat this as a formal vindication of Humean skepticism: induction cannot be justified in general because every inductive method presupposes a bias, and no bias is universally correct. Others treat it as irrelevant: Hume asked whether we are justified in reasoning from observed regularities to unobserved ones in the actual world, not whether some algorithm dominates on average over all logically possible worlds. Which reading would Hume adopt? Would he treat the theorem as an argument for skepticism, a triviality, or a red herring?

2. The inductive-bias response. A method can succeed on a restricted class when its bias matches the class. A Bayesian places a prior over hypotheses and updates. A Solomonoff-style universal prior assigns higher probability to simpler computable hypotheses and can be optimal up to a constant for computable environments. Does this solve Hume's problem or relocate it? Would Hume distinguish a psychological explanation of our inductive habits from a normative justification, and how would that distinction bear on whether a prior over possible worlds is a premise we are entitled to?

3. The meta-induction proposal. Rather than justify induction by a circular argument, one might show that inductive strategies are meta-inductively optimal: in a long run they track the most successful available prediction strategies. Would Hume regard this as a genuine answer, a pragmatic vindication, or a change of subject? How would he distinguish the claim that we cannot help but use induction from the claim that we are entitled to use induction?

4. A participant says the no-free-lunch theorems bite only when one quantifies over all possible problems, including arbitrarily discontinuous, uncomputable, or adversarially designed ones. Once we condition on the actual universe having some measure of simplicity, regularity, or computability, the theorem no longer supports global inductive skepticism. Would Hume accept the move from all possible worlds to our world? What would he say about the fact that the needed assumption about our world is itself something we seem to know only by induction?

5. Finally, suppose a philosopher offers Hume the following compact position: the problem of induction is a problem about justification, the no-free-lunch theorems are results about average-case performance over problem classes, and the two intersect only if one assumes a uniform prior over problem instances. Would Hume agree that this dissolves the apparent connection, or would he argue that the need for some such prior is precisely the modern form of his original problem? Give a verdict, and state which parts of Hume's own position would have to be revised, if any, to accommodate the modern mathematical results.
