I'm putting together a seminar session on transcendental arguments and I've realised I dont have a clear standard for judging the things. People fight about whether Kant's deduction works, whether Strawson's version works, whether you can run one for other minds or for induction, and it always turns into a fight about the individual argument before anyone has said what a transcendental argument is supposed to deliver in the first place. So give me the spec first, before I read another one.

What I want is the list of requirements anything calling itself a transcendental argument has to meet to be worth the name, and for each one the case or the worry that puts it on the list. Roughly the territory I mean, though push back if some of these arent real requirements:

- it has to be distinguishable from a plain a priori argument, or from inference to the best explanation, or the label is doing no work
- it has to say in what sense the "necessary condition" is necessary -- logically, conceptually, because we cant think otherwise, or just for creatures built like us -- and whether the argument survives under each reading
- it has to face the modest/ambitious question: does it reach how things are, or only what we're compelled to believe, and is the modest version worth having or is it the consolation prize
- whether it needs something like a verificationist theory of meaning to license the move from "we couldnt make sense of the alternative" to "the alternative is impossible", and what's left if that prop is pulled out
- the uniqueness problem: showing that X is a condition of our experience shows at most that X is one option among others, unless the alternatives can be ruled out -- and they have to be describable to be ruled out, which is where it gets awkward
- whether the conclusion is about the world or about our scheme, and whether that distinction can be drawn cleanly at all
- non-circularity: the premise (that we have experience of an objective world, that we think of ourselves as agents, whatever it is) cant already contain the conclusion, but if it's weaker than that its unclear the conclusion follows
- what "indispensable" can mean when our categories are also revisable -- science, conceptual change, the whole business
- whether it has to move a resolute sceptic or may be internal, and whether the internal reading turns it into a report on us rather than an argument
- what the relation is to a Kantian deduction specifically, and whether these things can be run outside epistemology at all -- for agency, for moral status, for the norms of discourse, for self-knowledge -- or whether those versions just inherit the same problems with extra steps

Then the part I actually care about: which of these cant be had together. I suspect there's a real dilemma buried in there, something like "either valid and trivial, or non-trivial and not an argument", and I want you to say where you think the trade-off actually sits rather than just listing the standard objections. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are house preferences of one camp (Kantians, the modest crowd, constitutivists, naturalists).

And one verdict at the end: which single requirement does the most to knock out candidates, and does the modest reading survive your list or is it just what's left once you give up on the ambitious one? Skip the full scorecard for the usual suspects, I'll do that in class -- I mostly need the criteria, plus a line on what a defender of the ambitious version has to give up.

Purpose, so you dont build me a Kant scholar's spec: I want something I can actually use to tell whether an argument in a paper I'm reading -- including in ethics, where people run these about agency and practical identity -- is doing any work or just restating a commitment. Dont worry about citations or a reading list.
