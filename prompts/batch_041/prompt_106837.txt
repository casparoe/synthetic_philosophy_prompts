I'm in a seminar on free will and animal minds and last week we did the digger wasp thing, which has been bugging me alot. The setup, as best I understand it: the wasp stings a cricket, drags it to her burrow, and before she takes it down she leaves it at the entrance and goes in alone to check the burrow, comes back out, drags the cricket a few inches closer, goes back in to check again -- little loop. Now if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she's inside, she comes out and just re-runs the whole loop from the top, and apparently you can do it to her over and over and she never catches on. My prof says: there it is, a creature executing a fixed routine with no real flexibility, and that's basically what it is to be a machine. Someone else said fine, but then what exactly is supposed to be different about a person -- and does flexibility even have anything to do with being free, or is it just a fancier kind of rule-following? Another person raised the obvious regress: even if you can step back from a rule, the rule for when to step back is itself a rule, so where does it bottom out.

Anyway I don't want to just pick a side yet. Before I sit through any more hot takes on insect minds and free will, I want the spec first: what does an adequate theory of behavioral rigidity vs. genuine flexibility -- and of whatever that contrast is supposed to have to do with free will -- actually have to deliver? Which cases it has to get right (the wasp, obviously, but also the stuff that cuts the other way -- the insects that learn, navigate novel routes, recognize individuals), which distinctions it can't collapse (a rigid behavior vs. a rigid agent, rigidity as a matter of degree vs. as a difference in kind, flexibility vs. meta-flexibility), which findings from the biology it can't just wave away, and what it owes us on the free-will question specifically rather than just gesturing at "instinct." And I want to know where the list is in tension with itself -- my gut says the requirement that rigidity comes in degrees everywhere in nature and the requirement that it mark a clean line between free agents and machines can't both be met, but you tell me. Give it to me as a numbered list, exactly five items, one or two sentences each, no intro and no wrap-up, and if two of the requirements fight each other then say so right in the item where it bites.
