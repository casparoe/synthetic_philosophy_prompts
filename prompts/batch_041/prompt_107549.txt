I'm about halfway through a novel set in London in the late fifties — my narrator is a man who reads about the Wolfenden report in the paper and decides the world has changed, and then gets picked up anyway — and I keep stalling because I can't get the moral thinking right. Period detail I can fake. The reasoning I can't.

Here's what I've been chewing on. I went back to Bentham's stuff on sexual morality and it's stranger than I remembered. The gist, as I understand it: the criminal law has no business punishing sex acts that hurt nobody but the people doing them; the pleasure the parties get is itself a reason against punishing, not merely a weight on the other side of the scale; and most of the disgust doing the real work in these arguments is what he called asceticism, the doctrine that pleasure is to be minimised, which he treated as utility's natural enemy. He worked this out at length and then never published it in his lifetime, which I find extremely telling and which I'd like to put in the book somehow.

So take the actual episode — the committee, the report, the long dead years in between, the 1967 Act that finally did it in England and Wales, and the fact that men were still getting prosecuted well after — and run the whole thing through Bentham's position. Who are the parties whose utility is supposed to count here? Which offences does his view drop that the committee kept, and which does it keep that the committee quietly let go (the street, the soliciting, the whole public-nuisance side of it — the committee had opinions about the street even when it wanted the law out of the bedroom)? The committee's big move was that private morality just isn't the law's business. Is that Bentham's argument or is it something else wearing his coat? I suspect it's much closer to Mill and that the two are being smuggled in as one thing, but tell me.

And here's the bit I can't get past, which is sort of what the novel is about. Bentham's calculus has to actually price the disgust of the neighbours — the offence to public decency, whatever you want to call it. If disgust is a real pain felt by real people, doesn't his own theory force him to count it? And if it does, doesn't the whole thing collapse into whatever the majority happens to feel on a given Tuesday, which is exactly what he was trying to escape? Or is there a principled way to keep it out that doesn't just smuggle in a rights framework he didn't have? I don't need you to rescue him. I need to know where the theory actually breaks.

Then the other direction, because I want the book to earn its ending: what does the episode tell us about the theory? Did Wolfenden and what came after vindicate the utilitarians, or does the way reform actually happened — the liberty framing, the decades of delay, the unfinished business — suggest his arithmetic couldn't have gotten anyone there on its own? Where is he just silent? Age of consent, consent itself, whatever else you think he never thought through.

I don't want a bibliography or a reading list, I can't use those. Just the analysis, and be honest about the places where the mapping stops working.
