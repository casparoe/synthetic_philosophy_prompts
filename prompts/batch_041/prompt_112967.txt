I'm closing a seminar on the infinite and want to end with a sestina — the full form, six six-line stanzas plus the three-line envoi — about Hilbert's hotel. The form should be doing the mathematics rather than decorating it: keep the same six end-words throughout, never substituted or synonymized, so that the poem's endings are a fixed set being permuted. That is the actual content of the hotel — every guest moves from room n to room n+1, nothing added, nothing lost, only re-indexed — and a reader should be able to track the same occupants through their changing positions, so choose end-words that make the permutation audible (a room, a guest, a count, a vacancy).

Then let the envoi hit the form's ceiling: six end-words can be permuted forever and no seventh will appear, which is precisely the gap between reshuffling a countable set and the diagonal move that outruns every listing. Give the finitist the turn rather than the last word: the objection that a rule for always finding room names a procedure we can always extend, not a completed totality that could be counted, and that a full hotel admitting one more guest shows only that the rule for counting has changed, not that the number of rooms has stayed the same. Leave that standing rather than settling it, so that someone who knows the mathematics finds it correct and someone who doesn't finds it a poem.
