I'm closing out the advance-directives week of my bioethics seminar and I want to end on Nolan's Memento instead of another dementia case, partly because the usual examples have gone stale on my students and partly because I think the film is doing something the literature doesn't quite have a name for. Leonard's whole life is an advance directive: he keeps a system of notes, Polaroids, and facts tattooed on his own body, and the later Leonard -- the one who wakes up with no memory of having written any of it -- has to act inside instructions handed down by a self he can't reach, question, or argue with. The film is rigged so we occupy the later self's seat, since the color scenes run backwards and we're as much in the dark about the earlier self's reasons as he is. So what I want is a reading of the film through the precedent-autonomy debate. The standard line, roughly Dworkin's, is that the earlier competent self's critical interests -- how she wanted her life to go taken as a whole -- outrank the later self's experiential interests, the comfort and preferences of whoever is actually here now; the counter-line, from people like Dresser, is that this quietly demotes the present person to a stranger whose life counts for less. Parfit sits in the background with the thought that identity isn't what matters and psychological connectedness is -- and Leonard has almost none of that across a night's sleep. Which way does the film come down, if it comes down anywhere, and where does it cheat?

Work with the details, because I think they're where the real argument is. He's a former insurance investigator, which means the man now writing directives for himself is the same man who once adjudicated whether someone else's incapacity was genuine and denied the claim -- and the Sammy Jankis story he tells is, on Teddy's account, his own story with the names filed off, so the earlier self whose wishes we're supposed to privilege is a self-deceiver whose report of its own life is unreliable. Then there's the annotation on the back of his Polaroid of Teddy, warning himself not to trust the man; and at the end, having learned that John G is a common name and that Teddy has been steering him, he burns the photographs that would let his future self check, tattoos a license plate onto his arm, and walks off as a man who has deliberately planted a false lead for himself. That seems to me to break the usual justification for privileging the earlier self -- that it's better informed and more rational about its own good. Leonard is neither, and yet the system only runs because he binds himself. And note that the directives only function because other people read them: Teddy, Natalie, both of them steering him by the notes they know he'll obey. That's the surrogate-interpreter problem, except the interpreter is hostile. Does the film treat that as a corruption of the directive system or just as what a directive system is?

The obvious objection is that this isn't the real case: Leonard isn't demented, isn't dying, isn't having his instructions honored or overridden by a hospital ethics committee, so maybe the analogy is cheap. Take that objection seriously and then tell me whether it matters, or whether the amnesia version is just the advance-directive problem with the third party removed and therefore cleaner. And finish with what the film shows that the argument can't: my students tend to walk away from Dworkin and Dresser thinking it's a tradeoff between respecting a whole life and respecting the person in front of you, and I'd like to know whether Memento makes that tradeoff look different -- or suggests the earlier self's authority rests on something other than being right, like ownership, or simply having been the one who did the wanting. If you were me, which two or three scenes would you put up on the board?
