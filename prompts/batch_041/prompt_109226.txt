Consider a striking asymmetry in how the common law of contract treats promises. A promise made in exchange for consideration — roughly, a bargained-for return — is enforceable. So is a promise on which the promisee has detrimentally relied, even without consideration, under the doctrine of promissory estoppel. But a bare, gratuitous promise is not enforceable, however deliberately or solemnly it is made: a promise to give a gift, to make a donation, to confer a benefit for nothing in return, will not be enforced. And when a promise is made to discharge what the promisor regards as a moral obligation — to reimburse a stranger who once saved his life, or to pay for care already rendered — courts divide, some treating the antecedent moral duty as sufficient to make the promise binding, others holding that a moral obligation is not consideration and that the promise is empty.

Two things are puzzling here. First, ordinary morality treats breaking a promise as a serious wrong even when nobody relied on it and no benefit was conferred, yet the law withholds enforcement from exactly those promises. Second, the moral-obligation cases run in opposite directions, as though the law had no settled view about whether it is the promise itself, the benefit received, or the reliance induced that does the binding work.

What conception of why promises bind does this pattern presuppose?
