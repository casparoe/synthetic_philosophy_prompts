I am a high school student who works as the lab aide for my chemistry class, and part of my job is keeping the answer key that records which substances count as soluble in water. The simple rule I was given—a substance is soluble just in case it would dissolve when placed in water—keeps failing me. Sometimes the test itself alters the substance so that it can never dissolve even though it otherwise would (I have seen this called a fink), and sometimes a substance really is soluble but a coating or impurity keeps it from dissolving until the coating is broken (a mask). Please write me a decision procedure I can run during a lab period, in about a minute per substance, that decides whether to record soluble, insoluble, or cannot tell from this test, while keeping straight the difference between a substance that lacks the disposition, one that has it but is masked, and one whose test finks it. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure must stop and hand the call to my teacher because it turns on judgment rather than observation, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: a powder that dissolves only after I scrape a shiny layer off each grain; a substance that always looks insoluble because the required test involves heating, and the heat breaks it down before it can dissolve; and a substance that does dissolve, but only after sitting in water for a week. Tell me what the procedure cannot do, where it is most likely to misfire, and what would convince you to revise it. Lead with the single most important rule of the procedure in your first sentence, then give the rest with no preamble and without restating my question. Write in American English.
