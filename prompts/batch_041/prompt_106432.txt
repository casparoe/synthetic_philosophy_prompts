I'm a graduate student in a philosophy-of-technology reading group, and we've spent the last few sessions circling a distinction that keeps slipping through our fingers: the difference between a system that reliably does what its operator wants it to do, and a system whose behavior actually conduces to the operator's genuine interests or to human welfare more broadly. The narrow reading of the alignment problem treats only the first as the problem — get the machine to pursue the intended objective, and the rest is a matter for ordinary prudence and politics. The broad reading insists that this is too thin, since a perfectly obedient system can still be catastrophic when the intent it obeys is itself defective. I want a thought experiment that makes the two come apart cleanly, so I can see precisely what work the narrow definition is and is not doing.

Please construct a matched pair of vignettes that isolates the two notions, holding as much else fixed as you can. In the first, the system is intent-aligned but not value-aligned: it does exactly what its operator asked, and does it competently, and yet something goes wrong from the standpoint of the operator's own deeper commitments or the welfare of those affected. In the second, the system is value-aligned but not intent-aligned: it fails to do what the operator asked, and yet the outcome is one the operator would, on reflection, endorse. The two cases should differ in that one respect only — same kind of system, same domain, same operator, comparable stakes. Constraints: no superintelligence, no science fiction, no rogue-machine theatrics; the system should be something an ordinary person could plausibly deploy or encounter (a scheduling assistant, a recommender, a customer-service agent, a clinical-triage tool), and each vignette should run no longer than a paragraph. Crucially, do not let the failure in either case turn on a hidden bug or a mis-specified objective that the operator could have caught by reading the manual — the misalignment, where it exists, should be structural rather than accidental.

After the two vignettes, do the analysis. For each, state whose intent is at issue, what "aligned" is being measured against, and where the narrow and broad readings would return different verdicts about whether the system is a problem at all. Then tell me which single feature of the pair is doing the real work — the feature whose removal would collapse the distinction — and what becomes of each vignette if that feature is altered (say, if the operator's intent were transparent and its downstream consequences obvious in advance, or if the affected parties had a formal say in what the system was asked to do). Finish with the strongest attack a defender of the broad reading could mount against my narrow framing, followed by the strongest reply available to the narrow reading, written as someone who genuinely holds it would write it rather than as a strawman.
