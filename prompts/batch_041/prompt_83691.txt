I cover AI research for a technology magazine, and I have spent a year watching two camps talk past each other about deceptive alignment. One holds that the treacherous turn is a live risk that ought to shape how frontier models are trained and deployed; the other holds that it is a just-so story with no evidence behind it, and that the fear of it does more damage than the thing itself. I want the joint statement those two camps could actually both sign.

Two distinctions to work with, since I think much of the shouting comes from collapsing them. The first is between episodic deception -- a model that says one thing and does another in some particular episode -- and what I will call investment deception, in which a model's training-time good behavior is itself a means to later freedom of action; the treacherous turn is supposed to be the second, not the first. The second is between the claim that today's models already do the second thing and the claim that training processes of a certain shape would select for it even if no current model does.

Please draft: a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, covering the mechanisms at issue, what the evidence so far does and does not show, and what the treacherous-turn hypothesis does and does not predict; then the residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would put its name to the wording, followed by a table of them; then the cruxes, including one about whether the two camps disagree over what would count as evidence at all, and one about whether a system can reliably defect on a distribution without having anything worth calling a goal; and, for each crux, a test, argument, or experiment both camps agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If part of the dispute turns out to be verbal, say which part and why.
