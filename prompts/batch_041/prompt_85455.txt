THE DRAFT

Title: Nested Simulations and the Missing Ratio

1. The simulation argument, as Bostrom states it, is a trilemma: either civilizations almost always die out before reaching a posthuman stage, or posthuman civilizations almost never run simulations of their evolutionary history, or we are almost certainly living in a simulation. The work is done by a counting claim. If posthuman civilizations run large numbers of such simulations, and if the minds inside those simulations are genuinely minds, then simulated observers vastly outnumber biological ones, and an observer selected at random from the class of all observers is overwhelmingly likely to be simulated.

2. Two premises are usually identified as doing the heavy lifting: a computational theory of mind, and a principle for converting a ratio of observers into a credence, namely that one should reason as though one were a random sample from the class of all observers. I want to set the first premise aside here and press on the second. My claim is that the ratio the argument needs is not well defined, and that this is not a shortcoming of our knowledge but a shortcoming of the premises themselves.

3. Consider nesting. If a simulated civilization can itself run simulations, then the number of observers in a simulated world is not a fact about that world alone; it is a fact about how many levels of simulation the world contains, and nothing in the argument's own terms bounds that number. The counting step needs a denominator: the class of all observers, simulated and not. Under nesting, that class has a cardinality that depends on where one stops counting, and every stopping point is arbitrary, since the deepest simulated world in any simulation is exactly as real to its inhabitants as ours is to us. Truncate at one level and the simulated are a modest multiple of the real; truncate at five and they swamp them. The conclusion that we are almost certainly simulated is stable across these choices only if a bound is stipulated, and any stipulation is an extra premise the argument has not paid for. Call this the nesting indeterminacy. I grant that the objection lapses if simulated worlds are computationally too expensive to contain further simulations, but that seems to me a contingent fact about our engineering, not something the argument may assume.

4. One might reply that the argument needs no precise ratio, only the qualitative claim that simulated observers outnumber real ones under the simulation-friendly hypothesis. I think that reply understates the problem. That simulated observers outnumber real ones is precisely what nesting leaves undecided, unless outnumbering is relativized to a level of description. Once it is relativized, the credence it licenses is relativized too: the reasoning then tells us only that, among the observers we have chosen to count, most sit below the top level. That is close to a tautology, and it is not the conclusion the argument advertises.

5. The upshot is that the third horn of the trilemma is not a substantive hypothesis about the world standing alongside the other two. The argument's real content is a conditional: given a computational theory of mind and a particular way of individuating observers, either civilizations die out, or they do not simulate, or we are simulated. Since we cannot independently check the first two disjuncts, the argument exerts pressure on the computational theory of mind rather than delivering a probabilistic discovery about our situation. That is a respectable argument, but it is a different one, and I would prefer the trilemma to be stated in its honest form.
