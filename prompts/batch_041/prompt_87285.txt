I am designing a ninety-minute undergraduate seminar on Aristotle's sea battle argument and the problem of future contingents, and what I want from you is a plan for teaching it, not an explanation of it. The students arrive having read the primary text once; they have a first logic course behind them (propositional and predicate, nothing modal or temporal); and this is the last session before a written exercise in which they must set out the argument's premises and then say, for each of four standard replies, which premise that reply has to give up. Assume nobody in the room has met the problem before.

Plan the session along the following lines.

First, the order of ideas, with the dependencies made explicit: what has to be established before the next move can even be formulated, which step is doing the real work, and where the argument is most likely to feel to them like a trick rather than a proof. I want the seminar to end with the fatalist conclusion still pulling on them rather than dismissed.

Second, the carrying example. The obvious candidate is the sea battle itself, but they will have just read it, and I suspect it is doing less work than it appears to. Should it carry the session, or should something else? If you keep it, say what it must be paired with; if you replace it, name the replacement and specify what it must have and what it must lack — in particular, whether it should be a case involving an agent who can deliberate, and whether it should be a case the students have a stake in. I want one example bearing the load, not a parade of them.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually surface, together with the point in the sequence at which I should head each one off. At a minimum I expect fatalism collapsed into determinism; the scope confusion that turns "it is true that it will happen" into "it must happen"; the conflation of a proposition's lacking a truth value with there being no fact of the matter; the thought that the problem is really about prediction or knowledge; and the idle argument, that if the outcome is already settled there is no point deliberating. Tell me which of these is the deepest and which are merely symptoms of it.

Fourth, a check for understanding at the end — one question, or at most two — that would show me whether they can distinguish semantic, metaphysical, and epistemic openness, the three senses in which the future might be called open, rather than merely recite the replies.

Fifth, what I should leave out at this level and say so explicitly, and what I can safely gesture at without developing.

No reading list, please; they have the text and nothing else.
