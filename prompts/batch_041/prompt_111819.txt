I'm a second-year PhD student in economics (mostly fieldwork, so honestly I'm not steeped in the theory end of my own field, let alone anyone else's) and I've somehow become the designated "money person" for a thing my friends are building, which I'm definately the wrong person for. I keep getting asked to adjudicate arguments that I'm pretty sure aren't economics arguments, and every time I open my mouth I almost say something and then don't.

The thing: a friend of mine — a nurse, no academic background but she reads a lot — is starting a neighborhood care co-op. Everything goes on a shared ledger in hours, and an hour is an hour: an hour of a neighbor's dentistry, an hour of a retired guy driving people to appointments, an hour of a 15-year-old mowing your lawn. You earn credits by doing stuff for members and spend them when you need stuff done.

The fights I keep getting pulled into:

1. She wants to charge a 2% monthly "carrying fee" on anyone sitting in debt — i.e. anyone who's spent more hours than they've earned — payable to the pot. She says it stops the ledger drifting permanently negative and stops free-riding. Her brother says that's just interest, interest is exploitation, and it lands hardest on the people who need help most: the single mum who's ill can't earn hours to pay off a debt that grows by itself.

2. A member with 90 hours banked wants to sell them to a newcomer for cash, 7 euros an hour, under the table. My friend says that's cheating. Her brother says it's her own fault for letting anyone accumulate 90 hours. Related: a member offers another member 20 euros to cover her shifts, and half the group thinks that's fine because the work gets done.

3. A landlord two streets over wants to join and buy 200 hours up front with cash, at a discount, then hand them out to his tenants as part of their rent. Nobody can articulate why this feels different from the dentist and the teenager, but it does.

4. The dentist has quietly stopped showing up. She bills 200 euros an hour at her practice. An hour on the ledger costs her 200 euros and costs the teenager nothing. So the expensive hours are exiting and the ledger is filling up with cheap ones, and the co-op is slowly becoming poor people serving other poor people.

5. Her founding document says that since everyone's time is worth the same, the system is inherently fair and can't produce inequality. I think that's carrying alot of weight and I'm not sure it's true, but I can't say why in a way she'd accept.

The nagging part is that I think these five fights are one fight, and an old one. I have a half-memory from an undergrad seminar of someone arguing there are two kinds of money-making, one belonging to the household and one not, with the second parasitic on the first, plus something about a shoe you can either wear or trade, and that all this is supposed to explain why charging for the use of money is different in kind from charging for work. I might be misremembering all of it.

So what do I tell her? I want (a) the actual issue stated plainly enough that I can explain it to a nurse and a teenager, (b) what that old view would say about each of the five fights specifically — I suspect the dentist is the interesting case rather than a practical nuisance, and I'd like to know why the answer isn't just that her hour is worth more — and (c) where the old view is weak or has been attacked, since I'd rather tell my friend "here's a real problem with your rule" than hand her a doctrine that collapses the first time her brother pushes back. If a repaired version survives, give me that too, even if it isn't the classical one. No reading list please — I won't read it.
