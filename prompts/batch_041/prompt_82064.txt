I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on philosophical method, and I would like you to draft one of the set pieces: an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have spent several decades arguing past one another. Treat this as a demanding writing task rather than a survey question; the value of the result lies in the precision of its formulations.

Adopt the role of a philosopher with deep expertise in mid-twentieth-century analytic philosophy — in particular in the exchange between Quine and Carnap over analyticity — and with a methodological interest in adversarial collaboration, that is, in having committed advocates of opposed views jointly state what they already agree on, isolate their remaining disagreements in wording each side can sign, and register in advance what would move them.

The two parties are these. (A) A Quinean: the analytic–synthetic distinction as traditionally drawn is not merely in need of repair but empty, and the accompanying apparatus of meaning, synonymy, and necessity does no explanatory work; the holistic picture of knowledge, on which no statement is insulated from revision, is the whole story we need. (B) A Carnapian: relative to a chosen language framework the distinction is perfectly respectable — analyticity is a matter of what follows from the formation and transformation rules and the meaning postulates of that framework — and questions that appear to demand a framework-free notion of analyticity are either internal questions to be settled by calculation within a framework or external questions to be settled by pragmatic decision about which framework to adopt. Neither party is to be a strawman: state each at the strength of its best contemporary defender, and do not attribute to either a thesis that its own advocates would disown.

Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you enumerate the candidate points of contention — the revisability of logical laws, the force of holism, the definability of analyticity, the internal/external question distinction, the bearing of semantic externalism, the question whether any of this is verbal — and say which you intend to set aside as merely terminological, and why, before you draft anything.

Then produce the document itself, in four parts.

1. A numbered list of claims both parties sign. These must be substantive rather than vacuous: what analyticity was supposed to explain (apriority, necessity, our entitlement to refuse to revise), which particular sentences each side counts as analytic and which it does not, what the disagreement is emphatically not about, and what any adequate account must deliver.

2. A disagreements table with columns: the question; the Quinean's formulation; the Carnapian's formulation; whether it is a crux; what would move each side. Each party's position must be phrased in wording that party would accept as its own.

3. The cruxes, each with (a) the sub-question, (b) why settling it would move at least one side, and (c) a case, argument, or scenario that both parties agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Two test cases must be handled explicitly: first, a community that revises a logical law under empirical pressure, so that a principle previously treated as constitutive of reasoning is given up; second, a case in which a speaker's linguistic dispositions are held fixed while the environment varies, so that the analyticity of a sentence is either environment-invariant or hostage to empirical chemistry. Say whether either case can be used by both parties, or only by one. In addition, both parties must take a position on whether a deliberately stipulated definition introduced in a program of conceptual engineering yields an analytic truth or only a decision about words.

4. A closing section stating exactly what remains in dispute, whether any part of it is verbal, and if some is, which part and what would dissolve it.

Work from the positions themselves: do not include citations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and do not rest any load-bearing step on the numbered premise of some particular paper.

End with a final line in exactly this form, giving the parties' joint classification of their residual disagreement:

Verdict: <substantive | verbal | mixed> — followed by a one-sentence summary.
