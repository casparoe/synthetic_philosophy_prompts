Start from a position I have defended for years and can no longer price: that the thermodynamic arrow is the fundamental arrow of time, and that the other asymmetries we live among — that we remember the past and not the future, that causes precede their effects, that our interventions alter later states and not earlier ones, that there are records of anything at all — are downstream of it. The account I would give runs: the fundamental dynamics are time-reversal invariant; the universe begins in an extraordinarily low-entropy condition; a statistical postulate then makes the subsequent growth of entropy overwhelmingly likely; and the rest follows from that growth. My claim is stronger than that this account is true. It is that the account is complete — that no further time-asymmetric ingredient is needed — and that any world in which the other asymmetries came apart from the entropy gradient would be one of thermodynamically negligible probability.

What would count against it? I want the defeaters sorted by how much they should move me, and I would rather have the arguments whose shape I can feel but cannot price than a rehearsal of the famous objections (argue from the substance; I will supply my own references). In particular: does entropy increase do the work I need it to do if the entropy that increases is defined relative to a coarse-graining we choose, while the fine-grained quantity is conserved under the dynamics? Is the time-asymmetry really in the initial condition, or has it entered through the statistical postulate instead, and what follows if it is the latter? What does the account say about a universe whose entropy is low at both temporal ends, so that two branches of increase meet in the middle? Does the growth of entropy yield a direction, or only an ordering, with records and counterfactual dependence requiring a further postulate? What should I make of the small time-asymmetries in the fundamental interactions, if they are genuine — a second, independent arrow, or a curiosity? And does the account's handling of our own evidence survive the point that fluctuation-observers would vastly outnumber observers like us?

I want to know which of these the account's defenders would themselves concede as counting against it, and which only its critics press; where the line falls between evidence that would be fatal and evidence that is merely awkward; and what the position predicts that its rivals do not, so that I can distinguish its evidential base from its free parameters. Say plainly, too, whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every apparent counterexample is met with the reply that the configuration is improbable, is anything still at risk, and if not, is that a defect in a metaphysical account of the arrow or simply the price of a statistical one? What would persuade me most is a time-asymmetry in the physics itself that made the thermodynamic story derivative rather than fundamental.
