I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on rationality and decision-making, and I am currently grading the final examination. The examination was closed-book and students had no internet access; they were instructed to reason from the material presented in lecture and not to cite sources. One question, worth 20 points, is reproduced below together with one student's answer. I would like a grade and the specific comments that justify it, in a form I can enter into my gradebook and hand back to the student.

**Examination question (20 points)**

A regional blood bank selects its donor-screening procedure as follows. It tests candidate procedures one at a time in a fixed order; it stops as soon as a candidate meets a pre-set adequacy level of 99.5% sensitivity, and it adopts that procedure, without checking whether any untested candidate would have been better.

A consultant objects on two grounds. (i) The bank is irrational, because a rational agent maximizes expected value over the options available to it. (ii) In any case, the bank's behaviour can be rewritten as maximizing — it maximizes the probability of clearing 99.5% sensitivity — so "satisficing" is only a label for optimization and adds nothing to the explanation.

(a) Evaluate objection (i) using Herbert Simon's distinction between substantive and procedural rationality. State precisely what each term covers, and say how the distinction bears on the consultant's charge.
(b) Evaluate objection (ii). Give the objection its strongest form, say what a defender of the satisficing framework should concede and what she should deny, and identify at least one prediction the satisficing framework makes that the redescription does not.

In both parts, say what role the adequacy level plays in the model and how, if at all, it is expected to change over time.

**Rubric used in writing the question**

- Objection (i): whether the answer identifies what the consultant's maximization requirement presupposes, and why the bank's stopping rule is not obviously irrational — 4 points.
- The substantive/procedural distinction: whether the answer says what each term covers and uses the contrast correctly — 5 points.
- The adequacy level as an aspiration level: its role as a stopping condition, and its expected behaviour over time — 4 points.
- Objection (ii): whether the answer distinguishes representability from empirical equivalence, and concedes and denies the right things — 5 points.
- A discriminating prediction — 2 points.

**Student's answer (transcribed in full)**

"The bank's rule is a stopping rule. It searches until it finds a procedure that is good enough, then it stops. The consultant's first objection assumes that search is free. Testing candidate procedures costs money, takes time, and delays the screening of donors, and these costs are not incidental; they are part of the decision problem. Once they are counted, 'maximize expected value over the available options' is not an option the bank has; what it has is a rule for deciding when to stop looking. Simon's point is that agents in this position adopt a level of performance that is good enough rather than best, and that this is not a failure of rationality but a response to it.

This is where Simon's distinction between substantive and procedural rationality does its work. Substantive rationality is the normative standard: a choice is substantively rational to the extent that it is the best means to the agent's given ends. Procedural rationality is the descriptive account of the process by which the agent arrives at a choice. So the substantive/procedural contrast is just the familiar contrast between what agents ought to do and what they actually do. Simon's complaint is that economics has invested almost everything in the first and almost nothing in the second, and his programme is to replace a normative theory of choice with a descriptive one.

The 99.5% adequacy level is the aspiration level. It is the threshold the bank applies in deciding whether a candidate is acceptable, and it is what stops the search. In Simon's framework the aspiration level is not a psychological decoration; it does real work, since without it there is no stopping point and the search has no terminus. It is also expected to move: a bank that repeatedly clears its threshold has reason to raise it, and a bank that repeatedly fails to clear it will lower it.

The consultant's second objection is stronger, and I think it is right. Any pattern of behaviour can be redescribed as maximizing something. Define the bank's payoff as 1 if it adopts a procedure that clears the threshold and 0 otherwise, and the bank is maximizing expected payoff. Since the redescription is available for every possible behaviour, no observation could distinguish the two accounts, and it follows that the satisficing framework has no empirical content of its own. The consultant is therefore correct that the vocabulary of satisficing picks out no different kind of behaviour. What the tradition does supply is a psychology of search — an account of how an agent feels its way to a stopping point — but a psychology of search is not a theory of choice.

A prediction the framework makes is that the bank adopts the first procedure that clears its threshold rather than the best available procedure. I cannot see how to convert this into a test that the redescription would fail, since the redescription entails the same choice."

**How to grade**

Assess the answer on the merits of what it says, not on whether it matches any particular published reading, and neither reward nor penalize the absence of citations. Where the answer takes a line that is defensible on its own terms but departs from a standard reading, credit it and say why. Distinguish errors that damage the argument's structure from imprecisions of phrasing, and be explicit about which parts of the answer are sound.

Return a single JSON object, and nothing outside it, with exactly these fields:

- `"score_out_of_20"`: number.
- `"letter_grade"`: string, one of A+, A, A−, B+, B, B−, C+, C, C−, D, F.
- `"criterion_scores"`: array of five objects, one per rubric heading, each with `"criterion"` (string, using the rubric's own wording), `"max_points"` (number), `"points_awarded"` (number), and `"comment"` (string, one to three sentences). The five `points_awarded` values must sum to `score_out_of_20`.
- `"errors"`: array of objects, each with `"quoted_phrase"` (verbatim from the answer, kept short), `"error_type"` (one of "conflation", "misattribution", "non_sequitur", "unsupported_claim", "omission"), `"explanation"` (string, stating what the answer says, what is wrong with it, and what the correct treatment would be), and `"severity"` (one of "minor", "moderate", "major"). Order them from most to least serious.
- `"defensible_moves"`: array of objects, each with `"quoted_phrase"` and `"why_it_is_defensible"`, for claims that a hurried reader might mark down but that should be credited.
- `"omissions"`: array of strings, naming anything the question asked for that the answer does not supply.
- `"hasty_grader_risk"`: string, naming what a quick read of this answer is most likely to get wrong in either direction.
- `"overall_comment"`: string, three to five sentences, written to the student.
- `"score_if_errors_were_fixed"`: number, on the same 20-point scale, stated as what the answer would earn if the errors you list were corrected and the omissions supplied.
