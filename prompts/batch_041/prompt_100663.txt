I'm the one they've roped into giving the after-dinner remarks at a small two-day workshop on comparative federalism — it's the retirement do for my old supervisor, who spent thirty-odd years writing on subsidiarity and, as far as I can tell, never once said outright what it actually meant. The room is genuinely mixed. There are two EU lawyers who know the subsidiarity protocol line by line (the eight-week window for national parliaments, the reasoned-opinion thresholds, the whole early-warning apparatus), a Swiss cantonal official who'll be thinking about the cantonal referendum on federal statutes, a German constitutional lawyer, an American who works on anti-commandeering doctrine, and then maybe half the room are partners, former students and family who have never read a treaty in their lives. I want six to eight minutes that lands for both halves.

The point I want it to carry — and I need the shape of the speech to carry it, not just to state it — is that every mechanism we've built to enforce subsidiarity is really a device for shifting the burden of proof, not a test for the right level of government. The EU says a third of national parliament votes forces a review, but then the Commission may maintain, amend or withdraw the draft and only has to give reasons; the Swiss let eight cantons pull a federal statute into a referendum; the Germans route it through the Bundesrat and the guarantee of the federal principle; the Americans let courts strike down commandeering but won't audit the substantive wisdom of a federal scheme. In each case the question "could the lower level have done this?" isn't answered by the mechanism — it's deferred to a body that gets to decide how much deference to give. So subsidiarity presents itself as a neutral procedural principle while always presupposing a substantive judgment about capacity, which is why it can be sincerely invoked by a centraliser and a secessionist in the same afternoon.

Could you write the speech? Keep it warm, it's a retirement and not a seminar, and let the comparative examples do the work without it turning into a lecture. Also tell me how confident you are in the factual claims you make about the different systems, and single out the one you're least sure of. End with a one-sentence summary as the last line, prefixed "Summary:".
