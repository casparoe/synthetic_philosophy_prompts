Since my mother began losing her sight, she has had vivid, silent hallucinations—figures in the room, sometimes familiar, sometimes not—and the question of how to describe what she sees has become the ordinary work of our mornings. I have been reading about the argument from illusion, and it has ceased to be a puzzle for me: the thought that a hallucination and a veridical perception can be subjectively indistinguishable, and that this is supposed to force us to posit a common experiential core, now sounds less like a skeptical hypothesis than like a description of our breakfast table. I find the disjunctivist reply compelling in the abstract, but I cannot pretend it is easy to live with when the person across from me is describing a visitor who is not there. I would like a poem that does the philosophical work rather than explaining it.

Please write a pantoum of five quatrains—twenty lines—using the standard repetition rule: lines two and four of each stanza become lines one and three of the next, and the final stanza's second and fourth lines are the first and third lines of the poem, so the whole poem closes into a loop. The poem should be spoken by someone in my position: a caregiver who is no longer sure whether a repeated line is a report of what the other person saw or a report of what the caregiver saw. Let the repeated lines migrate between those two possibilities without announcing which is which. The form should tempt the reader toward the common-factor theory—the same line, therefore the same experience—and the content should resist that inference in the way disjunctivism resists it: the second occurrence is not the same kind of event as the first, even when the words are identical. The poem should not resolve the issue in a closing moral. It should be accurate enough that a philosopher of perception would recognize the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist response, and it should be a poem first. No citations, no footnotes; I want the form to carry the argument.
