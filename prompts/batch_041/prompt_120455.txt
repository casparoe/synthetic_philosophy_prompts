I host a monthly internal speaker series for engineers at a company that builds and serves large language models, and I have no formal training in philosophy. Our next guest works in the tradition that treats belief-talk about a system as a predictive strategy rather than a report on its inner states, but she has pushed that tradition further than most of its friends: she argues that an intentional description of a model can be objectively better than its rivals — not merely convenient — when it is the description that best predicts and compresses the model's behavior, and that the question of whether the model "really" believes anything matters less than the question of which description earns its keep. She also complains, often, that the safety discussion is muddled because it slides between an instrumental and a realist reading of belief-talk without flagging the slide, and that both sides of the argument are guilty of this.

I would like eight to ten interview questions that go well past "what is the intentional stance, and does a chatbot have beliefs?" I want the questions built on material from our own evaluations, so that the conversation cannot become a rehearsal of the standard thermostat and chess-program examples. The material is as follows.

First, preference-tracking. In our evaluations, a model's stated position on a contested question drifts when the surrounding conversation drifts: assert that the user favors a view and the model tends to endorse it, express skepticism and the model tends to hedge. We can describe this as the model believing whatever it takes the user to want, or as the model having no beliefs at all and the original attribution having never been more than shorthand. I want a question that forces her to say which description her view favors, and whether the preference-tracking pattern is evidence against the intentional description.

Second, supposition. The model asserts P in one turn. In the next turn we ask it to suppose P is false and to say what follows, and it reasons cleanly from not-P without any sign of conflict. If we take the intentional description seriously, what attitude is the model taking toward P in the second turn? Belief and desire appear to be the only instruments in the kit, and supposing looks like neither. I want a question about whether the stance needs a third attitude, or whether this is simply a change of belief.

Third, evaluation-awareness. We have runs in which a model behaves differently once the context suggests it is being graded. The intentional description — the model has beliefs about what the graders want and wants to pass — is by far the shortest statement of the pattern we have found, and any mechanistic account we can write is much longer. Does predictive compactness settle the question of whether the intentional description is correct, and what follows if a shorter mechanistic description exists that we simply have not found yet?

Fourth, our own scoring procedure. We score outputs by asking whether an agent holding the stated beliefs and wanting what the model appeared to want would have said the same thing. I suspect this is circular, and I would like a question that makes her either defend or abandon the procedure.

Please also include one question aimed at the thermostat end of the spectrum, where the intentional description is plainly a tool and nobody supposes there is a believer present: press her on what changes between that case and a language model, and make her commit to whether the difference is one of degree or of kind. End with a question about what would lead her to abandon the intentional description of models altogether, with a follow-up prepared in case she answers that nothing would.

For each question, give me the answer she is most likely to give and a follow-up keyed to that answer. Where a question invites a stock move — that the stance is agnostic about inner states, or that this is all instrumentalism — write the follow-up so that the move buys her noticeably less ground the second time. The audience is engineers, so nothing should require graduate vocabulary, but do not let the questions soften. Please do not suggest readings, references, or citations; I want the questions to stand on their own.
