Exam question. In the first unit we distinguished three ways in which an artifact might be political: (i) procedurally, when its production or adoption is itself the outcome of a political contest; (ii) consequentially, when its deployment shifts the distribution of power, access, or opportunity among users, whether or not anyone intended that shift; and (iii) constitutively, when the artifact, simply by operating as it does, requires or imposes a particular social order, so that one cannot run this technology at scale without also having that arrangement of authority. These are not mutually exclusive. Using the fare-gate case from section — a transit authority replacing paper transfers with a registered stored-value card that carries a minimum balance and requires either a bank account or a registered phone number — say in which sense(s) the new fare system is political, and defend your answer. The interesting question is not whether it is political, but in which sense, and on what evidence.

Student's answer. "The fare gates are political in all three senses, and the third is the most important, since it is what refutes the technological determinist. Procedurally they are political because the agency picked the card system over a cash-based alternative after lobbying by the card processor; a procurement decision with winners and losers is politics in the ordinary sense. Consequentially they are political because they price out unbanked riders, who skew poor and elderly. The agency need not have wanted that outcome, and that is exactly why the consequential sense is stated without an intent condition. Constitutively they are political because the exclusion is built into the system's ordinary operation: a stored-value card system cannot be run at scale without a registry of identified accounts, so the gates bring into being a class of riders who are legible to the authority and a class who are not. And because the same function — collecting fares — could have been discharged by a coin turnstile, the design was a choice, and a design choice that produces a durable social order is exactly what the constitutive sense is meant to capture. This also disposes of the determinist, who holds that a technology's social effects follow from its intrinsic properties: if the gates' politics were intrinsic, no alternative design could have had different politics, but the coin turnstile shows that one could."
