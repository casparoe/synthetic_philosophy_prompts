Last night a friend and I argued for several hours about whether our own disagreement was rational, and we never got past trading intuitions. We had both seen the same public evidence and had ended up with different credences, and he pressed the line that if we are both rational and each knows the other to be rational, we cannot sincerely remain in disagreement, so any persistent divergence must mean one of us is misinformed or incoherent. I answered that this presupposes we already share a prior, which is precisely what is at issue. We kept collapsing a claim about what rationality requires into a claim about how people in fact reason, and back again.

I would like the document we should have written instead. Treat the two of us as two camps. The first camp holds that a common prior is a constitutive requirement of rationality: given common knowledge of one another's rationality and posterior beliefs, agents cannot agree to disagree, so any surviving divergence must trace to different information. The second camp holds that a prior is a choice of model or inductive bias — of background theory, of which hypotheses are even on the table — that is not itself forced by coherence or by evidence, so agents can rationally diverge even with full common knowledge of one another's reasoning.

Write the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between these two camps would produce. I want the following.

A numbered section of agreed claims: what both sides can sign about the formalism (what the agreement result actually assumes — a shared prior, common knowledge, a common information structure — and what it does not), about the distinction between normative and descriptive readings of the assumption, and about what each side would treat as evidence or argument bearing on the issue.

A statement of the genuine disagreements, each rendered in wording its own side would accept as fair to it.

The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side. I expect these to include whether the common prior is a coherence constraint or an empirical hypothesis; whether rationality can be characterized without reference to a shared prior; whether the impossibility of purely speculative trade among agents who share a prior and know one another to be rational tells against the assumption or merely redescribes it; and whether any non-arbitrary prior can be singled out at all, for instance by a rule that prefers hypotheses with shorter descriptions.

For each crux, the case, argument, or evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Make at least two of these concrete and somewhat novel: (a) two forecasters who observe the same data stream but whose priors come from different sources, one derived from a physical model and one from a symmetry argument; and (b) two machine-learning systems trained on different corpora whose disagreement persists after each is shown the other's outputs and reasoning.

Finally, tell me which parts of my friend's and my argument, if any, were merely verbal, and which reading of the common prior each of us was actually relying on.
