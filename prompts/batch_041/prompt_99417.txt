Write a satirical public-information leaflet — a numbered FAQ of roughly 650 words — issued by a fictional agency, the Office of Established Justice, in a state whose jurisprudence is uncompromisingly Pascalian. The Office treats as settled that justice is simply whatever long-standing custom has established; that people obey the law not because it is just but because it is established; that justice without force is impotent and force without justice is tyrannical, so the two must be married; and that the deepest political evil is civil discord, which is why citizens are discouraged from asking whether a particular law is just. The comedy should come from the institutional apparatus such a doctrine would actually require, not from jokes about the doctrine.

Include, in the deadpan register of a real government leaflet:

- a section resolving the regress — what makes the founding establishment just, given that it was not itself established — without ever conceding that the question is a problem;
- a procedure explaining how a successful coup, conquest, or revolution is reclassified, once it holds, as the establishment of a new justice, with the interval of time required for this reclassification left charmingly vague;
- a complaints procedure for citizens who believe a law is unjust, and a triage form that routes each complaint to the order that owns it — the order of force, the order of the mind, or a third and higher order that the Office acknowledges exists but does not itself administer;
- a section on the public reverence, or mystique, that the Office cultivates so that its authority appears to rest on something other than force;
- and at least one point at which the leaflet's logic visibly eats itself — for instance, the Office's own authority, being recent, cannot yet count as just by the Office's own criterion.

Keep the irony sustained and unwinking: a reader who has never heard of Pascal should be able to follow the leaflet, while a reader who knows his positions on justice, custom, force, and the several orders should recognize those positions being followed to their conclusion and find the exaggeration fair rather than strawmanned. Do not quote Pascal; give the Office its own formulations in its own voice.
