I'm a novelist — two books out, and the second one is basically an argument for this position in disguise, it's about a woman whose life comes apart through a string of events that, listed on a single page, sound like nothing at all. Which is how I got invited to my city's EA/rationalist reading group next week to argue against the torture-vs-dust-specks thought experiment: the one where you pick between one person being horribly tortured for fifty years and 3^^^3 people — the absurd tower-of-exponents number, more people than there are particles — each getting a single speck of dust in the eye. They've all read it a dozen times. I'm the evenings entertainment, basically.

Here's the line I'm taking and I need you to prep me for the question period. My claim is that the thought experiment is rigged by its own description. It needs "a speck of dust in the eye" to be two contradictory things at once: small enough that no single one of them is ever worth trading against the torture (or the case for sparing the tortured person falls apart), and big enough to be a real unit of harm that can be added up to something monstrous (or the number 3^^^3 does no work). But harm isn't a substance that sits inside a person in fixed quantities — it's a description of what an event does inside a life. The speck that is genuinely negligible — unnoticed, unremembered, gone by the next blink — is also the speck that can't accumulate, because there's nothing there to add. If it doesn't count, it doesn't count. My verdict is the boring one and I'll defend it: I'd spare the one person. I'm a lot less sure my argument for that verdict survives contact with people who do this for a living, which is kind of the point of asking you.

The room: fifteen or twenty people, mostly EAs and rationalists who know the argument cold and think the anti-aggregation side is a failure of nerve. Two philosophy grad students — one doing decision theory, so expect the "your preferences over outcomes still have to be representable somehow" line; one doing metaethics, so expect "you've slid from a claim about how harm is felt to a claim about how harm is valued, and that's a fallacy, not an argument." Two or three EA ops people who get impatient with philosophy and will ask what any of this changes about what to do. One AI safety person who will try to turn everything into a question about astronomical stakes and whether my argument accidentally commits me to a bounded utility function. And I'm pretty sure one quiet person in the back is a straight classical utilitarian who's been waiting to say the words "repugnant conclusion" for an hour.

What I want: the questions in the order they'll actually come, in their vocabulary, not in mine — eight or so, ordered by how much damage they'd do to me; for each, a reply I can actually say out loud in under two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft, so I know what I'm really conceding even when I don't say it. Flag the two objections I should just give up at the top rather than fight, because I'd rather spend the hour on the one that matters. And tell me the question I'm praying nobody asks. Don't hand me a reading list, I won't read it, and half of it is probably on a blog I can't get to from the green room anyway.
