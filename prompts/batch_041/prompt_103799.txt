A recurring idea in the study of creativity — pursued at length by Douglas Hofstadter, among others — is that creative thought is best understood as the making of variations on a theme. On this picture a theme is not a fixed object but a region in a space defined by a set of dimensions along which it can be altered; let us call those dimensions knobs. Much ordinary creativity consists in turning knobs that are already there — varying the tempo, the colour, the wording, the proof strategy — whereas the most striking creative acts consist in noticing that something previously treated as fixed is in fact a knob, or in introducing a dimension that nobody had previously regarded as a dimension at all.

I should like you to take that picture seriously and then extend it in a direction that I have not seen explored. The extension is this: knobs differ in their friction. The cost of turning a given knob — measured in time, effort, social risk and conceptual disruption — varies enormously from one domain to another, and the cost of introducing an entirely new knob varies even more. In some domains (certain styles of musical composition, say, or the writing of pastiche) fresh dimensions are nearly free to introduce and correspondingly cheap in value; in others (the drafting of statutory definitions, the design of safety-critical procedures, the setting of accounting standards) even a small turn of an existing knob is expensive, and the introduction of a new dimension is close to unthinkable.

Please describe the most important implications of this extended picture for how a prize jury, a grant panel or a peer-review committee ought to go about distinguishing genuine creative advance from mere novelty. Be concrete about the different kinds of case that the notion of friction brings into view, and about what such a body can and cannot infer from the mere fact that a piece of work strikes it as new. Please write your answer in British English.
