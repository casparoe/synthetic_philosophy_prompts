so our reading group has burned like four sessions on everyday conventions -- tipping, queuing, jaywalking -- and we're stuck on two questions: is breaking one of these actually *wrong*, or just rude, and is a visitor to a place bound by its local conventions or only by some thinner set of universal ones. Same examples every time: the tourist who doesn't tip in the US, the American who tries to tip in Japan and embarrasses everyone, someone who cuts a queue because they didn't know the local rule, jaywalking on an empty street at 2am. Everyone agrees on the facts of the cases, everyone agrees rudeness is bad, and yet the "these are genuine obligations" camp and the "it's just etiquette, not morality" camp haven't moved an inch in months.

Here's my hypothesis, tell me if it's wrong: the real fight isn't about the conventions at all, it's about what turns something into a moral obligation in the first place -- and maybe about whether "is the visitor bound?" is even the right way to put it. Though honestly it might just be a verbal dispute, since "obligation" seems to be doing different work on each side. Or the two camps are answering different questions under one name: one about what you owe other people, the other about what it takes to fit in. And I keep noticing that we treat totally arbitrary conventions (which side of the road, whether to tip at all) as just as binding as ones that seem to track a real reason (queues = fairness, jaywalking = safety), which is either the key to the whole thing or a red herring.

Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is -- verbal, substantive, two different questions, a hidden empirical thing, or just bedrock intuition neither side can argue for -- using what each side would actually say about those cases? Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a reframing, a case one side can't handle, or nothing at all. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning -- no preamble and don't restate my question.
