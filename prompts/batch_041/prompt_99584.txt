I have been teaching myself reinforcement learning in my spare time and reading philosophy of mind alongside it, and I keep running into a claim I cannot evaluate: that an agent accumulating negative reward is, or might be, suffering — that the reward signal is a stand-in for pleasure and pain. I do not think the claim is silly, but I have never seen anyone set out what a theory would have to do before we could take it seriously, and I would rather have the specification than another verdict.

Write the desiderata for a theory of valence in learning agents: what any account must deliver before we are entitled to say that an agent's negative reward is, for the agent, unpleasant, rather than just a number that lowers the odds of the actions that produced it. Some requirements I already suspect belong on the list, though you should add or drop them as the argument goes: it has to handle two agents with the same reward function but different learning arrangements — one that assigns credit within its own lifetime and one shaped only across generations, which never experiences the signal at all — and say whether they stand the same way; it must not collapse the quantity that steers behavior into the state that is felt, since a signal can push an agent around with nobody home to mind it; it has to sit right with what we know about creatures that process tissue-damage signals without, by most accounts, hurting; and it must say what would count as evidence for the felt reading over the mere signal reading, given that we cannot ask the agent and its reports are themselves just outputs.

Give me exactly five numbered requirements, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion, each naming the case or consideration that puts it on the list and marking whether you would treat it as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp. Where two of them cannot be satisfied together, say so in the entries concerned. American English.
