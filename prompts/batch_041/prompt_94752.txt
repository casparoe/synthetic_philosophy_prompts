I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working mostly on social ontology and the theory of norms, and I've spent the term trying to reverse-engineer a colleague's implicit theory of convention from the verdicts she hands down on examples. Let me call her R. Her touchstone is Lewis's 1969 book on convention, on the standard reading of which a convention is a regularity in the behavior of a population in a recurrent coordination situation — everyone conforms to it, everyone expects everyone else to conform to it, and everyone prefers to conform to it on condition that the others do, with all of this being common knowledge, and with the regularity being in some sense arbitrary in that a different regularity would have worked just as well. But R. is a fast and unsentimental judge of cases, and I suspect her operative rule is looser than the textbook one, or perhaps tighter in a different place. Her track record so far: she counts as conventions the rule of the road in a country where driving on the right is near-universal and everyone expects it; the use of the locally standard currency as a medium of exchange; the fact that a particular word, rather than another, names a familiar animal in a community's language; the practice, in a city where it is standard, of standing on the right of an escalator so that others may pass on the left; and the rule that the person who placed a phone call that got cut off is the one who dials again. She refuses the label in three other cases: a town where everyone happens to cross the same bridge at rush hour, because it is the only bridge and no one's choice depends on what anyone else does; the pupillary reflex, on the grounds that it is not the kind of thing that could be otherwise or be sustained by mutual expectation; and a single pair of friends who once agreed to meet at a particular café, on the grounds that a one-off coordinated choice is not a regularity at all.

Now a case from my own life that I want her to rule on, and that I suspect will split whatever rule she is running. In my department, it is universally expected that one dresses formally — a suit or the equivalent — for a doctoral defense. Everyone does it, everyone expects everyone else to do it, everyone would feel marked out for not doing it, and the practice is stable and self-reproducing; but it coordinates nothing in the game-theoretic sense, since the defense would proceed identically if everyone wore jeans, and the reason each of us complies is that we would be judged for deviating, not that conformity is a better response to a coordination problem the others are also solving. I want you to act as an expert in the theory of convention and the game-theoretic analysis of norms, and to work carefully rather than by free association. Please begin by brainstorming the space of rules that would fit R.'s full record — not just the strict Lewisian one, but any plausible competitor, including one that drops the coordination requirement and treats any arbitrary, mutually expected, self-sustaining regularity as a convention, and any rule that instead tightens a different condition. Then state, for each rule you identify, what it predicts about the formal-dress case, say which rule you think R. is actually running and why, give me your best guess at her verdict with your confidence, and — this is the part I care about most — name the further case that would cleanly separate the rules that survive, so that I could settle the question with one more example rather than a hundred. Flag any place where the record genuinely underdetermines the answer and say what principle she would be committing to either way.
