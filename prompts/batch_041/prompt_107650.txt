I have been trying, without success, to discover whether a certain shape of argument has a name, and I should be grateful for an answer that does rather more than supply the label.

Here is the shape. Take a construction — a sentence, or a quantity defined over sentences — that is not settled by the truth or falsity of its parts, and that everyone agrees is interesting precisely for that reason. Now impose a principle that looks far too mild to be worth disputing: that the probability of the construction is simply the probability of one of its parts on the supposition of the other. One would expect such a principle to tell you how to compute a number. What it in fact does, once it is combined with the ordinary rules of probability and the innocent assumption that the construction is a genuine proposition — that it has a truth value, that logically equivalent sentences receive the same probability, that probabilities combine as they always do — is to make the construction collapse. It turns out to hold in exactly the circumstances in which a blunt, truth-table reading of it would hold, and the very feature that made it interesting is squeezed out. The proof is elementary; nothing exotic is smuggled in.

I suspect that the conditional is the famous instance of this, the one everyone has in mind when they speak of the probability of a conditional. Yet I keep half-noticing the same shape in other disputes — about how much support one claim lends another, about how to measure the goodness of an explanation — and I cannot tell whether that is a genuine kinship or merely my own pattern-matching. So my question is twofold. First: does the collapse argument, in the conditional case, have an established name? Second: is there a name for the wider family of results in which an apparently weak and plausible constraint on a graded notion forces that notion to coincide with something coarser? If the family has no name, I should like one coined, with its edges marked. How does a result of this kind differ from an ordinary impossibility theorem, from a bare counterexample to a proposed principle, and from the everyday phenomenon of a formalism proving something its authors did not intend? I also want to know which assumption is doing the destructive work — the probability principle itself, the assumption that the construction is a proposition, or the licence to substitute logical equivalents — and what becomes of each of the familiar manoeuvres that try to keep the interesting case interesting by surrendering one assumption or another. The name is not what I am after; I want the anatomy.
