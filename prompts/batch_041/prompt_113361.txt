I've been going round in circles with two friends about Parfit's hitchhiker case — the dying bloke in the desert who can only get a lift into town if he promises the driver a hundred quid on arrival, even though he knows full well that once he's there he'll have no reason at all to pay up, so the only way to actually get the ride is to be the sort of person who keeps that kind of promise. We all agree on the standard answer: it can be rational to turn yourself into an agent with a disposition that you could never justify act by act, because the disposition is what gets you the ride in the first place. The trouble is that the standard answer always arrives with a toolkit attached — repetition, reputation, witnesses, written contracts, Ulysses-style self-binding, sheer habit formed over years — and I want to know what the case looks like when every one of those props is kicked away. (I'm doing a philosophy of action module and the seminar leader keeps saying "dispositions are rational if they pay", which definately can't be the whole story.)

So here's what I'd like from you: give me an example of a conceptual issue of this exact shape. That is, a one-off, anonymous, unrepeatable situation in which the agent would do better if they were a certain kind of person, but where the usual routes to becoming that person are all closed, and where the difficulty isn't merely that the disposition is hard to acquire but that the very reasoning which shows you'd want it would, if you genuinely took it in, stop you from having it — a disposition that is self-effacing in that way, or one you can only possess by remaining ignorant of the argument for wanting it. Don't hand me the stock illustrations; I've seen the sirens-and-mast thing, the believe-you'll-pass-the-exam thing, and the promise-keeping-in-a-small-village thing a hundred times. Something from ordinary working life would be much better — an interview, a negotiation, a favour between strangers, a hospital ward, whatever genuinely fits — and if you think the shape I've described is incoherent rather than merely rare, say so plainly and give me the nearest coherent example instead, with an account of exactly what breaks.

Then explain, in your example, what the agent would have to be like, why the payoff only materialises if the disposition isn't held for the sake of the payoff, and what the case shows about the difference between assessing the rationality of an act and assessing the rationality of a disposition (or of a whole character). A couple of hundred words of scenario, then as much analysis as you need. Please write it in British English — my tutor is fussy about spellings and I've given up argueing about it. Finish with a single line of the form \answer{...} containing a short name for your example.
