I'm in a comparative lit seminar on translation and I keep getting into arguments with a freind who does analytic philosophy of language, and we're stuck on Borges's Pierre Menard story — the one where a twentieth-century Frenchman produces, word for word and line for line, a few chapters of Don Quixote in the Spanish of Cervantes's own era, and the narrator insists that Menard's version is richer than the original even though the two are textually identical, because of everything that has happened in the world between the two acts of writing. My friend says the story proves meaning is all context and reception, so once you have the identical string of words you have everything, and any fuss about preserving form is just aesthetic nostalgia dressed up as fidelity. I think that can't be right, and the place I'd go to say why is Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot, which is a huge book in which he tries over and over to carry the form of one tiny Renaissance French poem Marot wrote to a sick young woman — syllable counts, rhyme, the whole tight apparatus — into English, under different self-imposed constraints each time, and treats each partial failure as telling him something. So: you're a philosopher of language and translation theorist, take both of these seriously, and read the Borges through the lens of translation under formal constraint. Is Menard the perfect translator — total fidelity, nothing lost? Or is he the reductio of one picture of fidelity, the translator who preserved everything except the reason anyone bothers? What I'm really after is whether there's a coherent notion of preserving form that is neither (a) trivially satisfied by copying the text across unchanged nor (b) an aesthetic preference we're smuggling in as an obligation, and whether the story plus Hofstadter's practice gives us one.
