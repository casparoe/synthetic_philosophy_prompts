I'm in a small reading group on early modern metaphysics and we've spent the last few weeks on Leibniz — the best of all possible worlds, the principle of the best, why a good God permits physical evil as a means and moral evil only as the fallout of the best possible scheme. Last Tuesday Ravi, who's writing a thesis on early modern ethics, turned up with an argument he says he built himself and now can't decide whether it's clever or rubbish. It goes:

(1) God, being perfectly wise and perfectly good, creates the best of all possible worlds, and this is it.
(2) If this world is the best possible one, then no alteration to it could make it better — any change would leave it as good as it is, which is impossible, or make it worse.
(3) What I ought to do is whatever would make the world better than it would otherwise be.
(4) So there is nothing I ought to do. Charity, reform, forgiving my brother-in-law — either pointless or a defection from a plan that's already optimal.
(5) Leibniz, meanwhile, holds that we're under a standing obligation to promote the perfection of the whole, that justice just is the charity of the wise, and that loving God and loving one's neighbour are the same disposition turned in two directions. So his metaphysics of the best eats his ethics: on his own principles, universal benevolence has nothing left to do.

I think something is crooked in there — premise (2) does something dishonest with the words "this world" and I can't say what — but every repair I try costs me something I'd rather keep. Take it apart: which step do I reject, what exactly is the mistake, and why did the step look fine until I stared at it? Then price the alternatives for me. If I keep the optimism and drop the universal benevolence I lose most of what I actually like about Leibniz; if I keep the ethics and weaken the optimism to something like "this world is good enough, or better than most", I can't see what survives of the principle of the best or of the claim that God permits evil only for the sake of greater goods. And does anything change if the best is read the way Leibniz seems to mean it — a maximum of order, variety and perfection over the whole series — rather than a world in which everything is as good as it could be at every moment? I'd rather keep (1) than (5), but I want to know what I'm paying.
