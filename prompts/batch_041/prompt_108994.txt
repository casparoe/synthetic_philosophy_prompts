A working group I sit on is drafting a policy for our analytics unit, and the draft's organizing principle is what its author calls Good's rule: because the expected value of information is never negative — the decision-maker can always ignore what they learn and decide as if they had never learned it — any data obtainable at no cost should be obtained before a decision is made. I don't trust the step from the theorem to the policy, but neither do I want to reject a sound result out of vague unease. Set out the most important implications of the theorem behind that rule for someone who actually has to set such a policy, rather than merely restating the theorem. I am especially concerned with three pressures that the theorem's assumptions may not survive: acquiring the information may perturb the very state it is meant to reveal; the decision-maker may not be an expected-utility maximizer and may positively prefer to remain ignorant of the odds, so that resolving the uncertainty leaves them worse off on their own lights; and learning the answer may change what the decision-maker values, so that the utility function in force after the experiment is not the one that was in force before it. Close with a definite verdict on whether "gather all free information" is a sound default rule for a real organization, and give your reasons for the verdict you reach.
