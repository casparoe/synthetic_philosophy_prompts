A regulatory asymmetry worth puzzling over. When a pharmaceutical company wants to sell a new drug, it must first persuade a regulator that the drug is safe and effective: the burden of proof lies with the proponent, trials are demanded in advance, and unresolved uncertainty counts against approval. When a manufacturer wants to sell a new industrial chemical — or to keep selling one that has been in commerce for decades — the presumption runs the other way in many jurisdictions: the substance may circulate unless and until the regulator assembles evidence that it causes harm at realistic levels of exposure; here the burden lies with the state, and unresolved uncertainty counts in favor of continued use. The same state, protecting the same citizens, thus treats the thought that we do not know whether something is harmful as a reason to say no in the clinic and as a reason to say yes in the warehouse.

I want the philosophical commitments this pair of practices presupposes. Begin with the most obvious candidate — the precautionary principle — but do not stop at the slogan. Ask what view of the baseline the asymmetry assumes: which state of affairs counts as the neutral, do-nothing case, and whether allowing a substance to remain on the market is being treated as an omission rather than as an ongoing act of the state. Ask what it assumes about the distinction between risk and uncertainty, and about whether the two contexts differ in a way that justifies opposite presumptions — individualized, consented, time-limited exposure against a known counterfactual in the one case, versus diffuse, involuntary, cumulative, population-level exposure against a contested counterfactual in the other. Ask whether the practice needs a specific decision rule at all, or whether it is compatible with several: a maximin rule, a rule that simply shifts the burden of proof, a rule that treats false alarms and missed harms as asymmetrically costly, or no rule beyond ordinary cost-benefit analysis with a thumb on the scale.

Then separate what the asymmetry strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Say whether a single commitment explains both halves of the practice, or whether it is a patchwork of commitments that happen to point the same way. Finally, consider whether the pattern is even an application of the precautionary principle or a departure from it — whether, if the principle were applied uniformly, both contexts would have to change or only one — and what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the implicit view with its most natural rival.
