My mother has been in an intensive care unit for three weeks, and I have spent those weeks listening to two people describe her condition in ways that seem to describe different patients. The neurologist speaks as though her death has already been established once the brainstem is gone; the organ-procurement coordinator speaks as though the real threshold is the moment her heart stops and stays stopped. I cannot tell whether these are two names for one event or two rival accounts of what death is, and the difference matters to what my family is being asked to consent to. I would like you to write the document that two honest partisans in this dispute could put their names to.

Act as a philosopher of medicine and bioethicist with a specialist's grasp of the conceptual foundations of death determination, and as a disciplined drafter of adversarial-collaboration statements. Before you write anything, brainstorm in a scratch space: list the sub-questions in play, the points on which the two camps merely talk past one another, and the places where a real disagreement is hiding beneath a verbal one. Then produce the statement, and keep the definition/criterion/test distinction explicit throughout -- the definition of death, the criterion that satisfies it, and the bedside tests that operationalize the criterion are three different things, and a great deal of this debate trades on sliding between them.

Treat the parties as follows. Party A holds the whole-brain criterion: death is the irreversible loss of function of the entire brain, brainstem included, and a body whose brain has ceased to function is a corpse even if a ventilator sustains its circulation. Party B holds the circulatory criterion: death is the irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function, and the brain criterion is at best a proxy for a fact about the body as an integrated whole -- a proxy that can come apart from the fact.

Deliver, first, a numbered section of claims both parties can sign: about the physiology, about the legal and clinical landscape they both accept, about the dead donor rule and what it does and does not settle, about what a successful account of death would have to explain, and about the standard objections each concedes are live against its own side. Second, a disagreements table: each row states one residual disagreement in wording that the side it belongs to would accept as its own view -- neither a caricature nor a truism -- and flags any row you judge to be merely verbal, with a note on how the parties could tell. Third, a crux section. I suspect the deepest crux is the distinction between a process being irreversible and its being merely permanent -- between "cannot be reversed" and "will not be reversed because no one will attempt it" -- and a second is whether death is a biological kind we discover or a status we confer. For each crux, name a case or argument both parties would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about how that case comes out. Use my mother's situation -- a destroyed brainstem with machine-supported circulation -- as one of the concrete cases, and state plainly what each side would say about her, and when.
