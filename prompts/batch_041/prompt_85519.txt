What keeps nagging at me is the irony: the guy whose name is on the stored-program architecture -- which, as I understand it, was basically Eckert and Mauchly's design that he wrote up and got credit for -- seems to have ended up thinking the brain was not much like a digital computer at all. As I read him: neurons are slow and flaky compared to vacuum tubes, so the brain has to buy its reliability with redundancy and statistics instead of precision; the signaling looks half analog (firing rates) and half digital (all-or-nothing spikes); the computation is low-precision and massively parallel rather than serial and exact; memory obviously isn't fetched by address the way a machine's is, which he seems to have found the deepest puzzle of all; and the central unsolved problem was the notation or language the nervous system uses, which nobody had cracked. So his name is on the architecture and also on the bottleneck people now complain about (the memory/processor traffic jam), and my hunch is he'd have found that funny and a bit beside the point, since his own picture of the brain was closer to what we'd now call analog/neuromorphic than to anything von Neumann-architecture-ish.
