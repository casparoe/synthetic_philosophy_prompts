I've been trying to get clear on whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and I want to test a distinction I don't think gets made cleanly enough. The stock cases everyone reaches for all turn on a pairwise conflict: two obligations, each apparently binding, no way to discharge both. I'm interested in whether a dilemma requires pairwise conflict at all. Take an agent under a great many obligations, no two of which are individually incompatible — each can be discharged in the world as it stands — but whose joint discharge is impossible, so that whatever she does she leaves something unmet without ever having flouted one obligation in favor of another. If the standard argument against dilemmas runs through agglomeration — ought to do A and ought to do B, hence ought to do A and B, and since ought implies can a conflict of oughts just shows one of them was never binding — then this case should be vulnerable to exactly the same argument, one step at a time, which is what makes it interesting; it looks like the moral cousin of the preface paradox. Give me a concrete example of such a case, detailed enough that I can see the joint impossibility and verify that no pair is on its own in conflict, and then say where you come down: does it count as a genuine dilemma, or does the very possibility of genuine dilemmas depend on there being at least one pair of considerations that cannot both be honored? A lot rides on whether the obligations are genuinely binding rather than merely prima facie, and on what we should say about the residue when one of them goes unmet. Don't reach for the stock examples; I've read those. Answer in continuous prose, with no headings, lists, bold, or tables — just write it out the way you'd explain it to me in an email.
