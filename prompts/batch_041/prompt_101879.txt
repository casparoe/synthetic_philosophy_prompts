I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on moral particularism and generalism, and I want to close the unit with a poem that performs the philosophical work rather than merely gesturing at it. Please write a sestina.

The subject is the particularist's central claim: that a feature which functions as a reason for acting in one situation may function as a reason against acting, or as no reason at all, in another — that the moral relevance of a consideration is a function of context all the way down, so that no finite inventory of features carries a fixed valence. I want the form to enact this rather than describe it. Choose six end-words that name features a generalist would be tempted to treat as having stable significance — for instance, harm, promise, pleasure, need, truth, consent — and arrange the poem so that each word, as it rotates through the stanzas, does a different job each time it returns: on one appearance it should count in favor of an action, on another against it, and on a third it should be inert, a feature that in that context matters not at all. A reader who knows the debate should be able to tell, at each recurrence, which of the three roles the word is playing, and should feel the argument in the fact that the same word cannot be pinned to a single role.

The six situations the stanzas turn on should be concrete and ordinary, the kind of thing a person might actually face, so that the poem stays about moral life and not about metaethics in the abstract. The envoi should gather the six words as the form requires but should refuse to deliver a moral: no summarizing rule, no closing verdict, since the point is precisely that the poem cannot be reduced to one. Let it end on the tension rather than resolving it.

Begin the response with the poem itself, with no preface and no commentary. Write in American English.
