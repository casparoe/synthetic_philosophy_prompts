I have been corresponding for several months with a man who has been on death row for more than a decade, and the correspondence has driven me back to Camus's late essay on the guillotine with a different set of questions than I had when I first read it as an undergraduate. Next month I am leading a two-hour discussion of The Stranger for an adult education group, and I want to read the novel through Camus's own abolitionism rather than through the usual absurdism-and-the-sun frame. The trouble is that the two texts seem to pull against each other, and I would rather have a reading that holds the tension open than one that harmonizes it.

The essay's case is largely impersonal: deterrence statistics, the irreversibility of judicial error, the asymmetry between a state that cannot give back a life and a condemned man who cannot be compensated for one, and the argument that no private murderer could match the state's premeditation, since the condemned is warned of the date and then held for months at the state's mercy. It also makes a subtler claim about unbelief — that in a pious age the penalty could be coherent because the condemned was handed over to a judgment that might still leave room for atonement, whereas in an unbelieving world the execution is simply an expulsion, a public declaration that a man is irremediable. Running through it is a further thought about what the condemned is denied: not only life but ownership of his death, which is why Camus treats even a self-administered death as a step toward humanity, and why his proposed substitute penalty is a life of labor rather than anything swifter, on the ground that labor can at least be undone. The essay also opens with a memory of his father's reaction to witnessing an execution — a man shattered by having seen a killing done in his name.

The novel, read through that essay, is not obviously an illustration of it. Meursault is condemned less for the shooting than for his failure to perform grief at his mother's funeral; the courtroom's real evidence is character, and the sentence falls on a man whom the proceedings have already converted into a monster. That looks like a dramatization of what the essay calls state revenge and the expulsion of the irremediable. But the final section cuts the other way: Meursault, waiting to die, arrives at something like lucidity and even reconciliation with the world, which is precisely the atonement the essay says an unbelieving world forecloses. And the essay's horror at the interval — the condemned knowing the hour and being held in suspense — sits oddly with a novel whose protagonist becomes most himself only once the outcome is certain.

What I want from you is a critical reading, not a summary of either text. Does The Stranger dramatize the abolitionist case in a way the essay's logic and statistics cannot, or does it supply a counterexample to one of the essay's premises? Which scenes should I put in front of the group — the vigil, the beach and the extra shots, the trial's interrogation of character, the chaplain, the final night — and what does each show that the essay can only argue in the abstract? Where does the reading fail? In particular, say honestly whether Meursault is a usable case for abolition at all, given how deliberately unappealing he is made, and whether the novel's formal choices — the flat first-person narration that continues unbroken through the trial and the cell — do any philosophical work or merely establish a mood. I am not looking for a reading list or a survey of criticism; I want the argument made against the texts, with the points of resistance marked. Please also say what the pairing adds that neither the essay nor a straightforward argument for abolition supplies on its own, and if the honest answer is that the novel and the essay are finally incompatible, say that too.
