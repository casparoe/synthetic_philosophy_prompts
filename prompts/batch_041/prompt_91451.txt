Ok so I need an outside opinion on an argument two of my friends got into at a bbq last weekend, because I genuinely couldnt referee it and its been bugging me all week. Context: I'm a backend engineer, never took a philosophy class in my life, so for all I know this is a solved problem and they were both improvising. Theyre both smart though. Marcus works in a nursing home (he's an aide, does the day-to-day care) and Dana is a lawyer who mostly does wills and estates, so both of them actually know this stuff from their jobs.

The case they were fighting about: a woman at Marcus's home (call her Ruth, not her real name) wrote a living will like 12 years ago, after watching her own dad die with severe dementia. In it she refuses, in advance, any feeding tube or artificial nutrition if she ever reaches severe dementia. Standard stuff. But she also added this extra paragraph, and this is the part that blew up the argument. Roughly: she says she knows that if she gets dementia she might seem happy, might smile at people, might reach for food and say shes hungry — and she says that if we see that, its not really her, that the woman we'd be looking at wont be her anymore, and we shouldnt let "that version" of her override what shes writing now, because the person writing the document is the one who gets to decide.

Fast forward, she now has severe dementia. She's in the home, she seems genuinely cheerful, she eats with her hands and enjoys it, she says she's hungry, she asks for more. No distress, at least as far as anyone can tell. The fight was about what happens when she eventually stops eating or gets an infection — do you follow the paper and let her go, or do you feed her / treat her because the woman in front of you is asking for food and seems to have a decent life?

Here's the back and forth, in order, as close as I can remember it.

(1) Dana said the directive is binding and the extra paragraph makes it airtight — Ruth specifically foresaw this exact situation and told us not to be fooled by it, so the only way to respect her is to follow the paper. She said the woman who wrote that document had a settled, considered, reflective view, and that's the gold standard we use for every other medical decision, so we dont get to throw it out just because the current version of her is pleasant.

(2) Marcus said the extra paragraph is the creepy part, not the convincing part. He said you cant bind a future person like that — its like him writing "if I ever turn into a Republican, ignore everything that guy says." He said the woman sitting in front of him right now is a person right now, with interests right now, and starving her to honor someone who is basically gone is obeying a dead hand.

(3) Dana said the dead-hand point cuts the other way. The entire point of letting people write these things is to bind their future selves; if the later self could always override it, no advance directive would ever mean anything, and you'd be handing everyones final months over to whoever they happen to be at the end, which is usually the least capable version of them. She also said Ruth didnt just state a preference, she gave reasons and anticipated the counterargument, and we normally treat that as the strongest possible evidence of autonomy.

(4) Marcus said her reasoning only works if the later self really is a different person, and Dana cant have it both ways. If its the same person, then she's just changed her mind, and we let people change their minds about treatment constantly — thats the default, not the exception. If its a different person, then the earlier Ruth was trying to control a stranger, which isnt autonomy, its just power over someone who cant fight back. So either way the paragraph doesnt do the work Dana wants it to do.

(5) Dana said "same person or different person" is a false choice, and that what matters isnt metaphysics, its that Ruth cared about her life as a whole, and we honor people by letting the whole-life view beat the moment. She also said Marcus is leaning way too hard on "she seems happy" — she also seems happy eating cake, that doesnt mean shes a chooser, and the apparent wish for food might be a reflex with nobody home behind it.

(6) Marcus said thats the most chilling part of her whole argument — Dana is now in the business of deciding which humans count as "anyone home." And he said it backfires: if there's nobody home, then theres nobody to harm, so why not just feed her? Her argument either treats Ruth as a person whose current wishes matter or as a non-person whose wishes dont — and if she's a non-person, then the directive has nobody left to protect either.

They went around like that for another twenty minutes and neither budged, and honestly I couldnt tell who was winning because every time one of them made a point the other one seemed to have a real answer. Can you go through it and adjudicate it move by move — not just who wins overall but who's right at each numbered step, and where one of them says something that sounds good but actually doesnt hold up? Also tell me what both of them are missing, because I have a feeling the whole fight is missing something and I cant put my finger on it. And if you had to say who's more right at the end of the day, who would it be.
