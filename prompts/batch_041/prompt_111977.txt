That sent me to Austin and the speech-act literature, and I'm now genuinely puzzled, because I keep feeling I already have all the machinery needed to describe what's going on, in the vocabulary I actually use: safe versus unsafe HTTP methods, idempotency keys, preconditions and validation, transactional commit versus rollback, acknowledgements and two-phase commit, event sourcing, side effects and callbacks, capability tokens, and the difference between a request that reports the world and one that changes it. I'd like you to take the core of speech act theory — the original constative/performative distinction and the reasons Austin himself gave it up, the locutionary/illocutionary/perlocutionary stratification, the idea that illocutionary acts are answerable to felicity conditions and constitutive rules rather than to truth, and the direction-of-fit classification of illocutionary types — and restate it as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary. Treat me as fluent in that vocabulary and not at all fluent in yours. Translate systematically, term by term where you can, and don't flatter the translation: if an analogy to HTTP methods or transactions only works for one or two of the illocutionary classes, say so rather than forcing it. My tech lead thinks this is a waste of a sprint, so I'd like the answer to be something I could defend.

Then give me the residue report, and be blunt about it. My suspicion is that everything normative drops out and what's left is a theory of side effects, but I'd rather you tell me than confirm me. Specifically: do sincerity conditions, and with them the very possibility of insincerity, survive at all when the "speaker" is a service with no inner state — or does insincerity require a genuine belief to fall short of, so that a misrepresenting system is not insincere but merely wrong? Is uptake anything more than an acknowledgement and a delivery guarantee, or is that comparison a pun that hides the fact that uptake is interpretive? Does the difference between an act that misfires and one that is performed but defective — a promise made without intent to keep, a verdict from a court with no jurisdiction — correspond to anything in a system whose preconditions are causal rather than normative, where failure to meet a precondition means the operation simply did not occur? And does the indeterminacy of what someone did — warned, threatened, or merely advised — have any counterpart when the operation's type is fixed by the spec and the only question is whether the call succeeded?
