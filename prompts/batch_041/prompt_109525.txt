Ok, so I'm putting together a little set of philosophy prompts and for this one I want a dialogue about the unilateralist's curse. The idea (and have the characters lay it out in the dialogue itself rather than assuming we all know it): when a bunch of people could each do something on their own, and the only thing stopping them is their own judgment that it's a bad idea, the thing tends to get done anyway even when most of the people looking at it think it's a bad idea — because it only takes one yes.

Give me a concrete setting instead of an abstract essay: nine people on the research committee of a mid-size institute, and under the current rules any one of them can unilaterally approve a protocol if a lead scientist asks them directly. No second signature, no vote. There's a request on the table right now (keep the science vague — contained, high stakes, mostly irreversible — but dont spend words on the details), and my two characters are arguing about what the rule should be and what a member should do in the meantime.

Dana: the curse is real and the committee should take it seriously. She thinks a member should only approve if they'd still approve after imagining that the other eight are looking at the same request and might say yes too — defer to the pool unless you have some positive reason to think your read on this one is better than theirs. But she also thinks the real fix is structural (two signatures required), because a norm that asks people to discount their own judgment wont hold up in practice.

Wes: the math is fine but oversold as a guide to what anyone should actually do. Make him argue these, not just list them. The whole thing assumes the nine members' mistakes are independent and scattered around the truth, but in a real committee everyone's read the same two reviews and the same preprint, so the errors are correlated and averaging them buys you nothing — deference then just freezes everyone onto the same wrong answer. Also, the actor can't observe who's in the reference class: the relevant pool isn't "everyone who could approve," it's "everyone who could approve and has actually thought hard about it," and he has no way to know how big that is. Also, the mirror problem — if some good outcome only happens when all nine say yes, then requiring unanimity means it fails whenever any one of them is wrong, so there's a curse against acting and a twin curse against not acting, and which one bites depends on base rates and on whether the action can be undone.

Then have Dana come back at him for real instead of conceding everything: if the errors are correlated, that's an argument for a rule that binds everyone rather than for trusting each person's own judgment, since no amount of individual deliberation fixes a shared bias; and "I've actually thought about it" is what every member will say about themselves, so it can't be the thing that settles who counts. She can also note that the curse only runs one direction when acting is individually cheap — the psychology stuff about people assuming someone else will handle it predicts under-action, but that's a seperate setting, one where acting is costly to the actor.

And Wes should come back at her: the deference rule is self-undermining, since how much I ought to defer depends on whether the others are following the same rule, so if the norm spreads, the value of deferring collapses; plus it hands people a respectable way to avoid owning the decision.

Both of them should be sharp — no strawman, no letting one of them win, and they should answer each other's actual points rather than giving speeches past each other. End on the live disagreement (rules vs. individual judgment plus better information sharing) without resolving it. No names of real authors or papers, no citations, dont invent who said what — just the argument. 900-1300 words.
