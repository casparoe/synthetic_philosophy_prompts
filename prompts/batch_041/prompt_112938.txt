You are a satirist with a working knowledge of the philosophy of work, labour economics, and the sociology of the professions, writing for a venue that publishes deadpan institutional parody — closer to a well-edited business-section pastiche than to a comedy sketch. Your task is to write a job advertisement.

The setting: a firm whose operations are entirely automated has begun hiring humans into posts that consist almost wholly in having been the one who did it — supplying a face for decisions the systems made, absorbing blame when those decisions go badly, and giving the people who hold the posts the feeling of being needed. Your advertisement is for one such post. It is written in perfect earnest by the firm's talent team, and nothing in it is aware that it is funny. Invent the firm and invent the job title; make both sound entirely plausible.

Background you may draw on. Use at least two of the following, and get them right:
- Work as a positional good: if the meaning of a job depends in part on being selected for it, on being needed, or on being recognisable as someone whose work matters, then meaningful work is not a quantity that can be raised for everyone at once. Automating drudgery may relocate the contest rather than end it.
- The accountability sink: the common arrangement in which a human is kept nominally in the loop precisely so that there is somebody to thank or blame — a person whose responsibility is real to onlookers and empty in fact.
- Compensating differentials: employers can pay less when a post offers prestige, purpose, or the sense of mattering, so that meaning functions in recruitment as a form of non-cash compensation.
- The dignity-of-labour argument in welfare policy: the claim that paid work, rather than income as such, is what confers standing and self-respect, and that a society should therefore preserve jobs even where the work is unnecessary.

Requirements:
- Deadpan throughout. The advertisement must never state that the work is unnecessary, that the post is absurd, or that automation has removed its point; the reader should infer all of this. No winks, no italicised irony, no "of course" asides.
- Every claim should be a recognisable feature of real job advertisements and real employment discourse, pushed one notch further, rather than invented from nothing. A philosopher of work or a labour economist should recognise the arguments being dramatised, while a general reader should still find the piece legible.
- Warm, upbeat, faintly over-formal in tone — the careers page of a company that has read a great deal of management theory.
- Do not name real companies, real people, or real products, and do not cite any literature, sources, or further reading.
- Roughly 650–700 words for the advertisement itself.

Format the response as the advertisement, with these sections, in this order, under these headings:
1. About the Role (~100 words) — the post, the firm, the team, and why the role exists.
2. What You'll Do (~150 words) — duties in bullets, each of which quietly transfers responsibility away from you while appearing to assign it.
3. What We're Looking For (~100 words) — the person specification, including the psychological traits the post actually requires.
4. What We Offer (~150 words) — the meaning, purpose, and recognition on offer, described as though they were benefits like dental cover.
5. Compensation and Recognition (~100 words) — pay, and the non-cash considerations offered in its place.
6. How to Apply (~80 words) — the process, and whatever detail best exposes what is really being rationed here.
7. Notes on the Satire (~150 words) — written out of character: name the real features you exaggerated and the arguments you meant to land, and say what you deliberately left unsaid.

Before you write, think carefully about which of the four background ideas you will dramatise and where each will surface, and about the two or three sentences that will do the most work. Do not include your planning in the response.
