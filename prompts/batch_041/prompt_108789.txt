I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of science working on formal theories of inductive inference, and I need an epigraph for the chapter of my dissertation in which I set out Solomonoff's universal prior and try to be honest about what it does and does not deliver for Occam's razor. I want a poem whose shape carries the argument rather than decorating it.

The constraint is this. Four numbered sections of unrhymed verse, in loose iambic pentameter, of eight lines, then four, then two, then one. Each section takes up a hypothesis consistent with the data that stands one step further out in description length than the one before it: the first section, being the longest, is given to the shortest hypothesis, and the fourth, a single line, to one three bits longer, carrying an eighth of the weight, so that the space the poem allots each hypothesis is proportional to the weight the prior assigns it. The poem's proportions should be the prior's proportions. The single line of the fourth section is the turn, the moment the inquirer sees why this weighting, though perfectly definite, cannot be computed: the sum runs over programs that may never halt, so the weights can only be approached from below and some of the mass is never recovered.

Make it accurate in the following respects, since these are the places I would notice sloppiness. The prior is a semi-measure rather than a probability, because programs that never halt contribute nothing. The exponential penalty on length is not a heuristic laid on top of Bayesian updating but falls out of the requirement that weights over a countable set of programs sum to at most one. The verdict about which description is shortest is relative to a choice of machine and survives the change of machine only up to a constant factor, which is a real qualification rather than a refutation. And the convergence guarantee is measured against the description length of the process that actually generated the data. The speaker should be someone trying to use the razor on a real sequence and repeatedly meeting the fact that the ideal inquirer is not one of us. No symbols, no formulas, no reciting terminology as though naming the result were the point; a reader who knows the theory should recognize it in the shape of the thing, and a reader who does not should still have a poem.

The fifth numbered item is two sentences of prose: the first saying how confident you are that the technical content is right and naming the single point you are least sure of, the second saying where the poem takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading, including, if you think it needs flagging, the looseness of mapping line-length onto program-length.

Exactly five numbered items and nothing else, with no preamble and no closing remarks.
