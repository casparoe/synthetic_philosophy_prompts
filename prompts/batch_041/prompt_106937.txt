I am a novelist, and in three weeks I am to lead a session at a writers' residency for a dozen fiction writers, most of them at work on books that turn on some piece of machinery or infrastructure — a dam, a telegraph office, a municipal water system, a scheduling algorithm. The session is billed, for the brochure's sake, as "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" I have an hour, a room with no projector, and an audience that reads widely and has no training in philosophy or in the history of technology. I do not want the lecture; I want the plan for it. My own reason for proposing the hour is a scene I keep writing and distrusting: a device does something to a town, and I cannot tell whether I am writing about the device or about the men who chose it.

Tell me the order in which the pieces must come, and why each depends on the one before it — what has to be in place before the question can even be asked, and what can wait. I expect to build the hour on one case. My instinct is the Long Island parkway bridges: the low overpasses that a certain master builder is said to have kept low so that the buses carrying the poor could not reach the beaches. It is the story everyone reaches for, which is itself a reason for caution; tell me whether it should carry the session or be demoted to a footnote, and if it should be replaced, what its replacement must have and must lack. I have been working with a rough three-part distinction of my own and would rather the session sharpen it than replace it: artifacts whose design quietly settles who can go where; artifacts that cannot be run at all without a particular kind of authority standing over them; and artifacts that merely make some arrangements easier than others. If the line between the second and third is the whole argument, say so, and tell me how to handle it in front of a room that will want it settled inside ten minutes. The misconceptions I expect are these — that any of it amounts to saying machines are evil; that it collapses into the harmless observation that people use tools well or badly; that determinism means the future is predictable; that an artifact is political only if someone deliberately made it so. Where in the sequence should each be met, and which of them will the bridges case make worse rather than better? Finish with the single question I can put to the room at the end that will show me whether they can now do the thing rather than merely nod at it — take some device they have not discussed and place it, and say what evidence would move it — and with what I should tell them outright that I am not going to explain. I don't need a reading list; the residency has a library and nobody uses it.
