I write a column on ideas for a mid-size paper and I've been rereading Pascal's Pensées for a piece I keep not finishing, and last week a friend who teaches civics pushed back on something I said. My line was that the Little Rock business in 1957 is the cleanest illustration you could want of what Pascal says about justice and force, and my friend said that's just the civics-class version of Pascal, that the real episode is messier and mostly embarrasses him. I want you to settle it. Here's the episode as I'd give it to a reader: Brown in 1954, the Little Rock school board's gradual plan, Faubus ordering the Arkansas National Guard to keep the nine Black students out of Central High in September 1957, Eisenhower federalizing that same Guard and sending in the 101st Airborne, the students finally getting in on the 25th, and then the Court the next year telling Arkansas officials in effect that they don't get to reinterpret Brown for themselves. Pascal's account, as I read him: justice without force is impotent, force without justice is tyrannical, so we should put the two together — and since we can't make what is just be strong, we make what is strong be just, which he more or less admits is a bit of a swindle, and he seems fine with the swindle, because he also thinks we never really know the just anyway and that in practice we obey established law and custom not because it's just but because it's established, and that the wise man obeys it for that reason. He also has that line about tyranny being when someone wants, by virtue of one thing, the deference owed by virtue of another.

So do the mapping in both directions. Who is justice without force here, who is force without justice, who is the tyrant in Pascal's technical sense, and what exactly is the federalization of the Guard — is that force being married to justice, or is it the strong being relabeled just, or something Pascal doesn't have a slot for? Then tell me where he breaks. My suspicion is that he can't handle the fact that the same Guard, same uniforms, same rifles, was the instrument of both Faubus and Eisenhower inside of two weeks, and that what changed wasn't the force or the justice but who was recognized as entitled to command — which smells like a third thing, authority or legitimacy, that he either folds into custom or ignores. He also has almost nothing to say about courts, procedure, or the three slow years between Brown and the paratroopers; his picture of the world is monarchs, armies, and custom, and this is a case where the decisive force shows up only after years of litigation, and I want to know whether that's a gap in him or whether he'd say the litigation was just another form of force in a nicer suit. And go the other way too: what does the episode do to his advice about the wise man obeying established law because it's established, given that the established law of Arkansas was the unjust thing and the Court overrode it? If Pascal has to either condemn the Court for unsettling custom or admit custom isn't the ground he said it was, say which and why, and don't force him to have a view where he really just doesn't. I'd rather have an honest account of what his framework misses than a tidy one that makes him right about everything.
