This is a student who has done well all term, whose prose is confident and well organized, and that is exactly why I want a second pass: the scores on this question will be used to set the curve, and I would rather catch a problem now than discover it in office hours during an appeal. Please grade the answer against the rubric and give me the marginal comments I would actually write, not a summary verdict. For each rubric line, say what the student got right, then say precisely what is wrong with anything that is wrong, whether it is a misattribution, a conflation of two different stretches of Kant's text, or a step that simply does not follow from what precedes it. The exam question: "Kant's reply to Benjamin Constant is famous for insisting that one must not lie even to a murderer who asks where one's friend is hiding. (a) Set out Constant's challenge as Kant presents it, including the principle by which Constant limits the duty to tell the truth. (b) Reconstruct Kant's reply, and explain carefully what Kant is denying when he rejects the idea that the murderer, or anyone, has a right to truth. (c) The essay is often read as a piece of rigorism in tension with the Formula of Humanity of the Groundwork. Assess that charge: is the essay's conclusion consistent with the Formula of Humanity, and does the essay argue from it? Answer all three parts."

The student's answer: "Constant's challenge is that the duty to tell the truth is not unconditional but reciprocal. It binds us only toward those who have a right to the truth, and since a murderer who means to use our answer to do harm has no such right, the duty lapses in his case; on Constant's view, answering truthfully would make us complicit in the crime he intends. Kant's reply turns on a distinction that Constant blurs. There is no such thing as a right to truth, because truth is not a possession that one person can owe to another or hand over to another; what can be owed is veracity, the duty to speak as one thinks. Kant therefore refuses Constant's condition, and he holds that the duty of veracity does not lapse when the hearer is a villain: the murderer is a rational being, and what is owed to rational beings is owed to him too, so a lie would wrong him. Kant concludes that one must answer the murderer truthfully. He adds that if one lies and the friend, having slipped out, is caught and killed, one is answerable for the death, whereas if one tells the truth one is not, which shows that the lie, not the truth, is what puts the friend in danger. Part (c) is where the essay gets its bad reputation, but I think the reputation is undeserved. The conclusion is consistent with the Formula of Humanity, and the essay in fact argues from it: to lie to the murderer is to use him as a mere means, a tool for protecting my friend, and the Formula forbids treating anyone merely as a means. The murderer example is thus an application of the Groundwork's second formulation, and it stands or falls with the universalizability test that the Groundwork works out in the false-promise example: a maxim of lying cannot be willed as a universal law, and that is why the lie is forbidden. So the rigorism is only apparent."

The rubric is out of 20 points: 4 for part (a), 8 for part (b), 8 for part (c). Within (b), the points are for stating Kant's conclusion, for the account of why there is no right to truth, and for saying what the duty of veracity is a duty to; within (c), the points are for separating the question of consistency from the question of derivation, for assessing the alleged tension, and for getting the relationship between the essay and the Groundwork right. Please assign points to each rubric line, give a total out of 20, and then add two short paragraphs: the first naming the single sentence in the answer that most needs correction, together with a corrected version of it, and the second listing anything the student got right that a hurried grader might have missed or wrongly marked down.
