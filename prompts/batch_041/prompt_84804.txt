I'm in a cross-listed history/philosophy seminar on the outbreak of the First World War and the war-guilt question, and after a whole term of it I still can't tell whether anyone in the room is actually disagreeing with anyone else. The historians are split the usual way: on one side the Fischer line and its descendants, who think the German leadership deliberately took the risk of a European war in July 1914 (Fischer's 1961 book, and the whole scandal it set off in West Germany), on the other the structuralists, who say nobody really wanted this war, that it came out of the alliance system, out of mobilization timetables that couldn't be stopped once started, and out of everyone's fear of being the one who mobilized second -- and then a third camp that explains it all by domestic politics, elites deflecting internal unrest at home. They're all reading alot of the same documents and none of them budges. Then the philosophers come in and say that even if you settled every factual question, blaming a nation is a category error, since there's no collective mind that could have intended anything; and the historians shrug and say of course they dont mean the whole German population, they mean the dozen men who made the calls, which makes the objection look beside the point. And underneath all of it sits the Versailles war-guilt clause, which (funny detail) doesn't actually use the word guilt at all -- it has Germany accepting responsibility for causing all the loss and damage -- but everyone treats it as either a legal finding or a moral verdict, depending on what they need from it that day.

My hypothesis, and I'd rather you test it than agree with me: there are at least three different questions wearing one name here. An empirical one about what actually caused the July Crisis. A methodological one about what can even count as a cause of a one-off event, where you can't rerun the counterfactual -- does a cause have to be necessary, sufficient, intended, a difference-maker? And a moral one about collective responsibility, which may not be the same kind of question as either of the others. If that's roughly right, tell me which parts of this are a genuinely stalled dispute and which are just people answering different questions under one heading, and in the latter case which heading is doing the damage. I also want to know whether the historians' fight is really empirical at all, or whether it's mostly a disagreement about standards -- how much weight pre-war memos get against what people actually did in July, whether a statement like Germany caused it can mean anything at all once you notice that everyone's moves were links in the same chain. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a shared criterion for causal claims in history, or just admitting that the moral question can't be settled by the historical one? Don't tell me who was to blame -- I want the shape of the disagreement, not a verdict.
