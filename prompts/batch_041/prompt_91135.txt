The arguments about Spinoza I follow on the rationalist blogs usually end with each side accusing the other of reading its own commitments into him, and I'd like to see the dispute done properly rather than caricatured. Write a dialogue between two competent readers of Spinoza who disagree about whether his political thought is democratic at bottom. The first holds that his identification of right with power gives democracy a privileged place: only where the power that governs is the power of the governed is a state not a foreign force bearing down on its subjects, and his defence of freedom of thought -- the commonwealth exists so that people may reason, and one that holds its subjects in check by fear and ignorance is weak -- is of a piece with that commitment. The second holds that this is wishful: on Spinoza's own account democracy is one arrangement of power among others, its virtues contingent and instrumental, the state's business to work on the passions of people who mostly lack adequate ideas, and the fact that he elaborated the machinery of aristocracy in detail while leaving the account of democracy unfinished tells against any principled allegiance to the many. Let them argue it out, testing each reading against hard cases -- whether a Spinozist must tolerate expression that inflames the passions, whether a state may legitimately shape what its subjects desire, what becomes of the argument when the multitude is ignorant and frightened -- and let neither be handed a weak version of his position. I want real back-and-forth: each should answer the other's actual point before moving on, and the exchange should leave the disagreement sharper rather than dissolved.
