Okay so I host this tiny interview podcast — it's basically me, a decent mic, and whoever will agree to talk for forty minutes — and the guest for the next episode is a philosopher who's book argues that moral sainthood is self-undermining rather than merely unattractive. Her central move, which she calls the absorptive-goods argument (I'd never come across the phrase before, so I assume it's hers), runs like this: certain goods — an actual friendship, being lost in a craft, caring about a subject or a person for its own sake and not as a means — can only be possessed by someone willing to be absorbed in them, someone for whom they are not up for renegotiation every time a better option appears; but the moral saint is constitutively disposed to weigh whatever she is doing against the moral value of the alternatives, which makes every activity fungible, and someone who is always ready to trade any pursuit for a morally better one cannot be absorbed in anything. So the saint secures the moral maximum at the cost of the goods that give a life its point — and the twist she adds is that this isn't just a sad trade-off but a contradiction, since a life stripped of those goods has nothing left for morality to be about.

I want questions that go after the machinery, not ones that let her restate the thesis for the fiftieth time. She has a stock deflecting move whenever anyone pushes back — she says that's a claim about a maximizing saint, not about sainthood, and then pivots to talking about fungibility — and I'd like the sequence to make that move progressively harder to reach for. Please give me eight to ten questions, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one question each on: (a) whether the fungibility premise is really built into sainthood or only into a certain maximizing picture of it, and what sainthood looks like if you drop it; (b) whether the argument proves too much, or whether there might be absorptive moral goods — someone genuinely absorbed in a cause, or in other people's flourishing — that would let a saint have the goods after all; and (c) what the argument is actually supposed to establish — that a saint's life goes worse for her, that the ideal is incoherent, or that we should simply stop treating it as an ideal — since those pull in pretty different directions. It would also be nice to have something that forces her to say whether the complaint is really a moral one at all or whether it's an aesthetic or prudential grumble wearing moral clothes, because I suspect that's where the soft spot is. Then close with one question about what would count against the whole thing, something she couldn't wriggle out of by redescribing the saint. And please don't ask her what drew her to philosophy.
