I'm a backend engineer, never studied philosophy beyond half a term of an ethics elective I've mostly forgotten, so forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary. A mate from work — Dan, he's the type who reads longtermism blogs at lunch and tries to convert the rest of us — got me onto the simulation question a few weeks ago, and we've been arguing about it ever since. His claim is that taking the simulation argument seriously should take the wind out of the longtermist sails. His reasoning, as best I can reconstruct it: longtermism gets its whole force from enormous expected-value sums about the far future — astronomical numbers of lives, stakes so big that you ought to be willing to sacrifice almost anything for a hair's-breadth increase in the chance of a good far future. But if there's a serious chance we're living in a simulation, then some of whatever we achieve out here is really the simulators' achievement, or might just be switched off, and — more to the point — our confidence that the far future is ours to shape should get spread across a whole lot of weird possibilities. Once you do that spreading, the astronomical numbers stop being astronomical, and the fanaticism, the bit where you'd burn everything down for a tiny probability bump, loses its grip. He calls this 'the sim discount', which I think is a bit cute, but there you go.

What I want from you is this: what would David Hume have made of it? Not a general tour of Hume, and not a wishy-washy "he might have said this, or then again he might have said that" — I want you to actually commit to where he'd have landed. Would he have agreed that the simulation argument dampens fanaticism about the far future, would he have thought the whole style of argument was bogus, or something else again? I know he was the big empiricist, sceptical about how far reason actually gets us and keen on custom and habit, but I genuinely can't work out how that maps onto probability arguments about being simulated, and every time I try I tie myself in knots. Walk me through it in a way a non-philosopher can follow, and end by saying plainly what you think Hume's verdict would have been — I'd much rather have a definate answer with reasons I can push back on than a balanced survey of the options.
