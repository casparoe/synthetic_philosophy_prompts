I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I recently sat in on a workshop on research evaluation where two participants — a policy economist and a machine-learning researcher — got into an argument I have not been able to settle on my own. I would like you to referee it.

The economist opened: Goodhart's law is close to vacuous. It says that a measure imperfectly correlated with what we care about will mislead us once we start optimizing it. True, but that is a fact about proxies, not a discovery about the world; it is an analytic remark dressed up as an empirical one.

The researcher: It is substantive, and the evidence is that the failure is not random. Measures do not decay evenly — they decay in a determinate direction, toward whatever is cheapest to produce. That the distortion has a predictable shape is a real claim about the world.

The economist: Then it is a claim about incentives, not measurement, and it has an obvious fix. Choose a measure that is causally downstream of the goal rather than a mere correlate of it — count what the goal itself produces. Then gaming the measure just is pursuing the goal.

The researcher: That only moves the problem up a level. A downstream measure is still finite, still a proxy, and an agent under pressure will find the cheapest way to produce the downstream effect. And there is a sharper point: a proxy has bounded range, while the goal usually does not. Optimize a bounded quantity against an unbounded one and the two must eventually come apart. The lesson is not to pick a better proxy; it is that any proxy can be optimized against.

The economist: So the law is a claim about unlimited optimization pressure, which no real institution applies. Real institutions are sloppy, and the sloppiness is protective.

The researcher: Granted, in part. But the damage I care about is not the agent's behavior — it is the principal's. Once the measure is in place, the principal begins to want what the measure measures. The metric does not merely distort the report of the goal; it reconstitutes the goal, so there is no independent standard left against which to check whether the metric has gone bad.

Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six moves, say who has the better of it and why. Then say what both of them are missing — in particular, whether the economist's downstream-measure escape works, and whether the researcher's closing point about the principal is a genuine extension of the law or a change of subject.
