I run a small reading group on philosophy of biology — a couple of grad students, two people from the local natural history museum, one retired entomologist — and I'd like a dialogue I can hand out, so I'd like you to write it.

Two characters, both sharp, neither one a strawman, standing in front of a display case arguing about the label. What's in the case is a ring species: a chain of populations around a valley where every neighbouring pair interbreeds, but the two ends of the chain meet on the far side of the valley and dont interbreed, they just share the ground. The museum has to print something on the card — one species, two, three?

A: the only real things are lineage segments — the stretch of an ancestral-descendant lineage between one splitting event and the next, monophyletic and nothing else. So "species" as a rank, a category with defining properties, isn't a natural kind at all, and the whole species problem comes from people hunting for the definition of a category instead of reading the tree. The ring, on this view, is one species; the fact that the ends don't interbreed is incipient speciation, not a boundary.

B: species are real, but as concrete historical individuals rather than classes with essences, and there really are several different processes doing work in nature — gene flow, selection, ecology, drift, splitting — so it's no wonder that different legitimate criteria carve the same chunk of life differently. Thats realism about several real patterns, not "it's all up to us."

Have them actually go at each other, real back-and-forth rather than two monologues. A should press that if conspecificity by interbreeding comes out non-transitive in the ring (each neighbouring pair interbreeds, the ends don't), then gene flow doesn't even give you a partition, so it can't be a real pattern on a par with lineage segments; and that B has no principled way to say which groupings are real — why the valley populations and not all the brown things? B should press that a "splitting event" is itself a matter of degree and gets murky fast with hybrids and asexual lineages, that a monophyly-only rule has to dissolve or rename a parent species the moment a small daughter population buds off, and that a smoothly evolving asexual lineage forces A either to call the whole thing one species or to make arbitrary cuts. Both should use the asexual and hybrid cases; neither gets to say "the facts settle it" for free.

Somewhere in the middle I want them to pin down what they're actually disagreeing about — nature, or the word, or both at once — and I don't want it to fizzle into "depends what you want species for." Take a side: tell me at the end which one has the better of it and why, and make one of them concede something real. A definite verdict I might argue with beats a survey. No citations, no names of researchers, no invented quotes — I don't need the literature, just the argument. Ten minutes' reading, tops, and then a short note afterwards on where the ring species case is doing more work than it can really bear, and which of the two positions you had to stiffen a bit to make the exchange work.
