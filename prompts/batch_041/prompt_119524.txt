I'm in a bioethics reading group and last week one of the other members — a palliative care fellow — put an argument up on the whiteboard and then had to leave early to cover a shift. She said she'd half-reconstructed it from something she read years ago and couldn't remember where, so I can't check a source, but I dont think that matters much here: the thing either works or it doesn't on its own. Here's what she wrote.

(1) Whether an outcome counts as something I did or merely as something I allowed depends only on the causal structure of what happened — if the outcome wouldn't have occurred but for my behaviour, I made it happen. (2) When I switch off a ventilator, my behaviour is a but-for cause of the patient's death in exactly the way it is when I push the plunger. (3) So if the difference between killing and letting die is a difference in causal structure, then in the ventilator case there is no such difference; both are killings. (4) And this generalises: for any case of letting die there is some description of my behaviour under which it is a but-for cause of the death — I stayed at my desk, I kept walking, I didn't open the door. (5) If two pieces of behaviour share a causal structure, no moral difference between them can rest on the doing/allowing distinction. (6) So the doing/allowing distinction marks no moral difference anywhere. (7) So if it is ever permissible to withdraw futile treatment, it is sometimes permissible to kill a patient, and any rule that separates the two — double effect, the standard medical-ethics line, whatever you like — is resting on nothing.

I keep going in circles on this. Every time I try to say where it goes wrong I end up denying something I'm sure of, like that switching the ventilator off is at least a candidate for being something I did rather than something I merely let happen. What I'd most like to keep is (7)'s antecedent, that withdrawing futile treatment is sometimes permissible; what I'd least like to be forced into is the claim that a doctor may give a lethal injection on request. Almost any other premise I'll pay. So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine when she wrote it, and what does rejecting it cost me in the cases that made me want it? I don't want just the name of the move, I want the price tag, including what it does to the cases where the two acts look least like each other.

Please take the role of someone who's spent a lot of time in this literature and has no patience for slogans from either side. Before you commit to a diagnosis, brainstorm three or four candidate places the argument could break and say why you're dropping the weaker ones — I'd rather watch you throw out a catch that only sounds clever than have you keep it. And if it turns out there's no cheap flaw here and the thing is simply valid, so that I really do have to give up a premise I like, then say that straight instead of manufacturing a defect; I'd rather be told I'm stuck. If I've set it up so it can't be answered well — if the numbering is hiding a step she skipped — say that first.
