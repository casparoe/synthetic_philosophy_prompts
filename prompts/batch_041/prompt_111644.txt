I teach a second-year undergrad module on philosophy of language — about 18 students, one 90-minute session — and I want to run something on Wittgenstein (language games, family resemblance, meaning as use) that doesnt collapse into me drawing overlapping circles on the board. Please design the whole session around the specific seed below rather than around the topic in general.

The seed: a deli. It has a menu, and over the years it has added items and quietly decided, item by item, whether each one counts as a sandwich — for pricing, for the combo deal, for what goes in the sandwich section of the board. Hot dog: yes. Burrito: no. Wrap: yes. Open-faced turkey thing: yes. Ravioli: no. Pop-tart: no, though the guy at the counter wavers. Folded slice of pizza: no, but he calls it one when he's joking. Calzone: he says no and then charges it like one. Two slices of bread with nothing in between: yes? Nobody's ever ordered it.

What I want them to run into: the sequence of decisions isn't capturable by any rule they can write down — every definition they try will have exceptions on the actual timeline — and yet it isn't random either. A regular can predict the next call pretty reliably, and would say a newcomer who got one wrong simply didn't get it. I want them to feel that: a competence that's real, shared, and teachable by correction, and still not statable. And I want them to notice it shifted over time with no announced change of rule.

So give me roles, materials, a timeline I can cut into cards (tell me the items and the order), a moment where they commit to a definition in writing and then get hit with the counterexample, and a debrief that maps what happened onto language games, meaning-as-use, and family resemblance versus essentialism. They shouldn't hear any of those terms until the debrief, and I don't want to lecture up front. Include the bit where one student is quietly briefed to run a slightly different private rule and gets corrected by the others — I want them to see learning-by-correction doing work no definition did. If they stall I can bring in the botanist-versus-greengrocer thing about tomatoes, but I'd rather they generate a second practice themselves.

Also: a run-sheet with timings, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what to do when it does, and an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce. My guess is the rule-following point proper — that even a fully shared practice underdetermines the next case — is out of reach here and I'll have to just say it, but tell me if you think the real gap is somewhere else.
