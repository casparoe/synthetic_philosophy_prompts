I have been defending a reading of Fitch's derivation that splits it in two, and I want to know what it is exposed to.

The derivation is usually compressed into a single claim: from the knowability principle, the factivity of knowledge, and the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, it follows that every truth is known. I want to separate the part of the reasoning that needs only those three ingredients from the part that needs more. The first part runs as follows. Take any truth p, and suppose that p is never known. Then p together with p's never being known is a truth, so by the knowability principle it is possible that someone knows their conjunction. But a knower of the conjunction would know p and would know that p is never known, and knowing that p is never known is incompatible with knowing p. So the supposition that p is never known is refuted. Nothing in that stretch of reasoning uses excluded middle or double-negation elimination, and I take it to be acceptable to an intuitionist. The second part is the step from there to the conclusion that every truth is known, and it is precisely there that a classical step enters: one has to move from the refutability of p's never being known to p's being known, which is an application of double-negation elimination.

My claim is that an antirealist who is committed to intuitionistic logic — as I take the Dummettian antirealist about meaning to be, since the manifestability requirement is what is supposed to make classical negation unavailable — can take the first part and refuse the second, and that the refusal is not an ad hoc dodge but the very refusal of double-negation elimination on which the position is built. I also claim that what survives is not a consolation prize: for every truth p, the hypothesis that p is never known is refutable, and something close to that is what the antirealist actually needs, since what they need is the incoherence of the never-known truth rather than the existence of an omniscient knower. I have considered re-indexing the knowledge operator to particular agents or times and, as far as I can see, that does not by itself dissolve the derivation; if you think it does, that is itself a defeater I want to hear.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from those that should make me abandon the position to those that are merely awkward, and I want the ones that a defender of the position would themselves concede, not only the ones an opponent would press. In particular, I want to know which ingredients of my first part are load-bearing in a way the antirealist cannot repair — the factivity of knowledge, the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, the applicability of the knowability schema to a proposition that itself mentions knowledge, and the assumption that the possibility operator behaves normally — and which apparent escape routes are real rather than relabellings, either of the standard restriction strategies or of the classical result. I also want the consequences of the position drawn out rather than softened: if the intuitionist antirealist must, for every truth, deny that it is never known, can they still assert that there are truths no one knows, or can they only refuse to assert it of any particular truth? If the latter, I want that stated as a consequence of my position and either defended or conceded.

Give me a verdict rather than a survey. If you think the position survives, say which objection is the strongest one that survives and why it fails. If you think it fails, name the single consideration that does the most damage and say what I would have to give up in order to keep the position. Do not close by handing the options back to me.
