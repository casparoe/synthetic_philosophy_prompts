I want you to write me a dialogue between two philosophers, and I'd like you to think hard about the strongest version of each side before you start writing — I don't want two people reciting their team's talking points at each other. You're a philosopher of the social sciences who's spent years on the metaphysics of social kinds — the Hacking/Searle collective-acceptance tradition, the realist pushback that says some social kinds are functionally defined rather than merely conferred, all of it — and you're good at keeping both characters at the same level of competence and making each one answer what the other actually said.

Here's the fight. Dana holds the conferralist line: money, race, and gender are all the same basic kind of thing. They exist because we collectively confer a status on them, and the conferral is doing all the work; everything else is downstream. Miles holds the realist line: money is a different animal. He says a conferral only sticks when it hooks onto non-social facts — stuff being divisible, durable, countable, and above all the standing need to settle debts and coordinate trade — so there's a fact of the matter about whether a conferral succeeds, and that fact isn't itself social. Dana thinks Miles has smuggled in a natural kind through the back door. Miles thinks Dana's view can't explain why you can't just declare pebbles to be money tomorrow.

I've got a distinction I've been kicking around and I want the two of them to put it under pressure rather than just nod at it. Call it conferral kinds versus equilibrium kinds. Conferral kinds exist just in virtue of acceptance; equilibrium kinds exist as a stable resting point that acceptance has to find, one whose details could have been otherwise but whose broad shape couldn't. The thing I want them actually fighting about is wether money is the second kind while race and gender are the first, or whether that's a distinction without a difference.

Three pieces of material I want them to use rather than just mention:

- A coal town around 1900 where the company pays wages in scrip redeemable only at the company store. Within a year the barber takes it, the priest takes it for a mass intention, and a farmer three miles out takes it for eggs. The mine floods, the company folds, the scrip is worthless — and then a rumor goes around that a buyer back East will honor it at forty cents on the dollar, and it starts changing hands at thirty. Nobody ever checks the rumor.
- Cigarettes as money in POW camps in the war: the stuff is also a consumption good, so the money supply literally burns up as people smoke it, and there's a real good sitting underneath it.
- A new regime that issues a token nobody wants, then announces it will accept that token, and only that token, in payment of taxes, and watches it turn into money.
