I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar on perspectival theories of truth and I want to check my own line before I have to defend it out loud, so: here's where I've landed, tell me whether it hangs together.

(1) Every assertion about how things are is true only from some standpoint. I don't think there's any such thing as a claim that's just true, full stop, with no silent from-where attached to it. (2) That isn't because we're trapped behind our descriptions: reality is itself many-sided, and that's why no single standpoint can exhaust it. The multiplicity is in the object, not only in us. (3) I'm also a scientific realist. Our best theories are converging on one correct account of nature, and when physics tells us there's no sharp position and momentum at once, that settles it — it isn't just one more useful perspective. (4) I keep ordinary logic for ordinary claims: fix a standpoint and a reading of the predicate, and every proposition is either true or false there, never both. (5) But I'm happy to say of one and the same thing that in some sense it is, in some sense it isn't, in some sense it both is and isn't, and in some sense nothing can be said of it — without thinking that reality contains contradictions. Predication just runs out. (6) And the whole list above is itself one standpoint, obviously. I've been reading the Jains on this and I think the many-sidedness picture is basically right; I just want to keep a few things they'd have thrown out.

So: which of these are actually pulling against each other, which only look like they do, how bad is each one, and what's the cheapest repair — and what would that repair cost me? I'm definately not looking for a lecture that ends with it all being fine.
