Write a short story set in an invented city where, for three generations, citizens have observed a civic ritual known as the mirror vigil. Once in a lifetime, on a night assigned by lot, a citizen sits alone in a bare room while a screen counts upward through the number of years that elapsed before her birth, and is required to contemplate that span as her own non-existence — the same non-existence to which she will return. The vigil was devised by a civic philosopher who took the Epicurean argument at its word: if what is dissolved is without sensation, and what is without sensation is nothing to us, then death is nothing to us; the ritual exists to make the symmetry between the time before a life and the time after it emotionally real, so that the second span loses its terror.

Center the story on a woman near the end of her life who has undergone the vigil and has spent years defending its logic, and put that logic under pressure rather than merely reciting it. The following should be live in the story, shaping its scenes and its ending:

- the deprivation account, on which death harms the one who dies by cutting off goods she would otherwise have enjoyed, together with the objection that this account asks us to compare her existence with something that is not a condition of anyone at all;
- the question of when the harm of death occurs, given that no subject exists at the time she is supposedly harmed — keeping distinct, if the story turns on it, the harm of dying (a process that can be experienced) from the harm of death (a state that cannot be);
- the symmetry between pre-natal and post-mortem non-existence, and whether the asymmetry in our attitudes — we do not mourn the years before our birth, yet we dread the years after our death — can be defended or must be exposed as an irrational partiality;
- the experiential requirement on harm (only what is felt by a subject can harm her) and the cases that seem to refute it, such as being betrayed or slandered without ever learning of it;
- whether a person can be harmed after she has ceased to exist altogether, for instance by the posthumous betrayal of her wishes or the ruin of what she cared about.

Invent at least one further element of the city's practice or doctrine that the characters disagree about — a rule, a ledger, an amendment to the vigil — so that their disagreement is specific to this world rather than a generic exchange of positions. No citations, references, or named authorities are needed; let the ideas be carried by the fiction.

Let the story reach a verdict: the character should arrive at a definite answer, in her own voice and through the story's events, to the question of whether her death will be bad for her, and the story should show why the answers she rejects fail her. Close with a brief afterword, in your own voice and no longer than a paragraph, stating which position the story commits to and why, and noting candidly where it takes liberties with the philosophical material or leans on a contested reading of it. Do not hedge there: name the position you have committed to and defend the choice.
