Last night I spent four hours arguing with an old friend who has talked himself into expressivism: he holds that calling an act cruel is not the reporting of a fact about it but the venting of an attitude, something nearer a boo than a belief. I put the difficulty to him as plainly as I could. His account is supposed to tell us what the sentence means, yet the very same words turn up in the antecedent of a conditional, where nobody is venting anything: one can utter, understand and reason with a conditional of the form 'if cruelty is wrong, then what happens in that shed is wrong' while holding no attitude towards cruelty at all. He gave me back something I have not managed to answer. 'Damn' goes on leaking contempt even when it is buried inside an if-clause — if that damn dog starts up again — although nobody in that sentence is cursing anyone. Embedding, he said, evidently does not strip a sentence of its expressive life, so my objection proves rather less than I had hoped. We stopped at two in the morning, both bruised, neither persuaded.

I should like a poem to send him, to close the evening properly. Please write one in five four-line stanzas, no fixed metre. A single sentence expressing a moral verdict on some concrete act should appear exactly once in each stanza, in identical wording, and it should carry the poem the way a refrain does. What must change is its grammatical position: in the first stanza it should stand alone as a flat assertion; in the second it should sit in the antecedent of a conditional; in the third it should fall inside something merely wondered at or denied; in the fourth it should be a question; and in the fifth it should return as a bare exclamation with no frame around it at all. The shape is the argument: a reader should see that the words contribute the same thing in all five places — the same verdict — while only some of the occurrences are actually issued. Do not settle which of us was right; end on the tension, and let the 'damn' point feel like genuine evidence rather than a curiosity. It should satisfy someone who works on this and still read as a poem rather than as a diagram.
