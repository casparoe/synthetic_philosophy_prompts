I'm a novelist and I run a monthly workshop for about fourteen writers -- mixed levels, we get a drafty church hall for 90 minutes, and what I've got to work with is a big whiteboard, a few hundred index cards and pens, no projector, phones get dumped in a box at the door anyway. I want to open one session with an activity that makes them feel the extended mind thing from the inside -- the idea that some of what we call thinking is actually happening out in the world, on the board, in the cards, in the room -- rather than hear me assert it. Writers should be the perfect audience for this, we all already think with notebooks and index cards and half-finished drafts, and not one of us believes it about ourselves.

Three things I specifically want them to walk into, not be told: that leaning on an external thing stops feeling like using a tool and starts feeling like just thinking; that taking that thing away feels less like losing an object than like losing a chunk of your own memory; and then the ugly follow-up -- if the whiteboard was part of my mind, was it part of yours too, and did we have one mind up there or fourteen.

So give me the game: rules, materials, a scoring scheme with small stakes (loser buys the round), and a timeline for the 90 minutes. Whatever it is, the scoring has to depend on stuff that lives on the board and the cards rather than in their heads, so that when I take it away the drop is brutal and measurable, and so the removal lands as an amputation and not as me being cute. Please dont name the thesis or its authors until the very end; I'd rather they back into it. Then a debrief that takes what actually happened and maps it onto the ideas, including the removal test and the coupled-system part. Tell me honestly what will go wrong the first time I run it and how to salvage it, and which piece of the thesis the activity can't give them no matter how well it goes. If it fits, I'd like to close on this line: "Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin?"
