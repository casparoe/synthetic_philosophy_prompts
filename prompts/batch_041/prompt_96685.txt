I've been going back and forth with a friend about the absent-minded driver, and his argument seems to prove to much. Quick setup: you're driving home, there are two exits, and you can't tell them apart when you reach one — same view, no signs, and you forget having passed the first the instant you pass it. Taking the first exit pays 0, taking the second pays 4, driving past both pays 1. You know all this. His argument:

(1) A rational agent always takes the action with the highest expected payoff at the point of decision.
(2) At the second exit, taking it pays 4 and driving on pays 1, so the rational thing there is to take it.
(3) At the first exit, taking it pays 0, while driving on leads to the second exit, where by (2) you take it and get 4, so the rational thing there is to drive on.
(4) So the rational plan is to drive on at the first and exit at the second.
(5) That plan pays 4, which beats the mixed strategy of driving on with probability 2/3, which pays only 4/3.
(6) So the standard expected-utility answer — randomize 2/3 — is wrong, and the driver should just follow the plan in (4).

But (4) is something the driver literally cannot do, since the two exits are indistinguishable. Still, (1)-(3) each look fine to me, and I cant see which one to give up. If I drop (1), what's left of decision theory? If I drop (2) or (3), what replaces it? And is the 4/3 answer actually right, or is there a better way to think about it? I'd rather keep (1) than (6), but I want to know what I'm paying.
