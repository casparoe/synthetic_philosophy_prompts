I was at a neuroethics workshop last week for a piece I'm writing on neural implants, and the only thing I've been able to think about since is an argument I overheard by the coffee urns between two people I'll call Ellen and Yusuf. Ellen is a postdoc who's drafting a paper around a thought experiment, and Yusuf is a philosopher who kept poking holes in it. Here's the experiment: a man has an experimental implant that sits completely idle while he's doing the wrong thing, but the instant he forms a decision to do the right thing, the implant fires, erases that decision, and substitutes a decision to do the wrong thing. So he can deliberate freely, and he can even decide to do the right thing, but no such decision ever survives long enough to turn into an action — the implant overwrites it before it reaches his muscles. In the actual story, of course, the implant never fires: he decides on the wrong thing himself, for his own reasons, and does it. (1) Ellen's claim was that this is a Frankfurt-style case, and it shows that the principle of alternative possibilities is false — he couldn't have done the right thing whatever he'd decided, and yet he's obviously responsible for what he did, so responsibility can't require the ability to have done otherwise. (2) Yusuf said that's too fast: all the case really shows is that he couldn't have *acted* otherwise, whereas he could have *decided* otherwise, so anyone who states the principle in terms of the will rather than the act — could he have willed otherwise? — just watches the counterexample bounce off. (3) Ellen shot back that that's a retreat, and a vacuous one: if a decision to do otherwise is guaranteed to be wiped the moment it's made, in what sense was it ever his to make? An "ability" that's systematically severed from anything that could follow from it isn't the kind of ability anyone was asking about, and she said she could build a second implant that fires at the moment of intention-formation and the same argument would still run. (4) Yusuf said the distinction isn't ad hoc — what makes the man responsible is that the actual history of his act runs entirely through his own reasons, and the implant is counterfactual scenery that never touches that history; so the case is really an argument for responsibility-as-sourcehood, which is a different thesis from the one Ellen says it proves, and she can't borrow the first to establish the second. (5) Ellen said sourcehood and the ability to do otherwise were never competitors in the first place, that the whole point of these cases is that the ability isn't part of what makes an act yours, and that Yusuf is just renaming her conclusion.

They went around twice more and then had to go to the next panel, and I could'nt referee it, partly because I'm not sure the example is even well built. Can you go through the five moves and tell me who's right about what at each step — not just who wins overall — and rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right? I'd also like to know whether the implant case actually shows anything, or whether it's rigged in some way that makes it useless, and whether the two of them are assumeing something they shouldn't. Plain language where you can, please; I have to explain this to an editor.
