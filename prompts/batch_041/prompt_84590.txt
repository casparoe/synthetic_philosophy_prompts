Me and two friends have been going around in circles for months on something that should have taken one evening: does the simulation argument dampen fanaticism about the far future? Let me lay out the actual disagreement, because I think thats the interesting part.

The simulation argument, roughly: at least one of these has to be true -- civilizations almost never make it to the point where they can run ancestor simulations, or they get there and choose not to, or we're almost certainly living in one. Fanaticism, the way we're using the word: you should maximize expected value even when the good outcome is astronomically big and the chance of it astronomically small, so your decision gets dominated by a sliver of a chance at an enormous future, like a civilization that fills the galaxy for billions of years.

Friend A: if there's a decent chance we're simulated, then anything we do about the far future either happens only inside the sim, in which case it doesn't touch the real far future at all, or it's at the mercy of whoever's running it, so the expected value of longtermist action shrinks and the fanaticism is defused.

Friend B: that's not how it works, because fanaticism is exactly the doctrine that survives shrinking. Cut the probability by a factor of a billion and the expected value is still astronomical. The simulation argument can only defuse fanaticism if it makes the value zero rather than small, and "probably simulated" doesn't do that.

My own guess is that they aren't even running the same calculation -- one of them is discounting a probability, the other is trying to zero out a value, and we never said which one dampen is suposed to mean. Friend A also throws in something I can't evaluate: he says the thing to reason from is the simulators' motive, i.e. if we're simulated then the fact that we exist is itself evidence about why, and that inference rather than the raw probability is what should move us; and he says you can't use the assumption that you're a random observer among all observers to argue you're probably simulated and then turn around and treat your own choices as evidence about what happens in base reality, because those two moves need different ideas of what makes you typical.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A fight about words, since we never fixed what damping means? A fight about the arithmetic -- expected value with tiny chances of huge payoffs, or with expectations that come out infinite or undefined? A fight about the world, i.e. the probability we're simulated, which none of us can settle? Is A answering "should I be a fanatic at all" while B answers "given that you're a fanatic, does simulation risk change your ranking" -- two different questions wearing one name? And what would count as progress here: a formal result about infinite expected value, a decision-theoretic argument that fanaticism is incoherent, or just admitting the question is ill-posed? I don't need sources or a reading list, I need the diagnosis and a straight answer about what would actually move it.
