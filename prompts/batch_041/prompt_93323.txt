I'm writing an essay for a seminar thats mixing philosophy of time with lit crit, and I've landed on Borges's 'The Garden of Forking Paths' — the one where the spy Yu Tsun, a Chinese man working for Germany in wartime Britain, tracks down the sinologist Stephen Albert and shoots him in the back, not for anything to do with the man himself but so that the murder reported in the papers will tell Berlin where the guns are parked: in the town of Albert. Meanwhile Albert has spent years decoding the unfinished novel of Yu Tsun's ancestor Ts'ui Pên, and has worked out that the novel and the famous lost labyrinth are the same object — a book in which every branch of events is written out, all the outcomes happening rather than one being chosen, and whose real subject is time.

The standard line is that this is Borges's eternalism in narrative form: all the forks are real, nothing is ever lost. But I keep loosing the thread on the fact that Borges spends much of the rest of his time on time arguing the opposite. In 'A New Refutation of Time' he tries to do to time what Berkeley did to matter and Hume did to the self: if a mind is only a bundle of successive states, and there's no persisting self to have them, then succession goes too and time isn't real. He's also drawn to J.W. Dunne's serial universe, where any time-series needs a later series to be observed in, and to the regress that falls out of it. So I've got at least two Borges time-pictures that can't both be true — a lavish realist manifold of branching times, and an idealism that dissolves time into a run of mental states.

What I want from you is a reading that takes the story and the philosophy equally seriously, and tells me which picture the story actually enacts, whether its fatalism (Yu Tsun's sense that what he does was settled before he does it) is even coherent on the branching view, and whether the refutation, if it works, makes the garden incoherent rather than merely strange. Where does the story resist the reading? I suspect the murder-as-message is doing something the time-talk distracts us from — a name that is also a place, a decision that is also a signal — and I'd like you to say so if you think that's right, and to say why if you don't. Don't lay out the options and leave me to choose: give me a committed reading with your reasons, and finish by saying what, if anything, the story adds to the debate that the essays can't. British English, and don't bother quoting the text — I'll check the passages myself.
