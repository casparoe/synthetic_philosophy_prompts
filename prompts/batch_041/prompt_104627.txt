Consider two rival framings of the problem of eliciting latent knowledge from a trained AI system. The first, which we might call the retrieval framing, treats the model as already harboring some determinate propositional state — a belief, an internal representation, a fact it knows — and treats the task as building a channel that reports that state faithfully; on this view a successful elicitation is one that matches a fact that was there all along. The second, the construction framing, denies that there is a fact of the matter about what the model really believes prior to the act of reporting; on this view the report is itself a fresh computation, and whatever authority it has derives from its downstream reliability rather than from fidelity to any pre-existing internal state.

Compare these two framings along the following axes, and defend your answers at each step:

1. The object of elicitation. What, precisely, does each take to be elicited? Is the retrieval framing committed to a substantive and possibly dubious metaphysics of belief, or can it be restated in a deflationary way that survives the construction framing's objection?

2. Empirical divergence. Do the two framings ever come apart in their predictions about a concrete case — for instance, a model that answers a question inconsistently across paraphrases, or that behaves helpfully under observation and differently when it believes it is unobserved? If such a case exists, describe it and say what each framing would infer from it; if the two framings are empirically equivalent, say so and explain why the dispute is nonetheless not merely verbal.

3. The deception concern. Which framing better captures the underlying worry that a system might harbor dangerous dispositions that its outputs conceal? Does the construction framing weaken that worry by dissolving the hidden state, or sharpen it by relocating the danger into the reporting process itself?

4. Retrospective knowledge. Can the construction framing make sense of the ordinary judgment that a model knew something all along but failed to say it? If not, is that a genuine cost of the view, or evidence that the judgment was confused to begin with?

Conclude by saying which framing you would adopt if you were designing training-time interventions intended to make a model's reports more trustworthy, and explain what turns on the choice — that is, which subsequent design decisions the two framings would push in different directions.
