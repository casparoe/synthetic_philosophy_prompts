Ok so I'm in this reading group on the old socialist calculation debate and two people in it have been going at each other for about a year and a half now, and I'm the one who keeps having to host. One of them is a full Hayek/Mises guy: he thinks a socialist economy literally cannot allocate resources rationally, no matter how smart or well-meaning the planners are. The other is a Lange person: he thinks that's just wrong, that a central planning board can do everything a market does by nudging prices up and down until supply and demand match, with managers just told to follow a couple of simple rules, and that the whole Hayekian fuss is really about incentives and politics rather than about whether calculation is even possible. I want to stop rehasing the same fights, so write me the document they should have produced by now: a joint statement of an advesarial collaboration that both of them could actually sign. Start with a numbered list of claims both sides accept -- about what's genuinely at stake, about what a price does in each system, about what would even count as a working planned economy, and about which parts of their fight are just about the word "socialism" or the word "calculation". Then a disagreements table: each remaining disagreement stated in wording that's neutral, plus, for each side, a formulation of its own view that the person on that side would accept as fair. Then the cruxes -- the sub-questions where, if one of them came out one way, at least one of them would have to give ground.

For each crux, give a concrete case, argument, or hypothetical that both of them would agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts about it. Stuff I'd want covered if it really is where the action is: (a) whether the board's trial-and-error price adjustment is the same kind of thing as prices emerging out of exchange, or whether it quietly assumes the answer, i.e. that the board already knows the demand and supply conditions it's supposedly discovering; (b) capital goods and the interest rate -- if there's no real market for capital goods, what does the managers' rule about setting marginal cost equal to price even tell them about whether to build a new plant, or which technique to pick for a good that doesn't exist yet; (c) whether this is a fight about information or about incentives -- Mises seems to have been making a calculation point and Hayek a knowledge point, and Lange answered the calculation version, so is part of their disagreement just that they mean different things by "rational"?; (d) the counterfactual: suppose the board has unlimited computing power and its managers are saintly and honest, does the Hayek/Mises side concede that the calculation objection falls away, and if so what's left of his position, and conversely what would the Lange side have to concede if the trial-and-error process turns out not to converge, or to need information that nobody in the economy actually has?

Don't bother with references, page numbers or a bibliography, I can't check any of it and neither can you, so just describe the positions in your own words and flag it when something is your reconstruction rather than something either side actually said. And if you find that some reading of one side's view makes it trivially true or trivially false, say so and set that reading aside instead of letting them keep fighting a straw man.
