I'm building up notes on the Nordic model and I keep bumping into something that looks like a contradiction. In Sweden, Norway and Finland basically anyone can look up what their neighbour declared as income and how much tax they paid -- the figures are public, newspapers publish searchable lists of everyone's earnings once a year, and people genuinely do snoop on each other. Yet these same societies are famous for strong privacy norms, discretion, not prying into other people's business. And in the US or UK, the idea that your neighbour could see your tax return would be treated as outrageous.

Take the role of a philosopher of political economy and think this through carefully. What does the practice of public tax records actually presuppose -- about privacy, about what income is (private property vs. a public fact about your contribution to a shared pot), about the relation between citizen and state, and about the moral standing of your fellow compatriots? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? And please be honest about how underdetermined it is: is the practice really committed to the flattering story the Nordic-boosters tell (mutual trust, the state as an extension of the community rather than an adversary), or is it just as compatible with a Foucauldian reading where mutual visibility mostly manufactures disciplined, compliant taxpayers -- or with a dull administrative story where transparency is simply a cheap tax-compliance technology? Brainstorm a few candidate implicit theories first, then commit to a reading, and finish by saying whether the thing could be exported to a low-trust society or whether what it presupposes is precisely the part you can't legislate. No need for citations or reading lists, just the argument.
