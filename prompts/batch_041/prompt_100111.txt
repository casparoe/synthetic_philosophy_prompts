I teach a 90-minute session in an interdisciplinary graduate seminar that mixes philosophy students with machine-learning students, about fourteen people seated at four tables, and I want an in-class activity rather than a lecture. The thing I want them to feel in their hands is the difficulty in the debate over whether a trained network genuinely represents features of its inputs or merely carries information that we can decode out of it. The specific asymmetry I want them to collide with is this: a feature can be perfectly recoverable from a system's internal states while playing no role at all in producing its outputs, and a feature can be causally central while being invisible to any method that only looks for human-legible properties. I want them to discover both halves of that asymmetry themselves, and to end the session unable to settle the question with the evidence they have gathered.

Design the activity around a black box that participants simulate rather than program, so it runs with index cards, envelopes, and no computers. It must let the researchers do two different things and feel the difference between them: observe the box's behavior and request its internal readings, on the one hand, and intervene on the box's parts and see what changes, on the other. Build in a moment where the two methods point in opposite directions, and a moment where the group must commit to a prediction on inputs they have not yet tried, with points rather than money at stake, so that believing in the feature has a practical payoff even though the evidence underdetermines the mechanism. The box's true wiring should be known to me alone until the reveal, and the reveal should be double-edged: it should puncture the natural reading of the decoding result without leaving the deflationary reading comfortable either.

Give me the full packet: materials and how to prepare them; roles, including how to brief whoever operates the box without the others overhearing; the rules and the order of play; a timeline for the 90 minutes; the scoring scheme; the reveal; and a debrief that maps each moment of play onto the corresponding part of the debate — realism versus instrumentalism about ascription, the gap between decodability and use, the underdetermination of mechanism by behavior, and the question of what would make one candidate feature rather than another the one the system represents. The philosophical vocabulary should arrive only in the debrief; nothing up front should name the positions. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: the toy has a designed ground truth and human-legible parts, and I need to know exactly which part of the real dispute that convenience leaves out, and what I will therefore have to supply myself in the debrief.
