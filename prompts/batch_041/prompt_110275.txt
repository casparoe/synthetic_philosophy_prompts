Ok, moral luck. I've read enough posts that circle the same three examples, so I want the actual spec instead: what does a theory of moral responsibility have to deliver to count as dealing with luck rather than dodging it? Give me the desiderata, with the case behind each. Cases it has to get right: two agents who reason identically from identical information, one of whom happens to cause a catastrophe; the audit-luck case, where someone is blamed only because they got caught while everyone else doing the same thing didn't; and the redescription case, where every physical fact is fixed but the verdict flips depending on whether we call it reckless driving or an accident. Distinctions it can't collapse: responsibility vs. blameworthiness, excuse vs. exemption, a bad decision vs. a bad outcome. Constraints it can't contradict: we really do blame worse outcomes more even when we know it's luck, and a theory that just calls that a bug is cheating; and you can't be blameworthy for what you couldn't have known.

Then tell me wich of those are genuine requirements and which are just one camp's intuitions, whether any pair of them can't be jointly satisfied -- I half suspect the decision-tracking and outcome-tracking demands are both non-negotiable in our actual practice, in which case the list is itself the result -- and which single requirement does the most to rule out candidates. Skip the scorecard for the usual views, I'll do that myself; the point is to end up with something I could actually use for institutional blame rules, like who eats the consequences after a bad outcome in a hospital or a company.
