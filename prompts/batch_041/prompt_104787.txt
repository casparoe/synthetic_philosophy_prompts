I'm a fourth-year PhD student in metaphysics, and I've been asked to open next week's work-in-progress seminar by presenting my own positive view on material constitution, so that the rest of the department has something determinate to attack rather than yet another survey of the options. I've been living with the statue-and-lump family of puzzles for a couple of years now and have settled on a package I'm attached to, but before I write it up I want a genuine audit of whether the pieces actually fit together. Here is where I've landed. (1) Constitution is not identity: when a lump of bronze constitutes a statue, there are two numerically distinct material objects occupying exactly the same region at the same time, and what distinguishes them is that they differ in their modal properties — the lump could survive being melted down and recast, whereas the statue could not. (2) Persistence is by temporal parts: material objects are four-dimensional and persist by having numerically distinct temporal parts at different times, and nothing is wholly present at more than one time. (3) Composition is restricted: there are no arbitrary undetached parts, so the left half of the statue is not itself an object, and there is no unrestricted fusion of arbitrary scattered things. (4) I'm a grounding physicalist: every fact about which material objects exist, and about what they are like, is ultimately grounded in the arrangement and behaviour of microphysical matter, with no brute macroscopic differences floating free of that base.

What I'd like from you is an audit rather than a lecture. Take the four commitments pairwise and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which merely look as though they are, and rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad; for each genuine tension, say precisely what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at the least cost and what I'd lose by adopting it. My own standing worry is that (1) and (3) sit badly together — if I let a statue and a lump coincide, haven't I already bought the bloated mereology that (3) is supposed to forbid? — but please don't let that steer you; if the real trouble lies somewhere I haven't flagged, say so, and if my worry turns out to be misplaced, say that too.

To keep this from collapsing into a rehearsal of the standard examples, please also run the package against three cases and report what it predicts and whether those predictions are consistent with the commitments above. (a) The familiar recasting case: the lump is melted and poured into a new mould, and I take it that the lump survives while the statue does not — I want to know whether (2) can even make sense of that asymmetry once both objects are temporal worms rather than things wholly present at a time. (b) A naturally occurring rock formation that, purely through erosion, comes to look exactly like a seated human figure, so that visitors unhesitatingly call it a statue: does my view say that a statue exists there, and does that answer follow from anything I have actually claimed, or am I quietly smuggling in an appeal to the intentions of a maker? (c) A single lump of bronze that is simultaneously a statue and a doorstop, which on my view threatens to yield three coincident objects rather than two — is that a reductio of (1), an artefact of how I've formulated (1), or exactly the kind of consequence I ought to accept and defend? I'd like the audit to close with a short statement of the position I should actually present — the minimal revision of the four commitments that survives the objections — rather than a survey of what others have said about these puzzles.
