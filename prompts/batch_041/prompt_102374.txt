I'm a second-year PhD student in aesthetics, and I've been asked to write a satirical piece for our department's annual magazine — the sort of thing that gets read aloud at the Christmas party and then quietly regretted. The idea grew out of an argument in our reading group, where a colleague kept insisting, with increasing heat, that one simply cannot know a painting is beautiful on someone else's say-so, and I found myself wondering what a society would look like if it took that claim perfectly seriously and wrote it into law. I'd like you to write the piece, and I'd like the comedy to come entirely from the philosophy rather than from sneering at the characters.

The form I have in mind is a mock agony-aunt column — 'Dear Aunt Verity', published in a broadsheet in a country where the non-transferability of aesthetic knowledge is settled law and settled etiquette. Aunt Verity's correspondents keep running up against the practical consequences, and their letters should each turn on a distinct puzzle: a widow whose husband left her his opinions of the Ring cycle in his will and who cannot decide whether she may now repeat them as her own; a man who subscribes to a service that has a professional connoisseur experience paintings on his behalf and who wonders whether the resulting beliefs belong to him or to her; a gallery guide legally required to recite the approved verdicts but forbidden to form any of his own; and a woman who suspects she has been mistaking her own aesthetic responses for the certified ones. Aunt Verity should answer in a deadpan, faintly bureaucratic, genuinely kind voice, and every reply must be internally consistent with the principle rather than winking at it — the humour should be legible to a reader who knows the literature on aesthetic testimony and should not collapse into nonsense for one who doesn't.

Keep it to roughly 700–800 words, in British English, with the letters and replies alternating. Please don't include any citations, reading lists, or references to real critics; the conceit has to stand entirely on its own. And do make sure the piece lands the genuine difficulties — the gap between knowing that a work is admired and knowing it to be admirable, whether testimony can ever transmit aesthetic knowledge, whether one can be wrong about one's own taste, and what a critic's expertise could possibly consist in — without ever stating them as theses.
