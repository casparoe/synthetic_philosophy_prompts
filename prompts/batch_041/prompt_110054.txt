Here is the question.

**Question 3.** Throughout, fix a finite normal-form game with finitely many players and finitely many pure strategies per player, and allow mixed strategies throughout.

(a) For a player *i* and a pure strategy *s* of hers, define precisely what it is for *s* to be strictly dominated and what it is for *s* to be weakly dominated, in each case permitting the dominating strategy to be mixed. Then give an example of a game in which some pure strategy is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy but is not strictly dominated by any pure strategy, and verify that your example has the required property.

(b) Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies is order-independent, whereas iterated elimination of weakly dominated strategies is not. Explain why, and give an explicit game together with two orders of elimination under the weak-dominance procedure that leave different sets of strategies.

(c) Explain the relation between the notions of strict and weak dominance and the notion of a best response, and say how that relation bears on the justification of iterated elimination as a procedure driven by assumptions of rationality and common knowledge of rationality.

Here is the student's answer.

**(a)** Fix a player *i* and one of her pure strategies *s*, and write *S*₋ᵢ for the set of strategy profiles available to the other players. A mixed strategy σ of player *i* strictly dominates *s* if *u*ᵢ(σ, *s*₋ᵢ) > *u*ᵢ(*s*, *s*₋ᵢ) for every *s*₋ᵢ ∈ *S*₋ᵢ; *s* is strictly dominated if some such σ exists. If all of these inequalities are weak and at least one of them is strict, then σ weakly dominates *s*, and *s* is weakly dominated if some such σ exists. Payoffs are linear in a player's own mixture, so *u*ᵢ(σ, *s*₋ᵢ) is the σ-weighted average of the payoffs of the pure strategies in the support of σ; this is why allowing mixed strategies on the dominating side is not a cosmetic change.

An example where it matters: let player 1 have three pure strategies and player 2 two, and let player 1's payoffs be

|  | L | R |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy 1 | 3 | 0 |
| Strategy 2 | 0 | 3 |
| Strategy 3 | 1 | 1 |

(Player 2's payoffs are irrelevant to the point and can be filled in however we like, since dominance is a condition on player 1's payoffs alone.) The mixture ½ Strategy 1 + ½ Strategy 2 pays 1.5 against L and 1.5 against R, so it strictly dominates Strategy 3. But neither Strategy 1 nor Strategy 2 strictly dominates Strategy 3 on its own, since Strategy 1 pays less against R (0 < 1) and Strategy 2 pays less against L (0 < 1). So Strategy 3 would survive a procedure that compared pure strategies only with pure strategies.

**(b)** The strict procedure is order-independent. The reason is that strict domination is not something a strategy can acquire: if *s* is not strictly dominated in the game as given, then *s* is not strictly dominated after any number of deletions either, since deletions only remove alternatives from the game and never add any. It follows that the set of strategies that will be eliminated is fixed before the procedure begins, and the order of removal cannot affect where we end up.

Weak dominance behaves quite differently. Consider

|  | L | R |
|---|---|---|
| T | 1, 1 | 0, 2 |
| B | 0, 0 | 0, 0 |

where each cell lists the row player's payoff first and then the column player's. Player 1's T weakly dominates B (1 > 0 against L, 0 = 0 against R), and player 2's R weakly dominates L (2 > 1 against T, 0 = 0 against B). Now eliminate in two different orders. If we first delete B, the remaining row is T against the columns {L, R}, and there R weakly dominates L (2 > 1), so L goes too, leaving the single profile (T, R). If instead we first delete L, the remaining game has rows {T, B} against the single column R, and now neither row weakly dominates the other, since both pay 0 against R; so nothing further is eliminated, and both (T, R) and (B, R) survive. Since (B, R) is a Nash equilibrium of the original game — B pays 0 against R, which is as much as T pays, and R pays 0 against B, which is as much as L pays — this shows both that the surviving set depends on the order of elimination and that eliminating weakly dominated strategies can eliminate Nash equilibria.

**(c)** A strategy is a best response to a belief — a probability distribution over the other players' strategy profiles — if it maximises expected payoff among the player's strategies against that belief. Strictly dominated strategies are never best responses: if σ pays strictly more than *s* against every profile the others might play, then σ pays strictly more than *s* in expectation against every belief, so *s* can never maximise. Weakly dominated strategies are never best responses either; the only difference from the strict case is that the dominating strategy need not beat *s* in every contingency, so the argument goes through with weak inequalities rather than strict ones. Since no rational player plays a strategy that can never be optimal, and since each successive round of elimination can be underwritten by one further level of the assumption that the players are rational and know one another to be rational, iterated elimination of dominated strategies — whether strict or weak — is justified by rationality together with common knowledge of rationality.
