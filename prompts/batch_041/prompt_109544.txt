I'm writing up a paper on hedonism for a seminar, and before I commit to a position in print I want to make sure the package I've assembled isn't quietly self-undermining. I've become convinced by the heterogeneity objection — that if you actually attend to the phenomenology, the pleasure of finally drinking water when you're parched, the pleasure of getting a stubborn proof to go through, the pleasure of a chord resolving, and the pleasure of hearing that someone you despise has failed share no single felt quality; 'hedonic tone' is a philosopher's fiction invented to save a theory. What I can't tell is whether the way I've tried to accommodate that concession leaves me with a position at all. So here is the whole thing, and I'd like an audit rather than encouragement.

(1) Welfare hedonism: the only things intrinsically good for a person are pleasures, and the only things intrinsically bad are pains. (2) The heterogeneity thesis just described: there is no common phenomenal property in virtue of which all pleasures are pleasures. (3) So 'pleasure' is a functional or role concept — a state counts as a pleasure roughly insofar as it is one the person likes for its own sake, or that plays the right motivational and evaluative role in her psychology — and not a phenomenal natural kind. (4) All the same, pleasure is good because of how it feels: the value of a pleasure is intrinsic to its felt character, not to the fact that it is desired, or that it was selected for, or anything else relational. (5) I think well-being admits of a single cardinal measure and enough interpersonal comparability to underwrite cost-benefit analysis and overall verdicts about whose life went better. (6) I'm a thoroughgoing naturalist: if something is good for someone, that has to be grounded in natural facts about her. (7) I think pain, unlike pleasure, does pick out a fairly unified natural kind — roughly nociception plus aversive affect — and I take the badness of pain to be the badness of that very state.

Go through these pairwise and tell me which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only look like they do, ranking the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad. For each genuine tension, say precisely what has to give and what the cheapest repair costs — e.g. if I have to abandon the functional analysis of pleasure, or the intrinsic-felt-character claim, or the cardinal measure, I want to know what I'm buying and what I'm losing by each. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and whether the resulting position is still recognizably hedonism or has quietly become a form of preference satisfaction with extra steps. I'd rather be told that one of my commitments is fine as it stands than be reassured that they all are, so please be willing to say that some of the alleged tensions are artefacts of how the objection is usually stated.
