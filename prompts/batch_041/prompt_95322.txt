Write a philosophical dialogue between two equally formidable classical Indian epistemologists, each defending his school's position without being made to play the fool.

The first is a Naiyāyika. He accepts four pramāṇas — perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda) — and he holds that a cognition's validity is not given along with the cognition but must be established afterwards, whether by a further confirming cognition or by the cognition proving itself in successful action (parataḥ prāmāṇya).

The second is a Bhāṭṭa Mīmāṃsaka. He accepts six pramāṇas — the four above, plus postulation (arthāpatti: the fat Devadatta who does not eat by day must eat by night) and non-apprehension (anupalabdhi: knowing that there is no pot here) — and he holds that a cognition is valid in its own right and known to be so, so that doubt about it requires positive grounds (svataḥ prāmāṇya).

Their dispute is occasioned by a question neither has seen posed in the texts. A merchants' guild has for a generation relied entirely on the reports of a single caravan-master about a distant valley: which passes are open, which markets pay, where water is found. Every report the guild has acted upon has been borne out. No member has ever gone to the valley; no report can be checked except by acting on it and seeing what follows, and even what counts as things going as predicted is described in the very terms the report itself supplies. The guild asks its two learned guests whether they knew all along, or only believed.

Set this up as a vāda — a debate conducted in good faith for the sake of truth, not a contest of wits — and let it turn on the genuine joints of the disagreement: whether testimony is a pramāṇa in its own right or a species of inference; whether trust in a speaker can be warranted by default, before any independent evidence of his reliability; whether confirmation through successful action certifies a cognition or merely defers the question, and whether the regress this threatens is vicious; whether the absence of any report of failure is itself a source of knowledge or is reducible to something else; and whether the Mīmāṃsaka's confidence in authorless testimony can be carried over from the Veda to a caravan-master, or whether the special case is doing all the work.

Let them argue concretely — the absent pot, the fat Devadatta, the reliability of the senses, the practical stakes of doubting everything — but do not let the dialogue decay into a tour of textbook positions. Each must press the other to a real concession or refinement, and at least one exchange should turn on a distinction that only one of them had been tracking until then. The debate need not be resolved; a standoff, or a convergence neither expected, is a fine ending. Neither character need quote or cite any text: the arguments must stand on their own. Give the two names and a brief setting, then let them argue for roughly 1,500 words.
