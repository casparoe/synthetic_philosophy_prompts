I'm the TA for an upper-level seminar that's cross-listed between philosophy of science and economic history (grading is a nightmare, but the reading fights are great), and I'm writing the take-home final. I want exactly one essay question on Malthus, and I specifically do NOT want the standard "was Malthus wrong because of the Green Revolution" essay — students can write that in their sleep and it tests nothing about the actual issue. Here's the angle I want to force. A friend in the department keeps saying Malthus was "right but early," and I've never been able to decide whether that's a genuine epistemic category or just a rescue operation that quietly makes the theory unfalsifiable, and I want the students to have to take a stand on it rather than name-drop Popper and Lakatos and run.

So I want a specific piece of apparatus they have to use. Make them distinguish, for any long-run prediction that overshoots its date, whether the factor that delayed the reckoning is endogenous to the theory's own mechanism or exogenous to it. My claim — which they should have to engage, agree or not — is that a prediction earns the label "right but early" only when the delay is produced by the theory's own stated dynamics (i.e. the theory itself predicts that as the pressure starts to bite, some check kicks in and pushes the date back), and that when the delay is instead explained by something the theory never anticipated, "right but early" is just a rescue dressed up. The question should ask them to (i) say what has to be true for "right but early" to mean more than a face-saving relabel, (ii) actually apply the endogenous/exogenous test to Malthus's own machinery — the checks, the way the mechanism is supposed to respond to pressure, all of it — and (iii) say what evidence would genuinely distinguish "right but early" from "wrong and patched," because that's the real crux and I keep finding students who can recite falsificationism fine but can't say what a fair test of a slow-moving theory would even look like.

Write the question itself, a model answer of roughly 600–700 words, and a marking scheme. Total 20 marks, split into 5 must-hit points with the marks for each, plus a one-line "how students typically blow this one" note per point. I want the scheme to seperate an A from a B: a B answer applies the test in (ii) correctly but treats the endogenous/exogenous line as obviously right; an A answer has to actually interrogate whether that's the right place to draw the distinction, or whether it collapses (e.g. under a broad enough reading of the theory everything becomes "endogenous," wich would make the test useless). And please don't let the model answer just assert that Malthus was right-and-early or wrong — it should land on the structure of the argument, not the verdict.
