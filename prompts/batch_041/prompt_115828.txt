hey, I'm taking an STS class (science, technology, and society) and we just wrapped the unit on wether artifacts have politics — the one where a bridge or a bench or a door closer gets called racist or democratic, and the class splits over whether that's a claim about what the designer intended or just about what the thing actually does to people, plus the objection that the exact same object can be political in one setting and totally innocent in another. (someone in section also claimed the famous bridge story is apocryphal, which broke a few brains.)

for our final we can do a creative piece instead of a term paper. I want to write the procurement package a city puts out for new public transit shelters, where the city keeps calling them "politically neutral." So: a Request for Proposals, written totally deadpan like it came out of a real municipal procurement office. Include a short purpose/vision statement, numbered mandatory technical requirements, evaluation criteria with point values, and a Q&A addendum where vendors send in questions and the city answers them. Like 700-800 words. Invent a fake mid-size city and fake vendor names, don't use real ones. The thing being made fun of is the idea that a design can just be neutral — not any real city or official.

The funny has to come out of the requirements themselves, no narrator winking at the reader. Nobody in the document should ever concede that the shelters have a politics; the city's own prose should only use the word "political" in order to deny it. Stuff I want it to land without ever saying it out loud: that "neutral" still means designed around a default user, and that default user is doing a suspicious amount of work; that the city can disclaim intent while the effect stays identical; that a vendor asks whether the same shelter counts as political on a park lawn and not on the sidewalk outside a homeless shelter, and the city's answer is a dodge a careful reader sees straight through; that the ad panels and the sensor data are where some of the real politics live, not just the armrests; and that the city credits the object itself with producing social outcomes (cutting loitering, boosting ridership) as though the hardware were the cause.

Somewhere a vendor should ask a question the city can't answer without contradicting itself, and the city should answer it anyway. Every requirement should be something a real procurement office could actually write without embarrassment. Someone who's taken the class should laugh at the right spots; someone who hasn't should still be able to follow it. American English, please — and no need for any real sources or citations, just make it up.
