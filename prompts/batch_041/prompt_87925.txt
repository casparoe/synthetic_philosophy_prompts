I am a doctoral student in moral philosophy, and for the past few weeks I have been circling a cluster of ordinary practices that seem to me philosophically loaded, and that nobody I know discusses together. In most common-law jurisdictions there is no general duty to rescue a stranger in peril: a competent adult who walks past a shallow pond in which a child is drowning commits no legal wrong and owes nothing to anyone. Yet those same jurisdictions have passed statutes shielding the rescuer who does intervene from liability, several civil-law countries make the failure to render easy aid a criminal offence, and the law does impose duties of rescue on parents, on those who created the peril, and on those who have taken a patient or a dependent into their care. In parallel, nearly every hierarchical institution — armies, police and fire services, hospitals, and increasingly universities and firms — honours members whose conduct is publicly described as exceeding what the institution demands, while the institution's own rules oblige those members to accept extraordinary risk; and many professional ethics codes are formally divided into an aspirational part that no one can be sanctioned for failing and an enforceable part that can end a career. I want a careful reconstruction of what this cluster presupposes, not a summary of what philosophers have written about it.

Please take the role of an expert in normative ethics and the philosophy of law, and start by brainstorming the candidate presuppositions before committing to a reconstruction, so that the final answer is argued rather than asserted. My central question is what must be true of the moral landscape for these practices to make sense as they stand. Do they presuppose that the morally good and the morally required can come apart — that there is a genuine category of the praiseworthy but optional, and that the deontic question of what one must do is not the same as the evaluative question of what is admirable? Or could the whole cluster be explained without loss by a theory that recognises only one dimension, on which the decorated hero has merely done what was required of her all along and the medals are an incentive device with no moral content? If a two-dimensional picture is presupposed, which version of it is strictly required, and which parts of the practice merely fit comfortably with it? Two further puzzles interest me. First, if the line between the required and the optional is drawn by the cost or the risk to the agent, then one and the same act — a soldier smothering a grenade, a bystander smothering a grenade — appears to be required in one case and optional in the other, which suggests the category is relative to role or relationship rather than to the act; is that coherent, and what would have to change in the practices if it were not? Second, the practices treat praise and blame asymmetrically: the rescuer is decorated, the passer-by is not blamed, though both faced the same situation. Does that presuppose that the standards for praise and for blame differ, or only that we have pragmatic reasons for a forgiving practice of blame?

Finally, tell me where the cluster is philosophically underdetermined — where incompatible theories would license exactly the same statutes, medals and codes — and what would actually have to change if we replaced the presupposed picture with its chief rival. Assume I have no library and no internet access, so reason from the descriptions I have given rather than reaching for the literature; do not give me citations or a reading list, and do not report what any particular philosopher is supposed to have argued, since I will supply the references myself.
