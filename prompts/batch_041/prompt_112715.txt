I'm a senior software engineer on a small platform team, and I have no formal background in philosophy — the problem below is a practical one I have to solve, not a research interest.

Our decision-support service sits between upstream components that each report a probability for some event and downstream systems that take an automated action on the basis of a single number. The upstream components are heterogeneous: some are mechanistic simulations of processes that, as far as I can tell, are genuinely chancy; some are statistical models reporting the frequency with which an event occurred in a historical population; some are human analysts typing a gut number into a form. The downstream actions are customer-visible and run a few hundred times a day — denying a transaction, repricing a quote, flagging an account — so a wrong number has real cost, but we never have time to convene anyone for a case-by-case ruling.

The rule of thumb I picked up somewhere, and which I suspect is the thing I actually want, is this: if I know that the objective probability of an event is p, then my own degree of belief should be p, and I should not second-guess the chance with my own hunch. What I need is that idea turned into something my team can run.

Please write a decision procedure, in the form of a spec we could implement: the questions to ask in a fixed order, the inputs each question needs, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure halts and emits a number, and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is not a matter of fact. It has to run in a few minutes per case, and it has to be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. The platform lead signs off on the thresholds; the on-call engineer runs the procedure.

The procedure has to survive at least these cases: (a) the upstream number is itself uncertain — a model that reports a range, or a distribution over probabilities; (b) the number is a frequency from a reference class, but the decision concerns a single token case that need not belong to that class; (c) the component's report is correlated with the outcome for reasons other than the chance it reports — we peeked at the outcome, or the component was fitted on data that contains it; (d) the component reports a probability about its own future output, or about a chance that will later differ from the one it reports now; (e) the source is a human expert with a track record, where deference to a calibrated record and deference to a chance come apart.

Then apply the procedure to three concrete tickets: a simulation component reporting a 30% chance of a weather event that our pricing depends on; a churn model reporting 0.8 for a specific user; and an analyst reporting 0.6 that a vendor will breach an SLA, whose last twenty calls were right fourteen times. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me to discard a number we built the pipeline around, and close with what the procedure cannot do.
