I am a philosopher who has spent a career arguing, following Sen, that famines are not natural disasters: they happen when people's ability to command food collapses — through wages, prices, markets, and policy — and not when food disappears. I have been asked to give the address at the dedication of a memorial to a famine that killed perhaps a quarter of the people in my grandparents' town. The council's inscription calls it a natural disaster, and I have accepted the invitation in order to say that this is wrong. But I want to refuse the opposite move as well. The famine was made by policy and institutions, and those who made it were culpable; and yet no one intended the deaths. I want the address to make that difference audible, rather than collapsing into either a natural disaster or a crime.

The record I have is spare, and I will use no other facts: the harvest that year was poor but not ruinous; grain went on leaving the district by rail well into the famine; and the relief works paid less than the price of a day's bread. Please write the address, about five minutes long, for an audience of descendants of the dead, the council that drafted the inscription, a priest, and a class of schoolchildren. It must honour the dead, refuse the natural-disaster framing, and make the difference between culpable failure and intended crime do real work — without becoming a lecture in political economy or an indictment of the people in the front rows.
