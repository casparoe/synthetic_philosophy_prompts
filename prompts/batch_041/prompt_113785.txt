I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on personal identity, and I want a classification instrument that will sort readers by what they actually believe about a question that is usually waved at rather than argued: whether one and the same self can be distributed across more than one brain. The view I have in mind — the one Douglas Hofstadter defends in I Am a Strange Loop (2007), where he treats the self as a pattern that refers to itself and concludes that a person can therefore be present, in partial and degraded form, in the minds of others who have lived closely with them — is normally stated as an aphorism and almost never cashed out. I want you to cash it out.

Give me a decision tree. Build the logical space first, out of the questions that genuinely divide the positions, and only then locate the actual positions in it; do not begin from a list of names. Every node must be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and no node may simply ask whether the self is a pattern or a substance — that is the conclusion, not the test. Where a combination of answers is left unoccupied, mark it and say briefly why it is empty: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The tree must be able to reach at least the following leaves: the view that the self is a simple indivisible thing that cannot be divided or shared; the view that you are the living organism and nothing more; the psychological-continuity view with a no-branching rule, on which copying is fission and fission is death; the degreed-pattern view I described above; the view that a self is whatever the surrounding community of minds tracks and models, so that the criterion is not inside the head at all; and the eliminativist view that there is nothing there to distribute. If any one of these cannot be separated from another by any question in your tree, say so rather than pretending otherwise, and flag every pair of leaves the instrument cannot distinguish.

I want the cases to be doing the work, so use at least these: a marriage of forty years in which each spouse has absorbed the other's habits of thought; a teacher whose way of thinking has been taken up by a generation of students; a hypothetical procedure that copies a fraction of your neural pattern into a second brain while you remain conscious and intact; a patient whose hemispheres have been surgically separated; and a person in advanced dementia alongside the carers who hold what remains of their characteristic patterns. Include as well a case in which the original is destroyed after the copy is made, so that it becomes clear whether distribution is doing any independent work or whether it is simply the old duplication problem wearing a new coat.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic, no tables.
