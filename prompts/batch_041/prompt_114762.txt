I'm putting together the final exam for a small upper-level seminar I TA — it's a course on rationality and game theory, roughly at the level of someone who's read a chapter of a decision-theory textbook and some philosophy of rational choice, not a math-econ crowd. One of the essay questions asks about the centipede game and backward induction. I want the question to force students to actually trace the reasoning rather than recite "backward induction is controversial," so I've written it around a specific six-node version and a claim for them to evaluate. Here's the question and one student's answer, and I'd like you to grade the answer. Please don't just give a letter or a number — I need a point-by-point assessment: which parts are correct and well put, which claims are wrong or unsupported and exactly why, and how the errors should affect the mark. Assume the answer is out of 20 and that a solid, mostly-correct answer with the main moves right would be around a 14-15. Be specific about what a stronger version of the answer would have said at the spots where this one slips, and flag anything that's wrong but easy to skim past, since I'm grading a stack of these and don't want to reward confident-sounding errors.

The question: "Consider a six-node centipede game. Players A and B alternate moving, A first. At each node the player to move can Take, ending the game and collecting the larger share of the current pot, or Pass, which grows the pot and hands the move to the other player. At the final (sixth) node, Taking and Passing lead to the same payoff, so the player is indifferent. (a) Explain the backward-induction argument in this game and state what it concludes about the first move. (b) State the assumptions the argument relies on. (c) Assess this claim: 'The centipede game shows that backward induction is the wrong solution concept for finite games, since rational players who understand the game will cooperate.'"

The student's answer: "In the centipede game the players alternate choosing Take or Pass. If you Take, the game ends and you get more than you would have gotten if you'd Taken one node earlier; if you Pass, the pot grows and the other player moves. Backward induction starts at the end. At the final node the player to move is indifferent, so we can suppose she Takes (or at least that Passing there gains her nothing). Move back one node: the player there knows that if she Passes, the game reaches the final node and the other player will end it, leaving her with less than if she Takes now, so she Takes. The same step repeats all the way back: at every node, Passing just hands a rational opponent a chance to end the game before you can collect the larger pot, so Taking now beats Passing. It follows that A Takes immediately at node one, and the game ends at once with a small payoff for A. This is really just the iterated elimination of dominated strategies applied to an extensive-form game — Taking is the dominant choice at every node, which is why the unravelling is airtight.

The argument needs each player to be rational and to know the structure of the game and the payoffs. It does not, I think, need common knowledge of rationality: the reasoning is carried out separately at each node, and at each node all that matters is that the player moving there is rational. So the assumptions are fairly weak.

On the claim: it's too strong, though it points at something real. What the game actually shows is a tension between what each player does individually and what they'd both prefer. The backward-induction outcome gives both players less than they'd get if they both Passed for a while, so there's a sense in which cooperation is 'rational' and defection isn't. But a solution concept is a claim about what follows from the assumptions, not a prediction about what people will do, so the fact that subjects in experiments Pass early doesn't refute backward induction — it shows they aren't perfectly rational, or don't have common knowledge of each other's rationality, which is a fact about people, not about the theory. One thing that does bother me: the backward-induction outcome is a Nash equilibrium but not a subgame-perfect one, since at later nodes the players' choices aren't best responses given what actually happens. So the theory isn't fully comfortable here either. Overall I'd say the claim is half right: backward induction isn't 'wrong,' but the game does expose something unsatisfying about it."
