For a seminar on perception I am writing, I want to lay out, before comparing any of the competing accounts, what a theory of perceptual experience actually has to deliver -- specifically a theory pitched as a response to the argument from illusion. My provisional sense of the landscape is that there is the sense-datum and representationalist family on one side and disjunctivism on the other, and that the whole dispute turns on a claim about whether veridical perception and hallucination belong to a common kind. What I want from you is the specification rather than the verdict: the cases the theory must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it must be consistent with, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Treat this as the thing I most need before I can judge any candidate, and keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases.

To be specific about what I mean. Among the cases, I include ordinary veridical perception; the classical illusion, a straight stick looking bent in water; total hallucination; and, more awkwardly, what I think of as a mixed or hybrid episode -- a subject looking at a real object while one of its apparent properties is contributed by the subject rather than the object, say a real mug whose apparent chip is not really there. I genuinely do not know whether such an episode should be classified as perception, illusion, or hallucination, or whether a theory may simply decline to classify it, and I would like that question to be on the list rather than assumed away. I also want the list to say something about whether a theory must handle experiences that seem to be about the subject's own body -- phantom-limb sensations, for instance -- or is entitled to treat perception of the external world as the paradigm and let those fall out as special cases. Among the distinctions I do not want collapsed: that between the phenomenal character of an experience and its epistemic standing; that between the causal role of the object in producing the experience and the epistemic role of the experience in justifying a belief about it; and that between the content of an experience and the content of the judgment based on it. As for results the theory cannot contradict, I have in mind the empirical facts of perceptual constancy, of cross-modal and adaptation effects, and of episodes in which a subject's experience appears insensitive to what the subject believes.

Two things I especially want from the finished list. First, a verdict on which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates -- my own suspicion is that it is whatever requirement forces the disjunctivist to give an account of why hallucination and veridical perception are subjectively indistinguishable, but I would like to be told if I have that backwards. Second, whether any pair of the requirements I have listed is jointly unsatisfiable, since if some subset cannot be met together, that fact is itself the most interesting result the list could yield. You can skip the scorecard against the named contenders; I will do that part in class.
