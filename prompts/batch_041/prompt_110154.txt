At our city council's public hearings, the chair now rules members out of order when they invoke religious conviction in debate, appealing to a standing rule that members give only reasons accessible to all residents regardless of their religion or philosophy of life. The chair describes this as Rawls's idea of public reason put into practice, and the summary I keep hearing is that public reason requires citizens to keep religious arguments out of politics and to argue in secular terms instead. I want to know whether that is right. If it is, the rule looks not merely defensible but obligatory; if it is not, I need to understand what public reason actually asks of citizens, whom it binds and on which questions, what it demands of a religious citizen who cannot translate her convictions into public reasons at all, and whether it is the kind of requirement a chair can enforce. Close with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states in one sentence whether the chair's reading of Rawls is correct.
