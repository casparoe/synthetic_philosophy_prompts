I'm TAing a first-year seminar on logic and its philosophy, and I've hit a wall: the students are perfectly happy with truth tables, but the moment we get to why anybody would give up excluded middle their eyes glaze over, and I've noticed the examples we keep reaching for are exactly the ones they've half-memorised off the internet, so they nod along at the conclusion without ever feeling the pull of the other side. What I want from you is a designed case — built to order, not just an illustration — in which a classically-trained reasoner and a constructivist are looking at the *same* facts and land in different places, and not because one of them knows less. Constraints, and I care about all of them: the case has to turn on a step that is valid classically and invalid intuitionistically — use either double-negation elimination or the De Morgan step from not-both to either-not-this-or-not-that, and tell me which one you used; both parties must agree on every atomic fact in the story, and there must be no further evidence to be had, ever, by anyone (so no unopened envelopes, no sealed ballot boxes, no someone-could-just-go-and-check, no future contingents or sea battles, no self-reference or paradox tricks); it can't turn on vagueness, on an ambiguous word, or on how confident somebody happens to feel; and the setting has to be ordinary — a kitchen, a workplace, a hearing — with no mathematics, no probability, no logic-puzzle furniture. One paragraph for the scenario itself, three or four people at most, and it should have a bit of pull to it, i.e. the classical answer should be the one that feels obviously right at first pass.

Then do the check properly rather than gesturing at it. Write out the proposition actually at issue, name the inference, give the classical derivation of it and say which bit of classical semantics is doing the work (bivalence, truth-conditional satisfaction, whatever you think it is), then give the intuitionistic reading of the same move — they've had the proof-reading of the connectives in lecture, so you can lean on it — and be precise about what the constructivist is and isn't entitled to assert, including what they *can* still say (I assume something like: it's absurd that the disjunction is false, but that's not the disjunction). Then the practical upshot in the story: what each of them does next, and be honest about whether the classical one's answer is *true* even though the constructivist won't assert it — I want to know whether the disagreement is about the facts or about entitlement, because that's the thing my students can't see. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and show me the version of the case with that feature removed (I suspect it's the undecidability of the two disjuncts — so give me the variant where they're decidable and both parties agree). Finish by telling me how each side attacks the case: the classical logician's line that this shows at most that the rule isn't constructively *justified* and nothing about validity, and the constructivist's line that the classical answer is a guess wearing a proof-shaped shadow. Oh — and one more thing, say whether calling intuitionistic logic the 'weaker' system is misleading here, because half my job is getting them past 'it just proves less'.
