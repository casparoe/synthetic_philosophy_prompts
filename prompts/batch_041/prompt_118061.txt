Here is the question.

Question 2.

(i) Hempel's satisfaction criterion holds, roughly, that an object confirms a hypothesis when the object satisfies that hypothesis — when the hypothesis, applied to that object, comes out true of it. The equivalence condition holds that logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by the same evidence.

Consider the hypothesis H: every raven is black.

(a) Using the satisfaction criterion and the equivalence condition, explain why a white shoe appears to confirm H. Be explicit about which of the two conditions is doing which piece of work, and about what makes the result feel paradoxical. (8 marks)

(b) A laboratory owns a sampler that selects a single object from the universe and displays it. Assume that the universe contains finitely many objects.

Version A: the sampler selects an object uniformly at random from among all the objects in the universe.

Version B: a technician has configured the sampler to reject everything that is black and everything that is a raven, so that whatever it displays is guaranteed to be neither black nor a raven.

In both versions, the object displayed is a white shoe. A colleague says: "The white shoe is a positive instance of a hypothesis logically equivalent to H, so this observation must raise the probability of H, and it must do so in both versions; the sampler's settings cannot make the observation evidentially inert."

Evaluate the colleague's claim. If the two versions differ, say exactly what the difference is and why it matters; if they do not differ, say why not. (14 marks)

(c) Return to Version A. Compare the confirmation that H receives from the display of a white shoe with the confirmation it would receive from the display of a black raven. Which is greater, and what does the comparison show about the force of the paradox? (8 marks)

Here is the script.

(a) The satisfaction criterion says that an object confirms a hypothesis if the object satisfies it. Take the hypothesis H, every raven is black, and apply it to a particular object x: it says that if x is a raven then x is black. A white shoe satisfies this, because the shoe is not a raven, so the conditional comes out true of it; in Hempel's terminology the shoe is a positive instance of the hypothesis. The equivalence condition then tells us that logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by the same evidence. H is logically equivalent to the hypothesis that every non-black thing is a non-raven, and the white shoe is an instance of that formulation as well, so by the equivalence condition the shoe confirms H. What makes the result feel paradoxical is that the shoe seems to have nothing to do with ravens. We expect evidence to bear on the subject matter of the hypothesis it supports, and the shoe tells us nothing about the colour of any raven. But the criterion is a purely formal relation between an object and a hypothesis, and the air of paradox is an artefact of that expectation.

(b) The colleague is right, and right for the reason she gives. The white shoe is a positive instance of a hypothesis logically equivalent to H, and the equivalence condition transfers the confirmation to H. Nothing in the satisfaction criterion or in the equivalence condition refers to how the object came to be in front of us, and I take it that this is not an oversight: the criterion is a logical relation between an object and a hypothesis, and logical relations do not depend on the apparatus that produced the object. It is worth adding that being a positive instance just is being the kind of observation that is more likely if the hypothesis is true than if it is false; that is what makes an instance confirmatory. So the shoe raises the probability of H in Version A and in Version B alike, and the colleague's claim that the sampler's settings cannot make the observation inert is correct as stated.

A Bayesian might object that in Version B the display of a white shoe was going to happen whether or not there is a non-black raven anywhere, since the sampler would have rejected every raven and every black thing, so the observation leaves her probabilities where they were. But that objection confuses the evidence with our expectations about it. What we expected to see is a psychological matter — it concerns how much we ought to sit up — whereas the question whether an observation is an instance of a hypothesis is settled by the relation between the observation and the hypothesis, and by nothing else.

(c) In Version A the shoe does confirm H, but only very slightly. A randomly drawn object is almost certain not to be a raven at all, so finding that it is a white shoe rules out only the possibility that the object drawn was a non-black raven, which was antecedently very unlikely; the discrepancy between the number of ravens and the number of non-black things is what makes the contribution so small. The display of a black raven is different: a non-black raven was a live possibility, so that observation is a genuine test of H and the confirmation is correspondingly much stronger. This asymmetry is the real dissolution of the paradox. The paradox depends on treating all positive instances as equal, and once we see how small the shoe's contribution is, the sense that the shoe confirms the hypothesis disappears. Indeed a criterion on which an observation that moves the probability by a hair counts as confirmation is at best a rough heuristic, and a serious theory of confirmation should replace it with something like a likelihood ratio.
