I am a novelist, and for the better part of a year I have been writing a book in which a man is replaced, cell by cell, by a functional duplicate of himself — slowly enough that he never once loses consciousness, completely enough that by the final page not a particle of the man who began the process remains. Throughout, I have kept Christopher Nolan's film The Prestige, and the Christopher Priest novel it adapts, at my elbow as a touchstone, and I would like you to read the film for me through Derek Parfit's teletransporter and his arguments about what matters in survival — a reading rigorous enough that I can steal from it as I revise.

What I want is not a synopsis of either the film or the philosophy. I want to know where the film is genuinely Parfitian and where it quietly cheats. Parfit's claim, as I understand it, is that personal identity is not what matters: that what matters — psychological connectedness and continuity, admitting of degrees — can survive the destruction of the original, so that a teletransported replica who remembers your life is for practical purposes as good as you, and that the horror we feel at the annihilation of the body is a residue of a belief we could, with effort, surrender. The film stages two rival answers to this within a single rivalry. Borden has no machine: his Real Transported Man is two men, an identical twin who lives half a life, so that "Borden" is two continuous streams sharing one name, one wife, one secret. Angier has the machine: his trick manufactures a double, and at every performance one of the two appears across the theatre while the other falls into a locked tank and drowns. I suspect the film wants it both ways — it wants the Parfitian thrill that the man who walks out onto the balcony is still him, and it wants the horror that a self is murdered nightly so that the trick will work — and I want you to tell me which impulse the film finally obeys, scene by scene, and precisely where it flinches.

Then push further, because this is the part I need for my own book. Parfit's most searching case is not the teletransporter at all but fission — the branching case in which two survivors have equal claim to be you, so that identity gives out entirely and what matters has to do the work alone. Argue with me about whether The Prestige is really a fission story disguised as a teletransportation story: whether Angier's nightly double is less a copy than a branch, and whether the film's tragedy derives from treating a branching as a death. And bring my slow replacement into the frame. If Parfit is right that continuity of psychology is what matters, then a man replaced gradually should have nothing to fear and nothing to mourn, since at no moment is there a break to grieve; but a story with no break is a story with no death, and I am not certain a novel can survive that. Tell me whether the film offers me any resources for making gradual replacement dramatic, or whether it secretly depends on the instantaneous version — the flash, the tank, the drowned body — precisely because Parfit's continuous case leaves a novelist nothing to write.

Finally, be honest where the reading fails. If the film is not ultimately about personal identity at all but about obsession, rivalry, and the price of a trick, say so plainly, and tell me what a Parfitian critic would have to give up in order to keep the reading alive. I would far rather have a reading that admits the film resists Parfit than one that flattens the film into an illustration of him.
