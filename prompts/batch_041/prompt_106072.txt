I have been turning over the old worry that no belief can really be justified: every reason seems to need a further reason behind it; if you ever stop, the stopping point looks arbitrary, and if you never stop, you never arrive anywhere. The usual replies — that some beliefs simply stand on their own, or that beliefs prop one another up in a circle — have never satisfied me. I should like you to argue for a different line.

Treat justification as a matter of degree rather than all or nothing, and picture the chain of reasons as a series in which each link supports the one before it a little less strongly than the link before that, with the strength falling away in such a way that the whole endless chain still comes to a finite, substantial amount — much as an endless series of numbers can have a finite sum. On that picture, the endlessness of the regress is no defect at all, and neither foundationalism nor coherentism is needed. Please make the case for this view: explain how it is meant to work, why it is not merely a restatement of the problem, and how it answers the objection that however small each remaining step becomes, there is always one more still owing, so the debt of justification is never actually paid. Argue for the position rather than merely surveying it, and write in British English.
