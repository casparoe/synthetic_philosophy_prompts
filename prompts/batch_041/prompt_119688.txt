I am the director of assessment for the national certifying body for industrial rope-access technicians — the people who suspend themselves from bridges, towers, and turbine blades to inspect and repair them. Our certification currently requires candidates to pass a written paper and to perform a set of observed practical tasks. A review commissioned by our board has recommended abolishing the practical examination. Its argument is that the written paper is cheaper, that its scores are far more reliable because they do not vary with the severity of the examiner, and that it tests the same competence: a candidate who can state the correct sequence of operations, the load limits, the failure modes, and the decision rules for abandoning a descent has demonstrated the knowledge the job requires, and the physical performance is a formality. As a concession, the review proposes replacing the practical with a recorded think-aloud in which the candidate works through scenarios, the transcript then being scored — perhaps, they suggest, by a language model — for evidence of the relevant knowledge.

I am uneasy about this but cannot articulate why, and I suspect the philosophical debate about knowing how and knowing that is where the real argument lies. Please set out the most important implications of that debate for my decision.

Several facts about our candidates make the question live rather than academic. Some of our most trusted technicians — people whose work has never failed an inspection — came through an unaccredited apprenticeship, cannot explain what they do or why, and would fail the written paper outright. We also routinely certify candidates who can recite the manual and then freeze at the first unplanned event on the rope. Our examiners disagree about what they are looking at when they watch a candidate work, and the reliability figures for the practical are poor.

I want the implications ranked by how much they should change what we do, not a survey of positions, and I want you to mark which of them hold only on the anti-intellectualist view — that knowing how to do something is a distinct kind of knowledge, constituted by an ability rather than by propositional knowledge — and which survive on the intellectualist view, that knowing how is a species of knowing that, perhaps under a practical mode of presentation. In particular: what does the debate license, and what does it not license? Does it support retaining an observed performance assessment, or only retaining some performance measure? What does it imply about the technician who performs the task faultlessly but cannot describe it — does she know how? Does the answer change for one who could once perform it and, after an injury, no longer can? Or for one who can describe it fluently and has never done it? And what follows for the think-aloud proposal: is articulacy a proxy for know-how, or for something else that we may or may not want to measure?
