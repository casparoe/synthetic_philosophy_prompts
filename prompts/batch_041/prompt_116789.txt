I have been reading decision theory in the rationalist blogosphere for the better part of fifteen years — the sequences, the annual review posts, the long comment threads that sprout under anyone who writes about Newcomb, Allais, or the distinction between risk and ambiguity — and I have recently joined a reading group that has just finished Buchak's *Risk and Rationality* (2013), the first time I have worked through the formalism rather than nodding along to a summary of it. One of the members, who is better at this than I am and who likes to construct arguments specifically so that they can be dismantled, offered the following as a demonstration that the book's central proposal proves too much, and while I can feel the draft coming from somewhere I cannot locate it. His argument, as he wrote it on the whiteboard: **(1)** Risk-weighted expected utility theory ranks acts by a rank-dependent sum — order the outcomes of an act from worst to best, take the increment in utility between each outcome and the one just below it, weight that increment by the agent's risk function *r* applied to the probability of getting an outcome at least that good, and add the lot; *r* is any continuous increasing map of the unit interval to itself, and the rule collapses into ordinary expected utility when *r* is the identity. **(2)** The risk function is not a belief about how the world is; it states how the agent wants her prospects shaped, how much she values security as such. Since there is no fact of the matter about whether security ought to weigh more than expected value, *r* is not the sort of thing that can be correct or incorrect: it is a free parameter, and every continuous increasing *r* is admissible. **(3)** What holds for *r* holds for the other non-doxastic parameters in any such representation — the curvature of the utility function, the rate at which an agent discounts future goods, the weight she gives to inequality between persons, the extra weight she gives to vivid or salient outcomes. These are attitudes rather than beliefs, and so equally unconstrained. **(4)** It follows that the theory's verdict on any act is only ever that the act is rational for an agent whose parameters happen to favour it; there is no act that some admissible setting of the parameters does not rank first. **(5)** So if the theory is right, no act is irrational, and it imposes no constraint on choice at all. **(6)** But a theory of practical rationality that certifies everything certifies nothing, so the theory cannot be right, and the casualty is (2): risk attitudes must after all be criticisable, and some risk functions must be irrational.
