I have been turning over a puzzle that I suspect is more philosophical than technical, and I would like you to help me rank the possible answers. The setting is what people working on interpretability sometimes call mechanistic anomaly detection. The idea, as I understand it, is this: we have a model whose behavior we have characterized over some distribution of inputs, we have some account of the internal machinery that produces that behavior, and we want to flag inputs where the model is handled by machinery that differs importantly from the machinery we thought we understood — even though the outward response looks entirely ordinary and every behavioral test we can run comes back clean. The trouble is that this project only gets off the ground if we can say when two pieces of internal machinery count as the same mechanism, and I cannot find a principled answer that does not collapse into behaviorism at one end or into a criterion so fine-grained that every forward pass is a fresh mechanism at the other. A colleague on the evaluations side insists the whole question is idle — if the behavior is identical on everything we can measure, then the mechanism is the same by stipulation — and I am fairly sure he is wrong, but I cannot say precisely why.

What I would like from you is a ranking of candidate criteria for mechanism-identity by how well each would serve as the working test in such a pipeline. Judge them not in the abstract but as something an interpretability team could actually apply to a trained network in order to decide whether the machinery behind a suspicious-looking case is the machinery they already have an account of. Assume the team has whatever tools you consider realistic: activation patching and ablation, resampling-based tests that ask whether a simplified account of a component preserves behavior under the relevant interventions, sparse probes, and full access to weights and training data. The candidates, in no particular order: (a) input-output equivalence over a reference distribution; (b) isomorphism between causal graphs over the variables of an explicit causal model of the computation; (c) the existence of a valid, possibly approximate, high-level abstraction of the low-level computation, in the sense of a mapping that commutes with a suitable class of interventions; (d) equality of response profiles across a fixed battery of interventions; (e) sameness of computational role, where two subcomputations count as the same just in case the rest of the system consumes their outputs in the same way; (f) identity up to the symmetries of the architecture, such as permutations of hidden units that leave the overall function unchanged; and (g) sameness of causal lineage, that is, whether the two pieces of machinery descend from the same training signal or the same data.

Please give me a tier list — say four tiers, best to worst — with each candidate placed in a tier and a score out of ten for each, so that items sharing a tier can still be separated. Defend each placement briefly. Then tell me which single criterion you would adopt as the default operational test and why, which two of the candidates are most often conflated in practice and what that confusion costs, and what conceptual or empirical development would most change your ordering. I care less about the ranking itself than about the criteria you are ranking by: I want to know what you take the purpose of a mechanism-identity test to be, whether you think that purpose is genuinely unitary, and where your ranking would have to move if it turned out that anomaly detection has to serve two incompatible masters — catching genuinely novel mechanisms, and not drowning the team in false positives. Please write your answer in British English.
