I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level seminar on propositions and truth-bearers — 14 students, uneven prep, two of them have seen a bit of Frege, most haven't done any philosophy of language past an intro, and one guy keeps trying to turn everything into formal logic. I want to run an activity that makes them feel the pull of Bradley's regress in their hands rather than hear me describe it, and I've sketched a mechanic I'd like you to turn into something actually runnable. Each team gets a tray of tokens: a few object tokens ("Socrates", "the ball"), a few property tokens ("__ is wise", "__ is red"), and a strictly limited supply of linking tokens ("__ instantiates __", "__ is joined to __"). They score by assembling a "live" structure, and the house rule for liveness is brutal — every token in the structure has to be wired into the rest by a linking token, and the linking token is itself part of the structure, so it has to be wired in too. Under that rule nobody can ever win, and the first 25 minutes should be them finding that out with their own hands, not being told. If there's time I want a second act with the same mechanic: the tokens this time are two premises and a conclusion, and liveness requires that the step from premises to conclusion be licensed by a token that's in the structure — they should hit the identical wall trying to get from modus-ponens premises to the conclusion by piling on another conditional premise, and the debrief should make clear it's the same engine running, not a cute coincidence. Don't name Bradley at the top, and don't name any of the standard escape hatches — the Tortoise included — the whole thing should come out of the room first.

What I need back: the exact rules and scoring, a materials list (cheap, printable, nothing that needs a screen or wifi), whether roles help and what they'd be (a referee who applies the liveness rule with no mercy? one team secretly allowed to propose a rule amendment?), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90, and then the debrief, which is the part I actually care about. The debrief should map what really happened onto the live positions: the moment a team declares one token "special" and exempt from needing glue (that's the unsaturated-slot move); the moment they say the whole arrangement just is one thing and stop asking (that's the monist / internal-relations way out, and I want to know how to get from there to Bradley's own positive view without lecturing); the moment they say the linking was never a thing in the world at all but something the team did (predication as an act); and the moment they point at how the tokens happen to be laid out on the table and treat the layout as what unifies them (the representation doing the work). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — my guesses are that a few students decide the game is rigged and quietly check out, and that someone will keep adding tokens well past the point of absurdity for the bit — and what to do in each case.

And be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce, because I'd rather say it out loud than have it quietly fail. My own sense: it can't show them that the regress is vicious rather than merely tedious, since I've rigged the win condition so the regress is literally unwinnable, which is begging a question the real debate leaves open; and it can't reproduce the Fregean option at all, because every token I hand out is object-like and there's no gap for anything to fill. Tell me what I have to supply in the last ten minutes that the game won't supply for me, and if the second act is too much for one session, say so and tell me which half to cut.
