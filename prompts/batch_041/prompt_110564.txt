My aunt has a progressive condition that is expected, in time, to change who she is, and she knows it. A few years ago, while she was still entirely herself, she wrote an advance directive that does more than list the treatments she would refuse. It names my cousin as the only person permitted to speak for her, forbids anyone from replacing him, bars her from being examined by any doctor he has not approved, and adds a clause I have never been able to shake: if she ever asks to revoke the document, the asking itself is to be treated as proof that the change she feared has arrived, and the request is to be set aside.

She has now asked. She is lucid, she has held the position steadily for two years, she can give an account of herself, and her reasons are not frivolous — the treatment keeps her alive and makes her miserable. My cousin refuses, exactly as he was instructed.

Write a short story about this, told from the point of view of the person who has to do the refusing. Give both versions of her their strongest case: the woman who wrote the document should not come off as a coward who could not face the future, and the woman asking to revoke it should not come off as someone whose judgment can be waved away because of what she has become. Let the story turn on the thing I cannot get past — whether a person may legitimately arrange, in advance, to be the one who always wins, so that any objection from her later self counts either as obedience or as proof of incapacity. Do not settle it at the end, but make the reader feel the full pull of both answers.
