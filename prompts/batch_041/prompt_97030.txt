ok so i've had a story idea stuck in my head for months and I can't get it to work, and I think the reason is that every time I sit down to write it I drift into the obvious version. The seed is that old thing from Dostoevsky — the cardinal who comes to the cell at night and lays it out: you handed people freedom and they can't carry it, what they actually want is bread and someone to bow down to, and its our job now to take that weight off them and be the ones who are miserable about it. I dont want to retell that scene. I want the same argument made somewhere that isnt a prison in Seville and isnt a generic dystopia either.

Here's the setup I've got. A mid-size coastal town, flattened by a flood about thirty years ago, rebuilt under a charter with an outfit called the Provision. Two clauses are the whole story. First: residents get housing, food, medicine and a guaranteed income for life, and in exchange they hand over civic decision-making to the Provision's board. Second, and this is the bit I'm attached to: the Provision assumes liability for what residents do. Not just civil liability — the clause says anything a resident does inside the town is done as the Provision's act, so the resident is not the responsible party. There's an office, literally a counter with a clerk, where you can file to have something you've already done absorbed. Absolution as an administrative function. I want the story to take that seriously, not as a gag.

The story itself: one night, the woman who runs that counter — twenty years at it, she drafted the liability clause herself as a young lawyer — talks to a visitor who has come to the office after hours and says nothing the entire time. One long speech. No reply, no reveal that the visitor is anybody divine, no twist that he's secretly the town's founder or whatever.

Three things the speech has to actually wrestle with, and I dont want any of them resolved:
- the difference between a town where people are content and a town where people have agreed to something. She should have an uncomfortably good case: theyre not lying to themselves, they know exactly what they traded away, and the people who'd sneer at that are the ones who never had to bury anyone.
- the absolution clause as the real price, not a kindness. She has to explain why absorbing everyone's guilt is a claim on them rather than mercy, and why she still thinks its the honest way to run a town. If I remember right the inquisitor has a line about the rulers letting the people sin and taking the guilt onto their own heads — I want her making that move, but in the language of liability and paperwork.
- what it costs her. She should be right about a lot, not a hypocrite, and genuinely unhappy in a way that isnt self-pity and isnt punished by the plot.

Around 1500 words, tight — cut scenery before you cut argument. Dont end on a kiss or any gesture standing in for an answer, and dont let anyone leave town at the end. The visitors silence should be doing work: I want her to keep half-guessing who she's talking to and keep getting it wrong.
