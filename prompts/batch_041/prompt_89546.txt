At a neighborhood meeting I attended, two residents got into an argument I couldn't referee, and what I want is an adjudication of the exchange rather than a verdict on the project itself. The proposal is a four-story, forty-unit building — twelve of the units below market rate — replacing a single-family house and a small parking lot on a street zoned for single-family homes.

The objector said the street is zoned single-family, that people bought in reliance on that scale, and that the building would shadow yards, add traffic, and crowd the elementary school; he isn't against housing, he said, but density belongs on the transit corridor three blocks away. The supporter answered that housing is a need rather than a preference, that the people who would live there are currently doubled up with relatives or commuting two hours, that the "character" argument is doing the work of keeping out people poorer than the objector, and that the infrastructure objection proves too much, since the objector's own house strains the same pipes and roads.

The objector replied that he isn't claiming a right to exclude anyone in particular, only a say in decisions about his own environment — a say the newcomers would also have once they arrived. The asymmetry, he said, is that his surroundings change without his consent while they gain, and since someone has to decide, the people who already live there are the ones with standing. The supporter said that consent of the governed is a doctrine about states, not streets; that the would-be residents never consented to being kept out; and that he is converting a contingent fact — arriving first — into authority, so that had he bought ten years later he would be the one locked out and would call it unjust.

The objector then said he has a right to the city too: the right to inhabit and shape the place one lives, not merely to be housed somewhere. The supporter, he said, seems to mean by that phrase a right to live wherever one likes, which is a different right and cannot be unlimited without collapsing into "developers may build anything." The supporter answered that the right to the city was always about use rather than exchange value — inhabitants shaping their place against the logic of property — which is why it favors the renters on the block over the landlord, and which does not license freezing the street in amber. She added that the objector's own principle, that one may not use the law to better one's position by worsening others', applies to the status quo as well: the zoning that casts no shadow and adds no traffic also locks people out, and he isn't counting the excluded as worsened because they were never here.

Please adjudicate this point by point — who is right about which specific claims, where each of them overreaches, and what, if anything, both are missing. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and without restating the question.
