I would like a comparison of two ideas from the Stoic tradition, one of them famous and the other usually left out of popular accounts of it. The famous one is the dichotomy of control: some things are "up to us" — our own judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions — while everything else, including our bodies, reputations, offices, and the outcomes of our actions, is not. The neglected one is the qualification the Stoics attach to the pursuit of the so-called preferred indifferents (health, wealth, the success of one's projects, the welfare of one's friends): these are to be pursued only "with reservation," that is, wanted conditionally — "if nothing prevents it," "as fate permits" — and regarded in themselves as indifferent to happiness, which depends solely on virtue.

Please compare the two. In particular: (1) Is the reservation clause an independent thesis, or is it merely the dichotomy of control applied to action? (2) What work does each do within the Stoic system, and what would be lost by keeping the dichotomy and quietly dropping the reservation? (3) Many contemporary readers take the dichotomy of control to license an attitude of not caring about outcomes at all; does pairing it with the reservation clause support that reading or undermine it? (4) How should a Stoic who accepts both ideas respond to the failure, through no fault of their own, of a project pursued with all the skill and effort the discipline of action demands? (5) Given that the Stoics held that everything happens by fate, in what sense can anything be "up to us" at all, and does the reservation clause presuppose or help to resolve that difficulty?

Throughout, keep separate what is settled among people who know the Stoic texts from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
