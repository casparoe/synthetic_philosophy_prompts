I sit on my county's biodiversity and ethics advisory panel, and we must vote next week on a permit for a developer who wants to clear a lot containing a very large ant colony -- a sprawling network of nests that seems to regulate its foraging and defense as a single unit, even though no individual ant knows what the colony is doing. Our ordinance protects individual organisms capable of suffering, and it says nothing about colonies, superorganisms, or collective minds. A local naturalist has asked us to treat the colony itself as a single moral patient, invoking the old thought experiment about an ant colony that appears to have a mind of its own. The developer's lawyer replies that this is nonsense: a colony is just a lot of ants, and if no individual ant is owed special protection, neither is the pile. I have no academic background, but I have been thinking about this on my own for weeks, and I am not satisfied by either side. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- approve the permit, deny it, or approve it with conditions -- and attach a time frame to whatever we require. It should also give the panel a workable way to decide whether a colony can have a mind in the relevant sense: for instance, whether it is enough that the colony behaves as if it has goals, or whether there must be some single subject that experiences something, rather than merely a collection of insects responding to local cues. Finally, it should engage the strongest consideration on the side you decide against, so that the opinion can survive the meeting rather than just win it. Write it for people who are not philosophers, but do not pretend the question is easier than it is.
