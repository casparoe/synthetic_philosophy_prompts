I retired some years ago after a career in radio astronomy, and I have spent the time since reading philosophy of science, which is how I came to see that a line I used to deliver over coffee about the Fermi paradox was carrying far more weight than I ever acknowledged. My line was that we have been listening for decades and heard nothing but noise, so either nobody is out there or everybody is hiding, and parsimony favors the first. I now recognize how much that stacks on premises I never defended: that our listening has been sensitive enough, over a wide enough range of frequencies and directions, for the null result to mean much; that any civilization worth wondering about would necessarily be loud and long-lived rather than brief or quiet; that we are entitled to reason as though we occupy a typical position in the galaxy rather than an observationally selected one; and, the premise that troubles me most, that "nobody is out there" is a single hypothesis rather than a bundle of very different ones, running from life almost never starting, through life starting readily but complex life being a fluke, to intelligence being common while detectable intelligence is fleeting.

What I would like from you is a credence, as a number, that the resolution lies on the rare side rather than the merely-undetected side. To make that precise: take the proposition to be that the expected number of signal-emitting civilizations in the observable universe at the present moment is less than one. Please do not simply hand me the number. Decompose it into the sub-questions it turns on — the probability that a habitable planet yields life at all, that life yields complex life, that complex life yields something that builds a transmitter, that such a thing remains detectable long enough to overlap with us, that our searches would in fact have found it if it were there, and whatever term you think best captures the observer-selection worry — give each a rough probability and a line of justification, then tell me how you combine them and which single term the final figure is most sensitive to. I would also like to know which of those terms is doing the most work in the gap between two people who land far apart: I put myself near 0.6, a former colleague now working on biosignatures puts it nearer 0.15, and in years of correspondence I have never been able to locate where our disagreement actually lives. Finally, tell me what sort of evidence or argument would move you most on the term you are least confident in, and mark plainly which of the terms are empirical questions that further astronomy could settle and which are conceptual questions that no telescope could settle, since that distinction is the reason I started reading philosophy in the first place.

Please write this as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; I print these out and read them at the desk, and the formatting only gets in the way.
