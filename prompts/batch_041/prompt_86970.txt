Having spent thirty-five years as a field geologist and only lately taken up the philosophy of mind in a reading group of other late starters, I have run into an argument that I cannot fault but cannot accept, and I would like help deciding where the trouble lies. It comes out of the literature on the extended mind — the thesis, as I understand it, that some of the processes that constitute a person's thinking take place partly outside the person's skin — and one of our number reconstructed it as follows.

(1) If a process outside the body plays the same functional role as a process inside it — if it is recruited in the same way, to the same behavioural ends, under the same conditions of use — then it is the same kind of cognitive process. (2) The notebook that a certain man with a memory impairment consults, in the very way the rest of us consult our memories, plays that role; so it is part of his memory. (3) Nothing in (1) or (2) turns on the notebook being paper rather than silicon, or on its being outside the skin; so any resource that is reliably on hand and taken at face value when consulted is part of the memory of whoever uses it that way. (4) A search engine is, for most people I know, reliably on hand and taken at face value; so it is part of their memory. (5) But a search engine belongs to no one user: it is the same engine for all of us, and what it returns depends on a corpus that all of us are continually writing to. (6) So the memory of each user includes the same thing, and any two users who query the same engine share a part of their memory. (7) Push the same reasoning one step further and the friend I ask for directions, the conventions of my old profession, and the language I think in all become parts of my memory. (8) If everything in my cognitive neighbourhood is part of my memory, then there is no principled line between my mind and the world, nor between my mind and yours. (9) But that cannot be right: I am one thinker among many, and I do not think your thoughts. (10) Therefore (1) is false.

I would like to know which step I should give up, why that step looked unobjectionable when I first read it, and what it costs to abandon it. If a step can be kept only under a reading narrower than the one written out above, I want to hear what that reading is and what it commits me to, since I would rather not surrender the first two steps if I can avoid it — they seem to me to be the whole of the idea — but neither will I pretend that the ninth step is anything I can bring myself to deny. Please price the alternatives rather than simply naming a culprit, and tell me which of them you would actually take.
