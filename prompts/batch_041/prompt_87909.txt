I run a 90-minute session in an interdisciplinary seminar on reason and decision-making — about fifteen students, a mix of philosophy and economics people with a couple of mathematicians, and no laptops allowed — and every year I face the same problem: the Arrow material lands as a theorem I recite rather than something they have lived through. I read a lot of rationalist blogs and forums, and I have grown allergic to the move where you state the conditions, gesture at the proof, and everyone nods; I want the impossibility to show up in the room, in what people actually do to each other, before I name it. So please act as a social-choice theorist who also designs experiential learning, think carefully and brainstorm a little to yourself about what a group of this size can be made to feel in ninety minutes, and then give me a complete, runnable activity: rules, roles, materials, a minute-by-minute agenda, and a scoring scheme with real stakes that does not require money or software.

The shape I have in mind, which you should feel free to restructure if you see something better, has two phases. In the first, the group must choose among several options through a sequence of pairwise majority votes, and I want them to stumble into a cycle — where one option beats a second, the second beats a third, and the third beats the first — so that the obvious democratic procedure produces no winner and, worse, the person who controls the order of the votes quietly decides the outcome. Let the agenda power be discovered rather than announced: one participant is the chair, has a stake in the result, and should not be told what their position can do. In the second phase, if it fits without crowding the session, have the group aggregate judgments rather than preferences: it votes separately on a set of linked propositions and on the conclusion those propositions support, the majority verdict on the propositions points one way and the majority verdict on the conclusion points the other, and the group has to argue about which answer it really gave. (I have seen this second puzzle called the doctrinal or discursive dilemma; treat it as the cousin and keep the session anchored on Arrow.) Then a debrief that maps each moment of frustration onto a condition of the theorem — unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship — saying which condition that moment was really about and what it would cost the group to abandon it. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: which part of the theorem's force comes from being a claim about every possible profile rather than the few you staged, and where the facilitator has to supply the generality by hand.

Finally, tell me the predictable ways this goes wrong the first time I run it and how to steer around them: what I do if the group refuses to cycle and a Condorcet winner keeps emerging, what I do if someone games the agenda so blatantly that the lesson becomes about that person rather than the structure, and what I do if the judgment phase muddies things instead of sharpening them. Write it as someone who has actually run rooms like this — with the debrief questions spelled out and a note on which single moment the whole session is really built around — and close with what the students should walk out seeing that they did not see walking in.
