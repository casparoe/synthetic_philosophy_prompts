I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend who works in development economics, and the argument left me with what I think is a genuine research question rather than just a bad mood. His position was that almost any moral disagreement between two parties who have to keep living or working together can be resolved the way trade resolves disagreement about prices: if each of us gives up something he ranks lower in exchange for something he ranks higher, both of us end up ahead of where we started, and nothing in that requires us to agree about the underlying rankings, or even to have a common currency in which the rankings can be compared. I found this persuasive as a description of what often happens and deeply suspicious as a theory of what ought to happen, and I could not articulate why before he went home. The best I could manage was that some of the things people surrender in these bargains are not the sort of thing that can be surrendered without ceasing to be the person who made the bargain — but I could not say whether that marks a real limit on the analogy or is just squeamishness about treating commitments as commodities.

I am applying for a small departmental fellowship that funds one semester of independent work, roughly twelve weeks, producing a paper of eight to ten thousand words, and I want to turn this into the proposal. What I want to press on is whether the gains-from-trade model of moral compromise is doing explanatory work or merely decorating an obvious point, and specifically whether it survives two things it seems to need. First, the model as my friend stated it treats each party's values as a ranking it owns and can revise or exchange at will; I want to know whether any plausible account of what it is to hold a value permits that, or whether some values are constituted by their non-negotiability — in which case the tradeable ones may be exactly the ones we care least about. Second, the model needs some medium in which concessions can be measured, and it is not obvious that how much of one's conviction one surrenders is even a quantity. I suspect the analogy works only where the disagreement has already been converted into a dispute over divisible goods, and that this conversion, rather than the trading, is the substantive move.

Please write the proposal. State the question so that it has a determinate answer rather than a mood; explain why the existing ways of thinking about moral compromise — as mutual accommodation, as a virtue of the person who compromises, as a second-best outcome when agreement fails — leave this open; describe the method, which I imagine as working through a handful of constructed cases with stipulated value-rankings to see which can be modeled as trade and which resist, plus some attempt to say what the resisting cases have in common; and say what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing. Also give me an honest estimate of whether this is a semester or a career, and tell me where the proposal overreaches. Do not bother with citations or a literature review; I will add those.
