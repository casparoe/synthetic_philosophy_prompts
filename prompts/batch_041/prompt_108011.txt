I am a retired experimental condensed-matter physicist. Since closing my lab four years ago I have been working through philosophy of physics on my own, with gaps I am well aware of, and I have now been asked by the organiser of an evening reading group to lead a single ninety-minute session on the dispute between substantivalists and relationists about space and time, taking Newton's rotating bucket as the point of departure. What I want from you is not the explanation itself but the plan for it: I will be the one standing at the whiteboard, and I would rather be told how to sequence the session than be handed a script to read out.

Please take the role of a philosopher of physics who has taught this material many times, both to specialists and to intelligent audiences with no mathematical training, and who cares more about how the material is learned than about displaying erudition. Think the problem through carefully before you write anything: consider what the two positions are actually disagreeing about once the technical formulations are set aside, and consider what a listener must already accept for the bucket even to look like an argument rather than a curiosity. Then give me the plan, in the five parts below, in connected prose rather than clipped bullet fragments.

1. The order of the ideas. Tell me what has to be in place before what, and why the dependency runs in that direction: an ordered chain in which each link is needed for the next to be intelligible. Where a link could be postponed, say so and tell me what would go wrong if I postponed it.

2. The carrying example. I want one case to do the real work, and I want to know what it must have and must not have to bear that weight. Here I have a prejudice that I would like you to test rather than accommodate. My instinct, after thirty-five years on superfluids, is to make the carrying case a bucket of superfluid helium rather than a bucket of water: the superfluid component resists being dragged into bulk rotation with the container, and when it does rotate it does so through quantised vortices rather than as a smooth circulating flow. Tell me whether this makes the session sharper or whether it is a trap, whether the audience will be able to see the philosophical point at all through the physics, and whether the example still separates the two positions or quietly collapses them. Check my description of the physics as you go and correct it if I have it wrong; do not let a bad example through out of politeness. If you reject it, give me the case that should carry the session instead, and explain why the obvious candidates, the standard bucket of water or a pair of masses joined by a cord and spun about their common centre of mass, are better or worse choices.

3. The misconceptions. Here are the ones I expect, and I would like you to tell me which is the dangerous one and which are harmless: that relationism is the view that space is a fiction or a mere manner of speaking; that the bucket experiment is an empirical refutation of relationism, settled by the data; that absolute space is the aether under a new name; that general relativity has since decided the question; and that the relationist's task is to say that the water rotates relative to the distant stars, which strikes me as confusing a claim about what motion is with a claim about what causes inertia. Add the ones I have not foreseen, especially any specific to this audience, and for each misconception give me the precise point in the sequence at which it should be headed off, together with the sentence or question that does the heading off. I do not want a list of corrections delivered in advance; I want the moment in the sequence at which each correction lands.

4. The check. Give me a single question or small task to put to the group near the end whose answers will show me whether they have understood what the dispute is about, rather than merely remembering what Newton said, and tell me what a good answer sounds like as opposed to a merely fluent one.

5. What to leave out. Tell me what I should not attempt in ninety minutes, and how to say so without making the session feel truncated or making the audience suspect that they have been given a simplified picture the truth will later contradict, and how to indicate honestly and briefly what lies beyond the edge of what I have covered.

Some constraints. The audience is a dozen people: two graduate students in philosophy with no physics beyond school, a lawyer, two retired engineers, and one autodidact who has read the popular books and will want to know whether the whole dispute has been superseded. Nobody will do mathematics, so any technical machinery has to be carried by words or pictures. They are not beginners at argument, only at this argument. Keep the register plain.

Finally, do not give me reading lists, citations, or the names of papers: I am not distributing anything and I have no interest in where a formulation comes from. I care only whether the argument, as you present it, can be made to stand on its own in the room. If you think my framing of the session is mistaken, if for instance the bucket cannot honestly be taught as a single argument that decides anything, say so briefly at the start and then give me the plan anyway.
