I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of economics, seconded one day a week to the research office of a mid-sized foundation, where I sit in on the monthly continuation reviews at which funded programmes are kept, cut, or escalated. Both the 'we have already put £2m into this' argument and my colleagues' reflex to dismiss it are being applied without any discipline, and I would like a procedure the programme managers could actually run. The standard view to operationalise is that past expenditure should be ignored because it is common to every option still open; but I do not think that is the whole story, and I want the procedure to preserve three uses of past spending that I take to be legitimate: evidential (earlier spending as a proxy for how many independent reviewers judged the project sound), strategic (a funder's known reluctance to abandon ship is itself a commitment that changes what grantees do), and self-binding (having paid upfront is what makes people turn up). Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to the panel because the question is one of values rather than facts; mark the points where a judgement call is dressed up as procedure. It has to fit on one side of A4, be runnable in fifteen minutes by someone who has never heard of the fallacy, and be signed off by the finance director. Run it on three cases -- a vaccine trial with a null interim result and half its budget spent, a half-built data platform, and a training programme paid for upfront whose main effect is that payment makes participants attend -- and tell me what the procedure cannot do and where it will misfire.
