I run a fortnightly lunchtime discussion group at a software company, and the next session is mine to lead. The topic I've settled on is the problem usually called consequentialist cluelessness: the thought that if the right action is the one with the best overall consequences, then the standard reaches into every downstream effect of what one does — including effects no one could have foreseen — and it is unclear whether any of us is ever in a position to compare two options on that basis at all. I've read enough to be dangerous and not enough to be sure of myself, and what I want from you is not an explanation of the problem but a plan for teaching it. Treat yourself as an experienced philosophy tutor who has taught this material to non-specialists many times. Before you commit to anything, brainstorm two or three possible orderings and two or three possible carrying examples, say briefly why you set the others aside, and then settle on one of each. What I need is the order in which the pieces have to go so that each step is motivated by the last: what has to be in place before the problem can even be stated, what the audience must have accepted for the worry to bite rather than sound like hand-waving, and precisely where the pivot from ordinary uncertainty to something worse than uncertainty should sit. Be explicit about the dependency structure — say which idea is doing the work at each stage, and why it cannot be moved earlier.

For the example that carries the whole session I have a candidate, and I'd like you to rule on it rather than simply accept it. My candidate is a choice that actually comes up in our work: whether to release an internal tool under a permissive licence or a copyleft one, where the argument on either side is about what happens decades later as the code is forked, embedded in other projects, or quietly abandoned. I like it because it is ours and because it isn't the charity example everyone has already heard. But I suspect it fails in a specific way — I think it gives me uncertainty about a describable set of outcomes rather than the open-ended kind — and if it fails I want you to say exactly what the carrying example must have and must lack, and to propose one that has it. The two stock examples I would rather keep out unless you can show they are doing something no other example can: the saved child who grows up to be a tyrant, and the donation to a distant charity.

Then map the misconceptions I should expect, and where in the sequence to head each one off. My own guesses, which I would like you to confirm, correct or add to, are these: that people will hear the problem as ordinary risk, or as 'we can't predict the future, so anything goes'; that they will treat it as an objection to consequentialism specifically rather than to any theory that tells you what to do; that someone will reach for a version of the butterfly effect and think that is the whole point; and that another will say the answer is simply to do what is locally reasonable and stop worrying, which I take to be an evasion but am not sure I can say why. Tell me which of these are genuine and which are mine. Give me one closing question that would show whether the group has grasped the structural point rather than merely been impressed by the scale of it, and say what you would leave out at this level and why. I would rather have a plan that is honest about its own weak joints than a tidy one: tell me which sequencing choice you are least confident in, what would make you revise it, and what I would see in the room that would tell me the order was wrong.
