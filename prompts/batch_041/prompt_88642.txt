I'm in a reading group that's spent most of this term circling the problem of logical omniscience and we've ground to a halt. The formal people say it's a feature and not a bug: a logic of knowledge is a logic of what follows from an agent's information, so of course the agent knows all the consequences, and if you want a psychology of actual believers go build one. The epistemologists say thats a dodge, because then the thing being modeled isn't knowledge at all, and anyway the fixes on offer (awareness, impossible worlds, explicit versus implicit knowledge, treating knowledge as a set of sentences rather than a set of worlds) either relocate the problem into an unexplained primitive or make the logic too weak to do the job it was introduced for. Nobody's moved in months. So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what the word knows is for, or two groups answering different questions under one heading, one about the closure of an information set and one about the cognitive reach of a finite reasoner? My working theory is that it's the competence/performance distinction over again, and I want to know whether that's a real diagnosis or just a way of renaming the problem. Take a case we kept fighting about — an agent who knows the axioms and rules of arithmetic but will never in the lifetime of the universe derive some particular true consequence — and tell me what each side is actually committed to saying about it, what kind of disagreement this really is, and what would count as progress given your diagnosis. Dont give me a survey of the fixes.
