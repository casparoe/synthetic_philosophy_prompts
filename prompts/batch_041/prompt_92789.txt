I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist is a scientist who studies consciousness. What draws me to her is a single methodological commitment she has adopted — the method usually called heterophenomenology, in which the scientist agrees to treat everything a subject says as a text to be interpreted rather than as a transparent window onto a private inner show. As a novelist I recognize the move at once: it is the stance I take toward my own characters, whose minds I never enter directly but only reconstruct from what they say and do. I want to understand the full range of positions a scientist — or a novelist playing at science — could hold on this question, and I would like you to build me a decision tree that sorts them.

Please construct a flowchart that begins from one starting commitment, namely that a science of consciousness should rest on evidence that can be shared and checked, and moves the reader through a series of branch nodes toward a named position at each leaf. Every node must be phrased as a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and every leaf should carry the name of the position, or the word unnamed if no one defends it, followed by one sentence naming that position's most awkward commitment.

Build the branches from the logical space rather than from a list of schools. I want the tree organized, as far as possible, around a question that matters to me as a writer: what kind of text is the subject's report? In my own notes I have been sorting the possibilities under four headings — the report as testimony, as symptom, as fiction, and as performance — and you may use those four as the first cut if it helps, but revise them if the logic demands it. A second family of branches should turn on who counts as the author of the report: the subject, the scientist, or the culture the two of them share.

The tree must be able to separate at least the following positions, which should appear as leaves: a disciplined first-person phenomenology that treats trained introspection as primary evidence; the heterophenomenological method that brackets the question of experience altogether; the view that first-person and third-person methods must constrain each other in a loop; the older introspectionist program that treats trained reports as raw data; the illusionist view that reports are the only real data and that experience is a trick of the mind; an enactivist or embodied view holding that neither reports nor brain data suffice without the lived body; and a deflationary view that the whole dispute is verbal. Add others if the space calls for them.

To force the distinctions apart, every leaf should be reachable by answering at least one concrete case. Please make the tree turn on cases like these: a blindsight subject who denies seeing anything yet guesses correctly above chance; a split-brain patient whose left hemisphere invents an explanation for a movement it did not initiate; a hypnotized subject who reports vivid experience on command; a subject who reports an experience the experimenter believes impossible; a perfect behavioral duplicate of an ordinary person; and a subject who reports nothing at all while behaving as if much were going on. Where two of your leaves cannot be told apart by any of these cases, say so explicitly rather than inventing a difference.

Mark any combinations of answers that no one occupies, and briefly diagnose each: is the position incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored? Keep the tree to somewhere between seven and eleven leaves. The reader I have in mind is a literate layperson, a fellow novelist in my workshop, so keep the prose spare and the labels plain, and name each position in ordinary language first, adding a technical name in parentheses only where a technical name is genuinely standard.

Do not supply citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I cannot look anything up, and the tree should stand on the distinctions themselves.
