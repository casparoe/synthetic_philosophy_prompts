I've been argueing with a friend about the replication crisis and I've ended up defending a view I'd like you to try to take apart. The view is this: the crisis is at bottom a problem of statistical inference, and the culprit is the frequentist machinery we all grew up on -- null hypothesis significance testing, the p<0.05 ritual, treating a continuous measure of evidence as a yes/no switch. Publication bias, p-hacking, the file drawer: all real, but I think they're symptoms the framework invites more than the disease itself. A field that did Bayesian model comparison with defensible priors and reported intervals and whole posteriors instead of stars would, I think, mostly stop producing the results that fail to replicate. So what would count against this? Not a lecture on Bayesian virtues, I want the actual defeaters, sorted by how much they should move me, fatal at one end and merely awkward at the other.

A few things I keep suspecting are problems, so tell me whether they land or whether I get to wave them off as not-real-Bayesianism. Same data, both paradigms: Bayes factors with vague priors often land in the same place as the p-value, so the switch might change the rhetoric and not the verdicts. Prior hacking as the mirror image of p-hacking, plus the ugly fact that with a big enough n a p of 0.03 can actually be evidence for the null under a reasonable prior -- so Bayes doesn't just tidy things up, it can reverse me. And the fields that have actually fixed their replication rates didn't switch paradigms at all; they pre-registered and got bigger samples and better designs, which makes the fix look like procedure rather than inference. There's also a version of my view I can't tell apart from the original: maybe the real sin is the significant/not-significant dichotomy and the misreading of p, not frequentism as such, and you can drop the ritual while staying fully frequentist -- report continuous evidence, no stars, pre-register. If that version is the true one, is my Bayes-flavored version just the same claim wearing a hat, and if so does anything about the crisis actually hinge on the paradigm at all?

Second thing I want: what the people who hold my view would themselves concede counts against it -- not the caricature a critic would put up, but what someone genuinely committed to it has to admit. Third: is there anything this view predicts that a rival doesn't, or is it the kind of claim that comes out true whatever the data does? Be straight with me if it's been insulated into unfalsifiability, where every bad result gets explained away as bad priors or not-really-Bayesian, and then tell me whether that's fatal for a view of this kind or just the ordinary cost of a methodological stance. To make it concrete: imagine a subfield goes fully Bayesian tomorrow, pre-registers everything, reports posteriors, and five years later replicates no better than the NHST crowd down the hall. Does that kill the view, wound it, or leave it standing? I'd definately trust that kind of counterfactual more than any philosophical argument, so tell me if I'm wrong to weight it that way. Write it as plain continuous prose please, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables -- just talk me through it like you would in an email.
