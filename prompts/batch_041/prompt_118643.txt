I'm a third-year philosophy student taking a seminar on consciousness this term, and we have just spent three weeks on illusionism — the view that phenomenal consciousness, as philosophers have traditionally conceived it, is a kind of user illusion: that introspection systematically misrepresents our own representational states as possessing phenomenal properties (private, intrinsic, ineffable feels), and that the explanatory task is therefore to account for why we are so convinced that there are such properties rather than to account for the properties themselves. Two of us have started meeting outside class to work out where the argument actually turns, and we keep generating the same two exchanges. A critic objects that the illusionist's position presupposes the very thing it denies: there can be no illusion of seeming, since for anything to seem any way at all just is for there to be phenomenal character, and a discovery about representation cannot make a headache stop hurting. The illusionist replies that this simply reinstates the misrepresentation as fact, that seeming is itself just another representation to be accounted for, and that the critic's appeal to first-person conviction treats as evidence precisely what is in question — whence the offer to explain the conviction away by way of the meta-problem, that is, by explaining why we judge that there is a hard problem at all. The critic finds that question-begging, since the judgement being explained is a judgement about a state which, on the critic's view, the illusionist has not shown to be illusory. Nobody in the room has moved in three weeks.

I want to flag the readings I have in mind, because I suspect the impasse partly depends on which of them is in play. Strong illusionism: there are no phenomenal properties at all, only representations as of them. Weak illusionism: there are phenomenal properties, but the illusion concerns their nature — they are not intrinsic, private, or ineffable, they are simply what a certain kind of access representation is like, and what is being denied is only a philosopher's technical posit. Critics likewise seem to divide between those who treat first-person seeming as an unassailable datum, something known by acquaintance and therefore not the sort of thing an empirical result could overturn, and those who argue empirically that illusionism is false because phenomenality demonstrably outruns cognitive access, with the Sperling-style partial-report results as the usual lever. My hypothesis — and I would like it assessed as a hypothesis rather than adopted — is that the stall is not one disagreement but the superimposition of at least two: a genuine and perhaps unresolvable dispute about whether there is any fact over and above the functional and representational facts, in which neither side can offer evidence the other must accept, and a largely verbal dispute about the word illusion, since in the perceptual case an illusion is a misrepresentation of a property that exists, whereas here an error theory is being run about a property said not to exist at all.

What I want from you is a diagnosis with reasons. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two parties answering different questions under a single name — one about the mechanism of introspection, the other about the epistemology of the first person? If a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work, identify it and say what result, experiment, or theorem would actually bear on it, including whether any empirical finding could in principle tell against the first-person datum. If the disagreement instead rests on starting intuitions that neither side can argue for, say so plainly and explain why further argument will not move it. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for reading suggestions; I want the structure of the disagreement, engaging the arguments as I have stated them (and correcting me where I have misstated them), together with a straight answer about what would count as progress here: a distinction, a change of question, a result, or nothing at all.
