For several years I have been arguing with a colleague who teaches a course on well-being, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether her objection to hedonism is a genuine difficulty or a piece of legerdemain. I should like a careful diagnosis, delivered as structured data rather than as an essay.

Her position is this. Hedonism, in its classical form, holds that pleasure is the only thing good in itself and that everything else is good only insofar as it conduces to pleasure. Her objection — she calls it the heterogeneity objection — is that the things we call pleasures have nothing in common. The pleasure of a hot bath after a long walk in the cold is a bodily warmth and slackening; the pleasure of finishing a proof one has struggled over is an intellectual click, a settling; the pleasure of hearing that a friend has recovered from an illness is a rush of relief. Look closely, she says, and you will find no single felt quality present in all three and absent from unpleasurable experience. Since there is no such quality, "pleasure" does not name one thing, and hedonism cannot be the doctrine that one thing is the only intrinsic good. What does unite the three, she says, is a structural fact: each is the satisfaction of a standing appetite — the body's demand for warmth, the mind's demand for resolution, the heart's demand for the friend's safety. She therefore proposes that we abandon the search for a single good and instead treat the satisfactions of distinct appetites as a plurality of goods, to be maximised in some weighted combination.

Suppose I grant her the phenomenal claim — that the three experiences share no felt quality. My question is the practical one that I take to follow from it. If there is no one pleasure, but only these separate satisfactions, how are the weights to be set? When I must choose between a life that satisfies bodily appetites abundantly and a life that satisfies intellectual ones, what exchange rate between the kinds should I use, and what determines it — her position, the nature of appetite, or nothing at all? I want to know whether there is a principled answer, and if the weights can only be stipulated, what that costs her.
