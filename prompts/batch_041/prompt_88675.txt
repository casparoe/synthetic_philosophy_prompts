I am trying to determine whether the following pattern has an established name. In debates over the allocation of authority among levels of government, one frequently encounters a particular justificatory structure. A decision is said to belong to the lowest level that can adequately handle it. When the adequacy of the lower level is specified, however, it turns out to depend on capacities—fiscal, administrative, legal, and legitimating—that the lower level possesses only because it has already been entrusted with the relevant decision, or with decisions of that kind. The higher level's own prior action, or the mere fact of centralization, is thus part of what makes the lower level inadequate or adequate. The result is that the subsidiarity test can be satisfied or failed depending on the baseline of authority one assumes, and the test itself cannot supply that baseline. I see this in arguments about EU competence, German Länder authority, Swiss cantonal residual powers, and US state-versus-federal regulation. It seems distinct from ordinary circular reasoning, because the capacities are genuinely generated by prior allocation; and distinct from path dependence, because the point is normative—the lower level's would-be competence is treated as a reason to grant it competence. Is there an established concept for this? If there is not, I would like a term and a definition that distinguishes it from subsidiarity, federalism, path dependence, and institutional capacity, and that says where the phenomenon stops. A bare name would not be useful; what I need is a precise account of what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries lie.
