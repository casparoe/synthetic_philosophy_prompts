I'm putting together the final exam for my decision theory seminar (mixed MA/PhD, they've had the standard expected utility material plus a bit of the St. Petersburg literature) and I want one essay question on the Pasadena game that a student can't answer just by having skimmed the usual "infinite expectation" stuff. The thing I keep noticing when I mark practice answers is that people lump it in with St. Petersburg and write a paragraph about diminishing marginal utility, which is exactly the reflex I want the question to defeat. So the question should force them to actually work with the payoff structure and the series, not just name-drop log utility.

Concretely: the game is a fair coin tossed until the first heads, and if heads lands on toss n the payoff is (-1)^(n+1) times 2^n over n. Please write (a) the essay question itself, (b) a model answer of about 600 words, and (c) a marking scheme that names the specific moves a top answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. In the marking scheme I want the A/B boundary to be something I can point at -- not "shows deep understanding" but "does X, Y, Z; if they only do X and Y it's a B." Five must-hit points, 20 marks total, and for each point a one-line note on how candidates typically get it wrong. Keep the model answer self-contained enough that a strong student who hasn't read the original paper could still have produced it.

Two things to please build in rather than leave optional, because they're where I think the real crux is: first, the sense in which the expectation is undefined rather than merely infinite, and why the natural ordering of the outcomes isn't a privilege we're entitled to; second, the move where you add a dollar to every payoff (so the modified game dominates the original outcome-by-outcome) and ask what that does to the claim that the modified game must be worth more. Don't let either of those be something a student can skip past with a hand-wave. Oh and the tone of the question should be a real exam question -- no "critically discuss," give them something with teeth.
