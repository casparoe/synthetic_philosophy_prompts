When a house changes hands, everyone involved quietly relies on a distinction that is almost never spelled out. The wall oven, the dishwasher screwed to the cabinets, the shelves bolted to the studs, the fence posts set in concrete: these are treated as part of the house, and the seller may not take them along. The seller's freestanding range, the microwave that plugs into an outlet, the bookcase leaning against the wall: these are the seller's own goods and leave with the moving truck. Lenders, appraisers, insurers, and tax assessors all lean on roughly the same test, which asks how firmly a thing is fastened and, more importantly, what it was fastened for. A thing fixed in place to serve the building belongs to the building; a thing fixed in place to serve itself does not. Then there is the wrinkle that shelves bolted to the wall of a leased storefront count as the tenant's personal property, since they were installed for the tenant's business rather than for the premises, so the same bolts can make a thing part of a building in one setting and a separate object in another.

Set beside that the way the same institutions handle time. A house a century old, with roof, siding, windows, wiring, plumbing, and interior walls all replaced, nothing original left but the frame, is still the same house: one parcel, one deed, one policy, one address, taxed and sold as a single thing. But let the house burn, and the code official asks how much of it was destroyed. In many jurisdictions, past a threshold, often half the structure's value, the lot's old protections lapse and whatever is built must satisfy the current code, as though the structure standing there were a new building rather than the one that was grandfathered in.

I would like to know what this pair of practices presupposes about when a collection of parts adds up to one thing. Does the attachment test commit anyone to the view that there is a fact of the matter about composition, independent of our interests and our legal conventions, or does it commit them only to the weaker view that a legal system has to draw a line somewhere and should draw it where people can predict it? The two halves of the practice look inconsistent, since a thing's identity survives the replacement of every part but not the sudden loss of half of it. Are these answers to two different questions, one about when parts compose a whole and one about when a whole persists through change, or are they one question answered twice and answered badly? If they are two questions, do the two criteria actually fit together?

Tell me which rival views the practice rules out. Could someone who holds that nothing is ever composed of anything, and that there are only particles arranged house-wise, run this practice unchanged? Could someone who holds that any collection of things whatsoever composes something? What about someone who holds that composition is fixed by our categories rather than by the world's joints? Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and tell me where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license everything the practice actually does. The trade-fixture wrinkle seems important here, since it suggests the operative criterion is not physical attachment at all but purpose, and purpose can shift with who is asking.

Finally, suppose we replaced the presupposition with the thought that composition is a matter of our concepts rather than of the world. Which parts of real estate, insurance, and code enforcement would have to change, and which would carry on exactly as before?

I would rather be told that my puzzle is confused than handed a smooth reconciliation. If the two halves of the practice are answering unrelated questions and nothing at all follows about composition, say so plainly. Flag the places where you are least confident, and tell me which of my assumptions you think is doing the most damage.
