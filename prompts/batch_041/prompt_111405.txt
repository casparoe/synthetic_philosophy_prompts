Interpretability researchers often claim that a trained network encodes a feature as a direction in activation space: when a linear probe or an activation difference recovers some property, the network is said to represent that property. The natural objection is that any invertible change of basis leaves every prediction the network makes untouched, and with enough dimensions a linear readout can be fitted to almost any labeling, so the feature directions can look like artifacts of our coordinate choice rather than discoveries. Give me your credence that the realist reading is correct — that for a given trained network there is a fact of the matter, independent of the basis we choose and of which probe we train, about which directions are the ones the network represents. Decompose that credence into the probability that representation can be given a basis-independent criterion at all, the probability that actual trained networks satisfy such a criterion if it exists, the probability that superposition and sparse coding genuinely privilege a basis rather than merely picking out a convenient one, and the probability that the downstream readout, rather than the activations alone, is what settles which description is right. Then combine the terms, say which one your number is most sensitive to, and say what result would move it most.
