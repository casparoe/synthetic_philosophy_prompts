I'm a science-and-ideas journalist — I write about AI, philosophy of science, the general "okay but what does this result actually mean" beat — and once a week in the spring I moonlight teaching a six-week evening course at the extension school called "Prediction and Its Discontents." The students are working reporters, two policy people, a couple of engineers, and a retired actuary who corrects me about alot of things. Nobody in the room can do measure theory and I don't want them to; what I want is for them to think clearly about what a theorem buys you and what it doesn't. The final is a take-home: one long essay, around 900 words, a week to write it, 20 marks. And no citations, no reading lists, no "further reading" — half of them have no library access and I don't care who said it first, I care whether they can reason.

The topic is the no-free-lunch theorems (Wolpert, I think, if I'm remembering the name right — doesn't matter) plus Hume's problem of induction, and I've rewritten this question four times because every draft lets them off with the answer I've now heard about forty times: "no free lunch says every learning algorithm is equally good on average across all possible worlds, so you always need some inductive bias, i.e. assumptions." True-ish, and worthless, because it treats "bias" as the solution when the whole question is what makes one bias defensible and another one just lucky. What I actually want them to confront is that the theorem is an accounting result: it's about an average over a class of worlds that's symmetric under reshuffling which situation gets which outcome, and that symmetry is doing every bit of the work. It says nothing about a learner facing one fixed world, and it's silent on justification — it hands you a conserved average, not an entitlement to the asymmetry you're betting on. Hume, meanwhile, isn't asking about average success rates at all. So the essay has to take a stand: is the no-free-lunch result a formalization of Hume's problem, an answer to a different question, or a distraction from it? To force them off the summary I've told them they have to run the whole argument through a toy case I use in week three, which I'll spell out here so you can bake it into the question: two forecasters watch the same sequence of bits. M just predicts "same as last time." S predicts "flip the last bit." Over the class of all binary sequences the two are exactly equally accurate on average — that's the no-free-lunch fact in miniature. But restrict to sticky sequences and M runs away with it; on alternating sequences S does. I want the essay to say precisely what that does and doesn't show, and to answer the heckle from the back row — "look, S wins on the alternating worlds, so there *is* a free lunch" — by being explicit about the measure you'd need for that to be anything more than luck.

Give me (a) the question exactly as it would appear on the exam sheet, (b) a model answer of about 700 words, and (c) a marking scheme worth 20 marks built on five must-hit points, each with its marks and a one-line note on how candidates usually blow it. Don't hedge on the central issue — I want the model answer to commit to a reading of what the theorem is actually doing and to let that reading drive the marks; if you think a common way of citing no-free-lunch in the AI-and-induction discourse is confused, say so and give your reasons rather than laying the options out and leaving me to pick.
