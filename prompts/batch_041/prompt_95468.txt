I teach the infinity session of a second-year philosophy of mathematics course — eighteen students, one ninety-minute meeting — and I would like the whole session to be a single activity rather than a lecture. The idea I want them to feel rather than hear is that a sequence of perfectly determinate steps can leave the end of the process undetermined. The machine I shall put to them is a lamp that is off at t = 0 and is switched at t = 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, and so on, so that its state is fixed at every time before t = 1 while nothing in the description fixes its state at t = 1. Design an activity in which the students generate the sequence themselves, commit in public — with something staked on being right — to an answer about the end, and then discover that the description they were given cannot settle their stakes. Give me the rules, the materials (assume a whiteboard and photocopiable cards), a minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the difference between the determined intermediate states and the undetermined limit, and onto the thought that a determinate final state would have to be the work of a last step, of which there is none. Include, staged rather than announced, the moment where they see that two complete specifications agreeing at every time before t = 1 may disagree at t = 1, with nothing observable to discriminate between them; and include a contrasting machine whose infinite sequence does have a limit, so that they see the failure is not about infinity as such. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I shall have to supply myself. Finally, separate what is settled among those who work on supertasks from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested verdict — for instance that the question is ill-formed, or that the lamp is on, or that it is off — as though it were the consensus. Please answer in British English.
