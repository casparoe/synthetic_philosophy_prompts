I've been reading around the boundary problem in democratic theory — the question of who belongs to the demos — for a couple of years now, and I've come to think of it as one of those debates in which every move is public, everyone has heard the replies, and yet the camps never move. I'd like you to diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicate it.

Here is how the two sides look from where I sit. On one side are those who treat the boundary problem as a genuine and possibly intractable paradox. Any decision about who is to be included in the demos must be made by some demos; if the demos is already bounded, the decision merely ratifies an arbitrary prior exclusion, and if it is not yet bounded, there is no body with the standing to make the decision. On this view every boundary is either undemocratic at its origin or circular, and no procedure can bootstrap a people into existence legitimately. On the other side are those who think the paradox is a pseudo-problem: it rests, they say, on a picture of a demos as a pre-political set of persons who then adopt a rule, whereas in fact a demos is constituted and reconstituted through ongoing practices of claim-making, contestation and mutual recognition, so the real question is never how the first boundary was licensed but how present boundaries are contested and revised. A third group proposes criteria — all whose interests are affected, or all who are subject to the body's coercive authority — and is met with the objection that any such criterion already presupposes the very boundary it was meant to fix.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the two main camps are not disagreeing about democracy's procedure at all but about the metaphysics of political collectives: whether there is any pre-institutional people for a boundary to be true or false of. I also suspect that much of the standoff turns on an ambiguity between a temporal reading of the circularity (which came first, the demos or the decision rule?) and a normative reading of it (what could give a demos the authority to bind?), and that some participants are covertly committed to a pre-political people while denying it.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, two camps answering different questions under a single heading, or a sign that the question is ill-posed? If a hidden empirical or formal question is doing the work, say which. Test your diagnosis against hard cases in which there is no inherited demos to appeal to — a secession, a charter for a newly founded city, an authority governing a shared river basin or the atmosphere — and tell me whether the camps' positions actually diverge there or merely re-describe the same facts in different vocabularies. Then say what, if anything, would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that none should be expected.

Work as a political philosopher at home in democratic theory and in the methodology of philosophical disagreement. Think carefully before committing; begin by restating each position in your own words so that I can see you have the debate in view, then diagnose. Keep the prose clean and the reasoning explicit, and flag where your own diagnosis is contestable. End with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing a label for the kind of disagreement and a short phrase naming what would count as progress.
