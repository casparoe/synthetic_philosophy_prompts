Every Febuary 2nd I get handed the same assignment, the Groundhog Day-is-secretly-philosophy piece, and every year it comes back as the Nietzsche thing: eternal recurrence, amor fati, love your fate. Before I file this one I want to try a different reading, the Bill Murray movie (Ramis, 1993) through Sartre, radical freedom and bad faith, Being and Nothingness rather than Zarathustra, and I want it to survive contact with the film rather than be a list of cute parallels. A few things I keep bumping into. Phil is stuck in a day he can't leave and can't change, and eventually knows it down to the second, which is either the cleanest thought experiment anyone has built for Sartre's claim that freedom isn't the power to change your situation, only the freedom to choose what it means, or a flat counterexample to it, depending on how you read the ending. The stretch where he decides he's basically a god because nothing has consequences: is that bad faith, or is it the closest the film ever comes to being honest about freedom? Sartre has the waiter who plays at being a waiter, but he also has people who flee the other way, into pure possibility, and I suspect Phil's god phase is the second kind, so tell me if I'm right about that. The Rita seduction run, where he rehearses her into falling for him and it fails every single time, feels like a dramatization of Sartre on why you can't get hold of someone's freedom by rigging it, and I want to know if that's a real argument in the film or just a romcom beat that happens to sound like one. Same with the homeless man who dies no matter what Phil does, night after night, which I read as Sartre on death as the point where the other person takes your possibilities out of your hands. And the ending is the part I'd actually be writing about: if he finally wakes up to the 3rd by turning into a decent man, has the movie quietly given up on Sartre and gone back to freedom-means-getting-what-you-want, or is there a darker reading where the ending is bad faith of the deepest kind, a guy who has remade himself into exactly the object the woman he loves can finally accept? Be honest where the film doesnt fit. If a scene won't sit in the Sartrean frame, say so instead of forcing it, and tell me what the movie does that the philosophy can't do on the page. No cites or reading list needed, this is for a general audience.
