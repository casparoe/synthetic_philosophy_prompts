I spent most of last night arguing with an old friend — she's a moral philosopher finishing a dissertation on the doing/allowing distinction, and she also sits on our hospital's ethics committee — and I came away unable to state the rule she is actually running. Her verdicts, as well as I can reconstruct them: a physician who gives a competent, suffering patient a lethal injection is killing her, and my friend insists this remains a killing even if it were the right thing to do; a physician who disconnects a ventilator at the request of a competent patient is letting her die, not killing her, even though the patient dies precisely because the machine stops; a physician who declines to start a new antibiotic for a patient who has refused it is letting him die; a bystander who fails to toss a life ring to a drowning swimmer is letting him die, and is doing something wrong if the ring was at his feet; a man who shoves a stranger off a bridge is killing him. What unsettles me is that these verdicts leave her underdetermined. They are jointly consistent with at least three rules: (A) killing is death from a positive bodily act, letting die is death from any omission, whatever the causal facts; (B) killing is death from a causal process the agent started or kept running, letting die is death from a process that would have run its course without her; (C) what matters is only whether death was aimed at or merely foreseen.

Here is the case I want you to predict her verdict on, because I think it splits those rules apart. A woman assembles and programs a device wired into a rival's insulin pump: at midnight it will deliver a fatal dose, unless she personally cancels it each evening. For a week she cancels; then, having decided he should die, she goes to bed and lets the clock run out. At the critical moment she does nothing — the machine administers the dose, if you like — but the machine is hers, and only her nightly cancellation had been keeping him alive. Does my friend call this killing or letting die? Tell me which of the three rules her earlier verdicts actually underwrite, what each rule predicts about this case, and your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons. Then name the single further case that would most cleanly separate the rules she is really running, and say why that one rather than the obvious variations.

Please write in American English. Tell me how confident you are in the prediction and in whichever rule you settle on, and single out the point you are least sure about — especially if you suspect her earlier verdicts do not fix any one rule and the prediction is a coin flip dressed up as an inference.
