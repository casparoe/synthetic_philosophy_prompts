I serve on the drug-policy advisory commission of a mid-sized state, and a week from Thursday we vote on the Civil Citation and Referral Act. I've been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance. I read rationalist blogs and forums more or less daily and have for fifteen years, so I would rather have a memo that names its crux and its weakest joint than one that hedges every sentence into uselessness.

The draft, as it will be moved: possession of up to one gram of any controlled substance other than cannabis, or up to twenty-eight grams of cannabis, ceases to be a crime and becomes a civil citation with a fine capped at $100, waivable in full on completion of a screening appointment. So far, ordinary decriminalization. The rest is where it gets strange.

1. Every citation is referred to a county Assessment Panel — a clinician, a sheriff's liaison, and a community member — which may order up to ninety days of supervised treatment. Refusing the order, or failing out of it, is a misdemeanor punishable by up to thirty days in jail.

2. Possession of any amount of a controlled substance together with more than $300 in cash creates a rebuttable presumption of intent to distribute, which remains a felony carrying the existing mandatory minimum.

3. Counties may opt out by referendum for four years, in which case the old criminal penalties apply within their borders.

4. The program sunsets in five years, and its treatment funding comes from a 40 percent reallocation of the corrections budget — so if citations displace arrests, the money that pays for the panels shrinks along with the arrests.

Our mandate is statutory and narrow: reduce overdose mortality and reduce racial disparity in enforcement. One commissioner argues this means we may not weigh anything else — not the wrongness of punishing self-regarding conduct, not the legitimacy costs of a two-tier map, not the fact that the panel converts a citation into coerced treatment. He says those are questions for the legislature, and that a commission which strays outside its charge forfeits the authority to be listened to. I think he is at least half right, and I want that argument treated as strong rather than waved away.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should end in a clear recommendation — adopt as written, reject, or adopt with amendments — and if amendments, state them in language I can move verbatim, with a time frame for implementation. Work from the provisions above and from first principles. You have no research staff, so do not reach for studies or statistics I cannot check, and do not cite literature. Argue.

I want the strongest case on the losing side stated at full strength, not as a foil. I want the memo to separate the parts of the objection that concern consequences from the parts that concern the state's standing to punish at all, and to say which of those this commission can properly decide.

Then tell me the crux. Name the single factual or normative claim that, if it went the other way, would flip your recommendation, and say where your reasoning is likeliest to fail — including which of the four provisions you are least sure about, and why. If the honest answer is that one of these provisions makes the whole scheme worse than the status quo, say that instead.
