Write me a parody of a scientific journal's internal editorial style guide — the dry, numbered-rules document that normally tells you when to hyphenate "state-of-the-art" — except this journal's big new section is about teleological language. The policy is that you're allowed to write "the heart pumps to circulate blood," but only if you either name a reasoner or file the sentence under the new Free-Floating Rationale Exemption, and the guide has to spell out the paperwork for that. The funny should come from getting the content right, not from inventing nonsense: the difference between a rationale being there and anyone representing it, why "to circulate blood" passes but "wants to circulate blood" doesn't, whether natural selection counts as a reasoner for these purposes, what the form looks like when an author claims the process itself "had reasons" with no mind anywhere in the building, and the running headache of a rule that keeps having to distinguish a function from a purpose from a goal from a "just-so" story. Around 600 words, formatted as numbered style rules with acceptable and unacceptable example sentences. Keep it totally straight-faced — nobody in the document should act like they know it's a joke — but a reader who's thought about Dennett-style free-floating rationales should feel like the author gets the view and is teasing it fairly, not strawmanning it. No citations or reading lists, obviously.
