I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have run into the phrase 'beyond the call of duty' twice in the past month in ways that seem to pull in opposite directions.

The first time was in a post-incident review. An engineer had spent a weekend tracing a data-corruption bug through an old service, and the write-up described the work as having gone beyond the call of duty. The phrase seemed to be doing two things at once: praising the work as unusually good and unusually costly, and quietly filing it somewhere outside the range of things the team could be asked to do, as though the very fact that it deserved praise placed it beyond requirement.

The second time was in a discussion of whether every on-call engineer should be required to write a short public postmortem after an incident. A manager objected that we obviously could not require that; the best engineers did it, but it would be beyond the call of duty to insist. There the phrase was doing work in an argument, converting a proposal about what the team owes into an impossibility. My own suspicion is that the phrase is mostly a way of praising without paying, but I do not trust that suspicion, since I cannot say what precise claim it would be denying.

I would like a menu of interpretations rather than a single verdict.

Please respond in the following sections, in this order.

1. "The readings" — the bulk of the answer, roughly 700 to 900 words. Enumerate the distinct precise claims the phrase could be making, stated as claims one could agree or disagree with, in ordinary language. I would rather have too many than too few; include the deflationary ones. At a minimum, separate: what fixes the baseline that the act is supposed to be beyond (a job description, a professional role, what a reasonable person could demand, a general moral requirement, or the example set by the best practitioners); whether the phrase denies that the act is required, denies only that it may be enforced or demanded, or denies neither and instead says something about the character of the person who does it; whether the line is the same for everyone or shifts with a person's abilities, costs, and opportunities; whether omitting the act is blameworthy, merely disappointing, or nothing at all; whether the act is claimed to be morally better than what is required or only costlier; and whether the phrase presupposes that the baseline is stable, given that praising an act once tends to turn it into the new expectation. Flag where two readings are incompatible with each other.

2. "Verdicts" — roughly 300 words. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and which readings are merely restatements of one another. Then answer directly: is there a reading on which the phrase can both praise an act and deny that it could be required, without contradiction? If not, say exactly where the contradiction bites.

3. "What each occasion needed" — roughly 250 words. Say which reading the post-incident praise plausibly expresses and which reading the manager's objection needs. Does the manager's conclusion — that we cannot require it — follow from any reading of the phrase, or does it require a separate premise about enforcement, blame, or what it is reasonable to demand of an employee?

4. "Bottom line for the postmortem proposal" — roughly 150 words. If the phrase is doing no real work in that argument, say so plainly. If it is doing work, name the claim I would have to concede for the manager's argument to go through, and tell me what I should ask instead.

Introduce and explain any technical vocabulary you use, since I do not know the terminology of this area, and do not give me reading recommendations or citations — I want the distinctions themselves.
