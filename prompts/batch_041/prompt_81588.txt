Bit of an odd one. I'm a novelist by trade, but I've somehow ended up on the council of the writers' centre I've been a member of for donkey's years (they roped me in the year my fourth book did unexpectedly well and I've never worked out how to resign gracefully). We run an annual prize for a first book of non-fiction, and we've got a proper row on our hands that I have to put into writing before the comittee meets on the 14th.

The shortlisted book is a travel-ish memoir by a young writer who spent two years in a country I'd better not name here. It has a long chapter that generalises, very confidently, about the people there: they are like this, they can't do that, they don't really believe in the other. Two of the judges have said they'll walk off the panel if we give it the prize, and about forty members have written in asking us to pull it from the shortlist altogether. The publisher says this is a straightforward case of a small group trying to police what can be written, points out that the chapter is affectionate rather than hostile, and says the author has every right to report what they saw. The author said something in an interview that I found genuinely affecting: that they spent two years trying to understand a place and this is what they came back with, and that we are asking them to pretend they noticed nothing.

The awkward thing is that I'm not sure I disagree with the generalisations. Some are probably statistically true of that country, or at least of the slice of it the author was living in. And I do something similar for a living. You can't write a novel in which every character is a deviation from the mean; the moment you write a family, a village, a trade, you're trafficking in types. So I want the opinion to face that squarely instead of just waving the word stereotype at it.

Here's the line I want to take, and I'd like you to test it hard and then write it up properly. Split generalisations about a people into two sorts. First, claims about circumstances: institutions, laws, histories, prices, what's available and what isn't. Those can be true, useful and actionable. Second, claims about dispositions: what the people are like on the inside, their character, their souls if you like. Those purport to explain an individual by their category, and the trouble with them isn't only that they're often false but that they're built so they can't be disconfirmed, because every exception gets redescribed as an exception that proves the rule. Then the part I really want the memo to get right: even where a dispositional claim is roughly accurate, the same sentence does different work in different mouths. In the author's chapter it may be a hard-won observation; on a leaflet at a rally it may be a threat. So whether we may say a thing and whether the thing is true come apart, and I need a principled way of telling when a generalisation about a people is doing acceptable work inside a book. I suspect the feedback loop matters more than the truth of it — claims like these get picked up by employers, landlords and immigration officers, and then start making themselves true — so please say something about that.

And don't let us off the hook on the resignations. I need to know what to recommend doing about the two judges who've threatened to walk, and I need an honest treatment of the strongest case for the publisher's side, because I think it's stronger than most of my colleagues want to admit.

So: a short written opinion I can circulate before the meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action — give it the prize, keep it on the shortlist and say nothing, keep it on the shortlist with a statement from the council, or pull it — with a time frame, and with the actual wording of any statement you want us to issue. It has to be something we can vote on, not an essay about the topic in general, and it should engage the author's position as though they were in the room. British English throughout, please, the whole thing goes out under the centre's name and our director is the sort of woman who notices.
