A colleague brought this argument to our reading group, and none of us could say where it breaks. (1) If time has a direction as a feature of the world, then some fundamental law must underwrite it; if nothing in the basic dynamics distinguishes earlier from later, nothing does. (2) But the fundamental dynamical laws are time-reversal invariant: every lawful history is matched by its time-reverse. (3) So no fundamental law distinguishes earlier from later. (4) Hence the direction of time, if it is real, must come not from laws but from boundary conditions — from the special way matter happens to be arranged. (5) But a boundary condition is a fact about the state of the world at some particular time. (6) To specify a state as holding at a particular time is already to deploy the very temporal ordering whose direction is in question. (7) So boundary conditions presuppose the direction they were meant to explain. (8) Therefore the direction of time has no physical ground at all. (9) Yet we plainly experience a direction — memory, deliberation, and causation all run one way, and experience is itself a physical process. (10) So the direction of time must be physical and cannot be physical.

If I have to choose, I would keep (9) over (1); what I cannot accept is the contradiction in (10). Tell me which step to give up, why that step looked compelling in the first place, and what giving it up costs — in particular, whether anything survives of the thought that thermodynamics can explain the arrow, and whether the same argument, unamended, would also indict any appeal to an initial low-entropy condition.
