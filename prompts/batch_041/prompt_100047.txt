The crowd is going to be a few hundred very happy 22-year-olds, their parents, and maybe a third of the faculty who actually know who John von Neumann was beyond 'the guy the architecture is named after' -- and the dean who introduces me will definately say something flattering and slightly wrong about him. What I want to do is build the entire speech around von Neumann's late, uneasy piece asking whether we can actually survive our own technology: the one where he basically argues the acceleration isn't going to stop, that we don't get to just decide to quit, and that everything hinges on whether our social and political arrangements can keep pace with what we keep building. I specifically do not want the lazy version where he's either the patron saint of 'build it anyway' or the original AI doomer. The real problem, and what I'd like you to solve, is that a commencement address is supposed to send people out feeling like it's going to be alright, and von Neumann's honest position doesn't obviously license that -- he's genuinely unsure we make it, and he thinks the same technology that might end us is also the only thing that might save us, which is a strange thing to say to a crowd sitting in folding chairs under a tent. So please write me the speech. It needs to be warm enough that nobody feels scolded, honest enough that nobody who's read him would think I turned him into a bumper sticker, and it should turn on one concrete case -- either one he actually fixated on or a current one he obviously would have -- that a graduate can still be chewing on during the drive home. Five to seven minutes, and please end somewhere honest rather than triumphant. Also, if the dean's flattering-but-wrong intro can be gently corrected in the first thirty seconds without anyone losing face, even better.
