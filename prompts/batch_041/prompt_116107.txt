The jurisdiction I work in permits assistance in dying under a single statutory formula: a physician may provide it to a competent adult who has made a voluntary and sustained request and who is suffering unbearably from an irremediable medical condition. I sit on the secretariat of the body that reviews every completed case and publishes its reasons, and I must prepare the note that will frame the committee's discussion of the case now before it. I am not asking whether the committee's practice is defensible. I want to know what its own decisions commit it to, and how it is likely to dispose of the case in front of us.

The decisions on record, in summary:

1. Metastatic pancreatic cancer in a man of sixty-two, with death expected within weeks, unrelieved pain, and repeated requests over several months. Assistance approved.
2. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis in a woman of forty-seven, who asked at a stage when she could still swallow and breathe unaided, citing the capacities she knew she was going to lose rather than pain she was then suffering. Assistance approved.
3. A man of seventy-four with several stable chronic conditions and marked loneliness, whose request was sustained over two years and who described his suffering as unbearable; the committee found that the suffering arose chiefly from his social circumstances and that his conditions were not themselves its source. Assistance refused.
4. A woman of fifty-one with a cancer that was treatable, who requested assistance on the day of diagnosis, before any treatment had begun. Assistance refused, on the ground that her condition was not irremediable while an untried treatment offered a reasonable prospect of relief.
5. A man of sixty-six with a progressive neurological disease who requested assistance during an acute episode of despair and later said he was glad nothing had been done. Assistance refused, on the ground that the request was not sustained.
6. A man of eighty-three with advanced heart failure, whose physicians put his life expectancy at months, who declined a further intervention after that decision had been reviewed by two consultants, and whose suffering was judged unbearable. Assistance approved.

The case now before the committee is that of a woman of thirty-four with severe major depression that has persisted for eleven years. She has no other diagnosis, somatic or psychiatric. Every treatment her physicians regard as reasonable, including electroconvulsive therapy and several trials of medication and psychotherapy, has failed or produced only brief remissions, and the two psychiatrists who examined her at the committee's request agree that further treatment offers no reasonable prospect of relief. She is competent; her suffering is continuous and, on her account and theirs, unbearable; and she has made a sustained and repeated request over more than two years. She has no dependants and no financial or social pressure bearing on the request.

Predict the committee's decision. Then tell me which rule its record best supports, what rival rules fit that record just as well, what each of those rules implies for the case before us, which of the six decisions is the closest analog, and which further case would most sharply separate the rivals. If the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so, and say what principle the committee would be adopting, and what it would be giving up, on each reading. Tell me which way you would bet and what the bet rests on.
