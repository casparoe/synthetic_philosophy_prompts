I'm a TA for a second-year philosophy of science unit, and the lecturer has asked me to put together a quiz for the first tutorial: something like a "which interpretation of probability do you actually hold?" thing, the kind that sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they happen to like. Could you write it for me? Eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case or claim with two or three answer options, and then a scoring key at the end that maps answer patterns onto the positions. The one thing I really want to avoid is it turning into a vocabulary test -- no question should just ask "do you think probability is subjective?" or list position names in the options, and ideally someone could take the whole quiz without having heard of half the labels and still land in the right place. Each question should be a case where the views actually come apart.

On which positions need to come out the other end: actual-frequency frequentism (probability just is the relative frequency in the observed or finite class), hypothetical/long-run frequentism (von Mises-style collectives), single-case propensity, personalist subjectivism with exchangeability (de Finetti-ish, where chances are just features of a coherent credence function), objective Bayesian / logical probability, Humean best-system chance (chances are the chances of the best systematisation of the actual history, so they can exist even in a deterministic world), and a deflationary option where there are no chances at all, only credences plus a norm for how they should respond to evidence. If you think there's an obvious eighth or ninth that my list is missing, add it and say why. I'd also like at least one leaf for a view I keep half-defending to my friends but have never seen named: that chance talk is real but always relative to a chosen reference class, and there is simply no fact of the matter about which reference class is the right one. Use your own name for it if it doesn't have one.

Some cases I'd like to see used, since the whole point is to be in the weeds: a coin that was minted, is biased, but will never be flipped (or flipped exactly once, ever); a radioactive atom that in fact decayed, where the question is what its probability of decaying was before it did; the probability that a particular raven is black when you already know its DNA; the probability that a randomly chosen natural number is prime (the limiting frequency is zero, which makes some answers look silly); the chance that a candidate would have won an election that was in fact never held; and a case where the reference class is genuinely ambiguous, like a given 45-year-old smoker's chance of getting lung cancer. At least one question should probe whether chance and credence can come apart at all -- i.e. whether knowing the chance is 0.3 commits you to a credence of 0.3 -- and at least one should turn on whether chance requires indeterminism or is compatible with a deterministic world, because that's what half the essays in this unit end up being about. Please flag any pairs of positions your quiz can't really separate, and note any combinations of answers that no one seems to occupy, with a sentence on whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
