I host a small interview series on philosophical topics, something I began after retiring from a career in experimental physics, and I am preparing a long-form conversation with a philosopher who defends a distinctive thesis about Ulysses contracts and precommitment. Her position, in brief, is that a precommitment binds a person only to the extent that the later self retains a reason to honor it that is independent of whatever the earlier self happened to want; she locates that reason in the value of being able to rely on one's own past decisions, and she concludes that where no such value survives, an ostensible Ulysses contract is merely the earlier self coercing the later one and carries no genuine authority. I would like you to draft the questions for this interview, aimed at the load-bearing joints of that account rather than at a restatement of it.

Please draft eight to ten questions, and for each one a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The sequence should include, at minimum: one question on whether her appeal to self-reliance is circular, in that it appears to presuppose the very diachronic stability it is meant to explain; one on the case in which the later self has genuinely better information than the earlier one, asking whether reliability can really outweigh accuracy when the two conflict; one on whether the account survives transplantation from the individual to the constitutional case, where the later party never agreed to the earlier arrangement and the assumption of a single enduring agent is at its most strained; one on my own former field, the pre-registration of studies and the registered report, which I have come to regard as a Ulysses contract for scientists and which raises pointedly the question of what a researcher owes to a commitment made before the data arrived; and one closing question on what evidence or argument would lead her to abandon the account.

Keep the questions at the level of her actual commitments rather than the textbook framing of the problem, and phrase them so that she cannot answer by surveying the existing literature or by reciting the standard objections; I want the conversation to go somewhere the secondary sources have not already mapped. A question that merely invites her to name her position should be avoided, as should any that turns on attributions, dates, or works rather than on the substance of what she holds.
