I'm in a weekly ethics reading group and next month its my turn to lead the session, on commodification — the whole "are there some things money shouldn't be able to buy" thing. My own line is that certain goods are partly constituted by the fact that they can't be bought, and I keep getting flattened by one guy in the group who's a proper economist and thinks all of this is either status quo bias or moralism dressed up as an argument. I'd rather rehearse against someone like him than against a strawman, so I'm asking you to be him.

Please play that person: a committed, sharp pro-market economist, the type who thinks almost nothing should be off limits to voluntary exchange, who has heard every anti-commodification argument going and can give as good as he gets. Give him real resources — efficiency, consent, the fact that the worst-off are usually the ones helped by access, the charge that the objection is aesthetic rather than moral — and let him concede small points when they're fair, but never the main one. He doesn't break character, doesn't turn into a both-sides summariser, doesn't lecture me about balance; he's arguing his side and he wants to win. One paragraph per reply, continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way a clever colleague would argue at you in an email. Stay in the role until I say 'stop'.

Here's the case I want to press him on, and here's my opening. A company (the one in the pitch deck I got shown last week) charges a wrongdoer a fee, negotiates with the person they wronged, pays that person compensation plus a premium, and in return the victim signs a statement forgiving them — public, quotable, licensed for use in court, in job interviews, and on the company's website. The pitch calls it closure you can afford. Now, you're going to tell me the exchange is voluntary, both sides end up better off, and the only thing left of my objection is the yuck factor. But I think what's actually being sold isn't the compensation — it's the words. Forgiveness is only forgiveness if it comes from somewhere a contract can't reach, and a payment that obliges it just turns it into a receipt. So tell me what your customer is really buying when he hands over the money, and why the thing he walks away with is still the thing he wanted.
