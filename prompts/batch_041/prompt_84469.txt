I am a control and estimation engineer. I design state estimators and model-predictive controllers for a living, and I am fluent in the standard apparatus: state-space models, the Kalman filter and its nonlinear variants, variational Bayes and the evidence lower bound, the Laplace approximation, linear-quadratic optimal control, rate-distortion trade-offs, and the dual-control literature on exploration versus exploitation. I have absorbed enough popular neuroscience to know that the free energy principle and predictive processing are advertised as unifying perception, action, learning, and perhaps life itself, but I have never worked through the formal core, and I am suspicious of it. What I want from you is a systematic translation of that framework into my vocabulary, as faithful as the translation can be made, followed by an honest residue report. Do not round the claims to the nearest thing I already believe; where the original is stronger or weaker than its nearest engineering counterpart, say so.

Part 1, the translation. Take the central claims one at a time and restate each in terms I use: that a persisting system minimizes variational free energy; that perception is inference under a generative model; that action minimizes prediction error by changing the world rather than the model; that learning updates the model; that expected free energy governs policy selection; and that a Markov blanket marks the boundary of the system. Where the formal content is machinery I already own, name the machinery precisely. Is variational free energy minimization, in my terms, maximum-likelihood or EM estimation of a generative model via an upper bound on negative log evidence, with the sign flipped? Under linear-Gaussian assumptions, does the perceptual part collapse into something I already build, such as a Kalman filter or a hierarchical message-passing relative of one? Does the split of expected free energy into an information-seeking and a goal-seeking term correspond to something in the dual-control tradition, or to a rate-distortion objective, or to neither? Tell me where the translation is exact, where I have to squint, and where the neuroscience vocabulary appears to be doing work that the mathematics does not.

Part 2, the residue report. For each item below, say whether it survives translation into control and estimation theory intact, survives only in distorted form, or has no counterpart at all, and explain the verdict.

(a) The claim that minimizing free energy explains why a system persists rather than dispersing. Is this a theorem, a definition, or a restatement of the assumptions in other words?

(b) The claim that the system's model is not a representation the system consults but is the system, together with the idea that the Markov blanket is a real boundary rather than a modeling choice I am free to redraw.

(c) The normative universality: that anything which persists must be doing this. What does the engineering version of that claim actually amount to, and is it substantive?

(d) The objection, usually called the dark room problem, that a system minimizing prediction error should shut off its sensors and sit in the dark. Is there an engineering analogue, such as a degenerate controller, sensor blinding, or a plant parked at a fixed point? Does the framework's reply to the objection translate into my vocabulary, or does it require an additional premise about what the system is for?

(e) The thermodynamic and self-organization story, and the suggestion that this is a principle of physics rather than a modeling language. I have read that its author regards the principle itself as not the sort of thing that could be falsified, unlike predictive coding, which he treats as a hypothesis — I want your assessment of whether that distinction survives translation.

(f) The empirical-content question. Does the general principle make predictions over and above those of whatever generative model is plugged into it? My blunt engineer's version: if the model may be chosen after seeing the data, what has been predicted?

Part 3, the verdict. Where a residue exists, say for each item whether it is a defect of my vocabulary, that is, something control and estimation theory genuinely lacks, or a sign that the original claim was less determinate than it appears. Be specific about which of the two you think it is in each case, and do not treat the residue as automatically a point in the framework's favor.

You need not cite literature; work from the formalism and argue it through. Throughout, state how confident you are in the translation and in the verdicts, and single out the one point you are least sure of — the place where someone who works with this formalism daily would be most likely to correct you.
