Write a short story set in a fictional regulatory office whose analysts must assign every proposed pest-control or land-clearing program a single expected-welfare score. The method is fixed by statute: an expert panel gives each affected species a probability of sentience, that probability is multiplied by the number of individuals killed and by an assumed intensity of suffering per death, and the totals are summed, with no threshold below which a probability counts as zero. A locust comes in at roughly one in a thousand; a soil nematode far lower.

Let the machinery run rather than merely naming it. The arithmetic should turn those vanishing chances into a score that dwarfs the suffering of every mammal in the region, so that the analyst's own spreadsheet implies the office should stop protecting vertebrates altogether. Show the analyst hunting for the faulty step and finding none — the prior, the intensity assumption, and the summing rule are each defensible and jointly monstrous — and give the office two colleagues who embody the two ways of refusing the result: one who holds that a one-in-a-thousand chance of agony is simply a small harm, to be discounted like any other risk; one who holds that discounting a creature's suffering by its kind is the habit we have most reason to distrust.

End on a concrete decision the analyst makes, not on a resolution of the argument.
