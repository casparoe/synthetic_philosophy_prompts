I write the ideas column for a general-interest science magazine, which mostly means I get to sit in on other people's arguments and then try to explain them to strangers. Last week a friend of mine who teaches economics showed me a thing in a game that I can't stop thinking about, and the anoying part is that I don't know whether what he showed me has a name or whether he just invented it at a dinner party.

Here's the setup as best I remember it. Two of us pick an option at the same time. I have three options, call them A, B and C, and what I get depends on both my choice and his. I only wrote down my own payoffs, which I'm pretty sure is enough for this bit: if he goes left, A pays me 0, B pays me 4, C pays me 10; if he goes right, A pays me 10, B pays me 4, C pays me 0. His point was that B is dead — not because A beats it (it doesn't, it loses badly when he goes left) and not because C beats it (same problem in reverse), but because if I flip a coin between A and C I get 5 either way, which beats B's flat 4 no matter what he does. So B can never be the right thing to do, and I can prove that without knowing a single thing about what he's going to do. Then he said: fine, B is gone, and now that B is off the table some of my other options start to look dead too, and you keep going, and sometimes what's left is one option each, which feels like a prediction of how the game will actually go. But he also said the answer you get can depend on the order you cross things off, which seems like it should be impossible if each step is airtight, and that you can end up crossing off options that a Nash equilibrium would have used — I know roughly what Nash equilibrium is and this didn't sound like it. And he said something about the leftover set being exactly the choices that survive if everyone is rational and everyone knows that everyone is rational and so on, which I think was his one-line version of a whole literature. I have to write this up as a glossary item for the newsletter, and "crossing things off until only the obvious thing is left" is not a glossary item. Is there a name for this? If there is, I need to know exactly what it covers and, more importantly, where it stops covering — the coin-flip trick and the order thing and the rationality thing feel like they might be three different ideas wearing the same coat, and if there's no established name I'd definately rather you coin one and tell me what it does and doesn't include than just say no. Please don't hand me a bare label; the label is useless to me if I can't say what it isn't.

I'd also rather have a hedged answer than a confident-sounding one, so please say how sure you are overall and flag the single claim you'd bet least on. And since this is going into a glossary with a fixed template, give it back to me as a JSON object with these fields: "established_term" (the standard name, or null if there isn't one), "status" (one of "established", "established-but-partial", "no-established-term"), "best_fit" (the nearest existing concept that does most of the work, or null), "nearest_concepts" (an array of objects with "name" and "how_it_differs", each saying what that concept captures about my example and what it leaves out), "definition" (one or two plain-language sentences defining the thing itself), "not_this" (an array of things it's commonly confused with, each with a short reason why it isn't that), "boundary_cases" (an array of two or three cases where you'd be genuinely unsure whether the term applies), "proposed_name" (a term you'd coin if nothing established fits, else null), "confidence" (a number from 0 to 1), and "least_confident_claim" (the one thing in your answer you'd bet least on).
