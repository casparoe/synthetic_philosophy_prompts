I retired about four years ago after a career in animal nutrition — mostly ruminant work, feed trials, the endless business of figuring out why two animals on the same ration put it to such different uses — and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy on my own, which is going about as well as you'd expect. At the moment I'm grinding through Nietzsche, and I keep snagging on the late stuff where he decides that the questions that actually matter for a thinker are food, climate, and where he lives; that these are one question and not three; and that you don't get to *choose* the answer, your instinct either has it or it doesn't. My friend Walt, who has read all of it twice, says I'm overthinking it. I think there's something real there and something ridiculous there and I can't get them apart, so I want to see somebody commit to the ridiculous version all the way.

So: write me a mock intake questionnaire and house protocol for a residential wellness clinic — something like a boutique health retreat, the kind with a laminated welcome folder — that has built its whole practice on that corner of Nietzsche. Call it whatever you like, around 650 words, formatted as numbered intake items with a short preamble, in the voice of the staff, entirely straight-faced, no winks at the reader, no narrator nudging me that this is a joke. The target isn't Nietzsche, it's the practice of taking one sick man's extremely personal physiological self-report and turning it into a program for strangers — and, underneath that, the wellness habit of dressing moral demands up as biological facts and biological facts up as moral demands. Funny to someone who's read the relevant chapter, legible to someone who hasn't.

Things it has to get right, because these are the ones that make it land rather than just being a goof: the merging of diet, place, and climate into a single question about the conditions of one's existence; the claim that these things are settled by instinct rather than by reasoning or general rules, which is exactly what makes them *not* universalizable and therefore makes any clinic a contradiction in terms; the hostility to alcohol and to heavy cooking as narcotics and as indigestion, and the corresponding praise of water, light food, southern air, and walking; the thesis that a thinker's supposed objectivity is usually a disguised physiological need, so that ideas get diagnosed as symptoms; and the autobiographical irony that the man handing out these verdicts spent his life shuttling between climates trying to find one he could breathe in. I'd like the reductio to arrive on its own — a form that asks patients to declare their own instinct and then reserves the right to tell them their instinct is wrong, that sort of thing, without anyone in the document noticing. One favor: don't put anything in quotation marks that's supposed to be his, and don't invent citations or page numbers. Paraphrase his positions in your own words instead; I don't want to have to go check whether he actually said it.
