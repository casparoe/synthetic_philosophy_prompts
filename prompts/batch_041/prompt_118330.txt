Right, so I need help writing a speech, and I want to do this properly, so here's the deal. Act as a philosopher who writes speeches for a living -- someone who actually knows the decision-theory literature well enough that a room of academics won't wince, but who can also write for a room of parents. Before you draft anything, brainstorm three or four possible spines for the speech and tell me which one you're taking and why the others lost (keep that part brief, I just want to see the choice get made). Then write it. Think carefully about the middle, that's where these things always die.

The occasion: I run a small one-year masters program -- eleven students, first cohort ever, the whole thing has 'decision theory' and 'global risk' in the title and the faculty dean still doesnt really know why we exist. Next month I have to give the commencement address, about eight minutes. The room is split almost exactly in half. One half is my graduates and the faculty, who will catch every term I use and will know if I'm fudging anything. The other half is their families, who have spent a lot of money and have at best a hazy idea of what their kid has been doing all year. My own parents are in the third row and my dad has asked me twice now whether it's a real degree. So the speech has to land for both halves at once, and it can't be a lecture dressed up in a gown.

The idea I want to build it on is acausal trade. In case you don't have it loaded: the thought is that two agents who can never communicate, and can't affect each other at all, can still each do well out of the other, because the way you decide is itself a fact about the world that the other side can read and act on. You never reach them; they just know what you'd do. The usual illustrations involve aliens, or separate branches of the multiverse -- I don't want either of those anywhere in this speech.

Here's my own spin, and it's what I actually want the speech to argue, so build on this rather than on the textbook version: what makes it work isn't shared interests, it's a shared procedure. You and the far-away party have to be the sort of reasoners who arrive at the same place from the same premises. Which means what we hand to the future, and what the future hands back to us, isn't our goals, it's our method. That's also the honest answer to the question every parent in that room is actually asking, which is what the tuition bought. It bought a procedure.

Now the part I care about most. There's an obvious objection and I do not want you to dissolve it. Roughly: either your cooperating and theirs are merely correlated, in which case nothing you do reaches them and there's no trade at all, just two people happening to behave alike; or it's more than a correlation, in which case you've influenced them in the ordinary way and nothing acausal is left. Put that objection into the speech plainly, near the middle, and leave it standing. I want the graduates walking out with the question open rather than closed. What the speech has to do is make the idea land in the room anyway -- so the parents feel the shape of it even if they couldn't say it back to me -- while never promising anyone that the universe rewards this kind of thinking. No false comfort. It should be a speech that's still worth giving even if the argument is wrong.

Two more constraints. One: there has to be a single concrete image the whole thing hangs on, something my mother could repeat at Thanksgiving, and it can't be aliens and it can't be a multiverse. Two: it has to end warm. These are twenty-four-year-olds who are frightened about the future and their families are watching them. Warm and honest aren't in tension here -- that's basically the whole point of the speech -- so don't trade one off against the other.

And please, no version of this where the speech is secretly about how wise the speaker is. It's about them.
