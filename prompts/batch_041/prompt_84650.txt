I'm the reluctant secretary of a fortnightly reading group that's been doing paradoxes since January — we meet in the back room of a pub, which is the only reason nobody has resigned — and two of us have been going round in circles about the Liar since about week three. I've been told to write the joint statement they should of produced, so the rest of us can stop listening to the same arguement. Here's where they stand. One of them, the gappy one, thinks the sentence "this sentence is not true" is neither true nor false: it has a third status, and the price of that is giving up the assumption that every sentence is either true or not true, plus a great deal of care about what "not" is doing. The other, the dialetheist, thinks that sentence is both true and false at once, a genuine contradiction, and that this is survivable as long as we stop treating it as a rule that anything follows from a contradiction; for him nothing is gappy, and "not true" just means false. Both of them agree that we start from the thought that a sentence is true exactly when things are as it says they are — applied to every sentence, including sentences about truth — and that the paradox shows something has to give: either some instance of that thought, or some piece of classical reasoning such as excluded middle or the rule about contradictions. Neither will accept simply banning the sentence or denying that it's a sentence, and they agree that a theory of truth has to be able to say of its own claims that they're true, which is where it starts getting nasty.

So what I want is the document they ought to have written: a numbered list of the claims both can actually sign — about the paradox, about what a theory of truth is for, about what the gappy view is and isn't committed to, about what the dialetheist is and isn't committed to, and about what would count as a fatal objection to a theory of truth; then a table of the precise disagreements, each one worded so that the person it's aimed at would accept the wording as fair; then the cruxes, and for each crux the argument, case or sentence that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I suspect two of the cruxes are what negation in the object language is supposed to mean — whether "not true" already means "false", or is merely the failure of truth — and whether a theory is allowed to have a status or a notion that it can't express in its own language; but say so if you think there are others, and say honestly which bits of the dispute are just about words. And please do it on particular sentences rather than in the abstract, because that's the only way this group gets anywhere: (a) "this sentence is not true"; (b) "this sentence is true" — I know, not a paradox, but they disagree about it anyway, which I find revealing; (c) one I threw in last week that nearly ended the evening — "either this sentence is not true, or it is neither true nor false". The gappy one says (c) is just the ordinary Liar in a party hat, handled the same way; the dialetheist says (c) is the sentence that shows the gappy view can't state its own position. I want each of them to set out what their theory predicts about all three before we look at whether it comes out the way they want. No citations or reading lists, by the way — we all know what's been written, I just want the two of them pinned down on paper.
