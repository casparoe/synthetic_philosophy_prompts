I teach a single-session upper-level undergraduate metaphysics seminar on material objects, and I want a classroom activity that makes students feel the force of the special composition question: when, if ever, do some things together compose a further thing? The group is fourteen students, the session is eighty minutes, they will have done no advance reading, and I can rearrange the room and prepare cards or props beforehand. Please do not let the activity open by naming the question or the standard answers. Arrange the rules, roles, and stakes so that the students write and defend a criterion of composition themselves, get trapped by it, and only learn afterwards what the trap is called.

Several ideas should do the work inside the game, and the students should run into them rather than be told them. First, the four stark positions and their costs: that any things whatsoever compose something; that nothing ever composes anything, so there are only the simples, if any; that composition occurs exactly when the things in question constitute a life; and the brutalist option, that composition sometimes occurs but as a further primitive fact with no informative criterion. Include, as the dead ends the students can discover for themselves, the two series-style answers that look promising and collapse: contact, which composes far too much and treats touching as though it were the right kind of relation, and fastening, which turns out to be circular because it presupposes the very wholes it was meant to explain. Second, the vagueness pressure: if composition is restricted at all, some collections compose and near-identical ones do not, yet such a difference cannot be a difference in composition, so either everything composes or nothing does. The activity should generate this pressure from a continuum of cases in which adjacent cases differ negligibly while the endpoints differ categorically, and in which the students are required to draw a line and defend where. Third, the problem of the many: at least one round should present a case that looks like a single object but turns out to be a great many almost-entirely-overlapping candidates, so that counting the objects present becomes impossible, and the pull of there being just one must be felt against the pull of there being too many to count.

Please deliver: the setting and materials; the roles, rules, and a scoring scheme that keeps them invested without real money; an eighty-minute timeline; the structure of a debrief that maps particular moments of the activity onto the special composition question, the vagueness argument, and the life-based and brutalist answers, and that shows how the ordinary objects they began with get re-described under each position. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the arguments that will collapse into it is just a matter of convention, the students who will refuse to play by the game's rules, the rounds that will run long — and how to handle each. Finally, be honest about one feature of the real debate that the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
