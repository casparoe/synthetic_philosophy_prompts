I keep running into a split in research culture that I can't quite make principled sense of, and I'd like you to tell me what it presupposes. The default is that a researcher needs no one's permission: she decides her own project is worth doing, decides her own result is sound, and publishes it; the community's correction is supposed to come later, through replication and criticism. In a few areas, though — human-subjects research, work with certain pathogens, some dual-use engineering — the same community treats unilateral action as off-limits and demands prior sign-off from a committee. What view of individual judgment does the default presuppose? My working guess is that the split is not arbitrary but encodes an implicit claim about how independent decisions aggregate — the point, familiar from discussions of what is often called the unilateralist's curse, that when many people each act on their own private judgment that an action is worth it, the action goes forward whenever the single least cautious or most mistaken of them acts, so a large group can be likelier than any of its members to do something it will later regret. If that is the implicit theory, then: does the autonomy default presuppose that individual errors are independent and self-correcting, or only that the costs of coordination outweigh the expected harm? Do the review-board exceptions presuppose the opposite — that in these domains error is neither independent nor corrigible — or could they rest on irreversibility alone, with no appeal to aggregation? Reconstruct the theory as tightly as you can, mark what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely fits, and say where it is underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license the same split. Then tell me what would have to change if we adopted the aggregation worry as a general principle instead of a carve-out. One wrinkle I want addressed: the aggregation worry is sharpest when actors' judgments are independent, but a research community is exactly not that — it shares paradigms, incentives, and prestige, so its errors run together. Does that correlation strengthen or weaken the case for prior review? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
