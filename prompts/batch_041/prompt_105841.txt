I teach a third-year metaphysics module on dispositions and laws of nature, and I am setting the final paper. I want a single essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the textbook glass-and-sorcerer examples of finkish and masked dispositions, so I have invented a case for it and want the question built around that case. The case, which the question should state in full, concerns a bacterium engineered in three strains. All three carry a gene for a toxin, switched on by a sugar the bacterium encounters in its environment. In Strain X, the sugar binds to and destroys the sensor protein that would switch the gene on, so the toxin is never made. In Strain Y, the sensor works and the gene is transcribed, but a protease that is always present in the cell destroys the toxin as fast as it is made, so none accumulates. In Strain Z, the sensor works, the toxin is made and secreted in quantity, and it would harm the target organism — except that the strain also secretes, constitutively and independently of the sugar, a compound that neutralises the toxin in the surrounding medium, so nothing is ever harmed. The question should also state the simple conditional analysis (something is disposed to M when S just in case it would M if it were S) and the standard contrast between finks, where the stimulus itself removes or installs the dispositional basis, and masks, where the disposition survives intact but something independent blocks the manifestation.

I want the question to make candidates do three things: classify the three strains and defend the classification against the objection that it is merely an artefact of how we draw the boundaries of the stimulus and the manifestation; assess whether any ceteris paribus repair of the conditional analysis — 'it would M if it were S, and no interfering factor were present' — can avoid being circular or trivial, given that all three strains defeat the unqualified version; and draw a conclusion about whether dispositional claims are analysable in terms of conditionals at all. It is a 45-minute question worth 25 marks, and it should not require candidates to cite any literature.

Please write the question, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme in a table with these four columns: Criterion (five criteria, five marks each, totalling 25); What an A answer must do; What a B answer typically does instead; The specific error that costs marks. The last two columns should name the mistake rather than say 'less developed'. Marking schemes for this module have to separate an A from a B by naming the moves each contains, so please make the discriminations concrete.
