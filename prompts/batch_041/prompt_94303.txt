I have been reading decision-theory blogging and forums for a long while, and I have a fairly settled set of verdicts about when preferences and choices across time are irrational. I would like to know what principle, or small family of principles, they add up to, and where that principle forces me to give one of the verdicts up. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

Intransitive tastes, pumpable. I prefer an apple to a banana, a banana to a cherry, and a cherry to an apple. A trader offers me a small payment for each adjacent swap, and I take all three. I end with the apple I started with, minus money. Taking all three is irrational.

Intransitive tastes, a single trade. Same three preferences, but the only trade I am ever offered is apple for cherry plus a small payment, and I know no further trade will ever come. Taking it is fine.

Intransitive tastes, no traders. Same three preferences, but I am alone on an island; nothing I will ever do depends on which of the three I end up with, and no one will ever offer me a swap. I still think there is a rational defect here, though I am unsure whether to call the islander irrational or to say instead that her preferences are defective.

A diachronic pump. Today I prefer A to B, and I know with confidence that tomorrow I will prefer B to A, not because I will have learned anything, and that the day after I will prefer A to B again. A trader offers me a small fee today to swap my A for a B, and again tomorrow to swap back. Taking both is irrational, and taking the first while knowing I will take the second is irrational too, even though at the moment of each trade I get what I most want.

A mere reversal, no trader. The same reversal of taste, but no trader exists and no swap will ever be offered; I simply want A more on Monday and B more on Tuesday. I do not think this is a rational defect at all. Tastes are allowed to drift.

Resolute refusal. In the apple-banana-cherry case, after two swaps I refuse the third and keep the banana, though I prefer the apple to it, because taking it completes a cycle I know leaves me worse off. The refusal is right, even though at that moment I am choosing what I less prefer.

Precommitment. The same refusal, arranged in advance: I sell my right to make the third swap for a small fee. I am more confident that this is rational than I am about the bare refusal, though the two end in the same place, and I cannot say what the difference is supposed to be.

The toxin. I am paid a large sum today on the condition that I now intend to drink a toxin tomorrow that will make me ill for a day; the money is mine whatever I do tomorrow. I form the intention sincerely and take the money. Tomorrow, with the money banked, I do not drink. Neither choice strikes me as irrational, yet I think there is a rational failing somewhere, in my dispositions rather than in any particular choice, since an agent who would drink is reliably better off in worlds where such offers are made.

New information. I prefer the risky fund on Monday, learn on Tuesday that it is a fraud, and prefer the safe fund. Obviously rational, and obviously unlike the mere reversal above.

What I want is the principle or principles behind these verdicts, stated precisely enough to generate verdicts rather than gestures; the case that motivates each clause; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated. I also want its verdicts on cases I have not listed: an agent who is transitive at every moment but whose preferences cycle with a period of two days, so that a patient trader can pump her without her ever being incoherent at a time; an agent who would pay to have her intransitive tastes replaced by transitive ones, losing nothing else she cares about; and an agent who is exploitable only by someone with a detailed model of her, not by anyone offering the standard trades. If a single principle can be had only by giving up one of the verdicts above, say which one and why.
