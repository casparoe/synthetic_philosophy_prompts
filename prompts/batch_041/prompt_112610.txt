I've now had versions of the same argument three times this term — a science writer's essay, a colleague from the physics department, and one of my own students — so I take it it's doing the rounds. It goes roughly like this: physics tells us the world is fields and particles at bottom; panpsychism tells us that experience is a fundamental feature of reality, like mass or charge; put the two together and every particle has some bit of experience going on. And if there's experience then there can be suffering, and if there's suffering then it counts morally. So the fundamental furniture of the universe is a moral patient, every collision or decay is a possible site of harm, and a utilitarian — whose business is minimising suffering — has to add the suffering of particles to the suffering of animals and people, at which point the sums are so vast that nothing about human or animal welfare matters by comparison.

I'm fairly sure that's not right, but I'm not sure wether it's one bad step or five, and I'd like to be able to say something more useful to my student than "that's a bit much". Could you take it claim by claim: what a philosopher of mind would accept, what they'd wince at, what's a conflation of two things the view keeps apart, and what's plain overreach — and then tell me whether any ethical conclusion survives once the metaphysics is tidied up? I'd especially like to know which premise the move from "there is experience" to "it counts morally" actually needs, and whether panpsychism by itself supplies it.
