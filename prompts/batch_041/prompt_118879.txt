I run the conditionality team in a benefits office, and last night I lost a two-hour argument with a friend who works in frontline social work. Her claim was that what I do all day is the less-eligibility principle of the New Poor Law with better paperwork — relief must never be as attractive as the worst available job — and that everything we now say about reciprocity is decoration on that old rule. I want to answer her properly, and I also want guidance my team can actually run.

Please write me a decision procedure for deciding whether to impose a sanction on a claimant: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points where the procedure stops with a decision, and the points where it must hand the case to a person because what remains is a value judgment rather than a finding of fact. Assume roughly forty cases a week, about twenty minutes each, a rulebook I have no power to change, and a supervisor who has to sign off the recorded reasons.

Build it out of the strongest version of the reciprocity argument — that a decent share may be conditioned on contributing, but only where the terms on which contribution is demanded are themselves fair — and keep it honest about the objection that responsibility-sensitive conditionality is harsh, because it holds people to choices made among options nobody has bothered to check. Three questions it must take a position on rather than dodge: whether a sanction is a price for a service or a punishment for an offence; whether the scheme's real justification is desert or the legitimacy of the deal in the eyes of those who pay into it; and whether a job with no due process and dismissal at will counts as an option at all.

Then run the procedure on three cases: a claimant who missed a compulsory interview because his daughter was in hospital; a claimant who quit a warehouse job after two weeks because the shift pattern made childcare impossible; and a claimant who turned down a permanent, local, above-threshold job because, he says, the manager shouted at him during the interview. I expect the third to come out badly for him — say so plainly and explain why the procedure produces that result. Finish by telling me what the procedure gets wrong and which cases it will misfire on, and whether I can use it at all without conceding my friend's point.
