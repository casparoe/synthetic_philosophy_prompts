I'm putting together a one-page decision tree for the applied-ethics unit of an environmental philosophy course, on the question of what, if anything, an individual is obligated to do about a harm that no individual act makes any perceptible difference to — the standard menu being a burger, a flight, a vote, and the general case of a harm produced only when a large number of people act in the same way. The students keep collapsing the whole debate into "consequentialist versus deontologist," and I want a diagram that forces them to distinguish the moves that actually separate the views. Please build it as a flowchart: each internal node is a yes/no or three-way question phrased so that a student can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to, and each leaf is a named position (or "unnamed" if the combination is one nobody defends), carrying one sentence on the commitment that makes it awkward. Start the tree at the point where a student grants the premise that their own token act — this burger, this flight, this ballot — makes no difference to whether the harm occurs.

I want the branch structure generated from the logical space rather than from a list of authors. At minimum the tree should separate: whether the token act is held to make an actual difference, an expected difference, or none; if none, whether the act is still thought to stand in a morally relevant relation to the harm (as a member of a set that jointly produces it, as a non-redundant part of a sufficient set, as a trigger at a threshold, or as participation in a shared practice); whether the ground of any resulting obligation is consequentialist, a duty of fairness or non-free-riding, or something expressive or aretaic; and whether the obligation is conditional on others' compliance or unconditional. Some of these combinations will be empty — flag each empty combination and say whether you take it to be empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to occupy it.

Please make sure the tree can handle at least four cases, and note any pair of leaves it cannot pull apart: a single vote in a large election whose margin is not close; a single burger purchased where the store's supply contract would have been the same regardless; a tipping-point case where one more act crosses a threshold and changes the outcome; and a case where the same harm would have occurred even had you acted otherwise, because someone else would have taken your place. Keep it to five or six internal nodes and eight to ten leaves, and mark the two or three leaves a student is most likely to confuse with one another.
