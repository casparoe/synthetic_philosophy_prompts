I write about decision theory, economics and the behavioural sciences for a general-readership magazine, and for the past few months I have been reporting on the loose family of results that travel under the heading of the value of information. An argument I picked up during that reporting has lodged itself in my head, and I cannot decide whether it is a genuine reductio or an artefact of how it is stated. Let me set it out as cleanly as I can.

(1) Take an agent facing a decision: a set of acts open to her, a set of states of the world, a utility function assigning value to the consequences of acting in a state, and a probability distribution over the states.

(2) Suppose that before she chooses, an observation becomes available to her free of charge — a test result, an instrument reading, the contents of a sealed envelope, anything on which she can condition her choice.

(3) Whatever she would have done without the observation, she can still do with it: she can set the observation aside and act exactly as she would have acted. So looking and then choosing can reproduce any outcome that choosing straight away could have produced, and it can do better, since for each possible observation she may pick whichever act now looks best.

(4) Hence the expected value of a free observation is never negative. Whatever the observation says, and however unwelcome it is, she cannot be worse off in expectation for having it.

(5) Nothing in the argument assumes that the observation is useful, pleasant or even action-guiding. It applies to any observation whatever, provided only that it is free and that she is at liberty to act on it.

(6) Therefore a rational agent can never have an expectation-based reason to decline free information, or to pay to be rid of it. A preference for not knowing is, in this sense, always an error.

Several of the people I have put this to tell me that (4) is not a conjecture but a theorem, and that the theorem is not the thing that is wrong. What I cannot square it with is a case I have been turning over for weeks, which I will call the envelope.

A woman is told that a sealed envelope contains the definitive answer to a question she has been asking for twenty years: whether her late father, whom she loved and trusted, knew about and concealed a serious wrong done to someone she loves. Nothing she can do will change the answer. Nothing in the envelope can guide any decision she will ever face — the wrong is long past, the father is dead, and no living person's interests turn on what she comes to believe. She can burn the envelope unopened, and she would rather burn it. Opening it will not make her act differently. It will only make her different: the rest of her life will be lived by someone who knows. I cannot persuade myself that burning it is a failure of rationality, and the people I have asked mostly cannot either, though they insist the theorem survives.

Nor is the envelope the end of it, if (6) holds. (6) appears to leave no rational room for resisting a general presumption of disclosure: the physician who withholds a diagnosis at a patient's request, the editor who suppresses a finding whose publication will cause pain and change nothing, the researcher who declines to run the confirmatory test because he can already guess how he will take the result. I do not think these are all the same case, and I do not think they are all obviously irrational, but I cannot say what separates them. One decision theorist told me the theorem is about what information does for your decisions, not about what it does to you, and that this distinction dissolves the whole problem; I could not tell whether that was a repair or a restatement of the difficulty.

So: which premise or step would you give up, and why did it look so plausible? I would most like to keep (3) — the thought that you can always act as though you had never looked seems to me to be doing all the honest work — and I would surrender (6) long before I surrendered (4). Tell me what each of the available exits costs, and whether the envelope is a counterexample to the theorem or merely a case the theorem was never about. Close by telling me what would change your mind: which consideration, if it turned out to hold, would move you to a different verdict, and where in your own reasoning you would expect the mistake to be.
