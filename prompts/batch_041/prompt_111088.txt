Quick context: I'm in a philosophy of biology reading group and we spent last week on free-floating rationales -- the idea that evolution can produce traits and behaviors that have a point, that you could state a reason for, even though nothing in the organism ever entertains that reason. The stock illustration is a ground-nesting bird doing the broken-wing display: it flutters away from the nest dragging a wing to draw a predator off, and there's a perfectly good reason for that behavior (it moves the threat away from the eggs) even though nobody thinks the bird has that reason in mind. Selection just found the trick. Fine so far, I like it alot.

Then a friend in the group pushed on it and now I cant tell whether his argument is a cheap trick or whether it really does show the notion is rotten. Here's his reconstruction, tidied up:

(1) A thing has a rationale when there's a consideration that counts in favor of it, whether or not anyone grasps that consideration.
(2) Natural selection can confer rationales without conferring graspers: traits and behaviors whose "reasons" are good tend to stick around.
(3) So the bird's display has a rationale, and so does a fever, and so does a seedling bending toward the window, and so does a beaver's dam.
(4) If something has a rationale, it is done for a reason.
(5) If something is done for a reason, it's an action -- something an agent does, where the reason is part of why it happened rather than just a fact about it.
(6) So the fever is done for a reason. So is the bending. So is the dam.
(7) And if the line isn't drawn at organisms, it isn't drawn at organs either: the heart beats for a reason, the immune system raises your temperature for a reason, and there's no principled place to stop.
(8) But if literally every mechanism operates for reasons, then "operates for a reason" has stopped picking anything out. A predicate that everything satisfies does no work.
(9) If instead we refuse to apply it to the fever and the tropism, we have to say what makes the bird's display different, and the only difference on offer is that we can state the bird's reason more easily than the fever's -- which is a fact about us, not about the bird.
(10) Either way the free-floating rationale explains nothing: it's either true of everything or it's a projection.

He takes this to show we should drop the notion and just talk about selection and function. I don't want to drop it -- (1) and (2) seem like the whole point of the idea, and I'd hate to lose (5) too, since that's what makes "for a reason" mean anything at all. But I can't tell whether the trouble is in his premises or in the way he moves between them.
