I have been trying, without much success, to locate the precise point at which a colleague and I disagree about genocide. We agree on the historical record; what divides us is a conceptual and moral claim. She holds, at roughly 0.15 credence, that genocide is at bottom a name for a very large quantity of ordinary wrongs — killings, rapes, deportations, the destruction of livelihoods and ways of life — aggregated and dignified with a term; I hold, at roughly 0.7, that the term picks out a distinct wrong, the destruction of a group as a group, which is not merely the sum of the wrongs done to its individual members. I would like your credence in the second claim, but the number interests me less than its derivation. If you judge the claim as I have stated it not yet precise enough to have a truth value, tell me what precisification you are answering under before you answer.

Please decompose the question, giving a rough probability for each component with a line of justification apiece. At a minimum I would want terms for: whether a group is the kind of entity that can be wronged as such, or whether all such talk is shorthand for harms to members — the contrast I have in mind is between Lemkin's ambition to protect groups and Lauterpacht's insistence that international law protect individuals whether or not they are targeted as group members; whether, granting that groups can be wronged, that wrong is anything over and above the harms to members; whether the legal category's requirement of an intent to destroy a group as such tracks the wrong or mislocates it, and whether the wrong resides in intent, in structure, or in outcome; whether the concept's moral authority survives the politics of its use, given that the term is scarce and rhetorically enormous and that the definition was narrowed, in part, to keep the conduct of the drafting states outside its reach; and whether the wrong concerns the group's existence or its members' bodies, which is the question I take the fate of cultural genocide — present in Lemkin's conception, whose objectives he described as running through "the disintegration of the political and social institutions, of culture, language, national feelings, religion, and the economic existence of national groups", and absent from the treaty — to test. Then combine the terms into a single figure, tell me which term that figure is most sensitive to, and say what argument or evidence would move it most.

Two further questions. First, which of our two numbers, my 0.7 and her 0.15, most likely registers a disagreement about the metaphysics — whether groups can be wronged at all — rather than about the ethics — whether, if they can, the wrong is weighty enough to bear the name; and does your own figure depend on which of us is right about that? Second, does your number move if I stipulate a case in which no one is killed: a group destroyed across two generations by the removal of its children, the suppression of its language, and the collapse of its institutions, with every individual member surviving? If it moves, say by how much and why; if it does not, tell me what that shows about your decomposition.
