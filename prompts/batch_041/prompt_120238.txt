I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums long enough to have absorbed the house style, which is to distrust the slogan and trust the case: state the verdicts you actually have, ask what rule could have produced them, then go looking for the case that kills the rule. I want to do that with a question I keep failing to settle. When a famine happens, what makes it a natural disaster and what makes it a political crime?

The standard starting points are familiar to me — Sen's entitlement approach, his much-quoted claim about famines and functioning democracies, Bengal in 1943, Ireland in the 1840s, China in the late 1950s and early 1960s. I am not asking for those again, and I do not want a reading list; I have read the obvious things. What I want is the rule. So here are my verdicts, stated as flatly as I can state them, including the ones I suspect do not fit together.

Case 1. A harvest fails from drought in a poor but functioning democracy. The government is elected, the press is free, opposition parties can make an issue of anything they like. Relief arrives late and unevenly, and a few tens of thousands die who would have lived under a competent response. My verdict: a natural disaster with an ugly political margin. Incompetence, not crime. I would not want anyone prosecuted.

Case 2. The same drought, but the province is a colony governed from far away. The people starving have no vote in the government that rules them, no press of their own, and no way to make their hunger anyone's electoral problem. Grain keeps moving out, because contracts are contracts and property is property. Relief is withheld partly on the theory that it would breed dependency. My verdict: a political crime. That nobody ordered anyone to starve strikes me as beside the point.

Case 3. Starvation as a weapon. A government or an army uses the denial of food as its method — besieging a city, clearing a region, cutting off a population it wants gone or wants to break. My verdict: a crime, and a categorically different and worse one than case 2 — murder at scale rather than negligence at scale.

Case 4. A procurement famine. The state sets grain quotas on the basis of reports of abundance it has every reason to doubt, punishes the officials who report shortfalls, and keeps collecting and exporting while the countryside dies. The leadership intended the numbers, not the deaths, and would have preferred that nobody die. My verdict: a crime, and I do not find the absence of murderous intent exculpatory in the slightest.

Case 5. A blockade. An outside power's blockade keeps relief from reaching a civilian population that is not the enemy's army. The besieging power could have let food through at a military cost it has accepted elsewhere for smaller gains. My verdict: a crime, and the criminal is the besieger.

Case 6. Stipulated and invented, to keep myself honest. Vorland is rich and has superb logistics; Kesh, its poorer neighbor, is hit by drought. Vorland offers aid. Kesh's government routes the aid to its own core supporters and lets the periphery — which votes for the opposition — starve. My verdict: Kesh's government commits a crime; Vorland is clean.

Case 7. Stipulated. A famine nobody chose. A currency collapse triggers a run on grain; traders, farmers, and consumers all act lawfully; no official had information that would have let them see it coming; the price of food doubles and the poorest tenth of the population dies at three times the normal rate. There is no policy to point at, no decision that could have gone otherwise, and no one who intended anything. My verdict: still a political crime, and I cannot say by whom, which bothers me more than the verdict does.

Case 8. Stipulated. An autonomous region inside a federation elects its own government, which mismanages its own food system into catastrophe. The federal government is legally barred from intervening, has no local administrative capacity, and would be committing a serious wrong if it sent in troops and grain over the region's objections. My verdict: a political crime, but the region's own government owns it; the federation is not a criminal here even though it could physically have done something.

Case 9. Stipulated, the near miss. A government sees a famine coming and averts most of it by means that are themselves unjust — requisitioning grain from another poor region at gunpoint, conscripting farmers into planting, jailing merchants who hoard. Far fewer die than would have. My verdict: the famine is not a crime, because it did not happen; but the requisitioning is a wrong, and the officials who ordered it are responsible for that wrong even though it saved lives.

The tentative rule I have been running, which I do not trust: a famine is a political crime when the people who died had no institutional means of making their survival someone's problem, and someone with the power to save them knew or should have known. Severity then scales with how much the responsible party stood to gain from the deaths. I can see that this gets cases 1, 2, 3, and 5 right, and I can see that it seems to convict Vorland in case 6 and to acquit the region's government in case 8, and I have no idea what it does with case 7.

What I want from you is the principle, or a small set of competing principles, that best fits these verdicts; what each one says about cases I have not listed; and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts I would have to give up. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list.

Return a single JSON object and nothing outside it, with exactly these fields:

- "candidate_principles": an array of two to four objects, each with "name" (a short handle), "formulation" (the principle stated precisely enough to apply mechanically, with any term you introduce defined), "cases_it_was_built_from" (case numbers), and "what_it_gives_up" (the verdict or verdicts it cannot keep).
- "recommended": an object with "name" (which candidate you would defend) and "why" (a short paragraph naming the crux).
- "case_by_case": an array with one object per case number, each with "case", "asker_verdict" (a few words), "principle_verdict" (a few words), "match" ("full", "partial", or "conflict"), and "disagreement_is_about" (the point at issue, or "none").
- "verdicts_to_abandon": an array of objects, each with "case", "asker_verdict", "replacement_verdict", and "why_the_principle_wins"; use an empty array if there are none.
- "unconsidered_cases": an array of two or three new cases of your own, each with "case" (a few sentences of stipulated facts), "predicted_verdict", "confidence" ("low", "medium", or "high"), and "principles_that_diverge" (which candidates disagree here).
- "crux_case": an object with "case" (one stipulated case), "rulings" (what each candidate says about it), and "why_it_discriminates".
- "threshold": a short statement of what the recommended principle implies about what counts as a famine at all, in particular whether a policy that quietly raises mortality among the poor without visible starvation is in scope, or "the principle is silent on this" if it is.
- "residual_uncertainty": what would have to change in the cases to change your recommendation.
