ok so im in a historiography seminar this term (its cross-listed with phil of science, which is why half the room is only there for the credit) and we keep having the same fight every week. i want to actually get somewhere with it before the essay is definately due.

the thing is my prof has this picture she uses constantly and everyone just nods along. she says: reading the history of a discipline is like reading the early drafts of a paper you've since finished. the groping paragraphs, the bits that contradict each other, the long footnote to something that turned out to be a dead end -- you read all of it as stages on the way to the final version, and the wrong turns only make sense in the light of where it ended up. her point is supposedly anti-smug: don't laugh at the past. but the payoff is that we should read a 17th-century treatise on motion as an almost-there version of what we do now, and treat the weird stuff in it (the theology, the vitalism, the astrology) as scaffolding that got thrown away once the building stood. so: not smug, but still read them as us-in-embryo.

then a guy in my section, who i think just likes arguing, says fine, but the past isnt a draft of our paper, its a different paper, and the moment you read it as a draft you've already made us the final version. he says the draft picture sneaks in a single author, a single goal, and one set of standards for what counts as good, and none of those are there. my prof says thats just a cliche and that it leaves you unable to say the field has gotten better at anything. so we're stuck, and i honestly cant tell if he's right or just being clever.

she also keeps bringing up the shoulders-of-giants thing, and someone in the back said the original medieval version of that image had us as dwarfs, not giants, which seems to cut against how she uses it.

can you audit this properly? what i want: (1) the correspondence laid out -- what in the source maps onto what in the target (author, draft, final version, editor, deadline, what "better" even means), (2) the places the mapping breaks, (3) which of those breaks actually matter for what the prof wants the analogy to do and which are just decoration, (4) whether her conclusion survives once the breaks are on the table or whether the analogy was carrying the whole thing, and (5) whether the guy's different-paper line is any better or whether it has its own problem (i suspect it does but cant say what). and if there's a better picture than either of theirs, tell me what it is. i dont need reading lists or citations, just the argument.
