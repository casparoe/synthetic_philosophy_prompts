I'm a novelist — fourth book, secondary-world stuff — and my editor keeps telling me the rituals in my invented societies read like set dressing. She's right, and I think it's because I never asked what any of them are *for*. A friend who does behavioral ecology told me to stop thinking about customs and start thinking about signals. Some traits are indices, she said: they can't be faked because they're physically tied to the underlying quality (you can't fake being the biggest stag). Others are handicaps: they only pay off if you're good enough to afford the cost, which is why the price has to fall harder on low-quality signalers than high-quality ones. She also mentioned signals of need versus signals of quality, that a purely conventional gesture can still be honest while interests line up (cheap talk), and that signals rot over time as receivers learn to discount them. I nodded alot.

Here's what I want. Put on your behavioral ecologist hat and your dramaturg hat, think carefully, and before you write anything, brainstorm a little about how signals fail in a world like mine. Then give me a procedure I can actually run on any ritual I invent — fifteen minutes, no theory, I'm definately not reading a textbook: ordered questions, what counts as an answer to each, where it says "this is just a cost, not a signal," where it says "cut this or go fix the economy," and where it hands the call back to me because it's an authorial question rather than a biological one. Then run it on three: the nine-day adolescent fast in the hills, the suitor's public gift of a season's spice harvest, and the daily stick of temple incense everyone burns. Make sure one of the three comes out somewhere I won't like — I'd rather find out now. And tell me what the procedure can't do.
