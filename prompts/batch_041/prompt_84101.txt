I write novels, and I've spent the last year and a half reading philosophy of probability on the side because the narrator of the book I'm trying to finish works in risk at an insurance firm and I wanted her to sound like someone who actually thinks about this stuff. Somewhere in there the reading stopped being research and turned into my own problem, and I've now landed on a pile of views that each look obviously right to me on their own and that I half suspect don't fit together. I've argued about it with two friends (one a statistician, one who does machine learning) until we were all just repeating ourselves, so I'd rather have a proper audit than another opinion. Here's the pile, roughly in the order I picked them up:

1. Credences aren't always sharp. Often the honest description of what I think is a set of probability functions, an interval when it's a single quantity I'm unsure about, and I mean that as a claim about the state itself, not just about my ignorance of some hidden sharp number I happen to be carrying around. Announcing 0.37 when all I've got is somewhere between a third and a half is false precision and I'd rather not do it.
2. I'm still a Bayesian, though. Each function in that set is a real probability function obeying the axioms, and that's what stops the picture from being a free-for-all.
3. When evidence comes in I conditonalize each member of the set on it and let the set be whatever survives. Nothing fancier.
4. More evidence is never bad for you. I'd have said that was the entire point of being an empiricist about anything.
5. If I genuinely know nothing about some question, a maximally vague state, every probability function on the table, is a perfectly respectable place for me to be, and I'm not obliged to fake a number just to be decidable. For acting, the rule I like is the one where an option is permissible if it maximizes expected utility for at least one member of my set (I think it's called E-admissibility). Maximizing the worst case feels too timid; acting as if dominance didn't matter feels too reckless.
6. I would never take a bet that's a catastrophe under most of the hypotheses I take seriously. That's just prudence.

One thing I keep chewing on, mostly because I want the answer to handle it: two coins that are either perfectly correlated or perfectly anti-correlated, both fair, where I start with a sharp one-half on the second coin coming up heads. I flip the first, it's heads, and my state about the second is now either certainty that it's heads or certainty that it's tails. I don't know what to do with that, or whether it's a problem for anything above or just a strange-looking feature. So here's what I want from you: tell me which of these actually pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, how bad each real one is, and what the cheapest repair is and what it costs me, ranked rather than surveyed. Also tell me which single revision would clean up the most. One request: keep what's settled among people who work on this seperate from what's genuinely contested, and don't let a contested view through dressed up as the consensus. I've been burned before by a very confident essay that turned out to be one camp's line, and I'd rather see the seams, including, if it applies, that one of my six items isn't really a well-formed thing to believe and I should stop trying to hold it.
