I hold the following view about corruption. Corruption is a universal moral category, not a local one. It consists in the abuse of entrusted power for private gain, where entrusted power is power that is supposed to be exercised on behalf of others, and private gain includes gain for oneself, one's kin, one's faction, or one's client network. On this view, gift-giving to officials and clientelist exchanges are corrupt whenever they breach the impartiality that the role requires. The fact that such practices are embedded in local norms of reciprocity, hospitality, or kinship does not exempt them; it may explain them, but it does not justify them. The Western anti-corruption framework, whatever its blind spots, is not parochial: it applies a standard that any society can be held to.

I want to know what would count against this view. Not the familiar relativist objection in its broadest form, but the specific observations, arguments, intuitions, and possible cases that should move me. I am especially interested in whether the view can distinguish itself from a self-serving imposition of Western political forms, and whether the distinction between a gift and a bribe is doing any independent moral work or is merely a legal artifact.

Please use the following pressure points, and feel free to invent others if they are sharper:

(a) The hereditary granary keeper. In a society where the office is hereditary and the keeper is expected to distribute surplus first to kin and then to the poor, with no local concept of impartiality attached to the role, is this corruption? If not, does that show the universalist view is false, or only that this is not a public office in the relevant sense?

(b) The legal expediting service. A city where permits are issued by officials who also run a transparent, published, legally authorized expediting service with fixed fees. Everyone knows the fees; the service is not secret. Is this corruption? If not, what does that do to the claim that corruption is about private gain from entrusted power?

(c) The functional equivalence problem. A campaign contribution that buys access, a lobbying retainer that buys a meeting, and a gift to a bureaucrat that buys a permit. If the first two are not corruption under the standard framework but the third is, is the standard consistent, or does it encode a distinction that cannot survive moral scrutiny?

(d) The internal critique. A postcolonial or nationalist critic says that anti-corruption discourse is a way for outside powers to delegitimize local political arrangements. Is that critique a defeater for the universalist view, or can the universalist accept it as a warning about enforcement while keeping the moral category intact?

In answering, do not give me a literature review, citations, or a reading list. I want the conceptual map. Structure your answer as follows:

1. The Position, Stated Plainly (about 200 words). Restate my view in its strongest form, making clear what it is and is not committed to.

2. What the Position Predicts That Its Rivals Do Not (about 300 words). What should we expect to see if it is true -- in cases, institutions, and moral judgments -- that we would not expect if the cultural-relativist or functionalist rival were true?

3. The Defeaters, Ranked (about 700 words). The observations, arguments, intuitions, and possible cases that would count against the position, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether the position's own defenders would concede it as a cost, and whether it could be absorbed by a qualification without loss.

4. The Insulation Question (about 300 words). Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability? If so, is that a defect for a view of this kind, or is it the price of a moral category that must be role-relative?

5. Verdict (about 150 words). What you take the position's overall exposure to be, and what single consideration would most move you.
