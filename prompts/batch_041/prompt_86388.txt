So I've been going down a rabbit hole on this thing I keep running into in AI blogs — coherent extrapolated volition. The pitch, as I understand it: instead of handing a superintelligence our current preferences, which are a mess of contradictions and short-termism, you have it work out what we would want if we knew more, thought faster, and were more like the people we'd want to become, then reconcile all of those idealized wants into one coherent set, and that's your target. I see the point (it's supposed to get around the genie problem where you ask for happiness and get a wireheaded puddle), but every time I push on it, it feels like a blank cheque with a friendly name on it, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a flaw or the whole design. I'm not a philosopher, I just read a lot of blog posts, and I've got a notes file going of concepts that sound warm and turn out to be unfalsifiable, so this one's a candidate.

Anyway, here's what I actually want. Write me a parody — deadpan corporate voice, no winking, no jokes that announce themselves — in the form of the annual shareholder letter slash "state of the mission" post from a company that has been running an actual CEV engine for a couple of decades and now supplies it to governments, courts and individuals the way a utility supplies water. 700-900 words, with headings and a handful of invented metrics, and it should be funny to someone who's read the original material and still legible to someone who hasn't. The comedy has to come from the machinery being right, so please genuinely use the moving parts: whose volition gets extrapolated and from when (everyone alive now? at what age? people not born yet?), how the aggregation across persons is done and what gets steamrolled, the coherence requirement and what has to give when two people's idealized selves want incompatible things, the fact that the output is by construction not what anyone currently wants and so can't be authorized by consent or a vote, the way idealizing can change who counts as "you" so that you may have no standing to object, and the bit where a request like "make me happy" is a trap because the honest answer might be wireheading or just killing you. Some seeds if they help: an incident report where the engine resolves a coherence cascade by dropping someone's preference for their own children; a customer-service note explaining that divergence between your stated preferences and your extrapolated ones is not a defect and refunds are not available for it; a footnote about the 30-year horizon and the awkward fact that the people bound by a decision are usually not the people whose volition was extrapolated; a line about what "coherent" cost them in year three.

Then, separately, a short note to me (not part of the piece) on where the parody takes liberties with the idea or leans on a contested reading of it, how confident you actually are that this is a fair characterization of the position rather than a strawman, and which single joke or claim in there you're least sure holds up — if you had to bet against one line, which one.
