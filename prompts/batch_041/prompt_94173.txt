I'm a third-year student currently taking a course on Hellenistic ethics, and I've spent the term reading Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius side by side with the flood of contemporary "Stoicism" content — podcasts, productivity newsletters, a meditation app my roommate swears by — and I keep noticing how much of the modern revival borrows the vocabulary of the dichotomy of control while quietly reversing what the doctrine was originally for. For a term project I want a satirical piece that makes this gap legible without ever lecturing the reader about it, so I'd like you to draft it for me. Please write an internal memorandum, roughly 700 words, in the deadpan register of corporate HR communications, issued by the "Office of Stoic Alignment" of an invented mid-sized company and announcing a mandatory resilience retraining program for its staff. The target is the corporate appropriation of Stoicism as an ideology of productivity and compliance — not any named living person — and the humour must come from the philosophy itself, so that someone who has actually read the Stoics laughs rather than winces.

To that end, the memo should be built out of accurate doctrine rather than generic self-help. It should make use of the distinction between what is and is not "up to us," but stretch it in the direction a real HR department would: the company's decisions, evidently, fall on the side of what is up to us, while the employee's objections, working conditions, and pay fall on the side of what is not — and the memo should treat this reclassification as a matter of settled doctrine. It can invoke the Stoic teaching that only virtue is good and everything else is a "preferred indifferent," then use that to explain why a living wage is, regrettably, among the preferred indifferents the firm is not obliged to provide; it can describe anger and resentment as false judgements in need of cognitive retraining; it can lean on the sage as the model employee and on apatheia as a wellness target, and it may gesture at cosmopolitan brotherhood in a way that happens to mean "we're all one team." Feel free to include a definitions section, a reclassification chart, or an FAQ, whichever serves the parody best. The tone should stay flat and sincere throughout — no winking, no narrator stepping out to tell us it's a joke — and the standard objections should be allowed to surface as consequences of the memo's own logic rather than as statements. A reader who knows the tradition should be able to reconstruct what is being satirized from the piece alone, while a reader who has never opened Epictetus should still find it funny as a portrait of a workplace.
