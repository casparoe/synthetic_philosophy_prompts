So I've been reading about how Darwin and Turing both pull the same weird trick: you take something that looks like it needs a mind behind it, and you show it getting built by something that plainly doesn't have one, so that "understanding" ends up downstream of blind mechanism instead of upstream of it. A friend of mine thinks the trick always works in principle, and that any time you think you've found a case where genuine comprehension is doing the work, you just haven't looked closely enough at the machinery yet. I'm not so sure, but I dont want a philosophy seminar answer, I want an actual case that I can poke at.

So: build me a thought experiment. Realistic setting — a hospital, a market, a workshop, a shipping office, something that could just happen — not a science fiction setup, and no longer than one paragraph for the scenario itself. The case needs two components: one that looks like the part that understands what it's doing, and one that plainly doesn't understand anything (a blind process, a fixed rule, a selection mechanism, a lookup table, whatever). Three specs it has to meet. First, the dumb component has to be doing the load-bearing work: yank it and the competence collapses. Second, yank the smart-looking component instead and the competence survives — degraded is fine, dead is not. Third, the dumb component has to be handling genuinely novel cases, not replaying things it was tuned on, so nobody gets to say it's just memorization. And dont cheat me on this: the smart component cant be decorative from the start, and the dumb one cant be a designer in disguise — no god's-eye tuner who understands the domain and hands the dumb process a perfect rule, and no infinite populations or infinite time. Keep it human scale. What the case is for is testing whether the inversion can be pushed all the way down, so a case that stipulates the asymmetry rather than generating it from the structure is a fail.

Give it to me in this order. "The case" — the scenario, one paragraph, ~150 words. "The removal tests" — walk through both deletions and what's left after each, ~150 words. "Verdicts" — what a comprehension-first view says about it, what the inversion view says, and what a middle view says (one that thinks understanding is a matter of the internal structure of the process rather than of the output), a short paragraph each, with the reasoning spelled out. "The hinge" — the one feature of the case that's actually doing the work, and what happens to the whole thing if I remove that feature, ~150 words. "How it gets attacked" — the best move available to someone who insists real comprehension is required here, and whether that move works, ~150 words. Then "Bottom line", 3-4 sentences.
