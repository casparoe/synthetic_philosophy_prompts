I spent thirty-five years as a population biologist and came to philosophy only after I retired, so I write as an amateur, and I would rather you take this request on its own terms than as an expert's brief.

On Saturday I am to give the memorial address at the funeral of my oldest friend. We met as graduate students, we published together for four decades, and he died at eighty-three after a short illness. He was also, for his entire adult life, a devout and entirely unshowy member of a small congregation; he took its doctrines seriously in a way I never quite understood and never once doubted.

The room will be mixed, and I know exactly how mixed. In the front will be his wife, his children, and perhaps forty members of that congregation, for whom this is a religious service and for whom he has not ceased to exist. Behind them will sit our colleagues, a dozen or more working scientists, several of them the sort of atheist who regards religious belief as a personal failing and will be listening to see whether I go soft. And in the middle will be people who knew him only as a good man and have no idea that any of this is contested. The congregation's minister will speak after me, and I would like to avoid any sentence that forces him to correct me or forces me to pretend.

I have been trying to work out what I can honestly say, and I have arrived at a position I would like the address to embody rather than merely announce.

First, I hold that religion is a natural phenomenon. I mean this literally: religious belief and practice are products of ordinary human cognitive dispositions and ordinary social dynamics, and they can be accounted for without positing anything supernatural. I do not find this deflating, and I do not think it licenses contempt. Explaining why a person came to hold a belief is a different question from whether the belief is true; I refuse to let my own account do work it cannot do. So the address must not insinuate that my friend's faith was a childish comfort, a weakness, or a failure of nerve. I would be lying if I said it, and he would have recognized the lie.

Second, and this is the part I want to do real structural work in the speech, I have come to think that what his religious practice did for him, and through him for a great many people, is best described in terms of trust. The congregation's observances were costly, public, and hard to fake, and precisely because they could not be faked cheaply they made each member's commitments credible to the others; out of that credibility came a kind of trust I have never seen matched among my colleagues. I believe this is a true account of the mechanism. I also believe that if I say it plainly at a funeral it will sound like a reduction, as though I were explaining the man's faith away by naming the social glue it supplied. I do not want it to sound that way, because I do not think that way. A practice can be fully explained and still be worthy; the explanation and the value sit on different levels, and I want the speech to make that felt rather than merely asserted.

Please write the address. It should run about six minutes aloud, and it should use plain language, with none of the technical vocabulary I would reach for in a paper or a seminar. It must console a room of grieving people, many of whom are not philosophers and some of whom are not believers, without my saying anything I would privately regard as false. It should let the naturalistic account constrain what I am able to say — I cannot promise that he is watching, I cannot pretend to share his hope — while leaving his congregation feeling that their friend was honored rather than diagnosed. And it should leave the atheists in the back rows with no reason to think I have abandoned an honest view, only that I declined to use his funeral as the occasion to assert it.
