I host a small philosophy-of-science podcast and next month's guest is a philosopher who works on economics and has spent years defending the idea that Malthus was right but early — her line is that the population-grows-geometrically-while-food-grows-arithmetically bit named a real mechanism, and that what actually failed were contingent background assumptions about farm yields and about what people do with rising incomes, so grading the theory on its failed prediction is unfair.

Draft me 9 or 10 interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and make them the sort of questions you can only ask if you've thought hard about how theories get rescued when their predictions fail. I want at least one each on: how her move differs from just saying the prediction was early, and what stops "right but early" from sliding into unfalsifiable; how to tell a real tendency whose effects are being masked from a mechanism that isn't actually doing any work; the demographic transition, which is what everyone throws at Malthus — refutation, or something his own revisions half-anticipated? (he kept rewriting the essay between editions, and I've never known whether to count that as tightening the theory or as the first rescue); and the reflexive angle — the essay was out in the world and people acted on it, so could its influence have helped falsify it, and is that vindication or cheating? Plus one question that forces her to name what evidence would make her drop the right-but-early reading rather than just refine it.

My co-host also wants her to react to a scoring rule he cooked up: judge a long-run prediction not by whether it came true but by whether the mechanism it named shows up as a live cause in our best current models of the thing. I think thats either obviously correct or a machine for never admitting a theory lost, and I'd definately like her pinned down on which. She's not precious about this stuff, so keep it conversational, and don't waste any of the ten on warm-up questions.
