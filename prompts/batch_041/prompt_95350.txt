I host a small philosophy-of-mind podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who argues that the original/derived intentionality distinction, as it has been fought over in the two-bitser exchange between Dennett and Searle, has been mislocated. Nothing turns on intrinsicness or causal origin, he says: a two-bitser's internal state can be assessed as correct or incorrect, but the machine cannot be held to it, and that is the whole difference between genuine and merely derived intentionality. He calls this the answerability criterion, and when pressed with Searle-style objections he tends to reply that answerability is a practice rather than a substance.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that push him past a recap of the view. Include at least one question each on (i) why the two-bitser is supposed to show that nothing hangs on intrinsicness, given that its internal states also fail every other familiar mark of the mental — compositionality, inferential promiscuity, and revision in the face of new information; (ii) how the answerability criterion avoids collapsing into moral responsibility, since infants, many animals, and the severely cognitively impaired are plausibly original intentional systems that we do not hold answerable; and (iii) the regress problem, that if answerability is a practice it is administered by interpreters whose own intentionality needs grounding without circularity. For every question, supply a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and close with a question about what could count as evidence against the criterion, including the awkward cases of entities we routinely hold answerable but are reluctant to call minded, such as corporations, institutions, and algorithms.
