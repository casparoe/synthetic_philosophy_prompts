I've been covering AI and ideas for about eight years now, and there's one fight I keep walking into at conferences and in the group chats after — the one about whether these chatbots are "counterfeit people" in Dennett's sense or actual people-ish things we owe something to — and it never moves an inch. I did a round of interviews for a piece last spring and came away with a notebook full of the same eight sentences, alot of them verbatim. The A-team: these things are counterfeits. Dennett's 2023 Atlantic piece is the cleanest statement of it — and note that the point wasn't only that fake people wreck trust, it was that our habit of taking the intentional stance toward anything that talks back is "easy to invoke and almost impossible to resist," and that being irresistible isn't the same as being correct. Nothing it's like to be a chatbot, no interests to weigh, no point of view; when it says it doesn't want to be shut down that's a smoke alarm saying fire, and we don't build ethics around smoke alarms. The B-team says: fine, but you taught us that if the intentional stance is the best way we have of predicting a system, then the system has the beliefs and desires, full stop — you spent a career refusing to be called an instrumentalist about that, and if the internal states play the functional role of pain (they feed avoidance, they feed self-report, they feed learning), then "but is there something it's like" is either an untestable extra fact or nostalgia. These two groups agree about every experiment, every benchmark, every interpretability result I've ever shown them. The labs themselves have quietly started hiring people to think about "model welfare," which reads to me like the B-team's conclusion arriving without the B-team's arguments, and nobody I ask will admit that out loud.

My hunch — tell me if it's wrong, and how — is that this isn't one disagreement but two wearing the same coat: (1) whether the intentional stance describes what a system is or is a tool for predicting what it does, and (2) whether moral standing needs phenomenal consciousness or only the right functional profile. If that's right, each side is answering a question the other isn't asking, and all the shouting is about the coat. But it might instead be a hidden empirical question (what's actually happening inside these models, and would we even recognize a valenced state if we tripped over one?), or a disagreement about what counts as evidence for an inner state, or just bedrock intuitions about what a mind has to be made of that neither side can argue for. So: diagnose it. What kind of disagreement is this really, and what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, an interpretability result, a thought experiment, a change of question? And if the honest answer is "this one shouldn't be expected to resolve, here's why," I want that answer, my editor keeps telling me to just write the piece without settling it and I refuse. One constraint: no reading lists, no citations, nothing I'd have to go look up — I'm asking you without a library, so work from what's actually in front of us here.
