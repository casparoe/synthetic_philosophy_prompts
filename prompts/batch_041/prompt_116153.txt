I'm a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy (dissertation on conservation ethics, plus a BSc in ecology from before I switched), and for the last year I've been the grad-student member of my town's deer management advisory committee. Seven of us, appointed by the town council, and we have to hand the council a written recommendation it actually votes on at its March meeting. I'm the one drafting it. I definately need the memo itself, not notes toward it.

The situation: too many white-tailed deer. Predators gone a century ago, no hunting in town since the seventies, and every lawn is a buffet. The understory in the town woods is basically gone, there are around ninety car strikes a year, and the herd is now bigger than the winter browse can support. The state wildlife vet says that if nothing changes we should expect a couple hundred deer to die slowly between January and April, and the survivors to be in bad shape by spring. Two winters ago a bunch of them starved on the golf course, which is how the committee got created in the first place.

What's on the table: contract sharpshooters to take roughly 300 deer over three weeks in January, expensive and loud; immunocontraception for the does, which the vet says won't move the population for at least three years and does nothing about this winter; supplemental feeding, which the state won't let us do and which everyone agrees just makes next winter worse; or nothing.

The committee is split and I have to write something four of them will sign. The chair is a retired large-animal vet and thinks a bullet is the kindest thing on offer; he keeps saying that a deer that starves for six weeks is worse off than a deer that never existed, so doing nothing isn't the safe option, it's a decision to make a lot of animals suffer. Our animal control officer says he's the one who gets the calls. The econ professor says the cull means fewer deer get to live at all, and if a deer's life is on balance worth living then we've made the world poorer, not kinder. One member is a hunter who thinks this is all overwrought, one volunteers at a wildlife rehab place and is genuinely torn. And the member I find hardest to answer says: fine, if we're killing animals to spare them suffering, why is it only the deer? There are more mice and songbirds in this town than deer, and most of them also die badly. Either we take animal suffering seriously as such, or we admit we're just managing the animals people can see and hit with their cars. I don't know what to say to that and I suspect it's the real issue.

What I need: a memo, two pages max, recommending one option (or a combination) with a concrete timeline and a date by which the council has to decide for the January window to even be possible, laying out the reasoning so the members who disagree can argue with it instead of feeling steamrolled. Two things I care about. First, take the do-nothing position seriously in its strongest form, not nature-knows-best, but the version where we genuinely can't predict what we're doing to a population when we intervene, and where the animals we kill for their own good cant be asked and can't be compensated. Second, say honestly how far our responsibility runs: if it runs to the deer, does it run to the mice, and if not, why not. If the memo ducks that, the hardest member takes it apart in ten minutes.

Style: I'm the junior person on this thing and half the committee thinks I'm the ethics guy, so it should sound like a person wrote it, not a journal article. No citations, obviously, just the argument. And front-load it, because the council reads maybe the first paragraph.
