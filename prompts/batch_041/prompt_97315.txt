I am a third-year undergraduate reading philosophy, and I am currently taking a module on decision theory and the philosophy of probability. Each week one of us opens the seminar with a short piece of verse meant to throw the week's reading into a different light, and next week it falls to me: the topic is the St. Petersburg paradox. I would like you to write that poem for me.

Please adopt the role of someone who is at once a philosopher of probability — at ease with expected-value reasoning, utility theory and the behaviour of divergent series — and a careful poet. Before drafting, think through for yourself which formal resource in verse could actually do the work of the paradox rather than merely describe it, sketch two or three candidate structures, and only then commit to one and write. I would rather you sacrifice ornament to accuracy.

What I want is a doubling poem: the verse should grow as the game grows. Use four stanzas whose line counts double from one to the next — one line, then two, then four, then eight — and let the sum at stake double in step with them, so that the poem and the payout run away together and the page itself begins to strain. The form must not be permitted to run away indefinitely: bring it to a halt at that eighth line, and let the halt — the moment the poem can no longer keep doubling — be the poem's turn, the place where the philosophical work is done. That turn should register the standard escape routes honestly: the logarithmic-utility move that Daniel Bernoulli proposed in 1738, on which the moral expectation stays finite even though the monetary one diverges; and the blunter point that any real bankroll is finite, so that past some n the doubling coin has nothing left to pay with. Then close with a single short line, standing outside the doubling pattern and deliberately un-doubled, that gestures at the deeper trouble: a cousin game whose payoffs alternate in sign, so that its expected value neither settles on a finite number nor runs away to infinity but depends on the order in which you add the terms — the thought that even an infinite expectation was, in a sense, a comfort. Sixteen lines in all, no padding.

A specialist in decision theory should recognise the paradox, the resolution and the objection, and should find the poem's shape tracking them; a reader who has never met the problem should still find it a good poem. Do not name-drop Bernoulli or any of the historical figures for their own sake — the poem should earn its allusions or leave them out. Write in British English.
