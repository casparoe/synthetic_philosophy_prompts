I'm a postdoc in philosophy, and next month I'm giving a paper at a small interdisciplinary workshop on responsibility and the criminal law. The paper defends a view I've been calling participant calibration. The short version: I argue that the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, guilt, forgiveness — are not justified by a fact about the agent's ultimate control or desert, but by their role in a distributed practice of expectation, repair, and relationship-maintenance. To be morally responsible, on this view, is to have a certain standing in that practice — to be a legitimate target of its demands and its repairs — rather than to possess a metaphysical property of being the ultimate origin of one's action. I also want to claim that this is not quietism: we can criticize and reform the practice, and the hard incompatibilist's call to drop retributive blame is best understood as an internal reform of participant calibration, not an exit from it. I've been calling the distinction between the causal ledger (who caused what) and the normative ledger (who owes what to whom) the two-ledger picture, and I use it to argue that determinism threatens the first ledger but not the second. I'm working in the Strawson tradition, but I want to push it in a more reformist direction than people usually do.

The audience is mostly criminal law theorists and doctrinal criminal law people who think in terms of mens rea, proportionality, and the expressive function of punishment; there are two Kantians who care about respect and accountability; one social psychologist who studies blame and emotion; and one retired judge who is skeptical of philosophy but cares about public policy. They are not Strawson specialists. They will have read the famous essay maybe once, in grad school, and they will be suspicious of anything that sounds like it lets people off the hook or makes responsibility a social construction. I'm especially worried about the Kantians and the judge.
