I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and each summer I run a session at a small ideas festival — a mixed crowd of about twenty adults, some academics and some curious generalists, seated at four or five round tables for a single seventy-five-minute slot. This year I want the session to be about escalation: why people who are perfectly lucid in the abstract keep pouring resources into a losing course of action, and why knowing better seems to make no difference. I want to do it with an auction rather than a talk. The game I have in mind is the one where a note is auctioned off in small increments, the highest bidder pays and collects the note, and the second-highest bidder also pays but gets nothing — the trap in which two bidders can drive the price past the value of the prize and both walk away worse off than if neither had raised a hand. Several people in the room will have read about it, so the bare demo won't teach them anything; what I want them to feel, in their hands, is two things the reading never delivers. First, that the escalation is a property of the rules rather than a failure of nerve or a lapse of arithmetic — that the trap closes on people who know exactly what is happening and are watching it happen. Second, that the obvious exits are themselves fragile: agreeing to stop, splitting the prize, capping the bids in advance. I want the escape hatch built into the activity as a live option at a specific point, so the group can actually try to take it and watch what becomes of the attempt, instead of being told afterwards that it wouldn't have held. And I want the auctioneer's side of the ledger to surface at some point — the fact that the only party guaranteed to profit is the one running the thing — because that is usually left out of the retellings.

I'd rather this not become a lecture with a token game bolted on; the rules and the timing should do the teaching, and the concepts should go unnamed until the debrief. No real money — tokens, or a running tally on a whiteboard, with a small non-monetary prize for the note. The tables should let me run several auctions at once and compare them, and I'd like at least one structural asymmetry between the tables so that the debrief has something to contrast. Please design it for me and format the response in the following sections, in this order:

- **The setup** (roughly 150 words): room layout, materials, group size, any roles, and how the tables are organized, including the asymmetry.
- **How the auction runs** (roughly 550–650 words): the rules, the bid increments, the payoff scheme in tokens, how the note is valued, the exact point and mechanism by which the escape hatch is offered and how a table takes it or fails to, and how you keep the auctioneer's profit visible.
- **The debrief** (roughly 400 words): a sequence of questions that gets the group to reconstruct the structure from what happened at their own table, what to name and at what moment, and how to use the contrast between tables.
- **What will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it** (roughly 250 words): the failure modes of the activity itself, not of the participants' reasoning.
- **What the activity cannot reproduce** (roughly 150 words): the feature of the idea that will have to be supplied by me, the facilitator, rather than felt by the room.
