My oldest friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about queueing, and we ended up somewhere neither of us expected. She has just returned from a country where the bus stop works on a free-for-all — no line, everyone pressing towards the door — and she told me, with a certain pride, that she had refused to join in. She stood to one side and let the crowd through, on the grounds that the practice was undignified and that nobody had ever asked her to agree to it. What unsettled her was the reaction. The people around her, who had no expectation that anyone would queue, did not treat her standing aside as harmless eccentricity. They treated it as a slight; and when she explained herself, they were not mollified but rather more annoyed.

We could not work out what they took her to have done. She had not harmed anyone, in any sense either of us could make precise. She had not broken a promise, or breached a rule she had accepted. She had not even made anyone worse off in the way that matters in a scrum, since the bus was not full and everybody got on. Yet there was plainly something there, and it was not nothing.

I have since started collecting instances of the same shape, and I would like to know whether the thing has a name. Standing on the left of the escalator; jaywalking across an empty road at three in the morning where the locals wait for the green man anyway; leaving no tip in a country where tipping is expected; keeping one's shoes on indoors. In each case compliance costs the compliant something, nobody can give a reason for the practice beyond the fact that it is done, and a breach that harms no one is resented all the same. The resentment, too, is oddly asymmetric: the compliant feel wronged, while the breacher feels no guilt, which suggests that whatever is going on is relational rather than harm-based.

Two further observations, which may or may not be essential, and which are really why I am writing. First, the resentment seems calibrated to how conspicuous the breach is, and to how visible the compliers' compliance was, rather than to the cost of compliance itself. Where the practice is broken out of sight — nobody knows whether the couple at the next table tipped — the sense of being wronged seems to evaporate; where the breach is on display, it is fierce. That is a strange thing for a genuine wrong to do, since genuine wrongs do not need an audience. Second, it seems to require that the practice still be alive. Where most people have already stopped doing the thing, the very same breach excites nothing at all, and the few who keep it up look merely quaint rather than wronged.

So: is there an established concept for what those people at the bus stop were tracking? I should say at once that a bare label will not satisfy me. I want the carving — what exactly the phenomenon is, and what it is not. In particular I want it distinguished from the following neighbours, which all seem too blunt: the general thought that a convention is binding simply by being a convention; the thought that one owes something back for benefits one has accepted from a shared arrangement; ordinary moral obligation, which clearly is not what is at stake here; and mere etiquette or rudeness, which feels too thin, because the bus-stop reaction had real heat in it. I also want to know whether my two further observations are part of the phenomenon or a red herring — that is, whether the wrongness really does depend on the breach being seen, and on the convention still being widely followed, or whether I have simply mistaken a feature of my examples for a feature of the thing.

And the visitor's case, which is where the argument started: does it matter that she had not agreed, did not know the practice, and could not have been expected to? Can a convention bind someone who is not a party to it, and if so, on what basis — or is the visitor's real offence something else entirely, such as failing to defer to the people whose home it is?

If there is no name for this, I would rather have a good coined one, with a definition and a few boundary cases, than a near-miss from the literature. The edges are what interest me: must the convention be beneficial on balance, must it be something one could opt out of, must it still command majority compliance, and does it matter whether the compliant could have avoided the cost themselves?
