I am trying to arrive at a principle of diachronic rationality, and I have a set of settled judgments that I want the principle to fit. The judgments concern agents who face sequences of choices over time, and especially the question of when such an agent is irrational rather than merely unlucky. The cases are these.

(1) *The cycle.* An agent prefers tea to coffee, coffee to water, and water to tea, all at the same time. Offered the pairwise exchanges at a small charge — her tea for coffee, her coffee for water, her water for tea — she accepts each, and after three exchanges holds what she began with and is out the charges. My judgment: her preferences are defective and she is irrational. The draining is a symptom of a real defect, not an accident of the tradesman's cleverness.

(2) *The commitment device.* An agent prefers, at six in the evening, to rise at seven; at seven she prefers to remain in bed. She therefore gives a friend five dollars in advance, returnable only if she is out of bed by seven. At seven, the prospect of forfeiting the five dollars gets her up. My judgment: entirely rational, and the arrangement is sensible. The payment is an instrument, not a defect.

(3) *The change of mind.* On Monday an agent prefers to spend Saturday at the shore and buys a ticket; on Saturday morning she prefers to stay at home and pays a small charge to exchange it. My judgment: not irrational. Her preferences altered, and paying to act on the preferences she now has is unobjectionable.

(4) *The foresighted agent.* An agent prefers, considering her life as a whole, not to be a smoker. She knows that at any given moment she will prefer to smoke. She therefore pays, now, for a treatment that will make smoking unpleasant to her later. My judgment: rational; the payment is a commitment device of the kind licensed by (2).

(5) *The improvident agent.* An agent in the situation of (4) does not anticipate her own future preferences. Each morning she buys a packet intending it to be her last, and each evening smokes it. My judgment: irrational, but the fault lies in her failure to predict herself. Her preferences need not change; only her beliefs about them need to improve.

(6) *The refuser.* An agent with the cyclical preferences of (1) knows that she would accept the exchanges and accordingly resolves in advance never to trade at all. My judgment: her conduct is blameless, but her preferences remain defective. She has spared herself the charges without repairing the fault.

(7) *The trader's mark.* An agent has no cyclical preferences at any moment. Her preferences do, however, change: at one time she prefers a violin to a bicycle, at another a bicycle to a violin. A tradesman arranges a long sequence of swaps, each of which she prefers at the time, taking a small charge at each turn, and repeats it indefinitely. My judgment: she is not irrational. Each of her preference states is perfectly orderly; the tradesman profits from change rather than from defect. Here, unlike in (1), I am unwilling to place the fault in the agent.

My tentative principle is that an agent is diachronically irrational just in case she is vulnerable to a money pump — just in case there is a sequence of exchanges, each of which she prefers at the time of making it, whose net effect leaves her worse off by her own lights. This gives the right verdict in (1). It is too broad, since it condemns (7); and it locates (5)'s fault in the wrong place, since (5)'s trouble seems to be a failure of foresight rather than of preference. It also acquits (6), whose preferences I nevertheless called defective. My suspicion is that the distinction I want turns on the difference between preferences that admit of no ordering at a time and preferences that admit of an ordering at each time but not across time, but I cannot state that distinction in a way that survives scrutiny.

I would like you to do the following. First, tell me whether any single principle delivers all seven judgments, or whether some must be abandoned; if the latter, say which, and why. Second, if a principle does the work, state it precisely, and identify for each clause the case that motivates it. Third, say what it predicts in four cases I have not considered: (a) an agent with cyclical preferences who is offered only two of the three goods, so that the trade closing the cycle never arises and she is never drained; (b) an agent with cyclical preferences who is charged nothing for the exchanges, so that she cycles back to her starting point at no cost; (c) an agent whose preferences are orderly at every moment and who correctly foresees that a tradesman will drain her by a long sequence of individually acceptable exchanges, each of which she still prefers at the time; and (d) an agent who lives for a single instant and makes exactly one choice, but whose preferences at that instant are cyclical. Fourth, tell me whether the principle you propose is a requirement of rationality at all, or only a requirement on agents who are to be represented as maximizing something, and whether it makes any difference to your answer whether the charges in these cases are large or small, or whether they exist at all.
