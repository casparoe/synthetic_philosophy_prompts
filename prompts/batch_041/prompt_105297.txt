I'm a philosophy PhD student and I sit on our regional health foundation's grants panel as the ethics member. We've got one pot of money and two proposals on the table: a big expansion of subsidised IVF for people who couldn't otherwise afford it, and an expansion of the local hospice. The chair's staff paper comes out against the IVF one on the grounds that bringing a happy person into existence isn't itself a benefit (adding a miserable person is bad, adding a happy one isn't good), so the new lives shouldn't count in the cost-effectiveness spreadsheet, and hospice wins. That feels too quick to me, and also suspiciously convenient. Draft the short opinion I'll read out at Thursdays meeting: a concrete recommendation about what we fund and how to weigh the two against each other, plus what I should say about the asymmetry itself -- in particular whether it can even be run in the case where the alternative isn't a miserable version of the same person but a different person never existing at all, and whether the two halves of it (adding misery is bad, adding happiness isn't good) can really be held together by one theory or are two theories wearing a trenchcoat. One thing the staff paper ignores: a lot of the people who'd take the subsidy would probably have had a kid anyway, just later or more cheaply, and I want the memo to say whether that changes how much work the creating-new-lives framing can do for us. Page and a half, no citations needed, and give the strongest version of the case I'm arguing against.
