I am a junior at a high school with a small philosophy club, which meets on Thursday afternoons and is run by a graduate student in philosophy who likes to close each meeting with what she calls a same-thing puzzle. I have been keeping a notebook of her rulings, at first as a joke and lately in earnest, because I have started to suspect that no single principle of hers generates all of them, and because my aunt and my uncle have been arguing about one of her puzzles at Thanksgiving for two years now. Her record on one family of cases is the part I keep returning to. She holds that a violin that has belonged to one family for four generations and has been played continuously, with its strings, bridge, soundpost, tailpiece, and bow hair replaced many times over, is still the same violin. She holds that the same violin, after its neck and scroll were snapped off in a fall and glued back on by a restorer, is still the same violin; that the same violin, after its belly was cracked and repaired with cleats and a small patch of new spruce, is still the same violin; that the same violin, after its varnish was stripped and redone, is still the same violin; and that the same violin, left unstrung in an attic for forty years and then restored, is still the same violin. She holds, on the other hand, that a new violin made last year by a luthier from new wood to the same measurements, pattern, and varnish colour is a different violin altogether, and that the family violin, crushed under a car into a heap of splinters, no longer exists — though she added, when my brother pressed her on it, that the wood itself plainly survives as splinters, and that the wood and the violin were never the same object to begin with.

The case she has been putting off is the one I would like help with, since she has promised to rule on it at our last meeting of the year. A shop takes on the family violin, which by that point has been in continuous use for ninety years, and over the following fifty years of annual restorations replaces, one part at a time, the belly, the back, the ribs, the bass bar, the neck, and the scroll, so that at the end not a sliver of the original wood remains. Throughout, the instrument is never out of use for more than a month at a stretch, never ceases to be a violin of the same pattern, and is entered in the shop's books and treated by the family as the continuing family violin; the aunt I mentioned insists that it will still be the family violin when it is entirely new wood, and my uncle insists that it will by then be a copy of the family violin.

What I want is less a bare yes or no than the reasoning underneath it. Which rules would generate her earlier verdicts, what does each of those rules predict about the fifty-year restoration, which prediction should I bet on and why, and what further case could I put to her that would force whichever rules are still standing to come apart?
