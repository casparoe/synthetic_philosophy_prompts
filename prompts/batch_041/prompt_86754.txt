So I've somehow agreed to give the after-dinner talk at our hospital foundation's pain clinic benefit next month. Five minutes, six tops, in front of maybe two hundred people: donors, half the clinic staff, my husband, who will mock me regardless. And two of the guests of honor are former patients, and I can't stop thinking about them together.

The first is a guy in his sixties who had a stroke and now has what our neurologists call pain asymbolia — he still feels pain, he can point to it, rate it an eight, tell you it's burning, but it doesn't bother him. He'll describe the worst pain of his life with a little smile, like he's giving you the weather. (If you don't know the term, just go off what I've described, don't make stuff up.) The second is a nine year old with congenital insensitivity to pain, meaning no pain sensation at all, ever, and she's got a burn scar on one arm and has broken I think four bones, because her body never tells her to stop.

Here's my problem. This year's appeal is basically 'a life without pain,' and I'm supposed to get up and sell that. But the kid in the front row IS a life without pain, and it isn't a life anyone in that room would want for their own kid. And the man with asymbolia has all the pain and none of the misery, which seems to mean the awful part isn't the sensation itself. So what am I actually asking people to fund?

Write the talk. It needs to move people to give, it needs to be honest about that split, and it can't imply that the people in the room who are suffering are suffering because they aren't tough enough or calm enough. No lecture, no TED talk about reframing, and no citations, I don't want a literature review at a dinner. American English please.
