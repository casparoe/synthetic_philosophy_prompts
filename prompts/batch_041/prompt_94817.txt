I am a high school student preparing a project on the moral status of insects, and I have been trying to reverse-engineer my own intuitions. I keep arriving at verdicts that do not obviously fall under a single principle, and I would like you to tell me what principle, or small set of principles, actually captures them — and, if none does, which verdict I should abandon. Please do not give me a reading list; I want the principle stated precisely and defended against the cases.

Here are my verdicts, stated as I actually have them.

A single housefly lands on my arm. I wave it away rather than swat it, but if I swat it I do not think I have done anything wrong. Vanishingly bad at most.

An exterminator clears a wasp nest by my door, killing roughly two thousand wasps. I would prefer relocation if it were easy, but I do not think the killing is wrong.

A biology class kills a thousand fruit flies for a genetics experiment. Fine, no misgivings.

The same class kills thirty mice. Not fine unless the knowledge is important; the mice are owed something the flies are not.

A silk scarf required boiling perhaps a thousand silkworm pupae. I feel a genuine unease here — noticeably more than for the two thousand wasps — though it is small.

Agriculture kills something on the order of a trillion insects a year through pesticides and harvest. This strikes me as a serious moral problem, possibly one of the largest there is, and I would support spending real resources on it.

If we could halve pesticide use and spare roughly five hundred billion insects at some cost to yield and price, I think it would be worth doing.

But I do not believe the total suffering of all insects should outweigh the total suffering of all humans and vertebrates, or even approach it. That conclusion I reject.

The difficulty, as I see it, is this. Cases three through five treat each insect as worth essentially nothing; a linear sum with a very small per-insect weight reproduces cases one through seven rather neatly. But that same sum, extended to the trillions in cases six and seven, predicts a total that dwarfs every other moral consideration, which contradicts case eight. So either aggregation fails at some scale, or the per-insect weight is not constant, or my case-eight verdict is incoherent.

To make the question sharp, imagine a dial that reports, for any species, a number between zero and one: the probability that a member of that species has experiences that matter morally. For insects I would set it low — maybe one in twenty — but not zero; for mice, close to one. I want a principle that takes a number of individuals and such a probability and yields a verdict.

Please give me the principle, or the competing candidates, stated precisely. For each clause, name the case that forces it. Then apply it to cases I have not settled: what does it say if we learn insects are certainly not sentient, and what if we learn they certainly are, with a fly's experience as vivid as ours? Does it matter whether the deaths are intended, as in silk, or are a byproduct, as with pesticides? Is there a coherent duty that attaches to destroying a whole colony rather than to each individual death?

Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should revise the principle or the verdict. If the only formulation that saves every verdict is an ad hoc rule — a tiny weight per insect that somehow becomes significant only when the numbers are enormous — say so plainly, and explain what is wrong with it.
