I write long-form features on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been commissioned to write a piece on whether it makes sense to say that AI systems have beliefs. I have roughly six weeks of reporting — call it fifteen or twenty interviews and a lot of background reading — and about 6,000 words. My working angle is that the live dispute is not whether chatbots are conscious but whether belief-talk about them is doing legitimate work, and if so, what kind. My notes keep sorting my sources into three ways of attributing beliefs: behaviorally, by treating the system as a rational agent whose outputs can be predicted from what it believes; mechanistically, by reading internal states and calling a decoded representation a belief; and institutionally — my own working label — by attributing beliefs the way the law and the market already attribute intentions to corporations, not because anyone supposes there is a mind in there, but because some entity has to be the bearer of the attitude for the purposes of responsibility, liability, and oversight. The intentional stance, in the sense Daniel Dennett gave the phrase, is plainly the ancestor of the first of these, and I would rather the piece not be a retread of that debate; I want it to land on the questions that are actually live now.

What I want from you is an agenda, not a survey. Of the open questions in this area, which three or four most deserve my six weeks, and in what order? Among the candidates I am aware of: whether the intentional stance survives when the beliefs in question are relative to a context window and shift with framing, so that the constancy we assume in ordinary belief attribution simply fails; whether a system's self-report about its own beliefs should carry any evidential weight at all; whether the mechanistic route yields a notion of belief that the behavioral route cannot, or merely a different vocabulary for the same predictive regularities; whether any of this bears on the moral-status question, or whether status turns on sentience and the belief question is a distraction; and whether the institutional stance is a coherent position or merely a way of changing the subject.

Rank them for my purposes and defend the ranking against whatever criteria you think appropriate — how much each question would change what the piece can claim, how many of the others depend on it, whether six weeks of reporting can move it at all, and whether it is already crowded with people making the same points. Be explicit about which questions are conceptual and will not be settled by anything I can do as a reporter, and which ones my reporting could actually resolve. Then tell me what would move an item up or down the list: what a source would have to say, or what a result would have to show, for me to reassign my time. Assume I have no appetite for a piece that concludes the question is merely verbal, unless you think that is genuinely the right answer and can say why. I do not need a reading list; I need to know where the weight of the argument lies and where my attention would be wasted.
