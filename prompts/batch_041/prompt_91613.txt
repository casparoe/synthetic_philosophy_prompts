I cover housing and urban policy, and there is a phrase I keep failing to pin down. At a zoning hearing last month, an organizer opposing a market-rate tower said the existing residents had "the right to the city" and that the approval process had therefore been illegitimate; the developer's counsel answered that no such right exists in law and that ordinary property rights settle the matter; and a council member, defending a modest upzoning nearby, said the right to the city simply means that everyone ought to be able to afford to live in the city that employs them. Three people, one phrase, three incompatible jobs for it to do, and I suspect at least two of them are talking past each other.

I don't want the phrase's history or a reading list. I want it taken apart. Lay out the distinct, precise claims "the right to the city" could be making, ranging from propositions almost nobody would deny to propositions that would reorganize how a city is governed. For each, state the claim so that it could be true or false; identify who is supposed to hold the right (current residents, all inhabitants, the presently excluded, future generations); what the right is a right to (use, occupancy, a say in decisions, affordability, appropriation of space); and whether the claim is legal, moral, or a claim about democratic legitimacy. Say for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and whether it would actually license any of the three positions I heard — and where one reading collapses into another, or cannot be made precise at all, say so rather than papering it over.

Give me the result as a table, one row per reading, with these columns:

- Reading: a short name for the distinct claim.
- Precise claim: one or two sentences, stated so it could be true or false.
- Kind: legal / moral / political-legitimacy / rhetorical.
- Right-holder: who is supposed to have the right.
- Object of the right: what the right is a right to.
- Verdict: platitude, substantive, or false.
- Defensible?: yes or no, with one clause of justification.
- Supports which speaker: organizer, developer's counsel, council member, or none.
- Notes: the strongest objection to the reading, or the reason it fails.

After the table, add a short paragraph naming which reading I should most suspect the organizer of intending, and which reading is most likely functioning as a slogan that does no real argumentative work in the hearing I attended.
