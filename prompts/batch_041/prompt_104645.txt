I'm a science journalist, mostly covering animals and food systems, and I've spent the last few months reporting on the farmed-insect boom — crickets, mealworms, black soldier fly — which keeps dragging me into a fight I can't stop noticing the shape of. The argument goes like this: insects are farmed and killed in numbers that make the human population look like a rounding error (people throw around figures like a billion billion), nobody actually knows whether they feel pain, and so even if the chance that a given insect is sentient is small, you multiply it by that many individuals and insect welfare becomes the biggest moral issue on the planet, bigger than factory farming, bigger than anything. Some people call it the scale argument, I think. Fine. What I want to know is whether there's a name for what happens next, and for the shape of the arguemnt itself.

Because here's the pattern. The whole force of the claim hangs on a product: one factor that's basically a census, and one factor that's basically a shrug. And in every version of this debate I've sat through, the two sides argue straight past each other by attacking different factors. The people making the argument say the burden is on you to show the probability is essentially zero — if it's even one in a thousand, just look at what happens. The people rejecting it say you'd have to show the probability is actually non-trivial before anyone should take it seriously. Nobody argues about the multiplication, and nobody argues about whether 'a big enough multiplier can rescue any probability, and a small enough probability can deflate any multiplier' is itself a reason to distrust this whole style of reasoning. I've seen the same shape in AI-risk talk and in precautionary-principle fights about new tech, so I don't think it's about bugs specifically. Is there an established concept for an argument whose entire weight rests on the product of a well-measured quantity and a barely-constrained one — and especially for the failure mode where each side burns all its energy on the factor the other side isn't even disputing?

I know it's somewhere near Pascal's mugging and near expected-value reasoning in general, but the mugging has a mugger stipulating the payoff out of nowhere, and here nothing is stipulated — the big number is a real count and the small number is an honest unknown — so I don't think that's it. If there's no name, coin one, but please tell me where it sits relative to Pascal's mugging, the precautionary principle, and plain scope neglect, and where the edges of the thing are. Like, does it still apply when the shaky factor is a genuinely known frequency rather than a range of competing guesses? And does it matter that both sides keep treating a guess as if it were a measurement the moment it gets multiplied by something solid?
