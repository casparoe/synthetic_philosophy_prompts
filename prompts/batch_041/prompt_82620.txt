Some years ago I retired from a career in experimental physics — thirty-five years at the bench — and since then I have been reading philosophy with more appetite than system. One problem has lodged in me and will not come out, and I would like your help with it in a particular form.

I had always assumed that when one theory displaces another, the successor is closer to the truth than the predecessor, and that this is most of what the word progress means. I now understand that this notion — truthlikeness, as the literature calls it — is far harder to make precise than I assumed, and I would rather watch two capable people fight over it than be handed the conclusion.

The difficulty, as I have come to see it, is this. The obvious proposal is that a theory is closer to the truth the more true consequences it has and the fewer false ones. But take two theories that are both false. If one is stronger than the other it will have more true consequences and also more false ones, and nothing tells me how to trade these off against each other. And I gather the trouble is worse than a missing exchange rate: on that sort of account no false theory comes out closer to the truth than another, which would be fatal, since the comparisons I care about are almost always among theories that are all false. The other proposal I have met with moves from consequences to resemblance: set out the ways the world might have been, order them by how much they resemble the actual world, and call a theory closer to the truth the closer to the actual world are the possibilities it permits. That is much more like what I mean by nearly right — but the resemblance between two ways the world might have been looks like something we supply rather than something we find.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers who hold opposing views on whether truthlikeness can be given a content worth having.

The first holds that it can. Give her the strongest resources rather than a caricature: the resemblance proposal above; the argument that the predictive success of our theories would be a miracle unless they were at least approximately true; and the historical case of the theory of light that posited a medium pervading all space, whose equations were carried over almost intact into the theory that abolished the medium. She should be ready to say what the notion of truthlikeness does that saving the phenomena does not, and to say what fixes the resemblance ordering if not our own sense of which differences matter.

The second holds that it cannot. Her best case: that every repair either collapses as the consequence-counting proposal did, or relocates the whole burden onto a resemblance metric whose weights we choose, so that the ranking presupposes the judgments it was meant to ground; that the medium case shows only that old equations can be recovered as a limiting case, which is a fact about mathematics and domains of application rather than about the old theory having been nearly true, since its central term named nothing at all; and that if two rival metrics rank two false theories in opposite orders, nothing in the world decides between them. She should concede whatever the realist gets right.

Keep them on the question whether the notion can be defined at all, not on whether we could ever know the ranking; I can see the second problem for myself, and it is the first that interests me.

Requirements: real back-and-forth, each responding to what the other actually said; neither a strawman; each willing to concede a point but not the main one; no names of philosophers, books, or papers, and no reading list, since I have no library here and want the argument itself; every technical term explained at first use; and no tidy reconciliation at the end. Close with the two of them identifying as precisely as they can what would have to be settled, or what fact, if any, would decide between them. No more than nine hundred words.
