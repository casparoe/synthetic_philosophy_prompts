Some years ago I promised my mother that the house she had lived in all her married life would never be sold out of the family, and I meant it when I said it. Some years later, when my daughter was seventeen and in a bad way, I promised her — and here too I meant it — that she would never have to live in that town again, which I could only manage by selling the house. I sold it, my daughter is now well and gone to university, and my brother, who regards the house as something our mother left to the two of us to keep, has not spoken to me since. What I cannot get past is not the grief or the quarrel but the conviction that I was in the wrong: not merely that the whole business was wretched, or that I hurt people, but that I did something I should not have done, and would have done something I should not have done whichever way I turned. That conviction has survived every argument I have been able to make against it, and I would like you to tell me whether it should.

Three things make me doubt my own reading of it, and I would rather have them addressed than set aside.

First, my brother's case against me is that I could have kept both promises had I arranged my affairs differently years earlier, so that the collision was of my own making. Is a collision you have brought about yourself the same sort of thing as one that simply falls on you? And if it is not, what does that do to my claim that I had no choice?

Second, a friend in a comparable position — she had to choose between keeping a promise and answering an emergency that no one could have foreseen — tells me that our cases are quite different, because hers involved two entirely different sorts of claim on her whereas mine involved two promises of exactly the same kind, both freely made, both to people I love. Is she right that this matters? And if it does matter, does it matter because claims of the same kind cannot genuinely collide, or only because a collision between them is harder to see?

Third, I remain aware of owing my brother something, even though I am not sorry for what I did and would do it again. Is that a debt, or only a feeling? And does the answer to that question tell me anything about whether I really was in the wrong either way, or does it leave the main question exactly where it was?

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Please write in British English.
