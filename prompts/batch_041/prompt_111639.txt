I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so sorry in advance if this is something that's obvious to everyone but me.

There's a distinction I keep needing in dialogue and I've never found a name for it. Three cases.

First: a character tells her husband she spent the afternoon at her sisters, and she didn't. She knows it's false and she wants him to believe it. That's a lie, I have that word.

Second: someone asks her if she's read the letter and she answers 'the letter is still on the hall table.' Every word of that is true, and the whole point of saying it is that the other person walks away thinking she hasn't read it. Nothing false was said, and yet she did something, and it isn't the same thing she did in case one.

Third, and this is the one that sent me down the hole: a woman stands up in a room and confesses to something she didn't do. Everyone in the room knows she didn't do it and knows why she's saying it, because they more or less made her. Nobody there can possibly be fooled. So did she lie? It feels wrong to call it a lie, since no one is deceived, and even wronger to say she didn't.

I have a vague memory that Augustine said something like a lie is a lie because of the intention to deceive, and that he wouldn't allow one even to save somebody's life. If the intention to deceive is the whole story then case three isn't a lie, which I definately don't buy. If it's the falseness of what you say that matters, then case two isn't a lie either, and that seems off too.

So is there a name for the case-three thing? And more to the point, where do you actually draw these lines — is the intention to deceive essential, does someone who says something false while believing it true count, and what do you call a false statement whose entire purpose is to be seen through? I'm less interested in a label than in the seams, so if nothing established fits, just say so and give me a term with the edges drawn in.
