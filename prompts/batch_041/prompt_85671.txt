so my logic seminar has spent basically the whole term circling the problem of logical omniscience, and the more we poke at it the less it feels like a technical wart and the more it feels like a haunted house. the thing i can't get out of my head: in the standard epistemic logics, an agent who knows something automatically knows everything that follows from it, gets every tautology for free, and — if they ever hold two beleifs that can't both be true — knows literally everything there is to know. which means an ideal reasoner can't learn anything by reasoning, because the conclusion was already sitting in the premises, and can't ever be surprised.

i want to close the last session with a short poem that enacts this instead of just describing it. the constraint i actually care about: make it a closed loop. the final line should be the opening line, word for word, so the reader finishes exactly where they started and has been handed nothing they didn't already have in the first line. and give it a refrain that is just a bare tautology — pick your favorite, something like "either it is or it isn't" — coming back every stanza, so that each time the poem tries to get somewhere, a sentence that was already true before it began swallows the gain. 4 to 6 stanzas, free verse, no need to rhyme.

the voice should be the ideal reasoner themself. i want one stanza where they try to not-know something and it fails — they can't put a consequence back in the box, they can't manage a genuinely new thought. and somewhere near the middle, the turn: the moment they clock that because they know even one thing they already know every tautology, so their mind is either empty or infinite and there's no room in between for a life. don't explain the logic inside the poem, let the shape carry it. if someone in the room already knows the problem they should feel it in their teeth, and if they don't, they should still come out a little spooked.
