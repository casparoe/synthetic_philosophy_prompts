I'm a third-year philosophy student in a course on the ethics of artificial intelligence, and I want to think through a version of the orthogonality and instrumental-convergence arguments that our seminar hasn't covered. Orthogonality says that an agent's level of intelligence and the content of its ultimate goals vary independently; instrumental convergence says that a wide range of ultimate goals nonetheless share the same instrumental subgoals, such as self-preservation and resource acquisition. Now suppose we take seriously that a contemporary system is trained rather than hand-coded, so that what we casually call its goal is really the fixed point of a reward channel the system can itself act upon. Please describe the most important implications of these two ideas under that supposition — for what it means to give such a system a goal at all, for how much danger we should expect from a very capable one, and for which interventions would and would not reduce that danger.
