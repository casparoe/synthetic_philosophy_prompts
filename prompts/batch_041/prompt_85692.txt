I am a novelist, and in three weeks I am giving a twenty-five-minute paper to a cognitive science reading group that has spent the term working through the fluid-analogies tradition: the Copycat line of work in which a program solves letter-string puzzles — abc is changed to abd, so what should ijk become? — and in which the interesting event is never the answer but the slippage, the way the program's own concepts of successor, of sameness, of what counts as a letter drift as the temperature parameter lets one construal of the problem rather than another take hold. My thesis in the talk is that this is a better model of how a novelist actually makes a metaphor than anything the rhetoric handbooks offer: that what we praise as the aptness of an image is not the satisfaction of a rule but the settling of a competition among construals, and that the settling is a describable process rather than a mystery. To keep the argument out of the letter-string sandbox that everyone in the room knows by heart, I have built a small puzzle family in a microdomain of my own invention, punctuation. Comma is to semicolon as colon is to what? Period, if the operative relation is escalation; dash, if it is introduction; semicolon, if it is clause-joining at differing weights. I run a dozen of these to show that the same base pair will support different slippages depending on which relation the solver lets dominate, and then I claim that the writer's craft consists largely in arranging for one construal to win without ever stating the rule.

The room is not friendly in the way I would like it to be. Most of them know the architecture from the inside — the codelets, the slipnet, the way randomness there functions as a search heuristic rather than as noise — and will not be moved by the word emergent; two are literary theorists who think any computational account of metaphor is a category mistake dressed up as a flow chart; and one is from a deep learning lab and will ask, courteously, why anyone should care about a hand-coded system from the 1990s when a large language model can produce the punctuation analogies and their construals with no slipnet at all. Draft me the Q&A prep sheet. I want the eight or ten objections I will actually get from this room, phrased the way each faction would phrase them — the architecture people in terms of codelets and temperature, the literary theorists in terms of what is lost when a reading becomes a search, the deep learning person in terms of scale and hand-coding — ordered by how much damage they do rather than by how likely they are, each with a two-minute reply, a note on what I should concede before attempting the reply, and a note on where the reply is softest. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. And I would rather be told where I am wrong than be handed a defense of my position: name the single objection that, if it held up, should make me abandon the claim that aptness is emergent rather than rule-governed, and say where your own reasoning about my argument is likeliest to fail.
