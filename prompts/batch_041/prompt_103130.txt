I belong to a local politics group that meets monthly, and for the past few months we have been arguing about two proposals: a statewide term-limits initiative that will be on the ballot in the spring, and a suggestion from one of our members that our city replace its elected council with a citizens' assembly whose members would be drawn by lot. The argument always ends the same way. One member, a retired teacher with no particular background in this subject, closes every discussion by saying that power tends to corrupt, and he treats that sentence as though it settled both questions at once — it is, in his view, the whole case for term limits, and when the sortition proposal came up he produced the very same sentence as the whole case for that as well. Occasionally he adds the second half of the line, that absolute power corrupts absolutely, though I cannot see that it changes anything he says. I have tried to push back, but I cannot even state clearly what I am denying. If I object that a particular officeholder seemed to have been dishonest long before he was ever elected, we end up trading anecdotes about individual politicians, and the conversation never gets around to the sentence itself. Meanwhile another member insists the saying is unfalsifiable and therefore worthless, since every bad officeholder confirms it and every decent one can be waved away as an exception, and I am not convinced that is right either.

What I want is a menu, laid out as a list I can take to the next meeting, of the distinct precise claims that one sentence could actually be making, with a verdict on each. I would like to know what exactly is supposed to be corrupted — a person's character, their judgement, their incentives, the information available to them, or their accountability to anyone who might check them — and whether the claim is a causal one, that holding power makes people worse, or instead a claim about selection, that people who pursue power are already bad, or that the good ones simply decline to compete for it. I would also like to know whether it is meant to be a claim about individuals at all, or about institutions that reward and punish certain behaviour regardless of who fills them; what work the word "tends" is doing, whether it marks a statistical claim, a rough rule with exceptions, or a tautology in disguise; and what standard of corruption is being applied, since I suspect that a merely self-interested politician and a genuinely wicked one are being run together.

Then tell me which of these readings are platitudes that nobody would deny, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false or empty. Most importantly, tell me which readings would actually support the term-limits argument and which would support the sortition argument, whether those two proposals need the same reading or competing ones, and whether either proposal survives on the readings that hold up. And I would like to know which reading people ordinarily have in mind when they use the line to end a conversation rather than to start one, since that is the version I have to answer.
