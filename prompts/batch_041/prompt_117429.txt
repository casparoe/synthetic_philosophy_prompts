I'm in a philosophy of biology reading group, and we've spent the last two weeks on the question of whether calling DNA a code is a harmless shorthand or a substantive claim about what genes do. One of the other students, who is convinced it's the latter and wants to bury it, wrote an argument on the board that I have not been able to get out of, and I'm suspicious of it precisely because it seems to prove far more than he needs. Here it is as he numbered it. (1) A code is a mapping from symbols to items, and what distinguishes a code from a mere causal regularity is that the mapping is arbitrary: nothing about the symbol itself fixes what it stands for. (2) The genetic code is arbitrary in exactly this sense. Nothing in the chemistry of a triplet makes it a fit for the amino acid it specifies, and the mapping is not even constant across life, since the codon UGA is a stop signal in the standard code but specifies tryptophan in vertebrate mitochondria; the same symbol does different work in different lineages. (3) An arbitrary mapping is a convention: it is fixed by the use of interpreters rather than by nature. (4) So the genetic code is a convention, and conventions require users. Since a cell does not interpret anything the way a reader interprets a text, we must either posit an interpreter, that is, a designer or a reader of the message, or conclude that the genetic code is a metaphor and that genetic information names no real feature of the world. (5) Any biology that wants to explain development by appeal to information has to take the first horn. (6) But no serious biology will accept a designer. (7) Therefore the informational vocabulary of molecular biology is at best a metaphor and does no explanatory work.

I would rather not give up (2). The non-universality of the code seems to me a genuine and striking datum, and I don't want to be the person who rescues the metaphor by insisting that the assignments are somehow read off the chemistry after all. But if I keep (2) and (3) together, I appear to be stuck choosing between a designer and the claim that all of molecular biology's talk of codes, messages, and programs is decorative.

Tell me which step to give up, why it looked acceptable in the first place, and what it costs me to give it up. In particular: can I hold on to a notion of information that does real explanatory work while conceding that DNA is not a code in the sense of (1)? If I can, what becomes of the intuition that a mutation is an error, a misreading, rather than just another chemical event, and do I lose anything else I care about by demoting the code talk to a metaphor? And does the argument, as he has laid it out, prove too much in a way that gives it away, or is its reach exactly what he claims it is?
