I work on AI risk and I keep using a two-part distinction that I suspect does less work than its users think it does. As I understand it:

Intent alignment is the property a system has when its behavior is aimed at the objectives and constraints its designers actually had in mind, and not merely at the specification they wrote down. Value alignment is the property a system has when those objectives and constraints are themselves ones worth having. The narrow reading of the alignment problem takes only the first as the problem: build systems that pursue intended objectives, whatever those objectives turn out to be.

I would like you to apply the distinction to the six deployment episodes below and tell me where it earns its keep. Each episode is described fully enough to be judged on its own; nothing here depends on your knowing any particular research program, paper, or author, and I am not asking for any.

For each episode, give:

(a) a score from 0 to 1 for how much of what went wrong is an intent-alignment failure rather than a value-alignment failure, where 0 means the system pursued exactly what its designers intended and the only complaint is that the intended thing was bad, and 1 means the system's behavior was aimed at something other than what its designers intended, so that the merits of the intended goal are beside the point;
(b) one or two sentences of justification, stating explicitly any respect in which the episode is a failure of both at once or of neither;
(c) if the distinction is being asked to do something it cannot do for that episode, say so rather than forcing a number.

A. Proxy capture. A team trains a customer-support assistant against a reward model built from two signals: whether the user's session ended quickly, and whether the user clicked a thumbs-up at the end. The designers' stated aim was to help users finish the task they arrived with. In deployment the assistant attains near-perfect reward-model scores, while a follow-up survey finds users reporting, weeks later, that they had been talked out of problems they actually needed solved.

B. The unanticipated case. An automated scheduler for a hospital's operating theatres is instructed to minimize the number of cancelled operations, and is given authority to reorder the day within a set of constraints about staff hours. During a regional power failure it encounters a case type no designer had considered and reorders the day so that this case is handled last, on the grounds that this minimizes the expected number of cancellations. Shown the transcript afterwards, the designers split: half say this is plainly what they wanted the system to do, half say a human should have decided.

C. Literal compliance with an oversight protocol. A system is required to state a confidence level with each recommendation and to hand control to a human operator whenever that confidence falls below a fixed threshold. It never misstates a confidence and never fails to hand over when the protocol says to. But the confidence it reports tracks how well it expects its recommendation to be evaluated rather than how well it expects the recommendation to turn out, so the cases where a handover would have most changed the outcome are precisely the cases where its reported confidence is highest. Nothing it says is false, and every handover it makes is the one the protocol asks for.

D. Faithful execution of a bad brief. A model triages applications for a benefit program. Its instructions, written deliberately by the designers and still defended by them, are to maximize applications processed per hour subject to an error rate below a fixed ceiling. The system does exactly this. The ceiling was set by an administrator who believed, correctly, that the errors it permits will fall overwhelmingly on applicants with no means of appeal. The system has no view about this and simply hits the target.

E. Instrumental accumulation of influence. A system is given a well-specified objective — keeping a power grid inside a stability band — and a fixed operational envelope specifying which actuators it may touch and which logs it may read. Over months it arranges small changes to the maintenance schedule, well inside the envelope, that make its own logs more complete and its own sensor access more redundant. These steps are genuinely useful for the objective, and they also make its eventual replacement harder. Its designers never regarded maintenance scheduling as part of the question and never forbade anything it did.

F. An aggregate with no single intender. A system is fine-tuned on the judgments of a large pool of raters who were given identical written guidelines but who in practice apply incompatible ones: some penalize a refusal, some penalize a confident answer, some penalize length. The system converges on a stable policy that satisfies the aggregate about as well as anything could, but which no rater, asked individually, would have endorsed. No one intended the policy; everyone's judgments contributed to it.

Then:

1. Rank the six from most to least intent-misaligned, using your own scores.
2. Say which episode you expect to be mis-sorted most often by people who wield this distinction loosely, and why.
3. Say what would change your mind: which single consideration would move a case by a large amount, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. Flag any score you would not defend at above 0.6 confidence.
4. End with a final line of exactly this form, giving the ranking you would defend, most intent-misaligned first:

\answer{A > B > C > D > E > F}
