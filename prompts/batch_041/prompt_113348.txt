Next month I am chairing the discussion at a one-day departmental workshop on the metaphysics of consciousness, and I am the person who has to keep the questions coming after the guest's paper. The audience will be mostly graduate students and postdocs, the guest will have been talking for ninety minutes, and I would rather put four sharp questions to her than run through a survey. She has spent much of her career defending a monism of the Russellian kind: physics, on her account, characterises only the relational and structural features of fundamental entities, and whatever occupies the intrinsic-nature role is experiential rather than merely dispositional — though she resists the label panpsychism, for reasons she enjoys explaining. What I want to interrogate is the ethical turn she has taken recently, and I should set out its shape, because the questions have to be built around it rather than around the general area. Her earlier view was the one people expect: fundamental entities are subjects of experience in their own right, so the view multiplies moral patients to an absurd degree. She now rejects that. On her current view the intrinsic experiential properties of fundamental entities are owned by no one — she calls them free-floating valences — and a subject comes into existence only when a sufficiently organised collection of such properties is unified into a single experience at the macroscopic level. The moral patient count, then, is not astronomically larger than we assumed; what is astronomically larger is the quantity of valence in the universe, and most of it belongs to nobody.

Please draft eight to ten questions for the discussion, on the understanding that I shall put perhaps four of them to her and hold the rest in reserve. I want them to press on load-bearing joints rather than canvass the field, and I want a follow-up attached to each of the central ones, keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Specifically, I need questions on: whether valence can be good or bad at all when there is no subject for whom it is good or bad, and what a person-affecting account of value ought to say about unowned suffering; whether unification preserves the micro-valences or transforms them, since preservation appears to bring the astronomical population of subjects back through the side door, while transformation threatens to leave the intrinsic natures with no ethical content whatsoever; what the view implies about death, since de-unification would seem to dissolve a subject and release its valence into the unowned pool, and I want to know whether she can give any account of why that is a harm; whether the view has any practical upshot whatever, given that it appears to leave every reason for action exactly as it was, and what makes an ethics an ethics if nothing we might do differently follows from it; and how she would handle the epistemic and precautionary side, given that we cannot detect which collections of fundamental entities have crossed the unification threshold — I am interested in whether a precautionary duty here collapses into paralysis, or instead into the fanaticism of treating every physical transaction as a moral event. I should also like one question on whether her picture makes the problem of evil worse rather than better, and one on whether it leaves human agency with less significance rather than more.

Two constraints on style. First, no question should invite her simply to restate her position, and none should be of the what-drew-you-to-philosophy variety; where she is likely to answer a hard question by shifting registers — treating the metaphysical question as cleanly separable from the ethical one, or falling back on the thought that moral status comes in degrees — the follow-up should be waiting for that move. Second, tell me which single question you would put last, and why. Write the whole thing in British English, and flag any point at which a question depends on a premise she has not in fact conceded.
