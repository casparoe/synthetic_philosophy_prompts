OK so I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of science — my own work is on causal inference in epidemiology, so I'm a bit of a tourist in formal epistemology — and my supervisor has voluntold me to give a 90-minute guest session in a colleague's MSc course on the foundations of statistics. The audience is stats and clinical epi masters students: they can do the maths fine, they've all had measure-theoretic probability, but they've never had to think about what probability *is* philosophically, and honestly most of them think the philosophy part is a soft add-on they can skip past to get to the estimators.

The topic is the reference class problem. I don't need you to explain it to me — I need a teaching plan. What has to be in place before the problem even registers as a problem for them (my hunch is the first move has to be getting them to see that probabilities attach to events-under-a-description rather than to the events themselves, but tell me if that's wrong or if something else has to come first); then the order the pieces go in, so that by the end they see why the "just condition on everything you know" line doesn't get you out — they will ALL say this in the first ten minutes, I guarentee it — and why more data doesn't rescue you either. I want one case that carries the whole session: a single concrete scenario we come back to three or four times and that gets harder each time we look at it. My instinct is something actuarial or clinical, a person who is simultaneously a member of two or three classes with genuinely different base rates and no fact of the matter about which class is *the* class, maybe with an insurance-pricing or medico-legal payoff so they feel the stakes. But I don't want the coin, the lottery, or Sleeping Beauty as the carrying case — they've seen all of those and they'll coast. Tell me what the case needs to have and what it must not have (a case where one class is obviously the right one would kill the whole point).

Then the misconception map: which misconceptions they'll arrive with — I'm expecting "it's a problem for frequentists, not Bayesians", "the narrowest or most specific class wins", "this is just the problem of induction wearing a hat", "the right reference class is whatever the data says it is" — and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, plus one or two they'll form *during* the session that I won't see coming. The thing I care about most: one question to ask at the end that would actually tell me whether they've got it, as opposed to whether they can recite the dialectic back at me. And finally, what to cut. I have 90 minutes and I know I'll want to talk about the exchangeability move and about how this bites in risk prediction and recidivism scoring; I'd rather cut one of those cleanly and say out loud that I'm cutting it than rush both.
