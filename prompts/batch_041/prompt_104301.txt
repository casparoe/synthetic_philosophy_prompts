I cover international law and ideas, and I want a case analysis of one episode: the 2004–05 dispute over whether what was happening in Darfur should be called genocide. In September 2004, Colin Powell told the Senate Foreign Relations Committee that genocide had been committed there and that the Sudanese government and the Janjaweed bore responsibility; the UN's International Commission of Inquiry, chaired by Antonio Cassese and reporting in January 2005, found that the government and its militia allies had committed war crimes and crimes against humanity on a massive scale, but concluded that a policy of genocide had not been pursued, because the specific intent to destroy a group as such could not be established, whatever the intent of particular perpetrators may have been. Analyze that episode through Lemkin's conception of genocide — his insistence that the crime is the destruction of a group's social and cultural existence and not only the killing of its members, and his account of it as a two-phase process in which the oppressor destroys the national pattern of the oppressed and imposes its own — and through the politics of naming that surrounds the legal category. Do the mapping carefully: what did each party take the name to do, legally, politically, and morally, and what did each think was at stake in withholding it? Then run the analysis in the other direction. Where does Lemkin's broader conception fit the facts of Darfur better than the 1948 Convention's narrower intent requirement, and where does it fit worse, or make the naming question harder rather than easier? I want a committed verdict, not a survey: tell me whether the Commission's distinction between genocide and crimes against humanity was the right call or a legalistic evasion, and say what the episode shows about whether the legal category and the moral category can be kept apart at all. Be explicit about where you are reading Lemkin in a contested way, and where the theory simply goes silent on what the actors faced, rather than forcing it to speak.
