You are a poet with a philosopher's training, and I need a poem built to a specification. For the past few months a small group of us (two physicists, a Sanskritist, and me) have been reading Schrödinger's late work on mind alongside the Advaita Vedanta he kept reaching for, and our discussion keeps snagging on one claim: that consciousness is never met with in the plural. As I understand his version of it, this is not a mood but an observation about how experience is given. You can add a second stream of experience to the world, and a third, but you cannot add two experiences together into one; the first person plural is never itself a subject that experiences anything, only a way of talking about several subjects who each do. From there he took the further step, under the pull of the Vedanta he was reading in the Tarner lectures of the mid-1950s, that the many are appearance and the one is real. I want a poem that takes that step seriously without going soft: no incense, no haze, and no pretending that the many are obviously unreal when grief, hunger, and death look so thoroughly individuated. Two thoughts I would like it to hold in tension at once: first, that a world containing many brains gives no special account of why experience should come in exactly one stream per brain rather than many or none; second, that whatever unity there is cannot be a matter of one person remembering being another, since that is not what the claim ever meant.

The form should do the work, so here is the form. Write a pantoum of at least four quatrains: the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and the final quatrain should close the circuit by taking up lines from the opening stanza again, so that the poem ends on something the reader has already heard and now hears differently. The constraint I care most about is this: each time a line comes back, it should be spoken by a different first-person speaker (a child at a window, a woman in a crowd, a fish in a net, a man being anaesthetised, whoever the poem needs), and the change in meaning should come from the change of mouth, not from punning. By the last quatrain the reader should feel that the migrating line was always the same voice, and that the many mouths were the appearance. The poem should earn the unity claim formally rather than asserting it, and it should be accurate enough that someone who knows the debate can say precisely what claim it commits to and where it stops short of Schrödinger's own position. I will read it aloud to close the last session, so it should survive being heard once, without footnotes.

Before drafting, think on the page for a few lines about which line can bear that much repetition and why, then give me the poem. I would much rather you commit than hedge: pick one reading of the unity claim, either the strong one on which the plurality is an artefact of grammar and the substrate is one, or a weaker one on which the many are fully real and what remains is only the unexplained fact that experience is unified at all, and write the poem so that it could not have been written from the other. Then, in a short prose note after the poem, tell me which reading you chose and why, and flag the places where the poem takes liberties with Schrödinger's actual view or leans on a contested reading of the Vedanta he was drawing on.
