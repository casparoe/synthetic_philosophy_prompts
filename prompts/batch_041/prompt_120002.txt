I'm a novelist, about halfway through a book about four men who decide to drink their way through a bad year, so everyones been telling me to watch Another Round, which I finally did, twice. I want to think about it through Plato's views on drunkenness -- not the Symposium, I've had enough of Alcibiades crashing the party, but what he argues in the Laws: that wine is a drug that strips off your self-control and shows you what you're actualy made of, that a properly run drinking session with a sober older man in charge is a kind of training ground for shame and self-command, and that wine is also the thing that lets old men stop being embarrassed and sing. Read the film through that. It's a lucky coincidence that the characters are literally running an experiment on themselves, so: does the film dramatize Plato's idea of drink as a test that reveals character, or does it quietly swap in something modern and medical, booze as a dial you turn up and down on your life? And that last scene, the dance -- is that the Dionysian chorus Plato wants to harness for the good of the city, or the loss of control he wants the law to steer? Tell me honestly where the analogy snaps, because I suspect it does: Plato's whole discussion is for a lawgiver founding a colony, so what survives when there's no city, just four teachers and a school? Finish by saying what the film knows about drinking that the argument can't reach.
