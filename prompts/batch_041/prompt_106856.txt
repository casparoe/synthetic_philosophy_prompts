I'm in a small reading group that's been working through self-reference paradoxes in legal systems — whether a constitution can lawfully amend away its own amendment clause, the basic-structure problem, that kind of thing — and last week we played a few rounds of Nomic, the game where changing the rules is itself one of the moves. It set off a long argument about which of the various self-amendment devices you can bolt onto a rulebook actually does any work, and which just move the paradox around. I've written up ten of them below. Most are paraphrases of things we invented over beers, so some are probably garbage, and a couple might be definately incoherent:

1. Entrenchment: a rule can only be changed by a supermajority, and the rule setting that threshold is itself set at a higher threshold, so the hard-to-change rules protect the easier-to-change ones.
2. Immutable core: a short list of rules no ordinary amendment can touch, plus one designated "revolution" move that can replace the entire rulebook a single time, after which the replacement rules are entrenched.
3. Prior-procedure consent: any change to the amendment procedure itself must be adopted under the amendment procedure as it stood immediately before the change, so you can't lower the bar for lowering the bar.
4. Self-reference ban: any proposed rule whose text refers to itself (like "this rule may never be repealed") is void on its face.
5. Universal sunset: every rule, including this one, lapses after N turns unless the current players re-enact it.
6. Consistency guardian: a rule that voids any enacted rule inconsistent with a higher tier, where the tiers are fixed by a rule at the very top that nobody may amend.
7. Exception clause: any player may, once per game, suspend all rules including this one for a bounded period, after which play resumes as before.
8. Dead-hand unanimity: amendments require the assent of every player who has ever played, including ones who quit years ago.
9. Symmetric reversibility: every rule change must be reversible by exactly the same procedure that enacted it, so nothing can ever be locked in permanently.
10. Interpreter's finality: one designated player rules on whether any change is valid, and the rule granting that power is itself interpreted by that player.

What I want from you is a tier list: sort these ten by how much you'd trust each one to stop a Nomic-style constitution from being lawfully dismantled from the inside — a faction using nothing but procedurally valid rule changes to turn the game into something the rest of us would say is no longer the same game — while still leaving the system able to adapt when it genuinely needs to. Use four tiers (I don't care what you call them), give a one-line reason for each placement, and tack a rough score out of 10 on each if that helps you be consistent. Two things I'd especially like: if any of these are incoherent or self-defeating rather than merely bad, say which and why instead of just burying them in the bottom tier; and if two of them can't really be separated on this dimension, tell me that rather than manufacturing a distinction. Some of them trade adaptability for safety in obvious ways, so if your ranking depends on how you're weighing those two against each other, say so. American English, please.
