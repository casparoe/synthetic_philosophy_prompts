I belong to a small reading group on practical rationality, and we keep circling back to a puzzle that none of us can get past. The setup: an eccentric billionaire will deposit a million dollars in your account tonight if and only if, tonight, you form the intention to drink a cup of a toxin tomorrow morning — a toxin that will make you violently ill for a day but do no lasting harm. Tomorrow, after the money is safely deposited, you are free to drink the toxin or pour it down the sink, exactly as you please. We all agree on the setup, the payoffs, and the timing, and yet we cannot agree on whether it is even possible for you to form the intention, or whether, if it is possible, it would be rational to try. What began as a fifteen-minute curiosity has turned into a year-long stalemate, and I have stopped believing we are actually arguing about one thing.

Here is the shape of the disagreement as I see it. Some of us hold that you simply cannot intend to do something you know you will have no reason to do once the payoff is secured — that intending is not subject to the will any more than believing is, so the offer is one you cannot take up no matter how much you want the money. Others insist you can form the intention, by self-deception, by sheer stubborn resolve, by reframing the toxin as a point of honor, or — a favorite in our group — by arranging things so that drinking becomes inevitable, for instance by taking a pill that will make you ill regardless, so that tomorrow there is no live choice left to undermine tonight's intention. A third faction treats the case as a structural discovery: that reasons to intend can come apart from reasons to act, and that any theory of practical rationality which ties the two tightly together is refuted by the example. A fourth thinks it is a parlor trick with no consequences for anything worth calling a theory. My own suspicion is that these camps are not disagreeing about a single proposition: one is pressing a metaphysical question about whether intention answers to the will, another a normative question about what one may intend for the sake of a reward, and a third a conceptual question about what intending even is, with the word doing quietly different work in each mouth.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey, and not a reading list — I will fill in the references myself. Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: a substantive dispute about the metaphysics of intention, a normative dispute about the separability of reasons for intending from reasons for acting, a verbal dispute about a word that two camps use differently, or several different questions wearing one name. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, and say plainly which of the camps I have described you think is confused and why. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a revised question, a formal result, a change in what we demand of a theory of rationality — or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why. Be explicit about what would change your mind and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
