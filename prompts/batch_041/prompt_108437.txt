I am a high-school student and a member of our school's philosophy club. This term the club has been considering whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, and next week each of us is to present a short position paper setting out our own view. Before I commit to mine, I want to check that the commitments I have accumulated actually hang together; I would rather discover a contradiction in private than have one pointed out from the floor. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a reading list, since I have no way to consult sources just now. What I want is an audit of my own position: which of the following claims genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to, how severe each real tension is, and what the least costly way to restore consistency would be.

Here is where I have landed. (1) Genuine moral dilemmas are possible: there are situations in which an agent is under two moral requirements, cannot satisfy both, and neither requirement is cancelled or overridden by the other. (2) Moral realism is true: there are objective facts about right and wrong that do not depend on anyone's attitudes or conventions. (3) Ought implies can: no one can be morally required to do something they are unable to do. (4) Ought agglomerates: if an agent ought to do A and ought to do B, then the agent ought to do both A and B. (5) In the case I plan to use, a student has promised to attend her grandmother's eightieth birthday — her grandmother is frail, and this may be the last birthday she is well enough to enjoy — and has also promised to be the sole witness at her best friend's courthouse wedding, which falls at the same hour and cannot be moved; the friend has no one else who can stand as witness, and the student can be at only one. I want to say that she is blameworthy whichever one she chooses. (6) A person is blameworthy for what she does only if she could have acted rightly instead — only if some available alternative would not have been wrong.

Please tell me which pairs are in real tension and which are only apparently so, order the real tensions from most to least severe, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be, together with what that repair costs. I would also like to know whether any single revision would clear up more than one of the problems, and whether any of my commitments is doing no work at all — that is, whether I could drop one without loss and thereby dissolve the most trouble. Be specific about which claim has to go if something does, and please do not simply tell me that it depends on my metaethics; I want to know what this particular combination commits me to.
