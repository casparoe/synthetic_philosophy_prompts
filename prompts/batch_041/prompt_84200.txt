I am a professor of philosophy, and I have been asked to give the memorial address at a celebration of life for my colleague and closest interlocutor, a philosopher of mind who died last month at seventy-three after forty years of teaching. The room will be mixed: roughly half will be philosophers of mind, cognitive scientists, and former students who know the debates about personal identity, fission, and the strange-loop tradition; the other half will be her family, neighbors, and old friends who knew her as a person rather than as a thesis. I want a eulogy of about eight hundred words—six or seven minutes—that lands for both halves without condescending to either. She had a loud laugh, a habit of entering a room mid-argument, and an inability to let a sloppy inference pass; those details can be used, but the speech should not become a collection of anecdotes.

She spent her last decade defending a view she called the distributed I. On that view, an I is not a substance or a soul but a self-referential pattern—a strange loop—sustained by brains, shared attention, memory, and mutual prediction. When someone dies, the pattern can persist in others, but only as a distributed pattern: in the way her students pause before a bad inference, in her brother's habit of finishing her sentences, in the questions her friends now ask themselves. She was adamant that this is not survival in the ordinary sense; the first-person perspective does not continue, and she would have considered it a betrayal to be told she is watching over us. She also insisted on a distinction that many of her critics missed: a pattern can be instantiated in several brains without there being a single subject distributed across them; the distribution is real, but it is not a single first-person perspective. The eulogy should therefore do real structural work with the distinction between a pattern continuing and a person surviving. Its arc should mirror the thesis: begin with the irreplaceable first-person particular—her voice, her way of taking up a room—then widen to the distributed traces in others, then return to the fact that no collection of traces adds up to the person who died. The tension should be acknowledged, not resolved. The speech should not say she lives on in the sense of an afterlife, nor that she is simply gone; it should inhabit the difficult middle. One concrete detail you might use: she used to test the thesis by stopping mid-sentence in seminar and waiting; if someone finished the thought correctly, she would say, there I am. At the memorial, that memory should be handled carefully, because it is both a consolation and a reminder that the there is not the here of a living first-person perspective. Do not turn the speech into a lecture on Hofstadter, and do not use technical terms like numerical identity, fission, or strange loop without explaining them in plain language. No citations, no reading lists, no quotations from real texts.

Please write the speech in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter. Do not open with the stock formula about being gathered here today, or any other stock eulogy opening. The tone should be warm, precise, and unsentimental—like a philosopher who loved her and is not going to lie at her funeral.
