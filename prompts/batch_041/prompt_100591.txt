I'm a second-year PhD student in cognitive science -- my lab works on animal cognition -- and for the last few months I've been writing down my gut verdicts about which things have moral weight, mostly to see whether I'm consistant. Can you tell me what principle, if any, my list adds up to? Here's the list.

Bacteria, plants, my thermostat: zero. Not low, zero -- there's nothing there to get wrong. A tick: I feel nothing at all. A fruit fly in my kitchen: I swat it without much thought, but I wouldn't quite say it counts for nothing. A bee: I'll spend five minutes shooing one out the window, and I'd feel genuinely bad about wiping out a hive. A crayfish or a lobster: I've started feeling bad about boiling them and I can't really say why, and I still eat them. An octopus: about as bad as a pig, maybe worse, which is strange given that its brain is organized nothing like a pig's. A fish: definately matters, I stopped eating them a few years ago -- but I still eat shellfish, which I'm pretty sure is inconsistent of me. A mouse: matters a lot more than a fish, and I can't give a reason why. A dog and a pig: basically equal, and far above a mouse, even though a pig is probably smarter than my dog. A human infant: way above all of them, even though the infant is arguably less cognitively sophisticated than the pig. A patient under general anesthetic: full weight, no hesitation, even though right now there's presumably nothing it's like to be them. A patient who's been in a vegetative state for a year: I still want to say full weight, and I notice that this has nothing to do with experience anymore. A sleeping person: full weight. Which makes me nervous, because by the same logic a sleeping mouse should drop to near zero, and that strikes me as obviously wrong. I'm also uneasy about the numbers: if a fly has even a trace of weight, there are astronomically many flies, so the aggregate should swamp nearly everything else I care about, and that doesn't feel right either. And I'm confident a thermostat is zero, but I'd feel worse about clear-cutting a forest than about smashing a thermostat -- I think that's aesthetic rather than moral, but I can't fully convince myself.

My hunch at a principle: a threshold below which nothing counts at all, then above it weight scaling with the capacity for valenced experience, then a precautionary clause that says round up when you're unsure. Does that get my list, or close? I'd rather drop one of my verdicts than keep bolting on epicycles, so tell me which one has to go and what the simplest repair is. And say what the repaired principle implies for cases I haven't listed: a brain organoid with a few thousand neurons, a small RL agent that's been trained to avoid a pain signal, an ant colony considered as a single entity rather than as its ants, and a mouse in a coma. Please write your answer in British English -- I have a supervisor who marks us down for American spelling.
