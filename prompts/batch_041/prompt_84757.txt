Ok so I've been going down a Leibniz rabbit hole again and I want to compare two ways of reading the best of all possible worlds, because I think they're doing different work and people (me included, until recently) slide between them without noticing.

Reading one, the maximizing reading: God surveys an infinite space of possible worlds, each one a complete maximal consistent set of compossibles, and picks the one with the most perfection, where perfection is some aggregate -- more harmony, more variety-with-order, more reality. The best world is the argmax. This is the reading where the whole thing is basically an optimization problem, and the hard part is making sense of a maximum over an infinite domain: if for any world there's a better one, there's no best, so Leibniz needs either a maximum (a world no other exceeds) or a story about why the ascending chain converges. I know he says stuff about infinite aggregates and about how God's choice isn't a survey but a single act, but I want the comparison to be about what the view needs, not just the patch.

Reading two, the structural/fittingness reading: the best world isn't the one that wins on a scalar; it's the one whose laws and constitution exhibit a kind of unity -- simplicity of means yielding richness of effects, maximal composibility, an order that lets the most variety coexist without chaos. On this reading, best is more like an aesthetic or organic property, closer to the fittingness of a proof than to a score. God doesn't compute a total, God recognizes the unique optimum because the rules of the best determine it.

So: which reading does the real work in Leibniz, and can they come apart? In particular, if the maximizing reading is right, does the structural stuff reduce to a mere heuristic for finding the max? And if the structural reading is right, does talk of maximizing become a metaphor, and if so what grounds the uniqueness of the best world -- why couldn't two worlds both be maximally harmonious but incommensurable?

There's also the striving-possibles thing from the earlier essays, where every possible has some pressure toward existence and compossibility is the filter. Is that a third reading, or just the mechanism for the first one? I genuinely can't tell.

And what does each reading do to the theodicy? The maximizing reading makes evil a cost in a global sum, which is where all the Pangloss jokes land. The structural reading makes evil something more like a necessary shadow or a condition of the order, which is harder to make precise but maybe less vulnerable to the simple arithmetic objection. Which one should I be defending if I want the view to be more than a punchline?

I don't need a bibliography or a tour of the secondary lit, I just want the two readings set up, the strongest version of each, where they actually conflict, and which one you'd bet is load-bearing. If you think the dichotomy is fake and they're the same thing under a different description, argue that instead.
