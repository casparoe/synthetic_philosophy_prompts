I'm putting together a small set of poems to open a reading group on social ontology, and I want one on how a convention gets made and then forgets it was made. Write me a villanelle in which a convention begins as just one arbitrary way of solving a coordination problem and ends up feeling like a rule that was always there and ought to be kept. No names of philosophers and no quotes from anyone -- the mechanism has to be carried by the form: the two refrains stay word-for-word identical the whole way through, but by the closing quatrain they should be doing a different job than they did at the opening, because the lines around them have changed what "this is just what we do" is up to. Make the convention small and invented -- which side of a footpath you keep to, a village rule about who greets whom first, something of that sort -- rather than a real law or a famous moral norm, so it stays in the weeds and doesn't just lean on the textbook examples. Anyone who knows the machinery (arbitrary equilibria, common knowledge, mutual expectation, conforming because you expect the others to) should be able to tell the poem knows it too, and anyone who doesn't should still find it a decent poem. Dont let it resolve into either "so its all made up" or "so its really binding" -- leave it sitting in the middle where the thing is neither. British English spellings please.
