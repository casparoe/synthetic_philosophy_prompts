Ok, kind of an odd request, but bear with me because I think theres a real project hiding in here.

Quick context on why I care. I've lived in the same neighborhood for about eleven years, and two years ago my landlord jacked the rent up enough that I genuinely thought I was done there. Long story short, I got pulled into a tenants group, and through that I somehow ended up as the one renter on our neighborhood planning committee, which reviews rezoning and variance applications. Sitting in those meetings every month has been an education. The fights are almost never about density per se, they're about who gets to decide, and I keep hearing the same phrase come out of everybodys mouth: the idea of a right to the city. The developer's lawyer uses it, the longtime homeowners use it, we use it. Everybody's citing the same idea to argue for opposite things.

Here's my half-formed thought. That idea seems to have two parts that people smush together: (a) a say in how the place gets shaped, i.e. a claim to participate in decisions, and (b) a claim to actually be able to stay and live in the place, to access it. And in my meetings those two pull apart hard. The people who have the say are often the exact people who use it to keep others from getting in or staying. So can a single right to the city coherently hold both parts, or does it have to pick one and rank the other under it? And if it has to pick, what does that mean for how a planning committee like mine should actually be set up?

I'm applying for a small eight-month fellowship from a housing justice org, and I need a proper proposal, maybe three pages. Can you turn the above into one? I want the question stated so it has a determinate answer (not just "explore the tension"), an honest account of why the usual positions on NIMBYism and on the right to the city leave this open rather than settling it, and the method. My instinct for the method is to model the right as a bundle of Hohfeldian incidents (claims, privileges, powers, immunities) held by different parties, incumbents vs newcomers vs people who'd live there later vs the city itself, and show the conflict is really between a power/immunity held by one group and a claim held by another, then pressure-test that decomposition against a handful of real cases from my committee. But tell me if there's a better way in, and why its better than just doing interviews or a case study.

Also: what would a result even look like here, what would count as the project failing (I'd rather know), and whats a realistic scope for eight months and a part-time schedule.
