I've spent the term arguing about Bernard Williams with a friend, and I've noticed that I keep defending a position I've never actually written down, so before I turn it into a seminar paper I'd like you to audit it for consistency. Here is where I've landed. (1) I'm a consequentialist about the criterion of rightness: whether an act is right depends on whether it produces the best overall consequences, though I would spell out the good in terms of the satisfaction of desires rather than pleasure, so I am not a hedonistic utilitarian. (2) Williams's integrity objection strikes me as a genuine and decisive objection to classical utilitarianism, and the part of it that bites is the alienation point rather than the demandingness point: a theory that treats an agent's deepest commitments and life-projects as valuable only insofar as they feed into an impersonal total has failed to take the agent's own standpoint seriously, and no amount of adjusting the calculus repairs that. I also hold that the objection does not depend on the projects being admirable — the sense of being asked to betray oneself is the same whether the project is a worthy one or a squalid one, and I take that to be a feature of the objection rather than a defect. (3) The one-thought-too-many verdict is correct: when a man saves his wife rather than a stranger, the thought that she is his wife ought to be the whole answer, and requiring him to add that impartial morality permits him to prefer her corrupts the motive rather than licensing it. (4) I also hold that a theory of rightness and a decision procedure are different things: a moral theory is answerable for what makes acts right, not for what an agent should rehearse in the moment, so it is no embarrassment to a theory if the people who live by it do best when they do not think in its terms — I am content to call consequentialism self-effacing in precisely this sense. (5) And at the level of cases, I think a parent who can save either their own child or two strangers' children should save their own child, and I mean that this is what they should do, not merely what they are permitted to do.

My friend's objection is that (1) and (3) cannot both be mine — that a consequentialist has no business telling the rescuer which thoughts he may have — and while I don't think that is quite right, I have a suspicion that she has put her finger on something and got the wrong pair. What I want from you is an audit rather than a verdict on Williams. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, ranking the genuine ones by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad; for each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me; and where an apparent conflict dissolves, name the distinction that does the work. Then tell me which single revision would clear up the most trouble at the lowest cost, and if the honest answer is that the package cannot be repaired and I have to choose, say so plainly and tell me what I would be choosing between.
