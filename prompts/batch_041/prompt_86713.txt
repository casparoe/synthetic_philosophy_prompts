I hold the following position in population axiology, and I want an honest map of its exposure.

Begin with a critical-level view: the value of a world is the sum, across the lives in it, of each life's welfare minus a threshold set above the neutral point — the neutral point being the level at which a life is neither worth living nor worth not living — so that adding a life counts for something only if that life goes better than the threshold. Now add my own modification. On my view the threshold is not a constant but a function of population size: it rises as the number of lives in the world rises, without upper bound, and it rises slowly — higher for a world of a billion than for a world of a thousand, but not dramatically so. For concreteness, suppose it rises by one unit for every tenfold increase in population, and suppose each world I ask about is a single cohort, so that one threshold applies uniformly within it. The rationale is that the standard below which a life counts as deprived rather than merely modest is not fixed by human nature alone; it depends on how crowded the world is, on what a person can reasonably expect given how much of the world's capacity is already spoken for. In a world of ten thousand people, a life at welfare 1 is comfortable. In a world of a trillion, it is destitution.

What would count against this? Rank the defeaters from fatal through serious to merely awkward, and mark which ones a defender of the view would concede and which she could reasonably dismiss. Please cover at least the following, and add anything else you think decisive.

1. Does the rising threshold actually avoid the repugnant conclusion, or only postpone it? Work out whether any increasing threshold function can both (a) refuse to rank a vast population of lives barely worth living above a modest population of excellent lives, and (b) avoid declaring every sufficiently large world worse than an empty one. If the view is caught in a dilemma between a bounded threshold and an unbounded one, say which horn a defender should take and what it costs.

2. Which standard adequacy conditions does the view violate, and how much should that worry me? Consider dominance, transitivity, the mere addition principle (adding lives worth living does not make a world worse), the avoidance of the repugnant conclusion, and the avoidance of the implication that it can be better to add lives that are worse than nothing than to add lives worth living. Which violations does the view share with every view in the area — so that the impossibility results are not a special objection to me — and which are peculiar to a rising threshold? In particular, can adding one more life make a world worse not only through what that life contributes but by raising the bar for everyone else, and is that a feature or a defect?

3. Rank these worlds under the rule I specified, and say whether the ranking is stable if the threshold instead rises per factor of a thousand or per factor of a million: A, a thousand lives at welfare 100; B, a million lives at welfare 20; C, a trillion lives at welfare 2; D, a quadrillion lives at welfare 0.5. Tell me which come out below an empty world.

4. A variant I am tempted by: fix each life's threshold at the moment that life begins, from the number of lives then existing, so that a life's contribution depends on how crowded the world was when it started. Value would then depend on the order of births: two worlds containing exactly the same lives at exactly the same welfare levels, differing only in the sequence that produced them, would differ in value. Is that implication fatal, or is there a principled story on which the order is genuinely part of what makes a life go well? Does the variant help or hurt on the other problems?

5. Insulation. Suppose I keep adjusting the shape of the threshold function to protect the verdicts I care about. At what point should I admit the view has stopped risking anything? Is there a formulation that stakes something real — some case where it makes a determinate prediction a fixed critical-level view does not? If the honest answer is that the view is well insulated, tell me whether that is a defect of my view or a condition of the subject.

What would move me most is a demonstration that the view's verdicts turn on a feature of a world that no one inside it could have reason to care about. If that is what the rising threshold buys me, I want to know whether I can pay it.

End with a final line of exactly this form: Verdict: <LABEL> — <the single defeater a defender of the view must concede, or "none">, where <LABEL> is one of ROBUST, QUALIFIED, EXPOSED, or UNFALSIFIABLE.
