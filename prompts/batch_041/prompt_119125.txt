I am preparing for a seminar on eighteenth-century political economy and have been working through George Berkeley's The Querist. I can follow the individual queries in isolation, but I am having trouble making them cohere into a single position on money, and I would like a careful reconstruction of the passage rather than a general summary of the work. The stretch that puzzles me most is the following:

22. Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating? Or, whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser?

23. Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?

24. Whether the value or price of things be not a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty?

25. Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?

26. Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?

What unsettles me is how this block sits with the earlier queries in the same work, where Berkeley asks whether the four elements, and man's labour therein, be not the true source of wealth; whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour; and whether any other means, equally conducing to excite and circulate the industry of mankind, may not be as useful as money. Read together with query 21, which ties wealth to industry and to a circulation of credit that may be transferred by whatever marks or tokens one likes, the earlier material makes money sound like a merely instrumental lubricant for an economy whose real substance is labour and natural resources, while queries 23 and 25–26 seem to assert something considerably stronger: that money is nothing but a ticket or counter, and that the denominations could survive the disappearance of the bullion supposedly backing them.

Could you help me understand what Berkeley is actually committed to here, addressed to a reader who has the text in front of them but no specialized background in monetary theory? In particular: What work does the phrase "ticket or counter" do, and is there a distinction between a ticket and a counter that matters for the argument? In query 23 he lists several rival accounts — intrinsic value, commodity, standard, measure, pledge — and appears to set them aside; is he rejecting all of them, or subsuming some of them under his own account? How, if at all, does the velocity point in query 22 connect to the ticket-or-counter claim? Does query 24's "compounded proportion" of demand and plenty leave room for money to have a determinate value that is not merely stipulated, or does it push toward the conclusion that money's value is purely conventional? And is it anachronistic to read this stretch as anticipating either a quantity theory of money or a chartalist or credit theory, or does the text genuinely point in one of those directions? I am not looking for a survey of secondary literature; I want the internal logic of these queries made explicit, including any tensions Berkeley leaves unresolved.
