I am the lead policy analyst for the budget committee of a mid-sized city council. Every budget cycle I have to screen several dozen proposals — a childcare subsidy, a property-tax credit for low-income homeowners, a transit fare reduction, a business-incentive package, a parks improvement program — before the council votes on them. The council members speak constantly about fairness and have no shared method for judging it, so the outcomes track whoever argued hardest in the previous meeting. I want to give my staff a screening procedure grounded in Rawls's veil of ignorance and the difference principle: something any analyst can run consistently in the two weeks we have, and explain to a council member in a five-minute conversation.

Please write that procedure — the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a decision, and where it must hand the matter to the council because the remaining question is one of value rather than analysis. I need the Rawlsian content to survive the translation rather than evaporate. So the procedure should be explicit about which facts the veil hides and why, since the choice of what to hide is itself a design decision and can rig the result; about the difference between raising the position of the worst off and merely narrowing the gap, given that the difference principle tolerates inequalities that benefit the worst off; about how to compare proposals whose benefits are not commensurable, which is the index problem; about whether to reason in maximin terms or in expected-value terms, and what an expected-value version of the same device would yield instead; about whether the worst-off group can even be identified in a city of this size, and what to do when it is internally heterogeneous; about how incentive effects and the sustainability of the chosen rule bear on whether it should be adopted at all; and about what priority, if any, the basic liberties and fair equality of opportunity retain when the decision concerns a single budget line.

It should also confront a doubt I have not resolved. The difference principle was framed for the basic structure of a society, and it is unclear what, if anything, it demands of a city council choosing among programs. The procedure should say honestly whether it is applying the principle or a surrogate for it, and what is lost in the substitution. If it turns out that the frame does not apply to decisions of this kind, say so, and give me the nearest thing that does.

Then run the procedure on three proposals of your choosing from the list above, including one where it returns an answer I will not like — a program that is efficient overall but that the procedure says we should not fund, or one the procedure says to fund that is unpopular with the council. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do and where an analyst will be tempted to fake rigor.

No citations or reading lists; refer to positions and arguments by description and I will supply the references myself.
