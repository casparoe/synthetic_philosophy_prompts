Question (20 points): An urn has 90 balls. 30 are red; the other 60 are black or yellow in some proportion you aren't told. You face two choices separately, $100 either way. Problem 1: bet on red, or bet on black. Problem 2: bet on red-or-yellow, or bet on black-or-yellow. Most people pick red in Problem 1 and black-or-yellow in Problem 2. Explain what's going on: what's the inconsistency, which principle does the pair of choices violate, and is the pattern of preferences irrational? Justify your answer.

--- start of student answer ---

Ellsberg's urn has 90 balls, 30 red and 60 that are black or yellow in an unknown proportion. In Problem 1 you choose between betting on red and betting on black; in Problem 2, between betting on red-or-yellow and betting on black-or-yellow. Most people prefer red in the first and black-or-yellow in the second.

This is inconsistent. Betting on red and betting on black have the same expected value: by symmetry, if you average over the possible compositions of the urn you expect 30 black balls, so there's no reason to prefer red in Problem 1. And Problem 2 is just Problem 1 with the same yellow event added to both options, so whatever you preferred in Problem 1 you should prefer in Problem 2 — if you liked red over black, you should like red-or-yellow over black-or-yellow. Choosing red and then black-or-yellow violates the independence axiom of expected utility theory, which is the sure-thing principle: adding the same event to two options shouldn't flip your preference.

What's going on is ambiguity aversion — people dislike gambles where they don't know the probabilities (ambiguity) more than gambles where they do (risk), even when the payoffs are identical. Ellsberg's comparative-ignorance hypothesis says the effect is strongest when you compare your own state of information to someone else's, or to a case where you do know the odds.

Is it irrational? That's contested. On Savage's picture you always have a subjective probability for black, so preferring red to black commits you to P(black) < 1/3, while preferring black-or-yellow to red-or-yellow commits you to P(black) > 1/3. No single probability can do both, so the pair of choices has no expected-utility representation. But the assumption that every event gets a precise probability is doing the work here, and theories like maximin expected utility keep the preferences while dropping that assumption.
