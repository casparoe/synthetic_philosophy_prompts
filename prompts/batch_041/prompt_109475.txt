Here's my paraphrase of the relevant chunk, pretty close to his own words:

"Augustine is the great absolutist about lying: no lie is ever permitted, not to save an innocent person's life, not to shield someone from violence, not even to keep a desperate man from doing something irreversible. What makes something a lie for him isn't the falsity of what you say — it's the will to deceive, the little split between what you have in mind and what you put into words. That's why a joke, or a figure of speech that nobody is meant to take literally, isn't a lie at all, and why he tells the honest man cornered by a murderer that he may keep quiet or change the subject but may not lie. He then sorts lies into eight kinds according to their motive, ranked from the ones that do the most damage to the ones that do the least, and there the severity softens: since what makes a lie wrong is the harm it does — to the person deceived, to the liar's own soul, to the trust that holds a community together — the lies at the bottom of the list, the ones that help somebody and hurt nobody, are the ones he treats as tellable when something serious is at stake. He never quite comes out and says that, but the ranking is doing the work. And when Scripture seems to reward liars — the Hebrew midwives lying to Pharaoh, Rahab hiding the spies — he insists the praise attaches to their kindness and not to their falsehood, which most readers have always felt was a dodge. The verdict of most philosophers since is that the position is just too harsh, and Kant's famous murderer at the door is basically the same conclusion with a different rationale: you must not lie."
