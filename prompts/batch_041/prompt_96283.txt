ok so I've spent a term reading about moral luck and responsibility and I've ended up with a pile of commitments that each seemed right when I adopted them, and I'd like someone to tell me whether the pile actually stands up. Here's roughly where I've landed. (1) Compatibilism: determinism doesn't threaten responsibility, because what responsibility needs is that your action flowed from your own values rather than from coercion or ignorance — not that you could have done otherwise in some contra-causal sense. (2) Constitutive luck is real and I find it genuinely disturbing: an enormous amount of what makes me the person who acts, from my capacity for impulse control to how much empathy I got handed, was never chosen. I dont think that kills blame, though, because blame is just registering whether the act came out of your own evaluative stance, and a stance can be yours even if you didn't build it. (3) I'm a retributivist about punishment: desert is what justifies it, and the amount should track culpability, not deterrence or social protection. (4) Resultant luck is real. Take two surgeons who are equally exhausted and make the exact same bad call, and one patient lives while the other dies — I think the one whose patient died is more culpable, and not merely the object of more regret. (5) And yet I think the law should punish attempted murder and successful murder the same, since the culpability is identical and the only difference is whether the gun happened to jam.

What I can't work out is which of the objections people keep making are load-bearing. A friend who's read a lot of the free-will-skeptic stuff says I'm running the classic dodge — quietly redefining desert as "the act came out of the agent's own values" and then keeping the retributive punch that the redefinition can no longer pay for — and honestly I half think he's right. Another friend says the thing I'm calling extra culpability in (4) is really just agent-regret, the special sorrow of someone who caused harm, and that regret isn't a judgment about blameworthiness at all, so I'm smuggling a feeling in as a fact. Meanwhile my own suspicion is that (1) and (2) don't sit well together, though I can't say why, and I'd rather be told I'm wrong about that than have it politely ignored. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture on the literature.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, worst problem first, no preamble and no wrap-up. Each item should be a sentence or two: name the commitments that are pulling against each other, say whether the clash is real or only apparent (and if it's only apparent, say what makes it dissolve), and say what would have to give. Make the fifth item the single cheapest revision that buys me the most consistency, and tell me what it costs.
