I am currently enrolled in a seminar on historical explanation and social science, and our use of the term "path dependence" has drifted: "locked in," "contingent," "irreversible," and "would have happened anyway" are now treated as a single claim. I would like to separate them by getting the underlying theory straight. Please set out the desiderata for a theory of path dependence — what such a theory must be able to do in order to count as adequate — where adequacy is understood relative to one purpose: serving as a diagnostic. Given a particular outcome (a technology standard, a legal rule, an institutional form, a settlement pattern), the theory should let me say with some rigor whether that outcome was path-dependent and whether the same outcome would have arrived anyway.

Produce the requirements list, and for each requirement give the case or consideration that motivates it and state whether you regard it as non-negotiable or merely as the preference of a particular camp. I have the following candidate demands in mind; I want you to add, cut, merge, and reorder them as the argument requires, and to explain the changes:

- It must distinguish path dependence from mere persistence. An outcome that survives because it is continually re-selected for its present merits is not path-dependent, however ancient it may be; only an outcome that would not be chosen again now, yet persists, qualifies.
- It must keep apart two questions the seminar conflates: whether the origin of an outcome was contingent (a small accident that was, at the time, reversible) and whether its persistence is self-reinforcing (the accident has since become load-bearing). The two can come apart in both directions.
- It must give a principled answer to the counterfactual in the central question — would the same outcome have arrived anyway? — which requires it to say what counts as the same outcome. Described finely enough, contingency is everywhere; described coarsely enough, robustness is everywhere.
- It must specify the mechanism that performs the locking (increasing returns, network effects, learning, coordination, sunk cost, political entrenchment) and distinguish mechanisms that genuinely lock an outcome in from those that merely retard its replacement.
- It must not be unfalsifiable: it must say what evidence would show that an outcome was not path-dependent.
- It must accommodate the fact that apparently locked-in outcomes are sometimes displaced, and say what that implies for the theory.

The usual illustrations — a keyboard layout, a videotape standard, a reactor design, a railway gauge — will do, provided the list also tells me what to do when the alleged inefficiency of the locked-in outcome is itself disputed, since that is where the seminar's cases most often stall.

I am most interested in whether some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied; if so, identify it and explain why, since that would be a result in its own right. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding the loose uses of "path dependence" that I encounter in the seminar.

To keep the list honest, I will be testing it against a case of this shape: a standards body fixes a technical specification early, largely because one influential member has already adopted it; decades later the specification is widely thought inferior to a rival that arrived too late, yet replacing it would cost more than the improvement is worth. I want the list to tell me which requirements that case satisfies and which it does not, and in particular whether the theory licenses the verdict "locked in" or only the weaker verdict "costly to reverse."
