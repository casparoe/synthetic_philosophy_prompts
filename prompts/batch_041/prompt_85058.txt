I have no academic training; I read history and archaeology on my own, mostly late at night, and I have a habit of pushing an argument further than the people who made it want to go. For about a year now I have been keeping a sort of ledger of the shift from foraging to farming, and I cannot get the two columns to behave. In the left column I have everything the standard account treats as the price of civilisation: bodies that got shorter, teeth that rotted once grain became the staple, iron-poor blood, the infectious diseases that crossed over from the animals we penned, a surplus that could be hoarded and therefore rulers who hoarded it, and a working life that seems to have lengthened rather than shortened ever since — set against an earlier arrangement that some estimates put at a twenty-hour week, with no landlords and no granary to be taxed. In the right column I have the awkward facts that spoil the romance: infants buried at rates that make our own mortality look gentle, famines, and evidence of violence among the very people we are supposed to envy. I raised this at dinner with friends and we argued for two hours; one of them said the comparison is meaningless because you cannot compare two arrangements that do not contain the same number of people, and the other said that is exactly what people say when they do not want to answer the question. I do not know which of them was right, and I am not asking you to tell me.

What I want from you is not an essay but a document. Write a formal complaint and the company's replies to it, as one file: a customer who has concluded that agriculture was a mistake and is writing to the firm that, in this fiction, sold it to us, asking to be restored to the previous configuration; and a support agent who agrees with him about almost everything. Six hundred to eight hundred words, laid out as a complaint reference number, a first message, three or four replies, and a closing satisfaction survey. The agent must never deny a single defect — every symptom the customer lists should be conceded and, wherever possible, itemised more precisely than the customer managed, and that is where the humour has to live: comedy of bureaucratic competence applied to an admitted catastrophe, deadpan throughout, no sarcasm, no winking. The customer, for his part, must not be allowed to idealise the old arrangement; the agent should have the infant mortality, the famine and the violence ready to hand and should produce them in a way the customer cannot wave away, so that the reader ends up holding two piles of facts that will not cancel. And the one thing the agent cannot give is the return. The reason must be a matter of numbers, stated flatly — the previous configuration cannot hold the present number of users, so a refund would have to be paid in users — and whether that is a reason or an excuse must be left exactly where it falls. Do not resolve it.

Two further conditions. Neither correspondent may cite a source, name a scholar, or refer to a book; the agent speaks only in policy and the customer only in grievance, so everything must be carried by the facts themselves rather than by authority. And the question I have been circling for a year — whether a world with more people living worse is better or worse than a world with fewer living better, and whether "worse off" means anything at all when the two worlds do not contain the same people — should never be stated by either of them. I want to finish reading and discover that I have been made to feel it. The last reply should contain an offer of compensation, and the compensation should be recognisably the sort of thing currently being sold to people who have read the same books I have.
