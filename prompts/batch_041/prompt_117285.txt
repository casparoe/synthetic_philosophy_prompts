I spent my working life as a population ecologist, and since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy with the aimlessness of a man who no longer has to publish. I keep arriving at a question I used to think was settled empirically and now see is not: when habitat is lost, degraded, or converted — or equally when it is restored — does the net welfare of the wild animals in the affected area rise or fall, and by how much, relative to what? Before I read any further, I want the specification of a theory that could answer that, not a survey of the answers people have given.

Write me the desiderata for such a theory: the phenomena it must accommodate, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the findings of ecology it must not contradict. Give the case or consideration behind each requirement. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which look to me like the preferences of one camp dressed up as necessities. And flag any pair or subset that you do not expect to be jointly satisfiable — I would rather be told that the list is inconsistent than be handed a list quietly trimmed until it is satisfiable.

Below are candidate requirements, each with the case that motivates it. Add what I have missed; tell me which of these is not a genuine requirement at all.

- Compensatory versus additive mortality. In many populations the deaths caused by a habitat change are not added to the deaths that would have occurred anyway; density dependence, predation, or winter starvation would have removed those individuals. A theory must decide whether it is counting deaths or counting welfare, since here the two come apart. Case: a migratory bird whose wintering grounds shrink, so that birds which would have died of competition on the breeding grounds instead die earlier and elsewhere.

- Standing population versus lifetime welfare. A hectare holding a stable population of a hundred short-lived, high-fecundity animals and a hectare holding a stable population of ten long-lived ones can look alike in biomass and trajectory while differing enormously in the sum of welfare over time. Does the theory require a flow measure rather than a stock measure, and can that be made operational?

- The sign of a typical life. A clutch of five thousand eggs with two survivors: if the short lives of the other four thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight were net negative, then protecting the clutch is a harm. Must the theory take a position on the sign of an individual life, or may it remain agnostic and still return verdicts?

- The sign of an intervention under predation. Restore an apex predator to a landscape and mesopredator numbers fall, so there are fewer individuals, while each remaining individual may fare better or worse. Where the number of animals and the welfare per animal move together, the folk heuristics — fewer animals means less suffering; being eaten is a harm — will sometimes agree and sometimes conflict. The theory must adjudicate the case rather than coincide with intuition about it.

- Baseline and identity. Reduction relative to what: the site before conversion, its pre-human state, the state it would have reached anyway? And whose welfare counts — the individuals actually displaced, or also those who would have existed under the other scenario and never do?

- Units and aggregation across kinds. Per capita, per hectare, per species, per lineage: these can disagree in sign for one and the same change. The individuals being summed differ in their capacity for welfare by orders of magnitude. Does the theory owe a rule for exchange, or only a warning?

- Holism versus individualism. Ecologists speak of the health or integrity of a system. Can that be a locus of welfare, or must everything be cashed out in the experiences of individuals? If the latter, does anything of the ecological notion survive, and if the former, how is it measured without smuggling in human preference?

Finally, two questions about what the theory owes me. Must it return a definite sign for a concrete policy — say, converting a forest to pasture — or is indeterminate an acceptable output? And which single requirement on your list does the most to eliminate candidate theories, or is most likely to be a prejudice of a camp rather than a genuine desideratum?

No citations and no reading list. I cannot use them, and a name attached to a view is no help to me unless the view is stated.
