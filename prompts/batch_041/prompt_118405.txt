I have been reading a handbook entry on the knowability paradox and would like an accuracy audit before I let any of it into my own notes. The section runs roughly as follows, in my transcription; I have kept the substance and the emphasis.

"The paradox was discovered by Frederic Fitch, who offered it as a refutation of verificationism -- the doctrine held by the logical positivists and later defended by Michael Dummett under the name anti-realism, according to which a statement is true only insofar as it could in principle be verified or known. The proof is short. Suppose every truth is knowable, in the sense that every truth is, or eventually will be, known by someone. Take some humdrum truth that in fact nobody knows -- suppose it is a fact about how the grains of sand were arranged on a particular beach on a particular afternoon. The conjunction 'p, and nobody knows that p' is itself a truth, so the principle requires that it be knowable. But it cannot be known: anyone who knew the first conjunct would thereby know that p, which makes the second conjunct false. The principle therefore refutes itself, and this is what finally killed off verificationism. Two morals follow. First, we are forced to admit that there are truths no possible mind could ever know; truth outruns knowability. Second, the argument depends on classical logic -- on the law of excluded middle, and on treating knowability as a yes-or-no matter -- which is why intuitionists, the anti-realists in question, have never been troubled by it. Anti-realists today do sometimes try to patch the principle by exempting propositions whose knowability would be self-undermining, but this is an ad hoc rescue of a thesis the argument has already destroyed."
