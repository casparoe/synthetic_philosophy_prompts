I've been reading Leibniz's Theodicy on my own, the old English translation thats floating around free online (I think from around 1951, but the book itself is 1710), and there's a stretch in it I keep bouncing off. Roughly, the bit I mean is where he's dealing with how God can be good and still let evil happen: he says God doesn't will evil, he permits it, and then he draws a distinction between two ways of talking about God's will. Taken one way, God wants the good of every single creature considered just on its own, and there's no creature whose misery he'd want for its own sake. Taken the other way, he wants the best arrangement of the whole series of things, and in that arrangement some creatures do end up miserable, and he goes with it anyway because the whole is better than any alternative. Then there's the part about justice that I can't fit with the rest. For Leibniz justice seems to be the same thing as wise benevolence, goodness joined to wisdom, and he's very insistent that God's justice isn't raw power and isn't just severity in punishing. I also have a vague memory that he says love of God is bound up with taking pleasure in other people's happiness, which if true seems like it should make the permitting of all that misery harder rather than easier.

What I can't work out is whether that first kind of willing is a real willing at all or just a way of speaking, and, bigger than that, whether any of this is meant as a model for how people should act or whether it's a special story about God with no implications for us. Because Leibniz seems to think there's a moral world sitting inside the natural world, where good deeds get rewarded and crimes get punished and the two line up perfectly, and if thats true then God can permit someone's suffering and guarantee it comes out right in the end. But I can't see the whole series and I can't guarantee anything for the person I'd be letting suffer. So when people say Leibniz has a morality and not just a theodicy, I don't know what they mean. Does justice being benevolence guided by wisdom mean an ordinary person who isn't wise can't be just? And does the permission thing ever carry over, could a parent or a doctor or a ruler ever be doing the Leibnizian move by allowing a smaller harm for a bigger good, or is the whole point that only an agent who sees the entire series and can make it up to everyone is allowed to do that?

I'd like it explained in the order Leibniz actually argues it, and I'd like you to be honest about which bits are a real philosophical position and which are just a guy in 1710 trying to keep God off the hook.
