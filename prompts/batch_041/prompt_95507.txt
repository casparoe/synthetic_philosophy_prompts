I am a graduate student in a seminar on probability and philosophy of science, and I am trying to decide how much of a lecture I recently heard on the reference class problem to accept. Here is the gist of the professor's account:

"The reference class problem is the difficulty of attaching a probability to a single case. To say how likely some particular event is, you have to put it in a class of similar cases and consult the frequency in that class. But any case belongs to indefinitely many classes, and the frequencies differ from class to class, so the probability of the case is simply whatever the class you chose says it is. The problem was raised against the frequency interpretation of probability and has never been adequately answered. Bayesians escape it, since they need no frequencies, only coherent degrees of belief. And in practice the problem does not arise, because any real application supplies a natural, maximally specific class; the working rule is to use the narrowest class for which you have reliable data. It is a philosopher's worry about the single case, not something that should change how insurers, doctors, or courts reason."

I am inclined to believe him, but two things I have run into make me hesitate. A public defender I know had a client scored by a risk-assessment tool used at sentencing: the state's model placed him in a class and reported a 43 percent chance of reoffending, a defense expert recomputed the figure on a narrower class and got 12 percent, and the court went with the state's number on the ground that it was the class the tool had been validated on. Separately, a relative who prices commercial insurance says her firm does the opposite of what the professor describes: when a small group's own claims history is thin, they deliberately blend it with the experience of a much broader class, weighting each source by how much data it carries, rather than using the narrowest class available.
