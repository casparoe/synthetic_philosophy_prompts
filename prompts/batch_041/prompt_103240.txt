Two research groups have spent years talking past each other about the foundations of solution concepts in games of perfect information. The first, which I will call the epistemic-program party, holds that common knowledge of rationality, once formulated with enough care, is the correct idealization for game theory and genuinely underwrites the standard solution concepts, backward induction included. The second, the idealization-skeptic party, holds that common knowledge of rationality either cannot be satisfied in the very games where it is invoked, or, where it is satisfied, does no justificatory work beyond assumptions that carry the real weight.

Draft the joint statement that these two parties could sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Conduct the whole discussion on the following fully specified game rather than on any textbook illustration.

Game C. Player 1 moves first and chooses Stop or Continue. If Player 1 stops, the game ends with payoffs (1, 0). If Player 1 continues, Player 2 chooses Stop or Continue. If Player 2 stops, the game ends with payoffs (0, 2). If Player 2 continues, Player 1 chooses Stop or Continue once more. If Player 1 stops at this third node, payoffs are (3, 0); if Player 1 continues, the game ends with payoffs (2, 4). Payoffs are common knowledge and the game is played once. Also address two variants: (i) Game C played twice in succession by the same two players, with payoffs summed and the whole arrangement common knowledge; and (ii) Game C with a grain of doubt, in which each player is certain of the other's rationality except for assigning some small positive probability to the other's making a non-maximizing choice at each node; state what happens as that probability tends to zero and whether the prediction is continuous in it.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign, covering the analysis of Game C itself, what the relevant idealizations are meant to represent, and what each variant shows or fails to show.
2. A table of the remaining disagreements, each row stating the point at issue and giving, in separate cells, the formulation each party would accept as its own position, phrased so that neither side is strawmanned.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one party. I expect one to concern whether common knowledge of rationality must hold at the outset of the game or at every node actually reached, and another to concern whether the grain-of-doubt variant reveals a discontinuity that undermines the idealization; but you should identify the cruxes as you find them.
4. For each crux, the argument or case both parties agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction about that case, recorded in advance.
5. An explicit statement of which disagreements you judge to be merely verbal, and what wording would dissolve them, and of what each party would count as an argument against its own position and whether there is anything both agree would actually move the debate.

Begin with a short joint preamble fixing what each party means by rationality and by common knowledge, and flag every place where the two sides are using those terms differently.
