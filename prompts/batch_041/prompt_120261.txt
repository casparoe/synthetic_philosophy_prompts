My friend Maya and I were hanging out with her friend Jae the other night and the three of us ended up in a two-hour arguement about how you're supposed to explain things that groups do. I never did figure out who won, and it's been bugging me.

The setup: we're all in this neighborhood group chat that over about a year turned into a place where everyone snipes at each other and all the reasonable people left. Maya says the only real explanation is about individuals. Every nasty message was some person deciding to send it, and to explain why that person sent it you have to talk about what they wanted, what they were worried about, who they were mad at. She says "the chat got toxic" is just a label for a big pile of individual facts, and a dangerous one, because it lets people off the hook, as if the toxicity did it and not them. Jae says no: the fact that the chat had gotten toxic is exactly what explains why a particular person piled on that particular day. It changed what they could expect from everyone else, and what would happen to them if they stayed quiet. And he made a bigger point: nobody intended the thing to get toxic, it came out of everyone reacting to everyone, so the pattern is real and it's doing work in the explanation.

Then they went a few more rounds. Roughly:

1. Maya: fine, but the pattern is still made of nothing but individual acts and individual expectations. List all of those and you've said everything there is. "The chat is toxic" is shorthand, a fact about your sentence and not about the world.

2. Jae: that's the move I can't stand, because you're never actually going to do that listing. "Reducible in principle" is a promissory note nobody ever cashes, and it can't tell a good explanation from a bad one. It also proves way too much. By that logic "it's 40 degrees out" explains nothing, since it's really just molecules jiggling.

3. Maya: but "we could reduce it if we bothered" is different from "there's nothing there to reduce." And when you say the toxicity made him pile on, I want to know where that's happening. Show me the mechanism. If the cause is a pattern, and the pattern isn't anywhere except in people's heads and actions, then you've just renamed the individual stuff and called it a cause. Here's my test: delete the individual people from the story. Is anything left? If not, the group thing was never a cause.

4. Jae: here's mine. Suppose you swapped out most of the members over that year and the same thing happened anyway. If the pattern keeps re-forming with different people, then what's explaining it isn't anyone's personality, it's the setup. And your test is rigged, because you want me to point at the spot in the world where the pattern is, and it isn't in a spot, it's in the relations. That's like saying a traffic jam has to be located in one of the cars.

5. Maya: the swapping thing doesn't show what you think. It might just mean the pattern is very likely given certain kinds of people, which is still a fact about individuals, just a statistical one. And "it's in the relations" — relations between what? Between individuals. So we agree.

6. Jae: no we don't, because you think an explanation has to name the parts and how they push each other, and I think an explanation just has to answer the why-question. Different questions, different right answers. You're not disagreeing with me about the world, you're legislating what counts as an answer.

It fizzled out around there. Can you go through it move by move — for each numbered move, is it right, wrong, or right about something other than what it claims — and then say who has the better overall position and what both of them are missing? And separately, are those two tests they invented (delete the individuals / swap out the individuals) actually any good? I couldn't tell, and I'd like to know if I should be using either of them.
