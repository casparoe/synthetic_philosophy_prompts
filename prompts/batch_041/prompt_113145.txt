I have been trying to settle, for my own purposes, when it is rational to follow a crowd, and my verdicts on a range of cases do not obviously add up to one view. I would like you to identify the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that best captures the pattern; to say which of my verdicts that principle forces me to give up; and to say what it predicts for cases I have not considered. Please state the principle precisely enough that I can tell where it draws its line, and name, for each clause, the case that forces the clause to be there.

Here are my verdicts.

A traveller with no other information chooses the crowded of two restaurants. I call this rational, though I suspect it is a different kind of rational from the next case.

A person holds a weak private signal that a venture will fail, then watches four others commit to it, and commits as well. I call this rational, and I would say she is in a cascade.

A hiring committee takes statements in a fixed order; the third member, whose own read is thin, endorses the candidate the first two praised. Rational.

A manager endorses a project he privately expects to fail, because his bonus depends on the chief executive's approval. Rational, but I resist calling it a cascade.

A voter casts her ballot for the candidate she expects to win, though she prefers another. Rational, and again I hesitate to call it a cascade.

A crowd at a fire stampedes toward a single exit. I call this irrational, and I cannot articulate why it differs from the restaurant case.

A researcher abandons a promising line of inquiry because everyone else has, though she has seen no new evidence. I call this irrational, even though it looks like the committee case.

I also think the reverse of the third case can be rational: if I have reason to believe the first two choosers were copying each other rather than judging independently, I should discount their agreement, even though it is agreement.

Two things about my verdicts puzzle me. First, they seem to track a mechanism (each person inferring from her predecessors' actions) at some points and a mere outcome (many people doing the same thing) at others, and I cannot tell which of the two I actually mean by an information cascade. Second, I hold that a cascade can be shattered by a single public revelation even when no one's private information has changed, and I take this fragility to support calling the earlier choices rational rather than merely excusable.

So: is there a coherent account of rational herding behind these verdicts, or are they two or three different phenomena I have been filing under one name? Where it cannot save all my verdicts, tell me which one to drop and why, and give me at least one further case the surviving principle decides in a way I would not have predicted.
