I help run a small satirical tech newsletter with a couple of friends (we're not journalists, we just have a mailing list), and the next issue is themed around AI hype, so I want to do a short industry-news item — headline, lede, two or three quotes from invented people with job titles, a throwaway line at the end about the company's funding round — reporting, completely deadpan, that a startup has shipped an assistant that is deductively omniscient: it believes every logical consequence of everything it believes, automatically, instantly, at no cost. The jokes should come out of the actual content of the problem and not out of generic tech-bro stuff, so please get the philosophy-of-logic details right rather than gesturing at them. Some raw material you can use or ignore: in the standard possible-worlds models of knowledge and belief, belief is closed under known entailment, so the startup's design decision isn't as crazy as it sounds; the trouble is that a thing which already believes all the consequences can never learn anything, never be surprised, and derives every tautology over and over (billing by derivation steps goes badly); if it ever comes to believe a contradiction it believes everything, so the engineering team's real job is contradiction-avoidance, and a customer complaint could be that after being told two inconsistent things about their own calendar the assistant began confidently asserting facts about the price of tea in China; there's also the awkward case where it believes each claim on a list and also believes, correctly, that not all of them are true. If you want to get more in the weeds you can bring in the distinction between implicit and explicit belief, or the idea that closure should only apply to consequences the agent is "aware" of, or the idea of worlds where logic itself behaves differently — but don't have any character in the piece stop and explain the theory, and don't make anyone wink at the reader. The piece has to be legible and funny to someone who has never heard of epistemic logic, while someone who has should be able to tell that whoever wrote it knows exactly what they're satirizing, so no howlers (don't, for instance, have the omniscient assistant be uncertain about anything, or have it fail to derive an obvious consequence; the joke is that it's perfect, not that it's broken). One of the quotes should be from a "concerned" in-house researcher who is worried about exactly one of these issues and can't quite articulate why it matters, and the ending should leave the reader with the real puzzle hanging rather than resolving it into a punchline — what it would even mean for something resource-bounded like us to believe all the consequences of what we believe. About 500-600 words, and please keep it flat and straight-faced throughout, like a real trade-press brief, no exclamation points and no joke-tells.
