I have spent the better part of a year in a reading group on the collapse of past societies, and I have ended up defending a view I am no longer certain I hold for the right reasons: that collapse is not a phenomenon in the world so much as an artifact of how we choose to look. The choice, as I reconstruct it, has three parts. We identify a society with its most legible apparatus — the palace, the tribute rolls, the temple, the state — so that when the apparatus fails we say the society failed, even when the people who paid for it are still in their fields, burying their dead roughly as their grandparents did, and in many cases living better than before. We pick a scale of analysis at which decline looks like termination: the ninth- and tenth-century emptying of the southern Maya lowlands reads very differently once the northern lowlands and the highlands are in the frame. And our evidence is written by those who lost the most, the literate elite whose world really did end and who are uniquely well placed to persuade us that their ending was the world's. Historians of Rome already made a version of this move once, trading 'fall' for 'transformation', and both the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE and Rapa Nui look at least as hospitable to the revisionist reading as to the catastrophic one. My interest is not purely antiquarian: I grew up in a town that lost its mill and half its population in about a decade, and I have noticed that when I call what happened there a collapse I am usually describing my own departure, whereas the people who stayed describe it as a change in how things are done.

I would like the case against this view, sorted by how much it should move me, with the decisive kept apart from the merely awkward. Concretely: what would a genuine collapse look like, such that the revisionist redescription would be strained rather than merely available? Are there findings — in the archaeology of any of these cases, in demography, or in the study of how knowledge and subsistence practices are lost rather than relocated — that would settle the matter, and have any of them already turned up? Does the position predict anything its rival does not, and if so, has any such prediction failed? I also want to know which of these considerations a defender of the view would themselves concede rather than wave away, and whether there is a real difference between conceding that something terrible happened and conceding that a society ended; I suspect much of the disagreement lives in that gap.

Finally, be honest about whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability. If any outcome can be redescribed as reorganization given enough time and a sufficiently generous redrawing of boundaries, is that fatal here, or is the rival view exposed in exactly the same way — after all, if the boundaries of a society are ours to draw, they are the collapse-realist's to draw too? What I would personally find most decisive is a case in which the revisionist reading requires me to say something plainly false about the people involved: where calling it a reorganization commits me to denying a loss that they themselves would have recognized as one. Tell me whether that last worry is an objection to the view or merely a complaint about how the view sounds.
