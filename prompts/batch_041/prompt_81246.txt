I am trying to understand John Stuart Mill's views on colonial rule, and I keep returning to the opening of Considerations on Representative Government, where he distinguishes two ways of conceiving political institutions. Here is the passage:

"By some minds, government is conceived as strictly a practical art, giving rise to no questions but those of means and an end. Forms of government are assimilated to any other expedients for the attainment of human objects. They are regarded as wholly an affair of invention and contrivance. Being made by man, it is assumed that man has the choice either to make them or not, and how or on what pattern they shall be made."

"To these stand opposed another kind of political reasoners, who are so far from assimilating a form of government to a machine, that they regard it as a sort of spontaneous product, and the science of government as a branch (so to speak) of natural history. According to them, forms of government are not a matter of choice. We must take them, in the main, as we find them. Governments can not be constructed by premeditated design."

My question is how this distinction bears on Mill's later treatment of dependencies, especially India. I suspect there is a tension: if forms of government cannot be constructed by premeditated design but must grow from a people's habits and institutions, then how can Mill justify a free state's rule over a dependency as anything other than an artificial imposition? Yet he also seems to treat such rule as a trust and a temporary tutelage. I want help understanding whether his account of dependencies is consistent with the organic-growth conception of government or whether it covertly relies on the very machine model he appears to reject. Please take a definite position on this: either Mill's colonial views are consistent with his general theory of government, or they are not. Give your reasons, and correct any misunderstanding in how I have framed the issue.
