I am preparing a seminar on the ethics of consent, and I find the customary division of defeaters into deception, coercion, and incapacity too coarse for the cases I want to discuss. Please treat the following eight candidates as items to be evaluated rather than as a settled taxonomy.

1. The consent-giver is deceived about a fact that the other party knows to be material to the decision.
2. The consent-giver is not deceived but is left ignorant of a risk that the other party privately judges negligible and that the consent-giver would judge material.
3. The consent-giver agrees because the other party threatens to withdraw a benefit on which the consent-giver has come to depend, though the other party had no prior obligation to provide it.
4. The consent-giver agrees to terms that are exploitative only because of an unjust background distribution, although the other party neither created that distribution nor benefits from it unusually.
5. The consent-giver understands the act and its immediate consequences but cannot grasp its longer-term significance, and the deficit is mild and fluctuating.
6. The consent-giver consents sincerely and with full information, then later reverses the decision and denies that the earlier consent was ever genuine.
7. The consent-giver agrees under time pressure that the other party caused but did not intend as a means of pressure.
8. The consent-giver's preferences have been shaped over years by the very relationship in which consent is sought, so that the refused option would not have occurred to them.

For each candidate, give a number between 0 and 1 representing the extent to which it should, on its own, invalidate consent that would otherwise be valid (0 = the consent remains fully intact; 1 = the consent is void), and then rank the eight from most to least invalidating. Where two candidates strike you as the same defeater under different descriptions, say so rather than manufacturing a distinction. After the ranking, address three questions: first, which of the three standard defeaters, if any, best captures each candidate; second, which candidates the standard framework handles least well, and what that reveals about its structure; and third, whether any candidate is not a defeater at all but rather evidence that consent was never present, and if so, which one, and what turns on the difference.
