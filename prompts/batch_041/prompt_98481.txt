I cover philosophy for a magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on the old case of the murderer at the door — whether you may lie to someone who asks where his intended victim is hiding. A Kantian friend whose rigor I mostly admire puts the case this way. (1) The duty of truthfulness is owed not to the particular person you address but to humanity as such; that is why a lie is a wrong to everyone and not merely to the one deceived. (2) A duty owed to humanity cannot be suspended by the unworthiness of the individual standing in front of you, so the murderer's design gives you no license to lie to him. (3) If the duty is owed to humanity rather than to the hearer, then it cannot matter whether the falsehood reaches him as a lie, as a misleading truth, as an evasion, or as a silence meant to deceive — the offense is the same. (4) Nor can you buy your safety by refusing to answer, since a refusal under these circumstances is itself meant to mislead him about what you know. (5) So the only course left is to tell him where your friend is.

I cannot accept (5); it strikes me as monstrous. But I also cannot give up (1), which seems to me the whole point of the duty and the reason it does not quietly collapse into a rule about minimizing harm. So tell me which of the remaining steps I should reject, and what rejecting it costs me — in particular, whether I can keep (1) while denying (3) or (4), or whether the price of escaping (5) is surrendering the ground of the duty itself. One concrete worry of my own: in my reporting I routinely refuse to answer questions rather than lie, and I would like to know whether that refusal is doing genuine moral work or is only a dodge that a rigorous Kantian should see through.
