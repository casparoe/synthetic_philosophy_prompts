ok so I've been voluntold to write up my position on the precautionary principle for a workshop, and before I make an ass of myself I want a consistency check. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Serious uncertainty about a really bad outcome is itself a reason to act, even when nobody can put a number on how likely the bad outcome is.
2. The PP is best read as a procedural rule rather than a decision rule: it shifts the burden of proof onto whoever wants to run the activity. On its own it never tells you to ban anything.
3. It has to cut both ways. The risks of *not* doing the thing -- not deploying the tech, not approving the drug -- count for as much as the risks of doing it. Precaution isn't a license for inaction.
4. Except: where the downside is catastrophic and irreversable, I do think the case for acting against the activity is stronger than the case for permitting it, even when a rough expected-value calculation would come out the other way.
5. I don't think we should be running expected utility on probabilites we invented. But I do want the choices that come out to be coherent -- stable, not order-dependent, not something I'd flip if you described the same options to me in a differant order.
6. And the PP has to apply to itself. A moratorium is also an intervention, with its own risks.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which ones only look like they do, and how bad is each? Rank them by severity rather than telling me they're all a bit tense, say what would have to give in each case and what that costs me, and then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most. I'm not fishing for a particular answer here, I genuinely don't know which one is the weak link.
