As a retired natural scientist who has only recently turned to philosophy, I have been reading Grice on implicature and speaker meaning and have become interested in a wrinkle I have not seen adequately discussed. Grice’s account of conversational implicature is hearer-directed: the speaker means something by relying on the hearer’s recognition of the intention. But drama seems to contain a different phenomenon. In Oscar Wilde’s The Importance of Being Earnest, characters constantly say things whose real point is aimed over the heads of their interlocutors at the audience—what I would call audience-directed implicature. I want a critical reading of the play through this problem. Does Wilde dramatize a kind of speaker meaning that Grice’s framework cannot capture, or can Grice accommodate it by treating the audience as a second addressee or by distinguishing what the speaker means from what the playwright means? Where does the play illuminate the distinction between what is said, what is conversationally implicated, and what is dramatically implied? And where does the play resist the Gricean reading—where is the meaning not recoverable from any intention, but instead an effect of staging, repetition, or the audience’s superior knowledge? Please avoid quotations and references; I want the argument, not a bibliography.
