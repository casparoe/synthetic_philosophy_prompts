Compose a dialogue between two interlocutors, Ada and Bram — both of them competent, neither serving as a foil for the other — on whether the relation "is better than" is transitive. They should argue from a single concrete case rather than from generalities, and should reason from the case itself rather than appeal to authorities; I care about the arguments, not their pedigree, so please don't have them cite a literature.

The case is a spectrum of lives. Each life consists of one unbroken stretch of a single unpleasant experience and nothing else of evaluative note, and the lives differ only in how intense that experience is and how long it lasts. At one end, call it L0, the life is exactly one day of the most intense agony a person can endure. Each step along the spectrum lowers the intensity by one just-noticeable notch and lengthens the experience by one day, so that the far end, call it F, is a life of a million consecutive days — some twenty-seven centuries — of discomfort so faint that it barely registers as unpleasant.

Two judgments pull against each other:

1. Each adjacent step is an improvement: the later life, being a notch less intense, is better than the one before it, even though it lasts a day longer.
2. The start is better than the far end: a single day of agony, however bad, is better than a million days of unremitting faint discomfort.

Applying transitivity to (1) yields that F is better than L0, contradicting (2). Ada holds that transitivity is not negotiable — that a relation which failed it would not be betterness at all, or could not serve as a ranking — and so she must identify which of (1) and (2) is the error and on what grounds. Bram holds that "better than" can genuinely fail to be transitive, and he must explain how without making the relation useless.

Have them press each other on at least these points:

- Whether the failure, if there is one, is mere vagueness — so that some sharpened relation beneath it is transitive and the puzzle is an artifact of borderline cases — or a real feature of value.
- Whether (1) and (2) are both deliverances of intuition or whether one of them is a theoretical commitment in disguise.
- The exploitation objection: if a person's judgments cycle in this way, a trader can walk off with their money by offering a sequence of trades each of which the person judges an improvement. Does this show the intransitive judgments are irrational, or only that they cannot be used as the basis of a certain kind of market behavior?
- Whether it matters that "better than" is strict. The adjacent steps might instead be described as "roughly equal," and it might be "at least as good as" that fails to be transitive while "better than" survives.
- A proposal a colleague of mine made, which I want them to assess: that "x is better than y" simply means that x's position on some underlying scale exceeds y's by more than a fixed margin, so that "better than" comes out intransitive by construction. Does this rescue the notion or change the subject?

I want real back-and-forth: each should take up what the other actually said, concede where a point lands, and not talk past each other. Close with a short, frank note on where each position is weakest, and if I have set the two up so that one of them cannot be defended, or if the spectrum as I have described it does not in fact generate the conflict I claim, say so plainly rather than staging a tidy draw.
