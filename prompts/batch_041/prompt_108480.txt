Please act as a philosopher of the social sciences with a strong grasp of the metaphysics of social kinds, and think carefully before committing to an answer — a brief brainstorm of the candidate concepts, followed by a decision, is welcome.

I have spent a decade arguing about generations in blog comment threads, and I keep trying to write a post about something I have noticed and failing, because I cannot tell whether it is a known phenomenon or something I am inventing.

The descriptions never stand on their own. Nobody says what a cohort is like; they say what it is like by contrast with the cohort next to it — this one is cynical where the previous was earnest, the next is anxious where this one was confident. Remove the neighbouring label and nothing is left: the content of the kind is the contrast, not any property its members share. Nor does the label stay outside the people it names. It is coined by people who are not in the cohort — journalists, pollsters, advertisers — and once it is in circulation members take it up as a description of themselves, so a trait originally imputed to them ends up genuinely shared. Meanwhile the boundary is always a round number of birth years, so those at the edge have the defining experience no more than the cohort on the other side of the line.

Is there an established concept for the kind of thing a generation label is — a category whose content is exhausted by its contrast with its neighbour, and whose members are made to fit it by its having been applied to them? If there is a name, I want the boundaries more than the label: which nearby idea fits, and where each of the candidates falls short. If there is not, coin one, define it precisely enough to exclude things, and tell me whether a nationality, a caste, or a religion is the same kind of thing, and which of the features I have described is doing the work and which is decoration. Close by saying what would change your mind — what argument or case would show that these are ordinary categories after all, or that I have misdescribed the pattern — and where your reasoning is likeliest to be wrong.
