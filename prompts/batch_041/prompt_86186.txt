Assume the role of a translator between two disciplines, and take the task slowly. I am a novelist. For twenty years my working vocabulary has been the vocabulary of narrative craft: protagonist and antagonist, stakes, the arc, scene and summary, point of view, the reader's sympathy, dramatic irony, the throughline, foreshadowing, the earned and the unearned resolution, the denouement. I know academic philosophy only as an interested outsider. What I want from you is a faithful translation of one philosophical position — strong longtermism, the view that the moral importance of the far future dominates that of the present — into my vocabulary, followed by a candid report on everything that fails to survive the crossing.

Begin, before the translation proper, with a short brainstorming pass. List the conceptual load that strong longtermism actually has to carry: the vast number of future persons, the claim that their interests count equally with ours, the asymmetry between bringing someone into existence and harming someone who already exists, the special weight of existential catastrophe, and the thought that we are unusually well placed to affect the very long run. For each item, ask yourself which of my craft terms is the closest available instrument, and say so plainly. Then write the translation itself as continuous prose, in my first person, as a novelist might reason about the moral shape of history.

Then the residue report, which is what I most want. Go term by term. Say what crossed cleanly, where a novelist's notion genuinely carries the philosopher's meaning; what crossed only with distortion, where you had to bend one vocabulary against its grain to fit the other; and what did not cross at all, where strong longtermism asserts something for which narrative craft has no expression. Give me your verdict on three candidate residues in particular: first, whether a person who does not yet exist and never occupies a single scene can be a character at all, and whether a failure here indicts my framework or the philosopher's claim; second, whether the difference between harming an existing character and failing to create a possible one has any narrative analogue, or whether narrative is constitutively unable to represent a life that was never begun; third, whether the aggregation of astronomically many future lives is exactly the kind of summary that my craft trains me to distrust, and whether that distrust is a prejudice or a piece of hard-won knowledge.

Throughout, do not smuggle my assumptions into the translation and present them as the philosopher's meaning; if you catch yourself doing it, stop and flag it. Where a single phrase must serve two masters, tell me which reading you chose and what the other would have been. Close by saying which part of strong longtermism, if any, my vocabulary cannot even begin to state, and whether that is a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the original claim was less determinate than it sounded.
