I spent my working life running controlled experiments and reporting p-values, and only since retiring have I begun to read the philosophy of statistics. I would like a short story, in five numbered scenes, each a compact paragraph, that dramatizes the disagreement between the frequentist and the Bayesian pictures of evidence and ends up somewhere near the replication crisis; no introduction or conclusion beyond the scenes themselves.

Set it in a fictional research institute whose director has imposed a house rule I find plausible but strange: every confirmatory experiment must be analyzed twice, once by a pre-registered significance test and once by a Bayesian posterior, both numbers must appear in the published paper, and the authors are forbidden to say which one they believe. One character, a graduate student, discovers that for the institute's headline result the two numbers point in opposite directions — the test clears the conventional threshold, yet the posterior, under the institute's standard skeptical prior, favors the null. She cannot see how the same data can be evidence for and against the same hypothesis. Let two other characters defend the two sides, and let the story take seriously what the disagreement actually turns on: what a p-value is a probability of, whether a prior is a legitimate part of an inference or a bias in disguise, whether it can be legitimate to keep collecting data while the result looks good, and whether the replication crisis is a failure of one statistical philosophy or of the incentives and publication practices that surround both. Do not settle the dispute by fiat, and do not let any character deliver a lecture that a reader could have gotten from a textbook.
