For the past several months I've been meeting weekly with two friends and our logic teacher to work through the basics of epistemic logic, and we keep running into the same embarrassment: on the standard semantics, any agent who knows anything at all turns out to know every logical truth and every consequence of everything she knows, including consequences no one has ever drawn. Our teacher calls this the problem of logical omniscience and treats it as a defect in the formalism to be patched. I have come to think it is not a defect, and I would like you to tell me what should count against my view.

The view has three parts. First, the closure built into the standard semantics is an idealization of the same species as the frictionless plane or the ideal gas: it describes a limiting case, and complaining that it misdescribes a person is like complaining that the plane misdescribes a tabletop. Second, and more strongly, I want to say that for knowledge specifically, as opposed to belief, closure under entailment is not an empirical hypothesis about human cognizers at all but part of what the concept of knowledge is; whatever else it may be, knowledge is the sort of thing you can rely on and reason from, so an agent who knows something, knows that it entails something else, and still fails to know the consequence is not a counterexample to a theory but a sign that the wrong theory is being applied to her. Third, this makes the whole repair industry look misguided to me: the attempts to weaken the semantics with impossible worlds, or with awareness-relative or resource-bounded treatments, either leave the strong closure in place and merely dress it up, or they buy realism about our logical shortcomings at the price of losing precisely the features that made the formalism worth having, namely that known premises support their consequences and that entailment is distributive over what an agent knows.

What I want from you is a map of the view's exposure. Which arguments, cases, or formal results should move me, and which of them would a defender of my position concede as genuine costs rather than dismiss as misunderstandings? I am especially interested in four things, though you should not restrict yourself to them. The first is whether the idealization defense is honest: a frictionless plane is at least approached by real surfaces as friction diminishes, whereas I cannot see any sequence of real reasoners converging on logical omniscience, so I want to know whether an unapproachable idealization is still an idealization or is simply a false description wearing an idealization's clothes, and what would settle that question. The second is what the counterexamples to closure do to my second claim: cases in which someone knows something and knows that it entails something else yet plausibly does not know the consequence seem to threaten the idea that closure is constitutive of knowledge, and I want to know whether the defender can reply that these are really cases of the agent failing to perform an inference rather than of knowledge failing to close, and whether that reply is available or is a dodge. The third is whether my move merely relocates the problem from knowledge to belief, since if closure is constitutive of knowledge but not of belief then the interesting agents are the ones with beliefs, and my victory may be verbal and empty. The fourth is whether the view can be falsified at all, and what would show that the idealization is not merely inapt but positively harmful — for instance a result that holds only under omniscience and misleads when applied to real reasoners, or a distinction the semantics cannot express. I would also like you to say whether the concept I am defending is one we have any grip on, or whether an agent describable only by the idealized semantics is no longer an agent in the ordinary sense. Please rank the considerations by how decisive they would be, distinguish what is fatal from what is merely awkward, and tell me plainly whether I have insulated the position from criticism by definitional maneuvers and whether that is a vice in a view of this kind.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write an email to someone who asked a long question and deserves a long answer.
