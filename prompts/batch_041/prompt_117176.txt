My friend is convinced I'm incoherent about the Allais choices and I can't tell if his argument is good or just smuggles in it's conclusion. The cases:

1: (A) $1M for sure, or (B) 89% chance of $1M, 10% chance of $5M, 1% chance of nothing. I take A.
2: (C) 11% chance of $1M, else nothing, or (D) 10% chance of $5M, else nothing. I take D.

His argument:
(1) In case 1, A and B pay out exactly the same thing (1M) on the 89% branch.
(2) In case 2, C and D pay out exactly the same thing (nothing) on the 89% branch.
(3) A rational ranking can't hinge on a branch where the two options are identical — that part washes out.
(4) So delete the 89% branch from both. Case 1 is now 1M for sure vs a 10/11 shot at 5M. Case 2 is now those same two things. Same choice, so it has to get the same answer; I give different answers; so I'm inconsistent.

My instinct is that (3) is where it breaks — in case 1 that branch is what makes A a *sure thing*, and "being sure" feels like part of the good, not something that washes out. But he just says then I'm stuck calling a preference of his irrational too. Hand him an urn with 90 balls, 30 red and 60 that are either black or yellow with the split kept secret: he'll bet on red over black, then turn around and bet on black-or-yellow over red-or-yellow, and that violates (3) in exactly the way he says I violate it. So either (3) is true and we're both incoherent, or (3) is false and his case against me falls apart.

Which is it — and if I have to give up (3), what am I actually paying? Can I keep liking the sure thing without giving up expected utility altogether, or is that a package deal?
