I'm in a philosophy of science seminar this term and we just did the raven paradox, and it's made me realize my gut verdicts about confirmation dont add up to any principle I can actually state. All the cases below are about the hypothesis that all ravens are black, and its contrapositive, that every non-black thing is a non-raven. Going on gut:

1. I'm in the woods, a raven lands near me, it's black. Big confirmation.
2. I'm eating lunch and notice my apple is green. Zero confirmation. Not a little, literally none.
3. I'm in a shoe shop and the rack is all white shoes. Zero. Same as the apple.
4. I check a field guide and it says ravens are black. Feels like strong confirmation, maybe as strong as seeing one myself.
5. A friend who's a serious birder texts that she saw a raven today and it was black. Some confirmation, but less than case 1, which is odd since she's more reliable than me.
6. A prof shows us a photo of a black raven. Confirms. Then she mentions she picked it out of a folder of photos of black birds, and now it feels like it confirms nothing at all. Same photo!
7. I run an actual survey: random sample of 200 birds from a list of all the birds in the county, check each one, all the ravens in the sample are black. This feels like way stronger confirmation than 1, even though 1 and 7 are the same kind of observation.
8. My desk is not a raven and not black. Zero, and it's zero because I already knew it was a desk before I looked.
9. But: I open a sealed box that could contain anything and there's a green apple inside. That feels like it confirms a tiny bit. Same observation as 2.
10. I see a black bird, think it's a crow, later find out it was a raven. It confirms, retroactively. When I thought I saw a raven and it turned out to be a crow, nothing.

The principle I'd tentatively offer is: an observation confirms a hypothesis when it's more likely to have happened if the hypothesis is true than if it's false. Does that get all ten? It seems to handle 6 and 9 and maybe 7, but I can't see how it makes 1 and 2 come out as far apart as they feel unless I add something about how the observation was generated. And I dont know what it says about the contrapositive — should the green apple count equally for the black-ravens claim and the non-black-things-arent-ravens claim, and does that even matter?

So: what principle, or small set of candidates, actually captures these? State it precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed (make one up that would really test it), and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't keep — if I have to give one up, which and why. Please dont just tell me to be a Bayesian and stop, I want the probability model that's definately doing the work. One more I can't place: does a black raven confirm all ravens are black and all ravens are black until 2030 and white after to the same degree, and if so is that a problem for whatever you land on?
