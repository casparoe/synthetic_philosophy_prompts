Two friends of mine, Rosa and Tariq, got into it at my place last weekend and I've been turning it over all week. It started because a mutual friend of ours won't shut up about the Robert Moses parkway bridges (the low bridges thing) and the "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" essay, and Tariq finally snapped and said the whole bridge story is probably a legend anyway, and even if it's true it's just one guy with a grudge and a budget — whereas Rosa said that's exactly her point, the budget *is* the politics. Then it got specific, because Rosa's brother works at a distrubution center that just replaced the paper pick-lists with a wrist scanner that times every pick and posts a leaderboard at the end of the shift, and Rosa said that scanner has politics built into it, because it turns "we want you to go faster" from something a supervisor has to say out loud, to a person's face, into a standing fact about the building. Tariq said that's the manager's politics, not the gadget's; the scanner just measures, and if the workers had the same data you'd call it accountability, not domination. Rosa said the design gives it away — real-time, wrist-mounted, no aggregate the workers can see, no way to export it — so the one use it's built for is the one use it gets, and a device that only ever records into a system you can't read is a device for one kind of politics. Tariq came back with the symmetry move: the same scanner with the same wires would be a tool of the workers in a co-op, and solar panels got sold both as rooftop co-ops and as giant utility plants, so the politics can't be *in* the object, it's in whoever's holding it. Rosa said fine, but some systems can't be run the other way at all — an assembly line isn't an assembly line if it runs at the pace of whoever's standing at it — and some arrangements don't merely happen to suit a hierarchy, they require one, and that's the difference between an artifact that has political effects and one that's political in its bones. Tariq said that distinction does no work, since "requires one" always turns out to mean "requires one given what we're trying to do and who's paying for it", and by then you're just describing ordinary politics and dressing it up in hardware.

They've asked me to settle it and I can't — I keep agreeing with whoever spoke last. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, rate each move (clearly mistaken / defensible but shaky / basically right) and tell me what both of them are missing? I'd especially like the "requires one" bit sorted out, because I can't tell whether Rosa is drawing a real distinction there or just a rhetorical one, and Tariq's symmetry argument sounds devastating right up until I try to actually use it on anything.
