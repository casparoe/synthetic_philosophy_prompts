I am putting together a two-session reading group for non-specialists — a handful of lawyers, an engineer, and two retired teachers, all sharp, none with any philosophy past an introductory course — on the norm of assertion: the question of what an assertion is governed by, whether truth, or belief, or knowledge, or something weaker such as justified belief. I do not want you to explain the debate to me; I know it well enough to see how badly it is usually taught. What I want is a teaching plan for the two sessions: the order in which the components have to be introduced and why each depends on the one before it, so that by the time the group hears a named norm proposed they already feel why anyone would posit a norm internal to the act at all rather than just observing that lying is wrong. Assume ninety minutes per session and a group that will interrupt.

The part I keep getting wrong is the carrying example. My instinct is to build the whole arc on a single case — a witness who sincerely and justifiably states something that happens to be true but that she does not really know, on the model of a stopped clock that reads correctly twice a day — but I suspect that case only separates two of the candidate norms and quietly presupposes the rest, so I want you to tell me whether it can carry both sessions or what it would have to have and lack in order to do so, and if it cannot, what single case should replace it and why that one rather than the obvious alternatives. Alongside the example I want the map of misconceptions, with the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. My guesses are these: that a norm of assertion is a moral or social rule about rudeness rather than a constitutive standard internal to the act; that to assert just means to say, so the standard concerns the truth of the sentence rather than the state of the speaker; that the knowledge norm must require certainty, which would make every hedged or probabilistic claim — the weather, the forecast, the lottery — an improper assertion and therefore show the norm to be absurd; and that if the knowledge norm is correct, a speaker who is simply and sincerely mistaken has done nothing at all wrong. I expect several more that I have not anticipated, and I would rather have them named and placed before the group raises them than discovered live.

Give me one question to put to the group at the very end of the second session that would reveal whether they have grasped the shape of the problem rather than memorized four labels, and tell me plainly what I should refuse to cover at this level and announce that I am refusing — I am thinking especially of the contextualist literature and of the relationship between assertion and practical stakes, which I suspect is where the discussion would collapse into name-dropping. Finally, I want the plan to make room for one candidate I have not seen taught in this connection and would like to try: the idea that the norm is not about the speaker's epistemic state at all but about the hearer, that an assertion is an invitation to rely on what is said and that the operative standard is whether the speaker is entitled to extend that invitation. Build the sequence so this possibility arrives as a live option rather than a curiosity, and tell me at which point in the arc it can be introduced without muddying the four standard candidates.
