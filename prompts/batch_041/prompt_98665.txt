I'm trying to state a naturalistic criterion for moral status and I keep failing to get it into a form I can actually test. The criterion I want is roughly this: a system matters morally when it performs computations of the right kind — computations that are globally integrated and self-representing, say — and not otherwise. Two things I have been taking for granted. First, that a system's physical organization settles, objectively and discoverably, which computations it performs; that is what lets me say that a brain performs the relevant computations and a rock does not, as a matter of fact rather than preference. Second, that the moral weight attaches to the computation and not to the material, so that the same computation realized in silicon would carry the same status as in tissue.

The step I cannot make is the one from "this system performs computations" to "these computations matter." If the physical facts settle which computations a system performs, then presumably they settle it at every level of description at once: the same system can be described as computing a coarse input–output relation or as realizing an exact micro-causal trajectory, and both descriptions are equally faithful to the physics. My criterion has to single out one of those levels as the morally relevant one, and I cannot find anything in the physics that privileges one over the others. I tried requiring that the computation be one to which the system is causally sensitive at the right grain — so that perturbing the realization would change the computation — but that only pushes the problem back, since the right grain is again something I seem to be choosing.

Does this mean my first assumption is simply false, and that nothing settles the matter until someone describes the system? If so, does the criterion collapse into "a system matters morally when it can be usefully described as doing X," which would make moral status depend on our descriptive convenience? Or am I running together the question of which computations a system performs and the question of which ones it implements, where only the second is supposed to do the moral work? Tell me which assumption has to go, and what the defensible version of the view looks like.
