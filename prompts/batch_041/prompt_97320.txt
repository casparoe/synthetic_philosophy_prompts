I chair the historical content committee of a publisher that is preparing a multi-volume history of twentieth-century conflict for upper-secondary classrooms. Each chapter must state, in terms that will withstand both academic review and diplomatic complaint, what responsibility a named state, government, or people bears for a war or an atrocity. The committee has four members — two academic historians, a teacher, and a lay member appointed by the board — meets for two hours a fortnight, and may defer at most one contested case per meeting; the editorial board may overrule us only if we have failed to show our reasoning. Every chapter raises the same question in a new form: whether to write that Germany sought the First World War, that the Allied governments imposed a punitive peace, or that a particular administration was responsible for a famine. We have no shared method, and our verdicts have been inconsistent from chapter to chapter.

Please turn what the philosophy of collective and shared responsibility, together with the historiographical controversy provoked by Fritz Fischer's argument in the early 1960s that Germany bore the chief responsibility for the outbreak of the First World War and had deliberately sought a war to become a world power, actually teaches into a decision procedure the committee can run case by case. The procedure must:

- ask its questions in a fixed order, and say what counts as a sufficient answer to each;
- keep apart the several things "responsibility" is used for — causal contribution to an outcome, moral culpability for a choice, legal or diplomatic liability, and the forward-looking duty to repair or prevent — and say which one it is assigning at each step;
- say how causal contribution is to be tested, including by a counterfactual test, and where that test fails, as with overdetermined outcomes and omissions;
- say when responsibility can attach to a people, a government, an army, or an economy, and when it can attach only to named individuals, given that a people is not a decision-making body;
- stop with an explicit refusal to attribute responsibility when the evidence or the concept will not carry the weight, rather than manufacture a verdict;
- mark the points where the question becomes one of values rather than of fact, and hand those to a named person or to the board instead of settling them itself;
- and state plainly what the procedure cannot do.

Then run it on three cases: (1) the responsibility of Germany for the outbreak of the First World War, in light of Fischer's argument; (2) the responsibility of the Allied governments for the terms of the 1919 settlement and for the continuation of the blockade into 1919; and (3) the responsibility of a people, as distinct from the government that acted in its name, for that government's crimes. At least one verdict should be one the committee will not welcome; say which, and why.

Do not cite works, authors, dates of scholarship, or reading lists, and write so that a reader who has not studied the literature can follow and apply the procedure.

Give the procedure as a table with these columns: Step | Question to ask | What counts as a sufficient answer | What to do if the answer is insufficient | Who signs off. Then give a single JSON object with one entry per case, each entry having the fields: "case"; "collective"; "causal_contribution"; "moral_responsibility"; "legal_or_diplomatic_liability"; "remedial_responsibility"; "group_agency" (one of "full", "partial", "none"); "verdict"; "confidence" (one of "high", "medium", "low"); "cannot_be_settled_by_the_procedure"; "residual_value_question" (what it is, and who must decide it).
