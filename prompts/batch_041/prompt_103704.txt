I'm a second-year undergraduate taking a course on the history of political thought, and our end-of-term assignment is an exercise in counterfactual intellectual history. In seminars we've been working through the debate over whether the shift from foraging to farming was, on balance, a disaster for human welfare — the suggestion that hunter-gatherers enjoyed relative material abundance and rough equality, and that agriculture delivered hierarchy, epidemic disease and unremitting labour in exchange for scarcity and toil. We've been asked to bring this into conversation with the classic early modern treatment of the state of nature.

Please write the essay for me. Suppose Thomas Hobbes could be brought forward and given the modern literature on hunter-gatherer affluence and on the agricultural revolution as a wrong turn. Set out and defend a verdict on what his position would have been. I'd like you to press hard on the difference between reading the state of nature as a historical description of how people once actually lived and reading it as an analytic device for isolating what is owed to political authority; and to say whether the modern thesis gives Hobbes any reason to revise his view or whether it leaves his argument essentially untouched.

One complicating wrinkle I'd like you to take seriously: a classmate has argued that the modern thesis actually vindicates Rousseau against Hobbes, on the grounds that if pre-agricultural life really was affluent and egalitarian, then the Hobbesian war of all against all looks like a by-product of settled, propertied society rather than its precondition. Engage with that claim directly and say whether it holds up.

Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides before committing, but don't end on a fence-sitting note — reach a firm conclusion and justify it. Please write in British English and in a formal register, and don't include any citations, references or reading lists: I need the argument, not a bibliography.
