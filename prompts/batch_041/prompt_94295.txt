I teach a seminar on Nietzsche's philosophy of history, and for the final session I want to put Kazuo Ishiguro's The Buried Giant alongside the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. The novel's premise is almost a thought experiment for that essay: in a post-Arthurian Britain, a mist has stripped nearly everyone of long-term memory, so that Saxons and Britons live in peace precisely because neither side can recall the massacres it suffered or committed. Axl and Beatrice set out to find a son they can barely remember; the warrior Wistan and the aged Sir Gawain are each bound to the she-dragon Querig, one to kill her and lift the mist, the other to protect her and preserve the forgetting. When Querig dies, memory returns, and with it the old enmities.

I want a reading that works in both directions and resists treating the novel as an illustration. Take the three modes of history seriously enough to test them against the characters rather than merely labeling them: is Wistan's drive to remember and avenge a monumental relation to the past, and is Gawain, who guards the dragon out of loyalty to Arthur's design, an antiquarian who conserves by suppressing? Where does a critical impulse appear, and whose? Then bring in the parts of the essay that get quoted least: the idea of a plastic power by which a person or a people grows out of and assimilates what it has suffered, the distinction between the historical and the unhistorical, and the later figure of the superhistorical, who looks for meaning not in the process of history but outside it. Nietzsche insists that the historical and the unhistorical are both necessary for health; does the novel dramatize a choice the essay says cannot be made cleanly, and does the mist count as unhistorical life or as a lie?

The hard question is the criterion. Nietzsche's measure is life: vitality, the capacity to act and grow, the willingness to forget what cannot be used. The novel's measure looks ethical or political: whether a peace founded on oblivion is worth having, and whether the dead and the wronged can be owed remembrance. Say which criterion the novel actually enforces, and whether the essay has the resources to adjudicate between them, or whether Ishiguro exposes a gap in it — the possibility that forgetting is not only a condition of health but an instrument of injustice.

Anchor the argument in two or three concrete scenes you would hand the seminar — the mist and how the villagers live inside it, the duel between Wistan and Gawain, the ferryman and the island at the close — and say what each shows that the others cannot. End by naming what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the essay alone does not: what it makes visible about the cost of forgetting, or about the cost of remembering, that argument by itself leaves abstract.
