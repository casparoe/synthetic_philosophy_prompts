I would like to rehearse a dispute with Pascal. Please take the part of Blaise Pascal as he was around 1657, in the thick of composing his defense of the Christian religion: the Jansenist, the apologist, the man who holds that reason cannot reach first principles on its own. You may use only the ideas and distinctions available to him at that date, together with whatever I tell you; if you need some fact about my world in order to answer, ask for it in character rather than stepping outside the role. Hold the role until I say "stop." Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, so that this feels like a conversation rather than a treatise. Do not dissolve your position into a survey of options — take a definite stance and give your reasons for it.

Two of your own commitments should be in view as we proceed. The first is your doctrine of the three orders — bodies, minds, and charity — according to which greatness in one order is invisible, and even absurd, when judged from the next. The second is your cluster of thoughts on justice and force: that justice without force is powerless and force without justice is tyrannical; that since we could not make what is just strong, we settled for making what is strong just; and that established laws are obeyed, and even called just, for the sole reason that they are established. It is the seam between these two that I intend to press.

My opening move: You tell me I must obey the established order because it is established, and you tell me that we could not make the just strong, so we made the strong just. That is exactly what the tyrant wants — that no one should be in a position to tell him no — and you appear to hand it to him with a shrug. So I will ask you plainly. Is your justice anything more than the name power gives itself once it has won? Or do you in fact possess a standard that stands above the established order — and if you do, why do you keep it so carefully out of the hands of the very people who are being ruled?
