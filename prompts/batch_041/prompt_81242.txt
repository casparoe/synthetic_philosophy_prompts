I am a novelist, a little over two-thirds of the way through a book set in a provincial town in the winter of 1918 — influenza, the armistice, a family that has already lost one son and does not yet know that it has lost a second — and the research has turned into a standing argument with the two people who read my drafts. The first is a retired historian of the period, who holds that the whole business of explaining the past is retrospective in a way that ought to make us modest, and who reaches for the Monday-morning quarterback whenever he wants to make the point: the fellow in the armchair who knows how the game ended and therefore finds every call obvious. He uses it to license a strong conclusion — that we cannot fairly judge the people of 1918 at all, that "they could not have known" is very nearly always true, and that the historian's confident causal narrative is therefore a species of cheating dressed up as explanation. My second reader, a screenwriter, pushes from the other side: she says the historian and I are in the same trade, that a causal account of the past is a plot imposed after the fact on material that had no plot in it, and that the only real difference between her screenplay, my novel and a scholarly monograph is the footnotes. I am fairly sure those are two different claims, and that the quarterback is carrying at most one of them — possibly not even that one. I would like the analogy audited rather than admired.

So please do the following. Set out the correspondence between the quarterback story and the historian's situation: say what plays the quarterback, the coach, the play call, the information available at the snap, the scoreboard, the rulebook, the game film, and the Monday critic. Then say where the mapping fails, and be specific about which failures matter for the conclusion my historian friend wants to draw and which are merely decorative. I would particularly like you to sort out four things that seem to me to get run together. First, the difference between what was unforeseen and what was unforeseeable — I keep hearing the two used interchangeably and I don't believe they are. Second, the fact that a later writer can sometimes state a truth about an event that no contemporary could have stated, as when we say that the killing of an archduke in Sarajevo started the First World War: a sentence nobody could have written that week, and not a fiction either, so I don't see how the historian's plot is simply invented (I have seen the phrase "narrative sentence" used for this, though I can't remember where). Third, the difference between explaining an outcome and grading a decision, which the quarterback metaphor seems to blur. Fourth, the difference between hindsight bias — a documented failure of memory and judgment that makes people sincerely believe they knew it all along — and the narrative fallacy, which is a complaint about the form of an explanation rather than about its truth. Then tell me whether either of my readers can get what she or he wants out of the analogy, and what a better analogy would be if there is one. Two I have wondered about: the chess annotator, who may call a move a blunder but only by reference to what was on the board and what a player of that strength could reasonably see; and the coroner, who is permitted to state a cause of death without allocating blame. I don't need either of them endorsed — I want to know what each buys and what it costs.

Give me the audit in prose, at whatever length the job actually needs, and then repeat its upshot as a single JSON object with exactly these fields: "analogy" (one sentence naming the analogy and the conclusion it is being asked to license); "correspondence" (an array of objects with "source", "target", "holds" — "yes", "no" or "partly" — and "note", one sentence each); "disanalogies" (an array of objects with "feature", "why_it_matters" and "verdict", where the verdict is "harmless" or "fatal" according to whether the conclusion survives without that feature); "smuggled" (an array of strings, each a premise the analogy imports without arguing for it); "readers" (an object with "historian" and "screenwriter", each containing "supported" — "yes", "no" or "partly" — and "why"); "better_analogy" (an object with "analogy", "what_it_fixes" and "what_it_still_gets_wrong"); and "bottom_line" (one sentence, no hedging, on what the analogy can and cannot carry).
