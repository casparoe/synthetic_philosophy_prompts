Last weekend a colleague and I burned most of a long drive arguing about cognitive penetration, and the argument kept collapsing into whether we were even talking about the same thing. She works in vision science and treated it as close to obvious that expectation shapes what you see; I kept insisting that most of her examples were either attention effects in disguise or cases where the cognition had been baked in long before the moment of seeing, and she kept pressing a constraint I could not decide whether to treat as substantive or as stipulation — that the penetrating state has to be about the same thing the perception is about, that its content has to line up with the perceptual content rather than merely nudge it from the side. We also never settled whether the synchronic-versus-diachronic distinction was doing real work or just relabeling, and I suspect part of our fight was verbal. I now have to run a ninety-minute session on the topic for a mixed reading group — philosophy and psychology graduate students, most of whom have heard the phrase, none of whom know the literature, and a few of whom will arrive convinced that the whole thing is either trivial or obviously true. Plan how I should explain it; do not explain it to me.

I need the order in which the components have to come, with the dependencies made explicit — what has to be in place before the next piece can even be stated, and which pieces I can safely postpone or drop. Tell me which single example should carry the whole session, and be specific about what it must have and what it must lack: it presumably has to be genuinely perceptual rather than a judgment in perceptual clothing, and it has to involve the influence at the moment rather than through learning, but I want you to tell me whether that second requirement is really required and how you would decide. Then tell me why the tempting candidates cannot carry it — ambiguous figures like the duck-rabbit, the Müller-Lyer lines, wishful or motivated seeing, expert pattern recognition in chess or radiology — and whether any of them can serve as a supporting case rather than the spine. I would rather you rule most of them out with reasons than spread the session thin across all of them.

Then map the misconceptions in the order the students will actually form them, not in the order I would prefer: that penetration just means bias or seeing what you want; that it is the same thing as top-down attention or as priming; that any causal influence of cognition on perception counts, which would erase the interesting distinction between an influence installed by learning and one operating at the moment; that the claim is about what people say or judge rather than what they experience; and that if penetration is real then perception is worthless and everything is interpretation. For each one, tell me exactly where in the sequence I should head it off, and whether it is better defused before the example or after it. Finally, give me one question or small task I can put to the group at the end that would show the distinction actually landed rather than just the vocabulary, and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud — I am thinking the predictive-processing reframing, the neural evidence, the modularity wars, and the semantics of the looks-locution, but I want your judgment on which of those to cut and which I cannot cut without the session collapsing. No readings or references, please; I only want the plan.
