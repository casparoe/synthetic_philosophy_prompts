So my two friends Mira and Dev — we're all in a decision theory reading group together — got into it for like an hour and a half at the bar last night over the absent-minded driver thing, and I still can't figure out who had the better of it. Setup, since it matters: you're driving somewhere and the place you want is off the second exit of a highway. Take the first exit and you're nowhere near it, call that 0. Take the second and you're there, call that 10. Blow past the second and you end up at a truck stop forty miles on, call that 3. The twist is that you're an absent-minded driver: when you come up on an exit you can't tell whether it's the first one or the second, they look identical and you dont remember already passing one, so at any exit you have no idea where you are. Mira said she's seen this puzzle before and the usual version has different numbers, but the numbers are beside the point; Dev said that was convenient.

Mira's line: decide on a rule in advance and then follow it, and the best rule is a random one — she worked it out and said you should stay on about 5 times out of 7 at every exit, which is worth about 3.6 on average, better than always getting off (0) or always staying on (3). She said the reason the answer feels obvious to people — stay on at the first exit, get off at the second — is that they're imagining themselves at the second exit knowing it's the second exit, which is the one thing you can't do. Dev came back with a napkin. He said that at an exit the only question is what to do right now with what you know right now, and that if you plug in her own numbers, the chance you're at the second exit is 5 in 12, so getting off is worth 5/12 of 10, about 4.2, while staying on is worth only about 3.3, since when you're at the first exit staying on just lands you in the same dilemma a second time. So by her own math, he said, the thing to do at the exit is get off, which means her rule isn't one you'd actually follow once you're standing there. Mira said the 5-in-12 is circular: that probability is only that high because you're assuming you stay on at the first exit. If you really do get off whenever the napkin says to, the chance you're at the second exit drops to zero and getting off is worth zero — the napkin is borrowing a probability from the exact plan it's telling you to ditch. Dev said fine, then the honest description is that there are two of you, you-at-the-first-exit and you-at-the-second, with different information and different interests, like a procrastinator or a smoker, and that the example only looks deep because you've stipulated one person who can plan but can't remember, which isn't a coherent agent — real agents fix this with a note, an alarm, a passenger, whatever, and the puzzle falls apart. Mira said a note doesn't help ('get off at the second exit' is useless if you can't tell them apart, and 'get off now' gets you off at the first one), and that 'a real agent wouldn't be like that' is just refusing to answer — she brought up drunks, people running on no sleep, people with dementia, and said the question is what the agent should do in the situation, not wether you'd want to be in it. Then they tried a variant: what if there's a passenger who remembers everything and will grab the wheel at the second exit? Dev said that settles it — the driver just never gets off, the passenger handles the second exit, full marks — so the whole thing is really about being alone with no memory. Mira said sure, but now there are two agents, so he's answered a different question.
