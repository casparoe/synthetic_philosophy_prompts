I run a small interview series in our department — one guest a month, low-key, and I write the questions myself, which I'm not great at. Next month's guest is an archivist-turned-historian: she spent about twenty years working inside the records office of a ministry in a country that had a serious security apparatus in the 70s and 80s, and now she writes about what she calls "the unfileable." Her claim is that the important silences in a state archive are mostly not the ones somebody decided to burn or redact; they're the things nobody could write down because there was no box for them — no series, no heading, no form to fill in. The silence, on her view, is an output of the finding aid. She's got a second claim too: that digitisation makes this worse instead of better, because keyword search only finds what somebody already named, so the machine keeps re-selecting the same records and the gaps get more invisible every year, since now it feels like everything is searchable. She's not a household name, so don't try to place her in a school of thought or guess who her influences are — work from those two claims as stated.

I want 9 or 10 questions. Treat it as your job for the next hour: you're a philosopher of history and archival theorist prepping this interview, and before you draft anything, spend a few lines brainstorming what her two claims actually commit her to, what the strongest objection to each one is, and where she's most likely to retreat into a safe answer — then build the questions so that retreat gets harder as the interview goes on.

Some requirements:

- At least one question that goes after the identification problem: how do you tell silence-by-category from silence-by-destruction when the only evidence you have is the archive itself? Include the follow-up for the answer she'll most likely give — she usually says something like, you stop treating the finding aid as a neutral index and read it as a document in its own right. Fine, but then what does that actually get you, and doesn't it just relocate the same problem one level up?
- At least one on responsibility. If nobody decided to erase this stuff, is anyone answerable for it? Does her view quietly let the institution off the hook, and does it matter whether the people doing the filing knew what they were doing? Anticipate the obvious comeback that bureaucratic drift is a form of power too.
- At least one on the digitisation claim, including the strongest counter to it — that search also surfaces things no one was looking for, and that the old paper archive had its own gatekeepers.
- One question that puts her two claims in tension with each other: if the silence is structural, why should a change in naming practice or technology do anything about it? Is she smuggling in an intentionalist fix for a non-intentionalist diagnosis?
- One that turns it back on us: does a historian who writes only from the archive end up reproducing the state's own categories, and what's the alternative — going outside the record entirely, and if so, isn't that a different kind of project?
- She deflects a lot with the line that this is a feature of any record, not a pathology of this one. Make the last two questions progressively harder to answer with that, and end with something concrete — what she'd want a researcher to do differently on Monday morning.

No questions about how she got into archives, no "who should we read" or reading lists, no asking her to name names or recommend literature. Keep the questions in her own vocabulary where you can, and write the likely answer plus the follow-up under each one so I can actually run the thing live.
