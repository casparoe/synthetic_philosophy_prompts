I'm building a tutorial handout on choice without preference — the Buridan's donkey thing, except I dont want the donkey and I don't want anything starving, and I want to get past the two stock moves ("just pick one, it's not really a problem" / "there's always some difference if you look hard enough"). So I'd like a case built to order instead.

Here's what I want it to do. One set of facts, three verdicts about it that come apart. The three ways of thinking about a no-preference choice:

(A) The maximising line. Rational choice is taking something at least as good as anything else on offer. If she has no preference between A and B, both are optimal, so there's nothing to criticise whichever she ends up with, and further deliberation has zero expected value. On this line her later remark — "I never actually chose, I just ended up with B" — is a confused description of what happened: ending up with B was the choice.

(B) The line that puts a genuine tie outside the space of reasons. Where nothing favours either option, the selection isn't an exercise of rational agency at all; it's on the level of a blink. So there's nothing to criticise, but nothing to credit either, and her "I never actually chose" is simply true.

(C) The settling line. The job of practical reason isn't only to land on the better option, it's to arrive at a determinate answer she can own and act from; the failure mode is not picking wrong, it's never settling. So she is criticisable here, even though neither option was worse and even though the outcome was as good as it could have been — the criticism lands on the act, not on the outcome.

So on the same facts I want {is her description accurate?, is there anything to criticise?} to come out {no, no} for (A), {yes, no} for (B), {yes, yes} for (C), and I want each verdict to fall out of the line rather than be asserted by me.

Specs for the case:

- The tie has to be airtight, and I want you to show it with an explicit symmetry argument: every feature that could give her a reason to take one over the other is mirrored in the other. No hidden asymmetry, and no tie-breaker rule dressed up as a reason — no coin, no dice, no lottery, no "take the one on the left", no "take whichever you heard about first". Any rule is a reason in disguise.
- The tie has to be a tie in the world, not just in her head. If it's a tie only because she doesn't know something, that's a different case and not what I'm after.
- No deferring, no exit, no handing it to someone else: she does end up with one of the two.
- Ordinary and moderate stakes — a flat, a job, a course, a holiday. No trolleys, no life support, no dying donkey.
- Scenario itself in one paragraph; the rest can run as long as it needs.

And the bit I think is the real difficulty: I don't see how you get (A) and (C) apart unless she can end up with one of the two without ever having settled it — the world hands her B, and the outcome is exactly as good as it would have been had she deliberately picked B. If drifting costs her anything at all in outcome, (A) collapses into (C) and the case goes boring; if she has to consciously pick, then (B) and (C) both lose their grip. Thread that needle. If you can't, say so and tell me which of the three you had to sacrifice, because that's informative too.

Then write it up in this order: the scenario; the symmetry argument; for each of (A), (B), (C) what it says and why, with the verdict derived rather than stipulated; which single feature of the case is doing the work — name it, and don't tell me "her psychology" or "the stakes"; what happens to each of the three verdicts if that feature is removed or reversed; and finally the strongest way a defender of (A) attacks the case, plus what the defender of (C) says back.

It's going on a handout I have to be able to mark, so no "well, it depends what you mean by rational".
