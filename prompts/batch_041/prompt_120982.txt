I finally watched Hacksaw Ridge and now I cant stop turning it over as a philosophy problem. Desmond Doss won't touch a rifle, gets hazed and court-martialed for it, then goes to Okinawa as a medic and lowers wounded guys down the cliff one at a time, and the movie frames the whole thing as heroic. But I don't think he's a pacifist, and I'm not sure the movie thinks he is either. He's not against the war — he enlisted, he wears the uniform, he's proud when they take the ridge back. He just won't kill, personally, and that seems like a different kind of claim than "this war is wrong." So which is it: absolute pacifism, conscientious objection, or a private vow that happens to sit where a moral position would? I want an actual reading, not a vibe. Take the real positions seriously — absolute pacifism, the just-war tradition, and the move I've seen where you grant just-war theory all its premises and then point out that no actual war ever satisfies them (I think people call this contingent pacifism but I may be mangling the name). Which one does Doss hold, which one does the film hold, and where does the film undercut its own hero — because Gibson clearly loves shooting the violence, and I honestly cant tell if thats a bug or a feature. Say where the film resists the reading too; I'd rather have the tension than a tidy answer.
