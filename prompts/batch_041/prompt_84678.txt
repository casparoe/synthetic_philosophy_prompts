I am an undergraduate taking a course on reinforcement learning and value alignment, and I have come to hold the following position: the underdetermination of an agent's preferences by its behaviour — the problem that motivates inverse reinforcement learning in the first place — is benign. Grant enough demonstrations, a feature space rich enough to express the relevant distinctions, and a sensible regulariser, and the residual ambiguities (reward shaping, the means–end problem, partial observability) either wash out or can be resolved by querying the agent counterfactually; what survives is the agent's preference structure up to differences no one should care about.

I would like to know what would count against this. Which toy cases, formal results, or empirical findings should move me, and which are merely awkward? In particular, I want a case in which two preference structures are behaviourally indistinguishable however much demonstration data one collects, yet diverge in a way that matters morally or for policy, if such a case exists — and if the benign view can only be rescued by counterfactual queries, whether that concession quietly changes the claim from one about inferring preferences from behaviour into something else. Tell me what a committed defender of the benign view would themselves concede, whether the position has been insulated from disconfirmation, and whether that insulation is a defect or simply what a view of this kind looks like. I would be most persuaded by an argument showing that the ambiguity is not merely epistemic but bites on the choice of objective we train on. Please answer in British English.
