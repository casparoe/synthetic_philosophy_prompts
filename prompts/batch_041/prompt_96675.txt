I'm a novelist — which means my grasp of probability is mostly vibes and the fact that my royalty statements never look like a bell curve. Anyway, I've somehow been asked to interview a guest at a small festival. He's written a book arguing that 'geometric rationality' (Kelly betting, expected log wealth, time averages rather than ensemble averages) is the real core of practical rationality, not just a gambling heuristic. I want to ask him things that make him actually work, not just restate 'bet proportionally to your edge.' Can you draft eight to ten questions, with a follow-up for each keyed to the answer he's most likely to give? I'll definately mispronounce something, so keep the questions speakable.

Some things I want covered, and please go into the weeds:
- The difference between maximizing expected log wealth and maximizing the probability of reaching some goal. I've heard that if your goal is to double your money before going broke, the optimal strategy can look nothing like Kelly — is that right, and what does he make of it? If Kelly is supposed to be the rational strategy simpliciter, how does it handle goal-directed betting?
- One-shot vs repeated bets. He'll say Kelly is for repeated bets, but most of life's biggest choices are one-shot. Is he smuggling in an ensemble of possible worlds, or a time-average argument, and does that actually work for a unique decision? I want the follow-up to pin him down on whether geometric rationality is a theory of rationality or just a theory of multiplicative growth.
- The ergodicity stuff. He'll say expected value is a trap because the ensemble average isn't the time average. Press on where the time average comes from when the process isn't stationary, when you can't rebalance, or when the 'you' across time isn't a single continuous agent. Is non-ergodicity a mathematical fact about the world or a choice of model?
- The bankroll problem. If the thing you're maximizing isn't money — lives saved, novels written, years of health — what's the log of? He probably says 'utility,' but then ask whether the theory has any content left, or whether it just says 'be risk averse in a particular way.' Can he give a case where geometric rationality and ordinary expected utility with a concave utility function come apart in a way that matters?
- The altruism case. If I'm betting on behalf of a large population, or on an existential-risk intervention, the 'bankroll' is the whole future. Does Kelly tell you to be infinitely risk averse with infinite stakes? And if not, what's the principled stopping rule?
- Insurance and hedging. What does geometric rationality say about buying insurance against a small probability of a huge loss — and does the answer change if the loss is existential? I want to know whether the theory has a principled account of when to hedge, or whether it just inherits whatever the utility function says.
- The 'deflection' problem. He's very good at saying 'that's just log utility' or 'that's a research program, not an objection.' I want a sequence where each follow-up makes that move harder to use, ending with a question about what evidence or argument would actually make him abandon geometric rationality.
- A literary/philosophical one, because it's my festival: does geometric rationality have anything to say about how a novelist should bet on a career — taking the safe advance vs. the risky option, or writing the weird book that might fail? Or is that just an analogy that breaks down because the payoffs aren't repeated and the bankroll is a person's life?

Don't give me a reading list or citations — he can't look things up on stage and neither can I. Just the questions and follow-ups, in the order you'd ask them, and make the follow-ups specific enough that he can't answer with a shrug.
