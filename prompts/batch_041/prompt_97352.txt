I sit in an interdisciplinary working group — two Aegean prehistorians, a Hebrew Bible scholar, a medievalist who works on Arthurian material, and a pair of philosophers — that has met for roughly two years on a single question: when does a legend become history? The standing cases are the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus. We cannot agree, and I have come to suspect we are not even disagreeing about the same thing.

The Aegean side treats the question as empirical and largely settled in outline. There is a fortified mound at Hisarlik with destruction levels, and Hittite records appear to name a place and a people resembling the Greek names for Troy and the Achaeans, so some conflict of some kind is historically respectable, while the Iliad's plot is poetry. The biblical scholar insists the Exodus is a different animal: the relevant text is not a war poem but a foundation narrative with a theological program, and neither the archaeology of Sinai nor the Egyptian record gives anything to hang a mass migration on, so "history" is simply the wrong category. The medievalist says Arthur is a third case again — a figure who may have a kernel in a post-Roman war leader, but whose entire surviving documentation is centuries later and demonstrably literary, so asking whether Arthur existed is like asking whether Sherlock Holmes had a real address. And the philosophers keep observing that "becomes history" is doing all the work and no one has said what it means.

My hypothesis — which I want you to test rather than accept — is that the impasse is not a disagreement about the evidence but about what a historical claim is a claim about. One side treats "X happened" as a claim about a minimally specified event that can be abstracted from every source that mentions it; the other treats "X happened" as a claim about a specific narrated episode, so that stripping away the details leaves you with a different claim that no source actually supports. If that is right, the three cases may not be three instances of one question but three different questions wearing a single name.

I would like you to do four things. First, reconstruct each side's position as charitably and concretely as you can. Second, diagnose the disagreement: is it verbal, substantive-but-empirical, a clash over standards of evidence, a case of the sides answering different questions under one heading, or a dispute resting on starting intuitions that neither side can argue for? Third, test my hypothesis against the three cases and say whether it survives. Fourth, say what, if anything, would count as progress.

To keep this concrete, engage two devices a colleague has proposed and say whether either survives scrutiny:

(a) the "minimum viable event" test — a legend has become history when there exists a description of an event so thin that it is compatible with every plausible reading of the sources and still worth asserting; anything thicker is interpretation, anything thinner is not an event at all;

(b) the distinction between an event being historical and an event being recoverable by historians, the suggestion being that our two camps are answering those two different questions under the single heading "did it happen?"

Also tell me whether any of the three cases is genuinely ill-posed rather than merely hard, and whether the three differ in kind along some dimension the group has not yet named. Conclude with your best account of what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, evidence that does not yet exist, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the dispute is a proxy for something else, such as what authority we want sacred and classical narratives to carry. Do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the diagnosis and the reasoning behind it.
