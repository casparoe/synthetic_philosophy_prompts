My friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether there is a real asymmetry between bringing a happy person into existence and bringing a miserable one into existence, and we got nowhere, so I want to test the question against a settled body of practice rather than against one more intuition. The practice is the law's handling of negligently caused births, and I have come to think it is stranger than either of us noticed.

In many jurisdictions, when a physician's negligence results in a child's being conceived or born whom the parents would otherwise not have had, the parents may sue to recover the added costs of raising that child. Where the child is born healthy, courts often refuse recovery altogether, on the reasoning that a healthy child is a benefit and not an injury; where the child is born with a severe impairment, recovery is more readily allowed, on the understanding that such a life imposes a heavier burden. But when the child himself, grown, sues on his own behalf and claims that he would have been better off never existing at all, the claim almost always fails, and it fails on a specific rationale: that existence and nonexistence cannot be weighed against each other, or that life, however burdened, is a benefit rather than a harm. So the very same negligently caused birth is a compensable injury when the parents assert it and no injury at all when the person who must live it asserts it. And in ordinary life, the failure to conceive a happy child is not treated as a loss, while the deliberate conception of a child destined for severe suffering is widely treated as a wrong.

What theory of value, of harm, and of the conditions under which someone can be said to be worse off does this pattern presuppose? Does it commit the law to the antinatalist asymmetry — that suffering created is a harm while happiness never created is no loss — or to the opposite view that existence is always a benefit, or to neither? Reconstruct the implicit axiology as precisely as you can, then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with: which parts would survive a replacement of the underlying theory of welfare, and which would have to change? I am especially interested in the apparent inconsistency between compensating the parents and refusing the child, and in whether any single coherent account of harm can license both. Tell me which rival views of welfare — hedonic, desire-based, objective-list, or the view that a harm requires a subject who is worse off than they would otherwise have been — would each license this practice, and which it quietly rules out. Finally, say what a legal regime built on the opposite premise, that existence can itself be a harm, would have to do differently, and whether the practice we have is philosophically underdetermined between the two.
