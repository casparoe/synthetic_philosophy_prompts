I am preparing a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation and its application in law and clinical ethics, and I want to begin with the criteria rather than the theories. Set out the desiderata that any adequate theory of causation by omission must satisfy, where "adequate" means: precise enough to adjudicate contested cases in which it is disputed whether someone's not doing something caused a harm, and disciplined enough to avoid collapsing either into the claim that any counterfactual dependence is causation or into the claim that omissions never cause anything at all.

For each desideratum give me four things: the demand stated in a sentence; the case, puzzle, or result that motivates it; what it rules out; and whether it is non-negotiable or instead the preference of one camp, in which case say which camp would deny it and on what grounds.

Your list has to accommodate at least the following, which I take to be fixed points — but tell me if any of them is not a genuine requirement at all, but a parochial feature of one legal tradition or one camp's intuitions:

- A pair of cases with the same outcome and the same counterfactual profile, differing only in whether the agent acted: a life-support machine switched off versus the identical machine never switched on. A theory must either treat these alike and explain away the persistent intuition that they differ, or treat them differently and say precisely what the difference consists in.
- Omissions are absences, and absences do not look like entities. The theory must say what the relata of the causal relation are in an omission case, or explain why that question is confused.
- The individuation problem: my failing to water your plant is also my failing to water every other plant and my not doing countless other things. If omissions are causes, the theory needs a non-arbitrary way to pick out the causally relevant one, and the obvious route — appeal to duties or norms — threatens to build normativity into causation itself.
- Chains that run through an omission which was itself produced by an earlier action: someone's manoeuvre distracts the only person in a position to intervene, and so the intervention does not happen.
- The legal asymmetry: liability for harmful action without any prior duty, versus liability for omission only where a duty exists; and doctrines such as loss of chance, where liability attaches even though the but-for test is not satisfied.
- The empirical asymmetry: the robust tendency of subjects to rate harmful omissions as less blameworthy, and omissions as more permissible, than otherwise equivalent actions, even when the costs are the same.
- The fact that fundamental physical descriptions contain no absences. If omissions cause, then either causation is not a fundamental feature of the world, or the causal story is not exhausted by physics.
- Overdetermination and preemption by omission: two omissions, each of which alone would have sufficed to prevent the harm; and a case where the first omission would not have prevented the harm had the second not occurred.
- The distinction between causation and causal selection: even granting that omissions are among the causes of an outcome, we pick out some of them for explanation and blame. The theory should say whether that selection belongs to causation or to a separate pragmatic layer.

After the list, do three further things. First, identify any subset of the desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, with the tension or argument spelled out and the cost of each way out. Second, name the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise popular positions, and explain why. Third, list any candidate desiderata you considered and rejected as not genuine requirements, with your reason for rejecting each.

Do not include a bibliography, citations, or a reading list, and do not lean on the names of authors or works — I will be circulating this without the surrounding literature, so refer to positions by describing them.

Return a single JSON object and nothing outside it, with these keys:

- "desiderata": an array of objects, each with "id" (short string such as "D1"), "requirement" (one sentence), "kind" (one of "case_to_get_right", "distinction_to_preserve", "consistency_constraint", "explanatory_demand"), "case_or_consideration" (the motivating case or result, two to four sentences), "what_it_rules_out" (the positions or moves it forbids), "status" ("non_negotiable" or "camp_specific"), and "camp_that_would_reject_it" (a description of the position, or null).
- "conflict_sets": an array of objects, each with "desideratum_ids" (array of ids), "conflict" (what cannot be had together), and "cost_of_each_way_out" (an object mapping each way out to its cost).
- "most_exclusionary_requirement": an object with "id" and "why".
- "candidates_rejected_as_prejudice": an array of objects with "candidate" and "why_not_a_requirement".
- "purpose_sensitivity": a short string saying which of the desiderata would drop off if the theory were needed only for explanation in the sciences rather than for adjudication.
