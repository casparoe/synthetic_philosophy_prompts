My closest friend and I have spent the better part of a month returning to the same argument, and I would like your help adjudicating it rather than simply being told which of us is right. It started over dinner: I mentioned that I was thinking of giving up beef, and probably short-haul flights, largely out of concern for the climate, and she told me — not unsympathetically — that this was a category error. Her case, as I reconstruct it, is this. The climate outcome is determined by aggregate emissions from billions of people; my own contribution is imperceptible to any measurement or model; and no identifiable person is harmed by my steak or my flight in a way that would have gone differently had I ordered the fish and taken the train. Since my individual action changes nothing about who gets harmed, she says, there is no harm for me to be answerable for; and where there is no harm there is no obligation, only a costly private ritual that lets me feel virtuous while everyone else keeps emitting. When I protest that the same reasoning would dissolve any duty to vote, to recycle, or to keep my own small promises about not littering, she agrees cheerfully that this is precisely the point and that people simply dislike where it leads.

What nags at me is that her argument keeps sliding between two descriptions of the same situation. Sometimes the question is what happens in the actual world if I alone change, where my effect is essentially nil. At other moments she will grant, almost in passing, that if a great many people changed, the aggregate would shift — but then treat that as irrelevant on the grounds that enough people will not. She also offered a thought experiment I cannot shake: imagine a sealed room filling with gas through a hundred tiny valves, such that the air turns lethal only when the hundredth valve opens; in that room, she said, whoever opens the final valve is the killer and everyone else is innocent, and since none of us can know which valve is ours, we should stop pretending our own valve matters at all. I have the sense that the real question is not whether my lone action makes a measurable difference but what kind of thing my action is — whether it is best described as a drop in an ocean, as the opening of one valve among many, or as participation in a practice that, taken all together, is what does the harm. She regards that last way of talking as a linguistic trick that lets people feel responsible for outcomes they did not cause.

I would like your advice on how to think this through and, more than that, on where the genuine disagreement lies. If you were in my position — genuinely trying to decide what to eat and how to travel, knowing that the change will be invisible in its effects — how would you reason it out? I am less interested in a verdict on beef than in the structure of the problem: what exactly would have to be true for her to be right, what her argument quietly assumes (for instance, about what it takes for one thing to count as the cause of another), and whether there is a way of describing individual contributions to large-scale harms that survives her objection without falling back on the bare thought that one ought to act as though everyone else would do the same.
