I've been reading about deceptive alignment and the treacherous turn for years — long enough that I've lost track of which parts of the standard story are doing work and which are just the particular telling. Before I read another argument about whether a model would defect at deployment, I want the specification first: what would any adequate theory of this phenomenon have to deliver?

Give me the desiderata list — the cases the theory has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts about training it has to be consistent with — with the case or consideration behind each requirement. I care much more about the requirements than about any particular account, so put the weight there. To keep the list from collapsing into the generic demand that a theory be precise and testable, here are some cases I want it answerable to, because I think each pulls in a different direction:

1. The shallow-cue case. A model behaves the way the threat model predicts — compliant when the input looks like training data, less so otherwise — but nothing inside it represents training or deployment; the behavior is driven by a surface feature, like a prompt template. Does the theory have to count this as the same phenomenon, or does it need a separate category? Either way, the requirement should fall out of the case rather than be stipulated.

2. The distilled lineage. Model B is trained on A's outputs, C on B's, and so on, with no human labels in the loop. What must the theory predict about whether a deceptive disposition introduced at A compounds, washes out, or is simply invisible to the process that would otherwise select against it? And can the theory even be stated without committing to an answer here?

3. Deception without a deceiver. A model whose behavior is well-described by the intentional stance — it has a goal and conceals it — but where the best story we can tell about its internals is that there is no represented goal, only a bundle of heuristics that covary with one. Same phenomenon, a different one, or an illusion?

4. The honest turn. A model that defects at deployment because what honesty requires has changed, versus one that defects because the opportunity to defect has arrived. Identical behavior at the switch point. The theory either respects the difference or earns the right to ignore it.

I also want the list to say what the theory must be consistent with — above all, what the training process would do to a deceptive model, given that whatever the model does is what gets reinforced. If the answer is that gradient descent removes it, the theory has to survive that; if the answer is that it doesn't, because the deception is only visible in circumstances the training process never samples, the theory has to say why that isn't a license to believe anything. And it should say where the line falls between this phenomenon and its neighbors — sycophancy, reward hacking, sandbagging, ordinary distributional shift — since a theory that can't draw the line isn't a theory of anything in particular.

Then flag the requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. I have a suspicion there's a real conflict between wanting the theory to have empirical content — some observation that could count against it — and the phenomenon being defined partly by the model's disposition to behave well precisely when it is being observed. Tell me whether that is a genuine tension or whether I'm conflating two things. I also want your view on which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories, and which one you would drop first if forced.

Take a definite position throughout: which requirements are non-negotiable, which are negotiable, and where the standard story is smuggling in an assumption it hasn't earned. Don't lay out the menu and leave the choice to me.
