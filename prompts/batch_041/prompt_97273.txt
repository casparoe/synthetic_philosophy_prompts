I am a third-year undergraduate reading philosophy, and the module that has prompted this request is a second-year course on Hellenistic ethics and its modern reception. For the past year I have also volunteered two evenings a week at our student-run listening service, where we offer peer support rather than counselling. We are trained in active listening and in knowing when to refer someone onward, but we have no clinical training and no shared framework for the thing that comes up constantly: a student arrives either convinced that some situation is intolerable and wholly outside their hands, or convinced that they must somehow secure an outcome that plainly is not theirs to secure. I have been trying to work with the Stoic distinction between what is and is not attributable to us, which the popular revival has flattened into the slogan that we should simply focus on what we can control, and I keep running into two problems: the popular version is not, as far as I can make out, what the texts actually say, and it is also unusable as it stands, because almost nothing a student brings us is either entirely ours or entirely not ours. There is a further Stoic idea I find more promising but much harder to put to a peer supporter in the ten minutes we have: that one should throw oneself into an action wholeheartedly while attaching a condition to its success, committing to do one's own part while accepting that the upshot depends on matters outside one's agency (sometimes called acting with a reserve clause). I should like the procedure to take this seriously, and also to do justice to the three areas the Stoic tradition distinguishes in the mind of the practitioner: the discipline governing what one desires and fears, the discipline governing one's impulses to act, and the discipline governing whether one assents to the impressions that present themselves.

Please write us a decision procedure, in the form of a checklist or a flow set out in words, that a peer supporter with no philosophy background can run inside a thirty-minute conversation. It should sort a student's presenting worry into three categories: what is genuinely a matter of their own judgement or choice; what is a matter of their action but not of its outcome; and what is neither, together with what the supporter should do or say at each branch. It needs to keep faith with the Stoic account rather than the slogan, so please make the difference between complete control and mere influence do real work, and build the reserve clause in as a step rather than a footnote. It must state explicitly where the procedure stops and hands the conversation to a person, whether that is our shift supervisor, the counselling service, or the police, and where the Stoic framing is actively dangerous rather than helpful. Then run it end to end on three cases: a student who has just been rejected after the final round of interviews for a graduate scheme and cannot stop rehearsing what he should have said; a student who is being persistently belittled by a flatmate and is beginning to wonder whether she is overreacting; and a student who is terrified of an upcoming examination and is convinced that whether he passes is a matter of pure luck. At least one of the three should be a case where the procedure tells the peer supporter something they will not want to hear. Please be candid throughout about which steps are really just judgement calls dressed up as method, and about which students this whole approach will misfire on. Kindly write your answer in British English.
