I'm on the board of a small land trust that owns a stretch of coastal marsh. Twelve years ago the founding board got spooked by a developer sniffing around and wrote a lock-in clause into the bylaws: the marsh can only be sold, leased, or rezoned by a 90% vote of the membership, and the clause itself can only be changed by that same 90% — no sunset, no escape hatch. Two odd details came with it: the founders also wrote a recusal rule barring anyone who voted *for* the clause from voting on any later amendment to it, on the theory that you can't be trusted to judge your own handiwork (so the one surviving founder, who still says she'd do it again, is barred from the vote); and there's a "reconsideration trigger" letting a single member force one membership-wide re-vote per decade if they can show the original rationale no longer applies. Now a developer has offered enough to fund the trust's actual conservation work for a century, the membership is split, and someone's floated dissolving the trust and re-forming under a new name to sidestep the clause entirely. I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thurdsay's board meeting: a concrete recomendation — honor the lock-in, amend it, or take the end-run — with reasons and a timeline, engaging the strongest version of whichever side you come down against rather than just restating the founders' intentions. Be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on self-binding and constitutional precommitment and which are genuinely contested; I don't want a contested position sold to me as the consensus. Since a lot rides on the bottom line, end with a final line reading exactly: Verdict: <honor/amend/end-run>.
