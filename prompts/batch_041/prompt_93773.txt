I have been reading arguments about whether violence has declined over the long run, and I keep noticing a structural feature of the debate that I have no word for. I am not asking you to adjudicate the trend itself; I want to know whether this feature of it has an established name, and if it does not, what it should be called and how it should be bounded.

Here is the feature. Suppose someone assembles a long series of estimates — killings, assaults, and the like — and reports that the rate has fallen by an order of magnitude. A critic objects that the series captures only a thin band of what violence is: it leaves out what states do to people who are not counted, what happens inside households, what deprivation and neglect amount to. The objection is not that the numbers are wrong but that the category is too narrow. What interests me is that the critic is usually right, and that the category has in fact widened over the very period whose trend is being measured. Things that were once not violence at all — corporal punishment in schools, coercion within marriage, cruelty to animals — are now violence, or near enough. So the instrument has been recalibrated, repeatedly, and always in the direction of the finding. And the recalibration is not opportunism: each widening is a genuine moral discovery, defensible on its own. What unsettles me is the asymmetry. I cannot think of any corresponding pressure over the same period to make the category contract, so the process that is supposed to be the subject matter of the measurement is also the process that sharpens it. It is as though the thermometer were designed by the fever.

I have seen something that feels like the same shape elsewhere — a poverty threshold redrawn as a society grows richer, so that the count can hold steady while the condition improves — and I do not know whether these are one phenomenon or two.

I do not think this is simply the charge of moving the goalposts, since no one here is arguing in bad faith and the goalposts are not being shifted to save a thesis; nor is it just the familiar warning that a measure degrades once it becomes a target, since nothing is being gamed. The ordinary point that observation is theory-laden also seems too weak, because the difficulty is not that the category depends on a theory but that it depends on, and drifts with, the very process it is meant to record.

So: does this configuration have a name? If there is an established concept for it, I would like to know which, and — more useful to me — exactly where the fit fails. If there is none, please coin one, define it, and say what it excludes. I would especially like to know what has to be true for the phenomenon to arise, and whether a moralized category is required or only a category whose edges respond to the same social process the trend describes; whether the problem is a defect in the claim, a defect in our ability to test it, or neither; and whether any measure of such a quantity can escape it, and what is lost by fixing the definition once and for all.
