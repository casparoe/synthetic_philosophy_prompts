I'm in a reading group on the metaphysics of the artificial, and I keep running into a pattern in how virtual worlds are actually governed that I cannot make sense of. Take any large online game or social VR platform. Its terms of service are emphatic that players own nothing: items, in-game currency, and even the account are described as a revocable licence, so the operator may delete them, wipe a server, or ban a player without compensating anyone, and no one is expected to treat the deletion as a taking. Yet the same operator aggressively enforces a prohibition on trading those very items for real money, restores a stolen or duplicated item as though it were a loss, and in some places has gone to court over it — conduct that only makes sense if the items are property with value that someone can wrongfully take. Meanwhile the platform polices harassment, threats, and in VR even simulated physical contact, but frames the remedy as a matter of community standards rather than harm: an expelled griefer has, officially, harmed no one, even though the targets report the same distress they would report from the offline equivalent, and the same platform markets its world as a place where real friendships and real feelings are formed.

What I want from you is the implicit philosophy of this whole arrangement. Reconstruct the view of virtual objects and virtual harms that the practice presupposes — what it must hold about existence, ownership, and welfare for this combination of denial and enforcement to hang together — and then tell me which parts of the practice genuinely require that view and which merely fit comfortably with it. I want you in particular to separate three things: what the practice strictly presupposes; what it is merely consistent with (a fictionalist account on which virtual items are useful fictions, say, or an institutionalist account on which they are real because a shared rule makes them so); and where the practice is simply underdetermined, so that several rival ontologies of virtual objects and several rival theories of harm would license exactly the same rules. Then tell me what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with the most defensible rival, both for an operator's terms of service and for how a legislature ought to treat virtual theft and virtual assault.

Please answer in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables; write it the way you would write a long email to a colleague who is curious about the topic but not a specialist.
