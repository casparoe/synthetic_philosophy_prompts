I am a first-year PhD student sitting in on a mind-and-cognition seminar and I have been handed the handout for the week on emotion, which has turned out to be harder than it sounded, because the whole room — me included — keeps collapsing the debate into a three-way contest between feelings, judgments, and perceptions and then arguing about which of the three wins. I have started to suspect that the triad itself is the problem, so what I want is not a list of positions but a decision tree of them. Build it from the logical space first: identify the questions that actually divide theorists and the combinations of answers those questions allow, and only then locate real positions in the resulting space, so that I can see which combinations are empty. Where a combination is unoccupied, say whether you think that is because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but nobody wants it, or because it has simply not been explored. Every internal node should be phrased as a question a reader could answer without already knowing which position the answer commits them to — a question about a case, or a claim to accept or reject — with the answer options spelled out on the branches, and no node may take the form of asking whether emotions are cognitive or whether they are judgments, since those are the conclusions I want the tree to reach rather than the questions I want it to ask. If there are two positions your tree cannot pull apart, say so plainly rather than adding a node that separates them only by relabeling.

The tree should turn on the following cases, which are what the seminar keeps stumbling over: (i) a sudden bang makes someone flinch and their heart pound, and a second later they realize nothing is wrong and the state drains away — is the flinch an emotion, and if not, what exactly is missing; (ii) someone is afraid of a spider they know perfectly well is harmless, and stays afraid for years — what is the fear getting wrong, and in what sense of wrong; (iii) a person who apologizes, avoids, and behaves protectively in ways everyone around them reads as guilt, but who reports feeling nothing — and, symmetrically, a person who reports intense fear with no behavior or physiology to match; (iv) the same racing heart before a talk, felt by one person as dread and by another as excitement, where the question is whether there is any fact of the matter about which it is apart from how it is taken up; (v) fear and grief directed at things that do not exist, as in a horror film or grief for a person who never lived; (vi) an emotion term in another language that maps onto no English emotion word and comes with its own script for when it is appropriate; (vii) the alarm and mobbing behavior of a bird, or what looks like jealousy in a dog; and (viii) a free-floating mood of irritability that is not about anything in particular. The distinction I most want to see the tree make visible is whether an emotion's evaluative content is the sort of thing one can be wrong about in the way one is wrong about a belief, or only in the way one is wrong about a perception — and I want to know whether that distinction does independent work or turns out to be the judgment/perception line wearing a different hat. A secondary interest is whether two emotions could share all of their content and differ only in what they dispose the subject to do.

At minimum the leaves should include a pure bodily-feeling view, an evaluative-judgment view, an evaluative-perception view, an attitude-first view on which the emotion is neither a belief nor a perception nor a bodily feeling but a distinctive evaluative stance toward an object, an action-tendency-first view, and a deflationary view on which emotions do not form a single natural kind at all. Name each leaf descriptively rather than by its defenders — I want the tree to sort people by what they say about the cases, not by which tradition they would cite — and give each leaf one sentence stating the most awkward thing it has to say about one of the cases above. Aim for six to nine internal nodes and six to eight leaves, keep the whole thing short enough to fit on two sides of a handout, and close with a brief note on which axes you treated as independent and whether you think they really are independent, since that is the assumption the whole tree rests on.
