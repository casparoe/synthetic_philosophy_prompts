I run the certification unit at a state natural resources agency. The legislature created a conservation tax credit for owners of what the statute calls legacy forest, and left the term undefined, so the definition is ours. Our current rule certifies a parcel when it meets three criteria: a structural complexity score computed from a standard plot survey, the age of the dominant tree cohort, and a documented history of no commercial harvest since a stated date. Every one of those criteria is continuous and noisy. Plot scores drift, cohort ages carry error bars, harvest histories are reconstructed from old aerial photographs and from what owners tell us, and the parcels themselves grade into one another across a landscape that has been selectively logged for a century. We process a few hundred parcels a year, have certified roughly nine hundred and denied about twelve hundred, and our denial letters now cite one another in ways that do not add up. Two owners have appealed on the ground that a parcel nearly identical to a certified one was denied. My reviewers are forestry technicians with no philosophical training and about forty minutes per parcel; any departure from the procedure needs my signature; and the statute is not going to change.

I want a decision procedure my unit can run on every parcel, grounded in the philosophy of vagueness — the sorites paradox and what is actually established about how vague predicates behave. Please write it as an ordered set of steps: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the matter to a person because the question is not one of fact. No names or reading lists; I want the machinery.

Specifically:

- The procedure has to separate three things we currently conflate: clearly in, clearly out, and genuinely unsettled. Within the third, it has to separate cases that more evidence would settle, where the trouble is our ignorance, from cases that no further evidence would settle, where the trouble is the term itself. Those two must be routed differently, and the test that distinguishes them has to be something a technician can apply in the field.
- It has to say what happens when criteria conflict, as when complexity is low but the cohort is very old and the harvest history is clean. Decide whether any criterion is a veto or whether trade-offs are allowed, and defend the choice.
- It has to handle chains: one parcel that differs from a certified parcel by a single increment, and a certified parcel whose management plan will shave a little off its complexity every year for thirty years. No single increment should be dispositive, but cumulative change has to be catchable, and I want you to show me where the sorites hazard hides in whatever cumulative rule you give me.
- It has to survive an appeal. That means it must not pretend the line we draw was discovered in the landscape rather than chosen by us. Give me the honest version, tell me exactly where we are choosing rather than measuring, and give me language for denial letters that states what the line is, why it sits where it sits, and why this parcel falls on the far side of it.
- Whatever borderline band you build will itself have vague edges. Say how the procedure avoids an infinite regress there, and whether the band should function as a verdict or only as a routing device.
- Tell me what to do with the cases that still cannot be settled, so that the outcome is something other than a coin flip.

Then run the procedure on three parcels: one that is clearly in under our rule but ecologically mediocre, one where the evidence is complete and the criteria genuinely disagree, and one that is certified today and will fall below the line in about fifteen years under its current management plan. At least one of these must come out in a way I will not like; say which one and why. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do, which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where a reviewer could game it.

Write in American English.
