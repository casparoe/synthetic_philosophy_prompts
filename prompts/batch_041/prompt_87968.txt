I'm a third-year history student writing a term paper for a course on historiography and the philosophy of history. The strand of the course I keep returning to is the debate over counterfactual history — the practice of asking what would have happened had some event gone differently — together with the connected question of whether a given outcome was inevitable or merely one possibility among several. For the paper I want to attempt something a little unusual: rather than summarize a position, I want to predict what Tolstoy would have made of this debate, given that his own philosophy of history, worked out at length in War and Peace, denies that individual wills and great men are the causes of events and treats history as governed by laws emerging from the aggregate of countless small actions. What makes this hard is that the debate is essentially a late-twentieth-century one; Tolstoy never encountered it, so there is no recorded answer to look up.

Here is the exchange I'd like you to reason about. One historian proposes that counterfactual history can be made rigorous in either of two ways. First, by controlled comparison: to decide whether an outcome was inevitable, locate two settings that were alike in the relevant respects until one of them lacked the supposed cause, and check whether the outcomes still diverged. Second, by robustness: call an outcome inevitable to the extent that it reappears across a wide range of small variations in the antecedent conditions, and contingent to the extent that it disappears under slight changes. A second historian objects that both proposals rest on a category mistake: episodes cannot be rerun, no two settings are genuinely alike in the relevant respects, and the resulting claims are therefore unfalsifiable and belong to fiction rather than to knowledge.

What I want is a prediction rather than a summary. How would Tolstoy have responded to this exchange? Would he endorse the comparison-based test, accept counterfactual reasoning only at the level of aggregate laws rather than particular events, reject both positions as misframed, or land somewhere else? Please be concrete about the arguments he would offer, and where his position would leave him without a good reply to one of the two historians, say so rather than papering over it.
