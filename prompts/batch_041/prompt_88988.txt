I am a novelist, three drafts into a book whose protagonist is a woman who has access to an oracle — an actuarial machine, in the version I am writing — which predicts her choices with perfect accuracy, and I have arrived at the chapter in which she must decide something in full knowledge of what the machine expects of her, and I cannot make her reasoning sound because I cannot make my own sound. I have been arguing about this for months with a friend who teaches decision theory and another who writes crime fiction, and their vocabulary has stopped helping: by their lights I am an evidentialist before lunch and a causalist after. What I want is not a reading list but the principle, or the small set of rival principles, that my verdicts on the following cases add up to, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is earning its keep. Here is what I actually think, in the order I wrote it down. First, the boxes: a predictor who has been right a hundred times out of a hundred placed money in a box last night; the opaque box holds a million if she predicted I would take it alone, and nothing otherwise; a clear box holds a thousand regardless. I take the opaque box alone, and my reason is that my choice is a symptom of the kind of person I am, and she is betting on the kind of person I am — which, said aloud, sounds like a claim that the money is still open, and I know it is not. Second, the glass box: same setup, except the box is glass, I can see the million in it, and therefore know she predicted one-boxing. Now I take both, and leaving the million looks like nothing but a mistake; a friend says the glass cannot possibly matter, since the money is placed either way, and I have no answer to him. Third, the herb: a gene makes its carriers enjoy a certain herb and, quite separately, kills half of them; the herb itself does nothing. I take the herb and enjoy it. Fourth, the ride: stranded on a road, I am offered a lift by a driver who says he stops only for people who will pay him a hundred on arrival, and once we reach town I could walk off and he would have neither recourse nor any way to find me. I would get in and pay, and I think stiffing him would be wrong rather than merely shabby.

Fifth, and this is the one I keep circling: a man in the market at Damascus sees Death look at him, flees to Aleppo, and dies there, learning afterwards that her appointment with him had always been in Aleppo. The stripped-down version is what disturbs me — Death's whereabouts fixed in advance by her prediction of my choice, so that whichever city I settle on is the city where I die, and I am told this in advance. My verdict is that I have none. The moment I notice myself leaning toward staying, staying begins to look like the fatal choice and flight like the way out; the moment I notice myself leaning toward flight, it reverses. Something is wrong with a question that behaves like that, and yet in the story the man does die, so I do not think I can call the case defective and go home. I invented a smaller one to test myself: a machine has already predicted which of two levers I will pull, and has arranged matters so that the lever it predicted pays nothing while the other pays a hundred, and I am not told which it predicted. Again I have no verdict; each lever looks like the losing one while I contemplate it and the winning one while I contemplate the other, which suggests the Damascus flip is not about death at all — though I cannot tell whether it is a fact about the world or a fact about how I hold a question in my head. My own tentative principle is this: a fact about what I will do can be a reason for me only insofar as it bears on something still open, so that I may treat my choice as a symptom of an unsettled matter but never of a settled one. It tells me to take both boxes through the glass, which I accept; to take the herb, which I accept; to take both boxes in the opaque case, where I keep one-boxing; and it says nothing whatever about the ride, since paying the driver is neither a cause nor a symptom of anything. So either the principle is wrong or I am, and I cannot tell which clauses to keep.

What I want, then, is a principle or a short list of rivals, with each clause tied to the case that forces it and each clause's cost made explicit; what each candidate says about the cases above; what each says about two cases I have not listed — an oracle who tells me, correctly and unchangeably, the last sentence of my life, and a version of the boxes in which I may pay, before the prediction is made, to have myself hypnotized into the sort of person who takes one box; whether the hypnotist case shows that commitment is doing the real work, in which case I would like to know what that leaves of the original verdicts; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which one I ought to surrender, since I would rather lose a verdict than buy a principle at the price of three epicycles. If it turns out that the whole family of cases is rigged against deliberation, say so plainly, and give me the sentence my protagonist can speak aloud that conveys it without sounding like a seminar. Plain prose, no citations, no bibliography — I am not writing a dissertation, I am trying to finish a novel.
