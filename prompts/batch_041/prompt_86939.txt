I am a first-year graduate student sitting in on a seminar on causal inference that is cross-listed with the philosophy of science, and I keep running into a slogan that the instructor treats as self-evident and that I cannot get to hold still: 'no causation without manipulation.' It first appeared when a classmate asked whether we are entitled to say that discrimination causes a racial disparity in some outcome, given that no one can randomize a person's race. The instructor replied that because race is not manipulable, the causal question as posed is either ill-formed or must be recast as a question about what would happen under some hypothetical intervention, and then moved on. It came up again a week later when someone asked whether the observational evidence on smoking and lung cancer establishes that smoking causes cancer: the instructor allowed the causal claim there, on the grounds that smoking is at least the kind of thing one could in principle intervene on, but insisted that the race case was different in kind rather than merely in degree. Our assigned readings have included material on counterfactual theories of causation, on which 'X causes Y' is analyzed in terms of what Y would have been had X not occurred, and material on interventionist theories, on which the causal relation is instead defined by the notion of an intervention; and I genuinely cannot tell whether the slogan is meant as a claim inside that debate, as a rival to both views, or as a piece of practical methodology that is being smuggled in to settle a metaphysical question.

What I would like is not a single correct reading but a menu. Lay out the distinct precise claims 'no causation without manipulation' could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical — for instance, a caution about what randomized experiments license, an operationalist claim about how causal quantities are to be defined or measured, a constraint on what counts as evidence for a causal claim, and a full-blown semantic or metaphysical thesis that the very meaning of a causal statement is exhausted by what would happen under manipulation. For each reading, say whether it is defensible, and say what it would commit one to about cases like the race case and the smoking case. Then tell me which of these readings the instructor has to be relying on for his dismissal of my classmate's question to go through, and whether the same reading is the one that licenses his confidence about smoking; if no single reading does both, say so, and say which of the two verdicts a defender of the slogan would have to give up. I would also like to know, for the strongest version, whether the 'manipulation' has to be something a human agent could actually perform, or whether any hypothetical intervention with a well-defined effect will do, since that seems to be exactly the fork on which the slogan either becomes plausible or collapses into one of the theories we have been reading.
