I've been reading a lot of Lakatos for a piece I'm doing on how we should talk about stalled research programs, and honestly I've swallowed his picture pretty much whole. The way I've got it: you judge a research programme by whether its progressive -- throwing off novel predictions that then get confirmed -- or degenerating, where it just patches itself up after the fact with ad hoc moves that accommodate whatever the data already handed it. There's a hard core you don't touch, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses you're allowed to keep fiddling with, and the rule is you don't ditch a programme just because it hits an anomaly; you ditch it when a rival is doing better and it's stopped predicting anything new. I like this because it lets me write about, say, the amyloid hypothesis or string theory without sounding like a naive Popperian who thinks every failed prediction is a death blow.

But a colleague pushed back over drinks and said id basically signed up for a framework that can't lose. So play it straight with me, no cheerleading: what would actually count against the Lakatosian picture? I don't just mean "somebody prefers Kuhn." I mean which observations about how science really goes, which historical episodes, and which structural problems inside the account -- the murkiness about what makes a fact "novel" (new to the world, or just new to the programme?), the hard core / protective belt line, the way the verdict seems to arrive only with hindsight -- would force me to give the whole thing up or seriously downgrade it. Which of those would a committed Lakatosian themselves concede counts against them, and which would they wave away? And when they wave something away, is that a legitimate move or a tell that the view's been insulated into unfalsifiability? Rank them for me, from the ones that would genuinely sink it to the ones that are merely awkward, and be honest with me if it turns out the whole apparatus is better read as a normative ideal about how science ought to be judged than as a description of what scientists actually do.
