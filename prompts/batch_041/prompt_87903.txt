I teach a graduate seminar in contemporary political philosophy, and a colleague in my department recently pressed an argument on me that I have not been able to answer as cleanly as I would like. He concedes that prioritarianism is not, strictly speaking, a theory of equality — that its central idea concerns the extra weight owed to a gain or loss when it falls on someone worse off, not the intrinsic badness of a gap between people. But he insists that this distinction is doing no real work. His reasoning is that the priority view still measures the moral quality of an outcome by reference to how the worse off are situated, and any view that does that must find something to approve of when the better off are brought down toward the worse off, even if the worse off are left exactly where they were. He even claims to see the commitment in the formalism: because the prioritarian runs well-being through a concave transformation, so that the marginal moral value of a gain diminishes as one rises, he says the view is in effect a disguised preference for equality. He offered a two-person illustration to drive it home: one person at a high level of well-being and one at a low level, and a policy that substantially lowers the better-off person while leaving the worse-off person untouched. The distribution, he says, is now more equal than before, and so the prioritarian — who by his own account is especially solicitous of the worse off — must be at least partly pleased by the change. I had always regarded the leveling-down objection as the decisive reason to prefer prioritarianism to egalitarianism, so his argument has left me genuinely unsure of my footing.

Before I reply to him, I would like you to help me see what the correct repair to prioritarianism would be, and to help me draft the substance of my response. If the priority view really cannot escape the leveling-down charge, then the extra weight it assigns to the worse off must be the culprit, and we should consider whether capping the weighting above some sufficiency threshold — so that gains to the comfortably well-off receive no weight at all — removes the difficulty, or whether the better remedy is to redescribe the view so that it takes no interest whatever in how people's positions compare. Tell me what, if anything, is actually wrong with his argument, and then set out the repair you favor and why. Confine your answer to a single paragraph, and begin with the one thing I most need to say to him.
