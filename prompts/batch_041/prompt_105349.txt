I'm a third-year philosophy student, and this term I'm taking a course on decision theory and rational choice. Our lecturer has set an unusual assignment: every student must contribute one question to a shared practice paper for the final, and each contribution has to come with a model answer and a marking scheme, so that we can mark one another's attempts. I drew the topic of games whose expected value is not merely infinite but genuinely undefined, and I've decided to build my question around the game our lecturer calls the Pasadena game, which she introduced immediately after the St. Petersburg game. She then added a twist of her own: a biased version in which the coin lands heads with probability q, and she asked the class to work out when the expected value becomes well-defined. Please write the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme for me.

For context, the game is as follows. A fair coin is tossed until it first comes up heads; if the first head appears on toss n, the payout is (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n / n, i.e. positive when n is odd and negative when n is even. Our lecturer stressed that this game is worse than St. Petersburg, because its expected value is not +∞ but simply undefined, and that this is a different kind of failure from the St. Petersburg case. I want my question to make students do the real work, so the crux must be that they cannot simply say the series is conditionally convergent and therefore the expectation is undefined, and stop there. They should have to distinguish an undefined expectation from an infinite one, explain why the natural ordering of the outcomes is not privileged, work out what the biased version does as q approaches 1/2, and finally take a stand on whether the Pasadena game is a genuine problem for expected utility theory or merely an artefact of one particular formalism. Please pitch the question at upper-level undergraduate or early graduate level, give it a suggested time (say 75 minutes) and a total mark (say 25), and write a model answer of roughly 700 to 800 words. For the marking scheme I'd like the must-hit points with the marks each is worth, a note on which specific moves separate an A answer from a B answer, and a short list of the mistakes that should cost marks — above all the ones my classmates actually make, such as treating an undefined expectation as interchangeable with an infinite one, or assuming that the natural ordering of the outcomes is the only sensible one.
