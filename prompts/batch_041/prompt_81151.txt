Before any candidate is defended in this seminar, I want the specification on the table. Set out the desiderata that an adequate theory of a person's well-being — of what makes a life go well for the one living it — has to meet: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect, the constraints it cannot violate, and the demands that pull against one another. The theory is wanted for ordinary work — ranking lives, choosing between them, saying what to want for someone — not for metaphysical completeness, and the list should be built to that purpose. Open with one sentence giving the bottom line: the pair of desiderata you think cannot be jointly satisfied, or, if the list is consistent, the single requirement that excludes the most candidates. Then give the list. For each entry, state the consideration or case that motivates it, say whether it is non-negotiable or the preference of a particular camp, and say what manner of theory it rules out.

The list has to be built with one candidate in view, which is the point of the exercise. Take the view — call it tranquilism, and treat the definition given here as authoritative whatever the word may name elsewhere — that the only thing of prudential value is the absence of craving and suffering, and that the presence of pleasure, delight, achievement, and understanding adds nothing at all to the value of a life. Choose your requirements with an eye to whether a view of that shape can meet them; do not relax a requirement to let it through, and do not add one that only a pluralist could satisfy merely because pluralism is comfortable.

I expect the list to say what a theory must do about at least the following:

- the experience machine, and whether a life may count as going well for someone who would refuse to enter it;
- two lives equal in whatever the theory counts as value, one improving and one declining — whether shape is itself a value or only a sequence;
- a being that neither craves nor suffers because it experiences nothing at all: irreversible coma, a rock, a person who never comes to exist. Can a theory that prizes only absences avoid ranking these above the living?
- the difference between a felt calm and the mere absence of disturbance. If the good is a hole in the stream of experience rather than a quality of it, what does that cost the theory?
- the relation between craving and suffering. If craving is a species of suffering, the view collapses into negative hedonism; if it is not, the theory needs a second intrinsic bad and must say why it belongs on the list;
- a life of many small pleasures containing no craving and no suffering, which tranquilism must call no better than a life of blank equanimity;
- the fact that desires and self-reports are shaped by what one has and has never had, so that the absence of craving cannot simply be read off what people say;
- the dimensions along which a single valence can vary — intensity, duration, number of subjects — and whether they remain commensurable when there is only one kind of thing to measure;
- the badness of death: whether a life can be worse for someone in ways that are never experienced;
- the line between a person's well-being and the meaning or worth of their life, which a theory of well-being is not entitled to blur.

Close with a short paragraph on whether tranquilism survives the list, and if it does not, which single requirement does it in.

No more than 800 words, with each entry kept to a sentence or two of motivation; I want the criteria, not an essay on any one of them.
