I'm a PhD student in the philosophy department and I've somehow ended up doing the marking for the second-year metaphysics module while the convenor is away at a conference, and I've hit a problem-sheet question on dispositions that I keep going back and forth on. The question I have to mark is: "A glass is brittle. In Case A the glass sits inside a tight shock-absorbing sleeve, so that whenever it is struck the sleeve absorbs the force and the glass does not break. In Case B the glass is made of toughened stuff that is not brittle at all, but a small charge embedded in it shatters it the instant it is struck. In Case C the glass is brittle, but a sensor detects an incoming impact and anneals it a fraction of a second before the blow lands, so that at the moment of impact it is not brittle any more. For each case, say whether the glass is fragile, and what the case shows about the simple conditional analysis of dispositions (i.e. the idea that something is disposed to break when struck just in case it would break if struck). Say also what the notions of finkishness, masking and mimicking each add to the discussion, and whether any of them cause trouble for the claim that fragility is a genuine property of the glass." One of the better students in the group wrote:

"The simple conditional analysis says that for something to be fragile is just for it to be true that it would break if it were struck. Case A is a classic finkish case: the sleeve makes it so that the glass doesn't break when struck, so the conditional 'it would break if struck' is false, and by the analysis the glass wouldn't be fragile. But intuitively it is fragile, because the sleeve is interfering from the outside, so the case is a counterexample to the analysis. Case B is a case of masking: the glass isn't really fragile, but the charge makes it look as though it is, since it breaks when struck. Case C is also a masking case: the annealing means the disposition isn't there at the moment of impact, so again the conditional comes out false even though the glass is fragile. So what the three cases together show is that the simple conditional analysis is too strong, and that we need to add a clause saying the disposition has to still be there when the stimulus arrives, which is basically the fix that Lewis gives. Notice, though, that masking and mimicking are fine for the conditional analysis as it stands; it's only the finkish cases that really threaten it. Finally, since the analysis fails, fragility can't be a genuine property of the glass in the way its mass is; it's more like a fact about how we talk about glasses, a shorthand for a set of conditionals that we happen to find useful. So the glass in Case A is fragile, the glass in Case B isn't, and the glass in Case C is fragile only in a loose sense."
