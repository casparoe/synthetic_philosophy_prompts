I'm TAing an upper-level philosophy of mind course and I'm working through a stack of finals. The essay question was:

"Chalmers argues that zombies are conceivable, and that this gives us reason to reject physicalism. A physicalist might reply by invoking the phenomenal concept strategy. (a) Reconstruct the conceivability argument. (b) Explain what the phenomenal concept strategy is and how it's supposed to block the argument. (c) Does the strategy succeed? Defend your answer."

I need to be able to defend the mark if they come to office hours about it. Student's answer:

"Chalmers's conceivability argument starts from the idea that we can imagine a zombie — a creature physically and functionally just like you or me, but with no inner experience, nothing it is like to be it. He claims that if zombies are conceivable then they're possible, and if they're possible then physicalism is false, since physicalism says the physical facts fix all the facts, so any physical duplicate of me has to be a phenomenal duplicate too.

The move from conceivability to possibility is doing all the work here, and that's exactly where the physicalist should push back. As Kripke showed with water and H2O, or Hesperus and Phosphorus, there are necessary truths we couldn't know a priori — we can conceive of water not being H2O, but that doesn't make it possible. So conceivability is not a reliable guide to possibility, full stop, and Chalmers's premise is just too strong.

A physicalist who wants to keep the conceivability of zombies without giving up physicalism can invoke the phenomenal concept strategy. This is associated with people like Loar and Papineau. The thought is that phenomenal concepts — the ones we deploy when we introspect a pain or a shade of red — are a special kind of concept, recognitional and partly constituted by the experience itself. Because of how these concepts work, we can't see a priori how a physical state could be a phenomenal state, and we can imagine zombies without contradiction. But that's a fact about our concepts, not about the world. The psychophysical identity is a necessary a posteriori truth, just like water = H2O. Once we see this, the zombie scenario is exposed as a trick of our conceptual apparatus, and the strategy shows zombies aren't possible after all.

So the phenomenal concept strategy gives the physicalist everything they need. It explains the explanatory gap — why there seems to be a gap between the physical and the phenomenal — while keeping the physicalist's metaphysics intact. And since it closes the gap, it also shuts Chalmers's argument down at the first step, because the conceivability of zombies is really just an artifact of our concepts and tells us nothing about what's metaphysically possible. I think this is the right response, and Chalmers's argument fails."
