Six months ago my father died of a progressive neurological illness, and three weeks later I was told I carry the same genetic variant. I have spent the intervening months reading about whether death is bad for the person who dies — Epicurus and his critics, the deprivation account, the symmetry argument — and writing down my own verdicts as they come, case by case, without much theory to hold them together. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here is what I think.

1. My father died at eighty-four, after four years in which he no longer knew my name or his own. I do not think his death was bad for him. Whatever was bad happened earlier, when the person I am mourning stopped being present in the body.

2. A colleague's son died at nineteen, in his sleep, when the car he was riding in left the road. He was happy, in love, three weeks from finishing a degree he cared about. I think this was the worst thing that could have happened to him — bad for him, not merely for us. He never knew it happened and never suffered a moment of it.

3. My great-aunt died at ninety-two, lucid, having settled everything she wanted settled and said everything she wanted to say; she told my mother she was ready. I do not think her death was bad for her at all.

4. A man in intractable pain who has wanted to die for a year and finally dies in his sleep: good for him. A man in perfect health with everything ahead of him who dies in exactly the same way, the same night: bad for him. Same event, same complete absence of experience.

5. Suppose I get a year, use it well, and go quietly at the end with nothing left undone. I still think that is bad for me — bad because of the years I do not get, not because of anything I undergo.

6. My uncle is contesting my father's will and telling people my father was a fraud. I believe this harms my father — not merely that it upsets me on his behalf. Something is bad for him now, when he does not exist, and it is bad for him because of an interest he had while he existed and can no longer hold.

7. The years before my father was born were not bad for him, and I do not think it is bad for him to miss the next century. But I dread the eternity after my own death and not the eternity before my birth, and I cannot justify the difference.

8. A woman who has finished everything she wanted and now wants nothing further — if she died tomorrow I would find that less bad for her than the death of someone with a project still half-built, even where both deaths are painless and unaware.

9. I keep going back and forth on the man who has been unconscious and unresponsive for a decade. I think he is dead in every sense that matters and that letting him go harms no one; but I cannot shake the thought that he is still there, and that if he is, his death would be terrible for him.

Where I feel the pull of a principle: something is bad for you when it deprives you of goods you would otherwise have had, measured from the standpoint of the life you were living, and the deprivation can be real even when there is no one left to experience it. But I can see that this lets in more than I want. If my father's posthumous reputation can be harmed by an interest he no longer holds, why is his death after the dementia not bad for him too — the loss of the years he would otherwise have had, and the thwarting of his wish to stay himself? And if the nineteen-year-old can be harmed without ever knowing it, what exactly is being harmed?

Please tell me what principle, or small set of competing principles, best captures the list; state each one precisely, say which of my verdicts it has to overturn, and tell me what it predicts about a case I have not listed — say, a person who never existed at all, or a person kept alive against a settled wish to die. I am not asking for a reading list. I am asking you to be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and not to present a contested view as the consensus.
