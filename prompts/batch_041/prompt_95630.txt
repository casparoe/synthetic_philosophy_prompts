For most of my working life I was a field geologist, and my research consisted almost entirely of reconstructing singular past events — the filling and desiccation of an inland basin, the retreat of a coastline, the timing of a glacial advance — from the traces those events left behind. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy in a fairly systematic way, and I have recently worked through Popper's writings on evolution along with a good deal of the commentary on them. Like most readers I absorbed the one-line summary years ago: that Popper judged Darwinism to be a metaphysical research programme rather than a testable scientific theory, and that he afterwards qualified or withdrew the judgement. What the summaries do not give me, and what I would like you to supply, is a clear and ranked account of the most important implications of that episode, with the reasoning made explicit rather than merely asserted, and going well beyond the observation that he eventually softened his position.

I ask in part because the charge of unfalsifiability seems to me to fall on my own discipline at least as heavily as on Darwin's. I never predicted; I retrodicted, and when a reconstruction was contested I defended it by appeal to consilience, to the coherence of the framework, and to the comparative success of the whole enterprise rather than by any decisive test. So I would like to know what Popper's treatment of Darwinism implies for (i) the epistemic standing of historical and reconstructive sciences generally, (ii) the demarcation problem and the wisdom of using falsifiability as the measure of a discipline's scientific credentials, (iii) the distinction between the claim of common descent and the mechanism of natural selection, which I gather he treated very differently, and (iv) the way his phrase has since been pressed into service by critics of evolutionary theory in public controversy and in courtrooms. I would also like to know what, on his own account of research programmes, survives of the complaint that adaptive explanations of the just-so variety are untestable — whether his framework licenses that complaint or dissolves it. A friend of mine, a retired evolutionary biologist, insists the whole affair is a storm in a teacup, that falsifiability was never intended to be applied to individual hypotheses, and that the episode tells us nothing about method; if he is right I should like to see the argument spelled out, because I am not persuaded. I am after the implications themselves, not a reading list.
