ok so I chair the risk committee at a small fund — about 30 people, we run roughly 400m, and we take up maybe three or four new position proposals a week. Since last spring one of my members, who used to be an academic, has been raising the same thing at every single meeting. She calls it the no-trade theorem. Her version of it: if prices are already efficient, if there are no noise traders or other non-rational people mucking about in the market, and if the whole setup — who collects what information and how — is common knowledge to everyone, then even if some trader does have private information, he can't profit from it. Because the moment he makes an offer, the offer itself reveals what he knows and gets priced in, so nobody accepts. And she says the same logic kills anything we might think we're doing: a rational counterparty would only sell us a thing if he knew something we don't. So either we're trading on inside information, which we don't do, or we're the sucker. She brings this up at every meeting. Half the commitee now thinks she's just being academic and tunes her out; the other half have started voting no on everything. We've gone from bad decisions to no decisions and honestly the paralysis is worse.

I need a decision procedure my committee can actually run on a proposal, in the room, in about twenty minutes, without me having to go get an economics degree. Questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure just stops and says no, and where it hands the decision back to us because the question is a judgment call or a value call that the procedure can't make. One thing I specifically want it to do: force us to treat the current price as a claim that somebody is making, rather than as a number that we happen to think is wrong. I also want it to be honest that nobody in that room shares a prior with anybody else, and that we can't actually verify what's common knowledge, so the theorem's assumptions are never really met — and to say what follows from that. If some step is really just a judgment call dressed up as a procedure, flag it as such.

Then run it on three live proposals:
- one where our analyst read the same public filing as everybody else and concluded the market has misread it;
- one where our edge is that we see satelite imagery of a supplier's parking lot filling up a few weeks before the numbers come out;
- one where the seller is a fund that's being wound up after redemptions and has to be out by Friday whatever the price is.

At least one of those the procedure should come out against, and it should be an answer I hate. Also tell me plainly what the procedure can't do and when I should overrule it. Don't give me a reading list or cite papers, just the procedure and the three worked examples.
