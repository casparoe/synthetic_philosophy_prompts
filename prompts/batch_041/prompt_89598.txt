I'm deputy chief of staff for the mayor of a mid-sized Rust Belt city -- first term, fourteen months in, and I keep getting handed decisions that I'm pretty sure are what people mean by "dirty hands," except nobody in the building has read any political ethics and so we re-litigate the same fight from scratch every time. Last month it was whether to have our economic development guy quietly signal to a developer which council members could be leaned on; the month before it was whether to sit on a consultant's report about our water system until after the bond vote; yesterday the mayor asked me, half joking, whether it would really be so bad if a certain personnel file got lost. The stakes are real -- people's housing, people's water -- and so is the fact that whatever we decide becomes public record sooner or later, and I've got maybe twenty minutes per decision and no lawyer in the room most of the time (and when there is one, he says everything's fine if its legal, which I'm fairly sure is a different question than the one I'm asking). So what I want is a decision procedure my office could actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it has to go up to the mayor because what's left is a values call and not a fact call.

The thing I want it built on -- because this is the part everyone around here gets wrong -- is that dirty hands isn't the same thing as "politics has costs." From a grad seminar I took years ago I kept two ideas I can't operationalize. First the puzzle: if the act is genuinely wrong, no amount of good consequences makes it not-wrong, and if it isn't wrong, then there's nothing dirty about it -- so a procedure that's just cost-benefit analysis has already lost the thing we're trying to capture. Second, the idea of a remainder or residue: even when someone is right to do the thing, they're still the author of it, and something is owed afterward -- an acknowledgement, restitution, a cost to themselves, some mark that this wasn't free. Make those usable rather than decorative. Concretely, for each step say whether it's a factual question, a predictive one, or a straight value judgment; say which of the distinctions I just gestured at actually survives compression into a checklist and which has to be dropped, and what we lose when it is; and be honest about the failure modes, including the cases where the procedure will give a confident answer that's wrong.

Then run it on three: (1) our development guy leaning on council members to get an affordable housing deal through, (2) leaking a confidential report to kill a contract we think is corrupt, and (3) the mayor asking me to lose the personnel file. If (3) is a case where the procedure says no and the mayor overrules it anyway, tell me what the procedure should say about what happens next -- including whether I resign, and how the answer changes if I don't.
