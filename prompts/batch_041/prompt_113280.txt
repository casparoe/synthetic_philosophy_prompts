I have been reading the effective-altruism and rationalist literature for years, and I have come to think that the standard worry about consequentialist cluelessness lumps together two phenomena that ought to be kept apart. Call an intervention diffuse when its long-run effects consist of an enormous number of individually trivial perturbations whose signs are plausibly symmetric across the ways the world might have gone, so that the expected value of the intervention exists but is likely to be small and cannot be estimated in practice. Call an intervention chaotic when its causal chain is sensitive to initial conditions in such a way that no feasible improvement in our information about those conditions makes the expected value of the outcome distribution converge.

Please describe the most important implications of this distinction for someone with a finite budget of money and time who wants to do the most good. I am especially interested in whether the two phenomena call for different practical responses — whether one permits robustness heuristics that the other undercuts — and in what the distinction implies for the ordinary practice of ranking charities by cost-effectiveness estimates quoted to one or two significant figures. Give me a committed answer: take a definite position and defend it, rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.
