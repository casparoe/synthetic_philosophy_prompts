The room: three economic historians from the California-school/revisionist camp, one hard cliometrician who thinks the whole Great Divergence debate is underpowerd and mostly hand-waving, a Qing-dynasty specialist, and two philosophers of science -- one who works on causation, one who's a pragmatist about explanation and thinks contrast classes are fixed by context and interests rather than by the facts.

The thesis I'm defending: the "Needham question" (why did sustained modern growth take off in Europe and not China?) is a contrastive why-question, and it's malformed in a particular way. The "not China" clause isn't doing the work of a real contrast class -- it's a foil -- and the asymmetry of the question (China as the deficit to be explained, Europe as the unmarked default) is smuggling in the explanation rather than any causal fact. Reframe it symmetrically (why Europe's windfall rather than its absence) and it either collapses into regional questions -- Yangzi delta vs. Lancashire, not nation vs. nation -- or turns into a "how possibly" question about Europe that has nothing to do with China.
