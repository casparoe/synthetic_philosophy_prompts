I've been trying to settle an argument I keep having with a friend, and I'd like a straight answer with the reasoning laid out, not a tour of what the various schools are supposed to have said.

Here is the case that started it. Imagine a country in which the office of the clerk who issues building permits pays a nominal salary, and everyone — the clerks, the applicants, the ministry, the newspapers — understands that the real compensation is a customary fee collected with each permit, and that the fee is precisely why the office is sought after. Nothing is hidden, no rule is broken on the books, and nobody is deceived; the arrangement is public and has stood for generations. My friend says this is obviously corruption, just corruption that everyone has agreed to call something else. I say that if no rule is violated, no trust betrayed, and no one misled, then "corruption" here is doing the work of disapproval rather than description, and what we are really arguing about is whether we like the arrangement, not what it is.

The wrinkle is that the law in some places draws a line in roughly the same territory: a small payment to a low-level official to hurry routine paperwork is treated differently from a payment made to win a contract, even though in both cases money passes from a private party to a public one in connection with an official act. So the line cannot simply be "any private payment to an official."

The claim I want a number on is this: there is a fact of the matter about whether an arrangement of the kind described above is corrupt, and that fact does not depend on the norms of the local community — so a community that regards such an arrangement as perfectly proper can still be mistaken about it.

Give me your credence in that claim, and decompose it. I want a rough probability and a line of justification for each of the sub-questions you think the number rests on — for instance: whether "corruption" names a functional kind, the private capture of entrusted authority, that can be read off the structure of a role rather than off the attitudes of a community; whether, even granting such a kind, the point of an office can be fixed without appeal to the participants' own understanding of it; whether what is happening when a community calls a self-dealing arrangement proper is a moral error or merely a different use of a word; whether the arrangements we bundle under one heading — a gift to a clerk, a party machine paying its workers with public jobs, a minister steering a contract to a relative, a payment to speed routine paperwork, ordinary lobbying — are one kind or several, in which case the question may have no single answer; and whether the cross-cultural disagreement is mostly empirical, about whether such arrangements actually deliver public goods, rather than normative. Then combine them into a single number, tell me which term that number is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move it most.

For what it's worth, I am around 0.25 on the claim and my friend is around 0.85. If you can, tell us which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives in.
