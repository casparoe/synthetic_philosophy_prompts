The question was:

"(1) Set out Nozick's truth-tracking conditions on knowledge, and explain why his account gives up the principle that knowledge is closed under known entailment — the principle that if S knows p, and S knows that p entails q, then S knows q. (2) Apply the account to the case below: does the subject know, and why?

Case. Halim has ridden the same commuter line for eleven years and believes that the 7:42 stops at his station. It does. He has never once checked a timetable; he simply believes it, and nothing has ever given him reason to doubt it. For the purposes of this question take both of the following as given: (a) if the 7:42 had not stopped at his station, Halim would still have believed that it does; (b) in every nearby situation in which he arrives at this belief in the way he actually does, the 7:42 does stop at his station."

And here's what the student handed in:

"Nozick thinks knowledge isn't a matter of justification at all, it's a matter of your belief tracking the truth. His account has four conditions: p is true; S believes p; if p weren't true, S wouldn't believe it; and if p were true, S would believe it. The third is the important one and it's what makes the view externalist, since you don't have to be able to tell that you satisfy it — the counterfactual just has to hold.

Closure fails because the third condition can hold for a belief about the world while failing for something you deduce from it. Take the brain in a vat. If I look at my hands in ordinary light, then if I had no hands I wouldn't believe I did — I'd see the stumps. So I know I have hands. But the belief that I'm not a handless brain in a vat fails the condition, because a vat is wired to make me believe whatever I believe now, so if I were a vat I'd still believe I wasn't one. So I know I have hands, I know that having hands rules out being a handless vat, and I don't know that I'm not a handless vat. That's the abominable conjunction, and Nozick accepts it: he thinks it's the closure principle that has to go, not our ordinary knowledge claims. Really it's a contextualist solution — the skeptic's standards are in play in the seminar room and ours are in play in the kitchen, and Nozick's point is that we get to keep the kitchen.

Halim's case turns on the sensitivity condition, which is really a condition about luck: if p weren't true you wouldn't believe it, so you're not just guessing. The question tells us Halim isn't guessing — in every nearby situation where he ends up with this belief, the train does stop at his station, so his confidence isn't an accident. So the condition holds, his belief tracks the fact, and he knows that the 7:42 stops at his station. That he never checked a timetable doesn't matter for Nozick, because checking would only give him a justification, and on this account justification isn't what knowledge is."
