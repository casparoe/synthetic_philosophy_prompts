I'm an applied economist. My day job is single-treated-unit causal inference: I estimate the effects of state-level policy changes with synthetic control and related potential-outcomes methods, and I'm fluent in that vocabulary — units, treated unit and donor pool, treatment path, potential outcomes, pre-treatment fit, SUTVA, overlap and positivity, in-space and in-time placebo tests, and the fact that with one treated unit my inference is a permutation argument rather than a sampling argument, so I lean on the placebo distribution instead of a standard error. What I know about economic history is close to nothing.

I want you to take the debate over the Great Divergence — and the older question usually filed under Needham's name about why the breakthrough happened in Europe and not in China — and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in my vocabulary. Note that the question is posed contrastively: not "why did sustained industrial growth happen?" but "why Britain, and not China?" Treat the main families of answers as competing hypotheses about a single estimand. Roughly: one family holds that something distinctive about European institutions, science, or culture made northwestern Europe the natural site of industrialization; a revisionist family holds that the leading regions of China and Europe were broadly comparable on the standard measures of market development, commercialization, and living standards well into the eighteenth century, so that the divergence was late, contingent, and small until suddenly it wasn't; and a third family emphasizes particular endowments — coal, and access to the resources of the Americas — as the thing that broke a tie rather than a standing European advantage. Don't worry about who said what; I don't want names, dates, or a reading list, and I'd rather you not guess at attributions. I want the shape of each position.

For each family of answers, state the estimand it is implicitly proposing, in my terms: what are the units, what is the treatment, what is the donor pool, what is the outcome, what is the pre-period, and what identifying assumption would have to hold for me to estimate anything. Then say what survives the translation cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression in my vocabulary at all.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Sort the leftovers into three buckets: (a) claims that are ordinary estimation problems and only need me to be told what to control for; (b) claims that the framework can carry, but only after I stipulate a definition of the units or the treatment, so that the substance lives in an arbitrary choice I would be making rather than in the data; and (c) claims that cannot be made in my vocabulary at all — with your view on whether that residue is a defect of my framework (something about constitution, about units being defined by the comparison, or about causes that admit no intervention) or evidence that the historians' question is less well formed than it sounds.

Concretely, sketch the synthetic-control study of the Great Divergence that I would run if I were foolish enough to run it: the treated unit, the donor pool, the outcome series, the pre-period, the covariates, the fit, and the placebo distribution. Then show me the exact step at which it stops being a study and starts being a story, and say whether that step is a data limitation or a conceptual one.

Finally, answer the well-posedness question in my terms rather than the historians': under what conditions on the estimand, the units, and the treatment would "why Britain and not China?" be a well-posed causal question? Which of those conditions are satisfiable in principle, and which only in practice? I already know the counterfactual is unknowable, so don't stop there — I want to see whether the question is ill posed or merely hard.
