Here's a case I keep chewing on and I want you to take it apart properly: the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act, the one that landed in 2022. Recap of the facts so you dont have to guess at them: Defra commissioned a review from LSE in 2020, the review of the evidence for sentience in decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs came out in November 2021 and concluded there was strong evidence for the crabs, lobsters and octopuses; the bill was then amended in the Lords to bring those invertebrates into scope. So the Act's definition of "animal" ends up as any vertebrate other than humans, any cephalopod mollusc, any decapod crustacean, plus a clause letting the Secretary of State add further invertebrates by regulation later. Insects are out. Nobody reviewed them, nobody put them in. And meanwhile the numbers are absurd: farmed insects are already somewhere up in the trillions per year as far as I know, and wild insects are many orders of magnitude past that.

Now run that episode through the scale argument for insect sentience -- the one that says a very low credence that insects are sentient, multiplied by astronomical numbers, swamps the ordinary evidence thresholds we use for things like whether a crab feels pain. Map it: who is the player, what were the actual options on the table, and what is the counterfactual difference the Act makes for a lobster or a fly? I suspect the answer is embarrassingly small -- a committee, a report, a ministerial reply -- and if recognition changes almost nothing causally, does the expected-value machinery even have anything to bite on, or is it pricing a choice whose practical stakes are near zero while the numbers are near infinite? Then the other direction. What does the episode do to the scale argument: confirmation, anomaly, or a case the argument never anticipated? Note that the review's remit was the real decision point -- the choice to scope the review to decapods and cephalopods is where the scale argument should have been aimed, not at the crab verdict.

I also want the fanaticism problem taken seriously. If a nonzero probability plus a big enough N licenses the conclusion, what stops it licensing everything? What is the non-fanatical version of the argument that survives -- bounding the credence, refusing to aggregate linearly over individuals whose moral status is itself uncertain, or something else? And notice the twist: if insects are sentient and most of them die badly in the wild, insect welfare and insect conservation may pull in opposite directions, so tell me which way that cuts for the argument.

Dont hand me a literature survey. I want the mapping first, then an honest account of where the theory goes quiet.
