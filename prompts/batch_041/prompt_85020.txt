A neurology service that assesses patients with disorders of consciousness runs on a composite practice, and I want to know what view of consciousness it presupposes. The practice has three parts.

The first is the bedside examination. A clinician repeatedly presents commands, watches for visual pursuit of a mirror or a face, tests whether the patient localizes a noxious stimulus, looks for any intelligible vocalization, and sorts the patient into one of the standard categories — coma, the vegetative or unresponsive wakefulness state, the minimally conscious state, locked-in syndrome. Responsiveness is what draws the line between the patient who is treated as having experience and the patient who is not, and the line has consequences: it shapes how much analgesia is given, whether life-sustaining treatment may be withdrawn, and how much moral weight the patient's apparent distress is allowed to carry.

The second is the research literature attached to the same practice, which treats the detection of covert consciousness — a patient who meets no behavioral criterion, but whose brain activity when instructed to imagine a task resembles that of a healthy volunteer doing the same thing — as the discovery of a consciousness the bedside examination had declared absent.

The third is an instrument now used in some clinics that bypasses behavior entirely: it measures how complexly and irreducibly the cortex answers a magnetic pulse, is calibrated so that awake subjects and sleeping or anaesthetized ones fall on opposite sides of a threshold, and is motivated by its developers with a theory I know only secondhand, on which consciousness is supposed to be identical with a maximally irreducible cause–effect structure.

What puzzles me is this. The first part of the practice seems to presuppose that a subject is conscious only if the relevant content is available to the systems that control report and action — that experience is constitutively something that can be used, broadcast, and spoken about. The theory behind the third part denies exactly that, locating consciousness in a structure with no essential tie to report, and implying both that a patient might be conscious with nothing to report and that report-like capacities might exist without much of a subject behind them. So the same clinic seems to be running two incompatible implicit theories at once.

Reconstruct the view of consciousness the practice presupposes. Say which rival views it genuinely rules out and which it merely happens to fit, so that several theories would license the same behavior. Say whether the bedside examination presupposes that responsiveness is a necessary condition of consciousness or only a defeasible indicator of it — and why the practice seems to treat the inference as defeasible in one direction (silence does not prove absence) but not the other (a response proves presence). Say whether the graded clinical categories presuppose that consciousness comes in degrees, and whether the theory behind the instrument can allow that given its insistence on a single maximal structure. Then tell me what would actually have to change — in diagnosis, in the ethics of withdrawal and analgesia, in what a family is told — if the implicit view were replaced by the one the third part assumes, and where the practice is underdetermined enough that nothing would have to change at all.
