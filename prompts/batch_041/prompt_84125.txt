I am in the upper sixth and have been asked by my school's philosophy society to open a session by setting out, in as few claims as I can manage, what I think about the limits of what a finite thinker can be required to know, and then to defend the set against everyone else. Having written the claims down in one place, I am no longer confident that they can all be true at once, and I would far rather be told that now, quietly, than discover it in front of an audience. I should say plainly that I am not after reading suggestions, or a survey of who has said what, or anything I would have to look up: I want the audit performed on the claims themselves, and I would be grateful if you would write your answer in British English.

Here is the set as it stands. (i) Knowledge is closed under entailment, and not only for thinkers who have noticed the entailment: if someone knows that p, and p entails q, then she knows that q. (ii) Believing something is not a matter of having a sentence stored inside one; it is a standing ability to answer, and to act, as though it were true. (iii) A perfectly rational agent believes every logical consequence of what she believes, and therefore believes every logical truth, since any proposition whatsoever entails each logical truth. (iv) Rationality is a standard I am genuinely answerable to, rather than a flattering description of an ideal calculator, and when I fall short of it I am at fault. (v) Nothing can be required of an agent that she is incapable of doing; no finite thinker can entertain infinitely many propositions; and someone who has all the premises of a short proof in front of her but has not yet put them together is at fault for nothing. (vi) There is a fact of the matter about what a person knows, settled by the world and her evidence, and not by what she would say if asked or by how long she would take to say it. (vii) Our computing club has a proof assistant with a set of axioms and rules built into it which has checked a long theorem; I want to say that it does not know the short corollary until somebody asks it, even though it would produce that corollary at once if asked.

Please tell me which of these are genuinely in tension, which only appear to be, and which are simply independent of one another, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. For each one, say what would have to give, what that costs, and whether some assumption I have not written down is doing the work — I suspect I am smuggling something in without noticing, and I would like to be told which of my claims is really resting on it. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what I would be left unable to say afterwards. If the set is straightforwardly inconsistent, say so without softening it; I would much rather have the diagnosis than a reassurance.
