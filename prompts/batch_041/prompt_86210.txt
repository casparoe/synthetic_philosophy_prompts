I have spent the past year on a small team building the decision layer for an in-home care robot — it reminds an elderly user to take her medication, decides when to wake a family member, and is expected to refuse requests it judges unsafe — and we have reached the point where we must commit to some underlying account of moral reasoning instead of continuing to patch a rule engine with exceptions. What I want from you is not a survey of candidates but the specification they should be judged against: write out the desiderata that any theory of machine ethics would have to meet to count as adequate for this job, where adequacy is indexed to a machine that has to act, in bounded time, on modest hardware, without omniscience about consequences or about other agents' intentions, and under review by people who will be held accountable for what it does. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and make the cases the ones we will actually face rather than the ones that circulate in textbooks: the robot's two internal models disagree about whether a user with early dementia may be helped to conceal a dosing error from the daughter who is her primary caregiver, and one action still has to come out; a policy that is locally efficient has drifted into concentrating its inconvenient refusals on the users least able to contest them; a user asks for something her own stated values rule out and the manufacturer's terms permit, and the robot has to decide whose morality it is implementing; a low battery forces a choice between spending the last charge on a long-shot attempt to reach a fallen user and using it to summon help. The list should also carry the constraints that come from outside ethics proper — a latency budget, robustness to the fact that the world and the user will drift away from the training distribution, and the ability to give an account of a decision to a review board that does not share the system's formalism — and it should name the distinctions the theory cannot be allowed to collapse, whether that is doing versus allowing, intended versus merely foreseen, blameworthiness versus liability to sanction, or the moral from the merely conventional.

Sort the requirements as you go into those that are genuine and those that are prejudices of one camp dressed up as requirements, tell me which pairs cannot be jointly satisfied and treat that as a result in its own right rather than a gap in the list, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. If it costs you little, close with one or two lines each on how rule-based deontic systems, expected-utility optimizers, preference-learning models, and case-based reasoners fare against the list — but the list is what I am after, so keep the scoring subordinate to it. A numbered list with a short paragraph under each item is fine; I will be pasting it into a design document, so it should read as a specification rather than an essay.
