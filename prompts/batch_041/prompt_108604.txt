I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in reproductive ethics — eighteen students, mixed philosophy and public-health backgrounds, one hundred minutes, one session — and I want the session on procreative beneficence to open with the students already having committed to it, rather than with me presenting the principle and watching them say how obviously sinister it is. What I have in mind is a three-round selection exercise, and I would like you to design it properly: rules, materials, a scoring scheme that needs no money and no arithmetic beyond what can be done in the head in a minute, a timeline that fits the hundred minutes, and a debrief. The room divides into six clinics of three, each clinic being a prospective couple plus a counsellor, and each receives a packet of four embryo profiles per round; every profile lists a handful of traits with a life-outcome score attached, and the clinic's standing instruction is simply to choose the profile with the highest expected total. The rule is deliberately thin — nobody is asked to endorse eugenics, only to prefer the better life for the child they will actually have. I want three things built into the mechanics. First, the scores are environment-relative: one trait on the cards carries a score that depends on the society described in that round's briefing, and in round two I flip the society, so that a trait that was mildly positive becomes mildly negative because the environment is hostile to the people who have it. Each clinic must then decide whether to keep selecting for it, and must notice that the rule it accepted in round one now instructs it to fit the child to the injustice rather than to the world it would have preferred. Second, one trait is positional: its score depends on how many other clinics in the room selected it, announced only after all six have submitted, so the clinics are in an arms race with one another and each clinic's best choice leaves every child worse off the more of them choose it. Third, clinics may decline to select, or opt for unscreened natural conception, but only by writing a single sentence on the score sheet explaining why; I want to see whether the room converges on the thought that not selecting the best-scoring embryo is a species of negligence, and whether anyone can say where that thought stops. When two profiles tie exactly, the clinic must break the tie by coin and record whether the coin felt like the right kind of answer.

Please do not name the principle or its critics until the debrief, and keep the briefing sheet free of the vocabulary of eugenics and enhancement: the students should arrive at the implications themselves. What I want them to come away with is not a verdict on the principle but the experience of having reasoned their way to it from premises none of them would reject, and then finding no non-arbitrary place to stop. The debrief is where the conceptual work happens, and I want it mapped back onto particular moments rather than delivered as a lecture: the round-two flip onto the thought that the principle makes the child's good hostage to the social environment; the round-three arms race onto the difference between a rule addressed to one set of parents and a rule addressed to a population, and onto the question whether the principle has anything to say about positional goods at all; the abstention option onto whether the principle merely permits or actually requires bringing the best child into existence; and the coin flips onto the arbitrariness the principle tolerates when two options are equal.

I would also like a short and honest section on what the exercise cannot reproduce, and I expect that to include at least this much: that the scores are stipulated rather than discovered, so the exercise settles by fiat precisely what is in dispute about whether a life with a given condition is worse for the person living it; that the comparison class is rigged, since the real question of who the child would otherwise have been raises the non-identity problem and the exercise never lets that bite; and that a room of outsiders will run the whole thing as a trolley problem about other people's children, which no improvement in the rules will repair. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I assume at least one clinic will refuse to play, that the arms race will produce a round of competitive spite, and that the debrief will yield a chorus of students insisting they would simply have loved the child they got — and how to handle each failure without rescuing the activity by explaining the point too early.
