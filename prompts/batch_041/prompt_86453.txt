For my high school history seminar I am writing a paper on the difference between legitimate historical revision and outright denial, and I have built an argument that I suspect proves too much. I would like you to identify the step that is doing the illegitimate work, explain why that step looked innocent, and tell me what I would have to surrender in order to repair the argument.

I do not doubt that denial of the Holocaust or of the Armenian genocide is false, nor that revising an accepted account of such events can be legitimate. My difficulty is that the argument below makes the distinction between the two rest on nothing more than the fact that one of them happens to be respectable. I have numbered the steps so that you can refer to them.

(1) The evidence for any historical claim is finite, and any finite body of evidence is compatible with more than one account of what happened.
(2) Therefore no historical account is uniquely forced by the evidence.
(3) Whatever is not forced by the evidence must be settled by something other than the evidence, namely background assumptions, prior commitments, or interests.
(4) Deniers adopt their accounts because of prior commitments, and they retain them even when the evidence runs against them.
(5) Revisionists also adopt their accounts partly because of background assumptions, since by (2) no account is forced.
(6) Therefore both are adopted for extra-evidential reasons, and neither is better supported by the evidence than the other.
(7) Therefore the difference between revision and denial is social rather than epistemic: it is a matter of which commitments the profession happens to tolerate.
(8) But the same reasoning would make the difference between an established scientific consensus and a fringe view merely social as well, and that seems to me plainly false, since it is not a sociological accident that the flat-earth view is rejected.
(9) So at least one of (1) through (7) must be false.

Which one should I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I am most attached to (1), because I cannot see how to do history without it, and to (8), because I cannot bring myself to treat the rejection of flat-earth claims as mere fashion. If you think the argument is beyond repair rather than merely misstepping, I would also like to know what a defensible version of the distinction would look like: what it is about denial, as opposed to revision, that makes it epistemically defective rather than merely unpopular.
