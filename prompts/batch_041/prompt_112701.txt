My roommate and I went at this for two hours last night and I still cant tell where it breaks. Hes a philosophy grad student, I'm not, so he kept saying I was missing something obvious. I don't think I am.

His argument:

(1) You may assert something only if you know it.
(2) You know something only if you can rule out every possibility in which it's false.
(3) His example: a lab tech reads an analyzer and says "this sample is contaminated." There are uneliminated possibilities — the machine swapped two tubes, she misread the display, a label peeled off and got stuck back on the wrong vial. She reruns it and gets the same reading, but agreement between checks is just more of the same kind of thing, not a way of ruling out the gap. No amount of rechecking closes every possibility, because every check leans on something she hasn't independently verified.
(4) So she doesn't know the sample is contaminated.
(5) By (1), she may not say it.
(6) He preempts the obvious fix, which is that she says "the analyzer says contaminated" instead. But that's an empirical claim about the world too, so the same reasoning eats it, and then eats the claim about how the display looked, and so on down. What's left at the bottom is only stuff like "it currently seems to me that..." — which tells the listener nothing they couldn't have gotten from her face.
(7) A norm that permits only that can't be the thing that makes assertion worth having in the first place.
(8) So (1) and (2) can't both stand. I want to keep (1); I really do think you shouldn't go around saying things you don't know.

Which one do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? He acts like (2) is just obviously true. But if (2) is what goes, I'm worried I end up "knowing" my lottery ticket lost because I'm 99.9999% sure, and then the whole thing flips into a different mess. Is the problem in (2), or in the move from (3) to (4), or in (3) itself?
