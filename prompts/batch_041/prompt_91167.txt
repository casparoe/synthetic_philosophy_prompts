Two mates of mine got into it at the pub over Bertrand's paradox. A says the whole thing shows the principle of indifference is a sham: you can get 1/3, 1/2, or 1/4 for the same event -- a random chord being longer than the side of the equilateral triangle inscribed in the circle -- just by describing the chord differently, so "indifference" is just a label for whatever measure you fancied in the first place. B says that's unfair. The event isn't well-defined until you say what physical process picks the chord. Pick two independent uniform points on the circumference and you get 1/3; pick a uniform point on a radius and draw the perpendicular chord and you get 1/2; pick a uniform midpoint in the disk and you get 1/4. Each is a different question, so there's no contradiction. A replies that the principle was supposed to tell you what to do when you don't have a process, and if it only works after you've fixed the process, it's not doing any work. B says it does work: it tells you to use the symmetries of the process, and the trouble is that "a random chord" has several symmetries and you have to decide which are relevant. A says that's just the problem: symmetry under rotations doesn't fix a unique measure on chords, and if you try to use the biggest symmetry group you get incoherence. B then says, what about picking a random point in the disk and a random direction through it -- that's a perfectly natural physical procedure, so surely the principle can rank it? A says it can't, because it's just another parameterisation, and anyway it doesn't give 1/2. B says it doesn't need to give 1/2; it just needs to be a well-defined process. They've asked me to referee and I definately couldn't follow all of it. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? And say what, if anything, both of them are missing about what the principle of indifference can and can't do.
