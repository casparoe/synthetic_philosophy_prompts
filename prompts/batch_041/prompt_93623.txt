I am a high school student preparing a piece for my school's philosophy and technology club, and I have been trying to think through the responsibility gap for autonomous systems without simply repeating the usual trolley-problem examples. The case I have been assigned concerns a fleet of autonomous delivery robots: their navigation policy is trained by a foundation-model vendor, fine-tuned by the delivery company that operates them, and then updated nightly by an automated pipeline that no human reviews in full, so that the weights from the previous day are overwritten and the exact decision path that produced a given action cannot be reconstructed afterward. One night, after a rare sensor-fusion failure that no engineer anticipated, a robot injures a pedestrian. Our club wants to stage a dialogue between two equally competent proponents of opposing views, and I would like you to write it.

The first interlocutor is Dr. Priya Nair, a roboticist and safety engineer who believes the responsibility gap is real: because no individual human satisfies the ordinary conditions for moral responsibility—knowledge, control, and intention—the deployment of such systems creates a genuine deficit that existing law and morality cannot absorb, and she favors new institutions such as a pre-deployment responsibility escrow, in which the firms in the chain must agree in advance on a liability schedule for classes of failure, with the schedule triggered automatically when a failure occurs. The second is Professor Tomas Lind, a philosopher of action who believes the gap is largely an artifact of an individualist picture of responsibility: once we attend to distributed, role-based, and corporate responsibility, there is no unmet demand, and proposals like the escrow are just risk-spreading dressed in moral language. Please have them genuinely engage each other rather than trade monologues: let Dr. Nair press the point that collective responsibility still needs an answer to who is blameworthy and why, and let Professor Lind press the point that faultless liability may compensate victims while leaving responsibility itself untouched. I would like the dialogue to move through at least three exchanges, to end without a forced resolution, and to leave the reader with a clearer sense of what would actually have to be true for the gap to be closed rather than merely managed.
