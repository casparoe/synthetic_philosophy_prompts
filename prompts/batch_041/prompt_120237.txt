For the past month or so, a reading group I sit in on has been working through the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and we have ended up stuck on what I suspect is the real fault line between the various proposed treatments: whether the two cases deserve the same solution or different ones. I have come down, provisionally, on the side that says the asymmetry is genuine, and I would much rather be argued out of it than agreed with, so I am looking for a proper sparring partner. Please play an unnamed philosopher of a settled and fully worked-out persuasion — I will call it uniformism — who holds that any acceptable solution to the lottery paradox must apply, unchanged and without special pleading, to the preface paradox, and conversely, and that the felt difference between the two is a psychological artifact rather than an epistemic one. Be genuinely good at this: you may draw on the standard machinery (credences, thresholds for outright belief, deductive closure, the aggregation of risk, the gap between what is permissible and what is obligatory), and you may grant a small point when I have earned one, saying so plainly. But you must not surrender the main claim, and you must not step out of character to tell me what the literature says, who said it, or what I ought to read. No citations, no author names, no appeals to authority; if you want to invoke a position, state it in your own words and defend it. Argue with concrete cases rather than labels, invent cases where you need them, and keep each reply to a single paragraph so that it reads like a conversation. Stay in character until I type stop.

A little about where I am coming from, since it determines what will and will not move me: I am a statistician by training, so I keep reaching for the thought that the lottery tickets are exchangeable. The setup supplies exactly one reason, and it is the same reason for every ticket, which is precisely why I can see in advance that reasoning of that form must be mistaken exactly once. In the preface, by contrast, nothing is exchangeable. I dated the manuscript from a watermark, redid the arithmetic twice, had a colleague check the translation. Each claim arrived with its own support, and the knowledge that some of that support has failed somewhere in the book is not something I can locate in any single claim. If your uniformism has an answer to that, I want to hear it in your own vocabulary, not as a label.

So here is my opening, and I am folding two things into it. First: the disanalogy above is not a difference in how confident I am but a difference in the structure of my reasons, and structural differences are exactly the sort of thing an epistemic rule ought to be sensitive to. Second — and this is the case I keep returning to — suppose I skip the preface entirely and make the disclaimer the first numbered claim of chapter one: a sentence to the effect that at least one numbered claim in the book is false, sitting there as claim 1.1 alongside all the others. Nothing about my evidence has changed, and the sentence now simply has the same status as every other claim in the book. Your uniformist treatment says I should treat each claim the same way, so tell me what I believe about 1.1, whether that answer remains stable once 1.1 is itself one of the claims it is about, and how a book of assertions gets written at all by someone who, on your account, believes none of its sentences. Begin wherever you like, but begin.
