One of them claimed Occam's razor is basically a taste thing -- a scientist prefers simple theories the way someone prefers plain furniture -- and I wrote the following to prove him wrong, except it came out proving him right. Now I think it proves way too much, but I can't say where.

Background I'm taking for granted, so tell me if the rot is in here instead:

- Fix a universal Turing machine U. The Kolmogorov complexity K_U(x) of a string x is the length of the shortest program that makes U print x and halt.
- Write each hypothesis as a string. Solomonoff-style prior: P(H) = 2^-K_U(H), roughly halving the probability for every extra bit of description, then condition on the data. This is the standard way of making "simpler = more probable" precise, and it comes with convergence results -- the mixture's predictions home in on the truth in the usual senses.
- Invariance: for any two universal machines U and V there's a constant c, depending only on U and V, such that K_U(x) and K_V(x) differ by at most c for every x.

The argument:

(1) A prior is rational only if its verdicts aren't an artifact of an arbitrary choice of description language. Otherwise "simpler" just means "shorter in whatever language I happened to pick", which is no guide to anything.
(2) The Solomonoff prior is defined relative to U. Swap U and you change K, so you change every hypothesis's prior.
(3) Take any two hypotheses H1 and H2. Build a machine with H1 hardwired: one line prints H1. On that machine H1 is very short and H2 costs whatever it costs to encode H2 from scratch. Build the mirror-image machine with H2 hardwired and the ranking flips. So for any pair of hypotheses there's a machine that makes the first one simpler and a machine that makes the second one simpler.
(4) So there's no machine-independent fact about which of two hypotheses is simpler.
(5) By (1), a simplicity prior whose verdicts depend on an arbitrary choice isn't rational.
(6) So the Solomonoff prior isn't rational -- and since it's the canonical formalization of Occam's razor, neither is the razor.
(7) So when a simple theory and a baroque one both fit the data, there's no rational reason to prefer the simple one. It's taste, training, fashion, whatever.

I'd rather not end up at (7), and I'd like to keep the spirit of (1) -- I do think "it's just taste" is too cheap. But I'll give up whatever has to go. Which step is the bad one, and what does giving it up cost me?
