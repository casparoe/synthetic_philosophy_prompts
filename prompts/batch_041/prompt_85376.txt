Two of my friends, one finishing a PhD in ethics and the other in cognitive science, had an argument at my kitchen table that neither has let go of, and they have asked me to referee. I was only a bystander and I don't have a side. The occasion was a mood-tracking app I'd been given, which logs what you do, asks you to rate how much you enjoyed it, and then tells you which activities to do more of. The whole thing assumes that pleasures can be placed on a single scale and added up.

Here is how it went.

(1) The ethicist said the app is incoherent from the ground up, because pleasure is not one thing. The taste of a peach, a hot shower on a cold morning, the satisfaction of finally getting a proof, the sour glow of hearing that a rival was passed over, the relief when a toothache stops — set these side by side, she said, and there is no common element: no shared sensation, no shared feeling-tone, nothing that all of them have and that unpleasant things lack. No common element means no single scale; no single scale means no summing. So any theory that treats pleasure as one quantity must be quietly selecting an arbitrary subset of the things we call pleasant and calling the rest something else.

(2) The cognitive scientist said that proves too much. The metaphysics of pleasure does not constrain arithmetic. We compare heterogeneous things constantly — you can decide you would rather spend the money on a trip than on a sofa without any unit common to trips and sofas. The app does not need a common sensation; it needs a ranking over activities, and it can read that off my choices.

(3) The ethicist said that is a change of subject: then the app is measuring preference, not pleasure, and preference is a different theory of well-being wearing a hedonist's coat. If I would rather finish a difficult book but enjoy the cake more while I am eating it, hedonism says the cake wins; the app, if it is using my preferences, will tell me to read — and then it is not telling me what pleases me, only what I think should please me.

(4) The cognitive scientist said she was assuming that a pleasure has to be a felt quality that arrives and gets registered. The better account is that a pleasure is a state you like, or take pleasure in — an attitude directed at something. Liking a proof and liking a peach are then the same in kind and different only in their objects, and the fact that the sensations have nothing in common is exactly what you would expect and no threat at all.

(5) The ethicist said that leaves the attitude unexplained. Liking with no felt quality is either a disposition to behave, which is behaviorism and false, or else a second feeling laid over the first, which puts him back where he started. She then offered what she called the killer case for both of them: relief. When a bad headache stops, there is no positive feeling anywhere in the experience, only the going-away of the pain — and yet nobody thinks relief fails to count, or counts for little. Worse, the relief is greater the worse the headache was, so its magnitude seems set by the pain rather than by anything positive. So either pleasure is not a feeling at all, which is his move, or pleasure is not what makes a life go well, which is what she had been saying.

(6) The cognitive scientist said relief is liked, and that this is all that is needed; he said she was treating liked as though it had to be a warm glow, when being glad that the pain stopped is a real state of the person, neither a disposition nor a sensation.

I would like an actual verdict rather than a summary of the positions they are each drawing on — I don't care what the labels are, and I don't want a reading list.
