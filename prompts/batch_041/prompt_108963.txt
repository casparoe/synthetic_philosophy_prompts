Adam Smith's account of the impartial spectator is normally summarized as the thesis that we judge our own conduct by imagining how a well-informed and disinterested observer would regard it, and that this imagined observer becomes the internal judge we consult. I want to press on a feature of that account which I think is under-discussed. If the standards of the internal judge are not innate but assembled out of our experience of how actual spectators respond to us -- we learn what pitch of resentment, gratitude, or ambition is warranted by watching the people around us react -- then the internal judge is hostage to the audiences that formed it, and a change of audience is a change of conscience. Consider the phenomenon sometimes called audience capture: a writer, advocate, or researcher who spends years addressing a particular readership gradually comes to hold the views that readership rewards, and experiences the change as the arrival of conviction rather than as drift. Someone has proposed a remedy: periodically write down what you would conclude if no one were reading, and treat that as the verdict of the impartial spectator.

Give me the most important implications of Smith's position here. Specifically: does his account imply that audience capture is a corruption of the internal judge or merely the ordinary operation of sympathy, and what turns on which of the two it is; can the proposed remedy work at all, given that on Smith's account the judge is assembled from the responses of real spectators, so that there is no audience-free standpoint to write from; what do his distinction between the love of praise and the love of praiseworthiness, and the place he gives to self-command, imply about what a workable remedy would have to look like instead; and can a person in the grip of capture be a reliable judge of their own condition, or must the diagnosis come from outside? Where the account is silent or leaves a question open, say so rather than forcing a verdict.
