Two friends of mine, Ana and Ben, spent most of a long drive arguing about whether I could have come from a different sperm and egg. I stayed out of it, and now I'd like you to adjudicate.

Ana: take the actual sperm and egg that produced me. Imagine a counterfactual in which a different sperm fertilizes that same egg. Whoever grows up in that scenario, it isn't me. I am essentially the product of that particular pair, so no, I could not have come from different gametes.

Ben: the sperm that won was one of millions, and which one won came down to timing and chemistry. Had the winner been a fraction of a second slower, a different one would have arrived first and, on your view, a different person would exist in my place. That makes my existence turn on a flagellum twitching; an origin can't be that fragile.

Ana: fragility isn't an objection — plenty of necessary truths rest on trivial contingencies. Given that that sperm won, the person who resulted is me, and no other gamete could have produced me. A table made from this particular hunk of wood could not have been made from a different hunk, even though which wood it is makes no difference to how the table looks.

Ben: two cases break that. First, the zygote I came from could have split into two embryos, both of which would have come from the same sperm and egg. If origin fixed identity, those twins would have to be one person, which is absurd — so origin is not what makes me me. Second, if the one-cell embryo that became me had had a gene edited before it divided, the resulting person would still have been me, merely with a different trait; swapping the sperm is just a larger intervention of the same kind.

Ana: twinning shows that origins aren't sufficient, not that they aren't necessary. And editing a gene alters a property of the individual, whereas a different sperm yields a different individual. If anything the editing case helps me: same origin, different genome, still me.

Ben: necessity without sufficiency is cheap. Countless things were necessary for my existence — my mother surviving childhood, for instance — without being constitutive of my identity, so you owe an account of why origin is special. And in the split-embryo case, had one half been destroyed early, the survivor would plainly have been me even though it is not the whole zygote, which suggests that the "individual" your view leans on was never the zygote.

Please work through the exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating, and noting what each of them is missing. Then say which position survives: in particular, whether origin can be necessary for identity without being sufficient, and what the twinning and gene-editing cases should be taken to show. Keep the modal question — could I have come from different gametes? — separate from the question of what fixes or constitutes an individual's identity.
