In the Meno, Socrates compares true belief to the statues of Daedalus that run away unless they are tied down, and suggests that knowledge is true belief fastened by an account of the reason. Argue for a definite position on a narrower version of the Meno problem: is the superiority of knowledge over true belief state-relative or task-relative? Defend the task-relative answer — knowledge is better not because of any intrinsic property of the knowing state (stability, reliability, aptness, credit) but because knowers can do things with their beliefs that mere true believers cannot: retain them under challenge, transmit them as testimony, integrate them with other commitments, and be held responsible for them. Assume, for the sake of the argument, that truth is the fundamental epistemic good, so that any further condition on knowledge must earn its value either intrinsically or through its contribution to truth. Then say what follows for a true belief that is acted on once and then discarded — never challenged, communicated, or reused: does knowledge still have extra value there, and if so, where does it come from? If not, embrace that conclusion and say why it is less embarrassing than it looks. Commit to a position rather than surveying the options.
