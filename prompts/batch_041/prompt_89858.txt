I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on theory change: 16 students, mixed philosophy and economic history, no economics prerequisites. I want an activity that makes them feel the specific predicament of a theory that misses the deadline it set for itself and is then vindicated over a longer horizon — where being right and being justified come apart, and neither the early verdict nor the late one can be read off the evidence. No lecture and no open debate: the verdicts should fall out of something they do with their hands, and they should be committed to those verdicts before they can see how it ends. Assume no historical or economic knowledge; everything they need must be on cards I hand out. Don't give me a reading list.

Two pieces of material, both of which I'd like the design to use.

The doctrine card, which is the only statement of the theory the participants get:

"In an economy whose output per head is limited by land, a sustained improvement in the productivity of land is converted into more people rather than into better lives. The conversion works through births and deaths, and therefore takes about two generations to complete: within fifty years of a productivity gain, the gain has been absorbed and real wages are no higher than before. Any improvement in living standards that survives longer than two generations must therefore have some cause other than the productivity gain itself."

The two-generation clause is the crux, and I want the design to make it bite: it is what makes the early verdict defensible and the later revision principled.

The data, from a fictional country, to be revealed one interval at a time:

| Interval | Years | Population (m) | Yield per acre (1750 = 100) | Parishes using the new rotation (%) | Real wage (1750 = 100) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1750–1775 | 10.0 | 100 | 0 | 100 |
| 2 | 1775–1800 | 10.9 | 115 | 20 | 105 |
| 3 | 1800–1825 | 12.2 | 130 | 45 | 107 |
| 4 | 1825–1850 | 13.5 | 138 | 62 | 102 |
| 5 | 1850–1875 | 15.0 | 141 | 74 | 94 |
| 6 | 1875–1900 | 16.6 | 145 | 78 | 87 |
| 7 | 1900–1925 | 18.0 | 150 | 80 | 83 |
| 8 | 1925–1950 | 19.2 | 155 | 81 | 81 |

For my eyes only, not the participants': the series was generated with population responding to real wages at a one-interval lag, fixed acreage, and the new rotation as the sole source of productivity change — a one-off level shift that diffuses to roughly four fifths of parishes and then stops. So the doctrine's mechanism is real and the improvement is genuinely absorbed, but absorption takes about a century rather than two generations, and the honest signal that the shock is nearly exhausted is on the board from interval 4.

Two devices I've already settled on and would like the rules built around. First, a sealed falsification contract: before the data begin, each team writes and signs a dated refutation condition for the doctrine, naming a column, a direction, and an interval. Second, a reason log kept on the back of that card: whenever a team revises its condition it records the reason and ticks one box — whether that reason would have been available to it had the data come out the other way. I want that box to be genuinely difficult to fill in honestly, and I want the room to end up disagreeing about how to fill it.

What I need from you: teams and roles, including something for a team that declares the doctrine dead in round two, since I don't want half the room sitting out; a round-by-round structure with a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; the materials; a scoring scheme that requires no money; the debrief, mapping named moments in the activity onto the philosophical issue; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; and a frank statement of which feature of the predicament the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply in words. I'd also like a short closing note for me on the real Malthus case — the 1798 doctrine, the durable rise in living standards it did not anticipate, and the later claim that Malthusian dynamics describe pre-industrial economies rather well — to be delivered without citations.
