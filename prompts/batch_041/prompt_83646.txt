Now consider a sequence O1, ..., O25, where O1 is a small population of very good lives and O25 is a vastly larger population of lives that are barely worth living, and where each outcome in the sequence is better than the one before it by a small margin that, taken on its own, looks uncontroversial (slightly more people, slightly lower average quality, nobody made worse off, and so on). (a) Reconstruct the argument from the adjacent comparisons plus transitivity to the conclusion that O25 is better than O1, and say whether the argument is valid. (b) A classmate responds: 'So betterness is intransitive. But we can keep transitivity for "at least as good as" and let "better than" be the intransitive relation. Since decision theory and the money-pump argument only need "at least as good as" to be transitive, nothing important is lost.' Assess this response. (c) What are the options for someone who wants to resist the conclusion, and what does each one cost?"

And the student's answer: "(a) The argument is valid. From the 24 premises — O2 better than O1, O3 better than O2, ..., O25 better than O24 — plus transitivity of betterness, induction along the chain gives O25 better than O1. No further premise is smuggled in; in particular nothing about total utility is used, only the 24 comparisons. The conclusion is the repugnant conclusion. (b) The classmate's response is the cheapest place to give ground. Betterness being intransitive isn't as crazy as it sounds — we already accept intransitive relations like 'beats' in rock-paper-scissors — and what decision theory really needs is that 'at least as good as' is transitive, since the money-pump argument is driven by the transitivity of 'at least as good as'. So we can hold that 'at least as good as' is transitive and complete while making 'better than' intransitive. What this amounts to is that some of these outcomes are incommensurable — there's no fact of the matter about which is better — which is a familiar idea in the literature on incomparable values, so it isn't ad hoc. The only cost is that 'better than' no longer supports the usual aggregation. (c) The options are to reject transitivity, to reject one of the 24 comparisons, or to accept the conclusion. The second looks weakest: the sequence was built so that no single step is a big deal, so wherever you draw the line it'll be an arbitrary sharp cutoff, which is exactly what Sorites arguments make embarrassing. So I'd reject transitivity — the spectrum argument shows betterness isn't transitive, and we should stop treating it as a strict ordering."
