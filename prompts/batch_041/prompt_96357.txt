I am a novelist preparing an application for a six-month fellowship at a humanities institute, which asks for a short research proposal of about fifteen hundred words. My half-formed idea grows out of the historical novel I am finishing, about a provincial rebellion that was crushed. Reading around the subject, I keep finding that defeat gets narrated in three quite different registers, and I suspect the difference is philosophically load-bearing rather than merely rhetorical. A lost cause may be vindicated (the battle is lost, but the future belongs to us), purified (defeat stripped away the compromises that victory would have imposed), or transfigured (the sacrifice becomes the point, and the original political aim quietly drops out). I want to know whether these are three genuinely distinct positions, or whether the third collapses into a theodicy of defeat — a device that makes any loss meaningful by moving value from the goal to the suffering. Please write the proposal: state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain why the obvious accounts leave it open (the claim that history is written by the victors, the idea that martyrdom purifies a cause, the historian's treatment of myth as simple error); set out what I would actually do across the six months, testing the three registers against cases I know well — the Confederate Lost Cause, Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk — and against the demands of fiction, where a novelist wants the emotional charge of the myth even while doubting its truth; say why this is a conceptual question rather than an archival one; and say honestly what would count as failure. My own suspicion is that the project fails if I can distinguish transfiguration from ordinary propaganda only by whether I happen to approve of the cause, and the proposal should be willing to say so. Keep it to the question, the method, the stakes for someone who writes fiction, and the failure conditions; I will supply the citations myself.
