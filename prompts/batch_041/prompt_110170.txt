I teach a seminar on literary translation and we close the term on the question of form — what happens when the thing you have to preserve isn't the sense but the shape. Hofstadters Le Ton beau de Marot has been our touchstone all term (all those English stabs at one little Renaissance French poem, no two of them agreeing about which sacrafice is the acceptable one), but I don't want you to summarize him or reproduce any of the versions in the book — I want something new, for the last day of class. So: a short story, 1000 to 1800 words, whose spine is a single invented poem. You make the poem up — ten to fourteen lines, a refrain that keeps coming back, and a tight metrical or syllable constraint that English makes almost impossible to honor — and it can appear in French, in deliberately approximate French, or just be described closely enough that we feel the vise it's in. Then put three or four complete English versions of the whole poem inside the story, from different hands or from the same hand across drafts, and make each one defensible and each one fail at something different: one hits the syllable count exactly and goes tone-deaf, one keeps the refrain word-for-word and turns the poem's argument inside out, one is gorgeous and has quietly stopped being a translation of anything. A workshop table is the obvious staging — I keep picturing five people, five versions of the same little poem, and coffee nobody's drinking — but set it wherever the pressure actually lives. Nobody in the story should explain the moral; if a reader closes it thinking that fidelity was never a single quantity, that's the story working.

Since I'll be arguing with my students about this afterward, give me one short paragraph after the story, in your voice rather than the narrator's, about where it cheats: which trade-offs you rigged, which version you'd actually defend, and whether the premise that these are rival translations of one original even holds up — or whether I've handed you a task that's rigged or confused, in which case say so plainly instead of performing the exercise politely.
