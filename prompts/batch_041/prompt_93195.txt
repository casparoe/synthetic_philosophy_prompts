I am a philosophy professor working on a paper in political philosophy about state apologies for historical wrongs, and I have reached an impasse. My considered verdicts on a range of cases no longer obviously cohere, and I want a principle that does better than a list. I am not asking for a literature review or a menu of options; I want a committed answer, with reasons, and an honest accounting of what it costs. Please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. A state whose government ordered a massacre 150 years ago, and whose current government is the legal successor, owes a formal apology to the descendants of the victims. A state that was conquered and replaced by a new regime that explicitly repudiates the old one, and that has since built a different political identity, does not owe an apology for the old regime's crimes. A state that did not commit a wrong but knowingly inherited and continues to benefit from stolen land owes reparations to the dispossessed, but does not owe an apology, because an apology is for one's own wrong. A state whose government committed a wrong that was legal under its own law at the time still owes an apology. A state that apologizes for a historical wrong while maintaining institutions that continue the same injustice has not given a genuine apology; the apology is owed but not delivered. A state that apologizes without naming the wrong or identifying the victims has not given a genuine apology. A state that apologizes for a wrong committed by a private company operating within its borders, where the state itself was not complicit, owes no apology. A state whose citizens, acting as a mob, committed a wrong against a minority, and whose government failed to protect the victims, owes an apology. A state that apologizes for a wrong that is still ongoing, such as an occupation, owes an apology, but the apology is incomplete unless it is accompanied by ending the wrong. A state that apologizes for a wrong committed by a previous government that was not democratically elected still owes an apology. A state that apologizes for a wrong committed by a colonial administration that was never part of its own political system, and whose territory was not the metropole, owes no apology. A state that apologizes for a wrong committed by a group that is now part of the state but was then a separate polity owes no apology. A state that apologizes for a wrong committed by its ancestors, where the current population includes both descendants of victims and descendants of perpetrators, still owes an apology. A state that apologizes for a wrong by issuing a formal statement in a language the victims do not speak, with no translation or other effort at communication, has not given a genuine apology. A state that claims continuity with a predecessor state for purposes of treaties and debts cannot disclaim continuity in order to avoid apologizing for the predecessor's wrongs.

My tentative principle is this: a state owes an apology if and only if it is the same agent as the wrongdoer, or it has voluntarily assumed the identity of the wrongdoer. That principle seems to fit some of the verdicts but to conflict with others, especially the ones about legality at the time, mob wrongs, non-democratic predecessors, divided descendant populations, and selective continuity. I would like you to tell me whether the principle can be repaired, or whether a different principle does better. State the principle precisely, show which case motivates each clause, say what it implies for a case I have not listed -- a state that apologizes for a wrong committed by a predecessor state that it has always denied was legitimate, but from which it nevertheless inherited a seat in an international body -- and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, I must give up. I am asking for a definite position, not a survey of the options.
