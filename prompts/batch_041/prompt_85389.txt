I have a set of gut verdicts about comparisons that flip when the data are split, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. In each case I give the numbers and what I think.

1. A trial of drug D against placebo, 100 patients per arm. D happened to be given mostly to the sicker patients: among mild cases D saved 9 of 10 and placebo 72 of 90; among severe cases D saved 36 of 90 and placebo 2 of 10. Overall D saved 45%, placebo 74%. Verdict: D works; the overall comparison is the misleading one.

2. Two intensive care units, A and B, 100 patients each, with the same case mix on admission. A ventilates almost everyone: 90 ventilated, 45 survived, and 8 of its 10 unventilated patients survived. B ventilates 20, of whom 9 survived, and 60 of its 80 unventilated patients survived. Overall A 53%, B 69%; within each group A is ahead. Verdict: B is the better unit. Who ends up on a ventilator is a consequence both of how sick the patient was and of the unit's own habits, so the two groups are not comparing like patients, and here I would rather read the bottom line.

3. A firm pays men more than women overall, but within every job title women earn at least as much as men. Verdict: the within-title comparison does not show the firm is fair, because who ends up in which title is part of what I am asking about. I go with the overall gap.

4. A crop trial where the reversal appears only when the plots are split by which of two technicians recorded the yields. Verdict: nothing here; I would report the overall difference and treat the split as noise.

5. Life expectancy in two countries. Country A has a much younger population and a slightly higher crude death rate, but lower death rates in every age band. Verdict: both numbers look right to me, for different questions — the crude rate for how likely a randomly chosen resident of A is to die this year, the age-adjusted one for whose health system is doing better — and I cannot tell whether that makes my verdicts inconsistent.

6. The case I keep going back and forth on: two diets compared on weight loss, with results split by how closely participants stuck to their diet. Adherents do better on both diets. Sticking to a diet is partly a matter of what the diet is like to live with and partly a matter of the person.

I suspect the principle is causal: pool the data unless the variable I am splitting on is a common cause of both the treatment and the outcome, in which case split. Is that right, or does one of my verdicts have to go? State the principle as precisely as you can, say which verdict it cannot accommodate, and tell me what it implies for a case I have not given — a variable that is a common cause of treatment and outcome but is itself partly shaped by the treatment, such as a severity score taken a few days in. If no single principle fits, give me the two best candidates and what each costs me. I would also like to know whether the principle is genuinely about causes or is only the thin instruction to answer the question that was asked, and if it is the latter, whether that is a defect.
