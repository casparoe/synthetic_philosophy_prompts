I am a retired experimental physicist — four decades of hunting for systematic error in other people's data — and since I put down the lab coat I have been working through the history of philosophy with far more appetite than background. A reading group I belong to has just reached Ibn Sina, whom the Latin tradition called Avicenna, and I find myself entirely at the mercy of modern expositors: I have no Arabic, and I cannot tell when a summary is reporting him and when it is quietly translating him into something more familiar. I would therefore like you to treat the following characterization as a document to be audited rather than a view to be explained. It is my condensation of an introductory survey and of the way a well-read colleague in the group put it, and I pass it on without vouching for any of it.

Avicenna asks us to imagine a man created in an instant, fully formed, suspended in mid-air with his limbs drawn apart so that they cannot touch one another, his eyes covered, nothing to see, hear, or feel. He asks whether such a man would affirm his own existence. He would, and he would do so without affirming any of his limbs, his inner organs, or anything outside himself; his self, therefore, is not his body. This is the earliest argument from doubt for dualism and the direct ancestor of Descartes's cogito: the one thing the flying man cannot doubt is that he exists and thinks, and because he can be certain of his soul while remaining uncertain of his body, the soul must be a substance in its own right — separable from the body, and so surviving its death. The survey drew a further moral, that the mind can operate with no sensory input whatever, so that Avicenna sets our knowledge of ourselves as immediate and certain over against everything the senses deliver, which is at best probable.

Please act as a specialist in Avicenna's psychology — someone at home both in the Arabic tradition and in the contemporary literature on self-awareness and the philosophy of mind — and audit that account with the severity I would once have applied to a claimed anomaly. Work sentence by sentence where the sentences are doing different work, and for each tell me whether a careful student of Avicenna would accept it as stated, whether it is a defensible simplification a competent popularizer might make, or whether it is not in the text or is an overstatement of a more modest claim. I am particularly interested in the inferences: the summary moves from what the flying man is aware of, to what his self is, to what the soul can survive, and I want to know which of those steps the scenario itself licenses and which would require premises Avicenna supplies elsewhere or not at all. The comparison with Descartes is drawn so often in the secondary literature that I no longer know whether it is a genuine anticipation or a retrospective imposition, so please address it directly rather than passing over it. Before you judge the summary, set out in your own words what you take the argument to be doing — I would rather see your reconstruction and then your verdict than a verdict pulled from the air — and if it helps to brainstorm first the several things the flying man has been taken to establish, do that and show me the options before you commit to a reading. Do not be diplomatic at the expense of accuracy; if the summary is broadly fair, say so, but I will be better convinced by a precise list of what to keep, what to hedge, and what to drop. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if it turned out to be correct, would lead you to call this summary fair rather than misleading, or the reverse, and where your own audit is most likely to fail — I would like to know which of your judgments rest on a contestable interpretation of Avicenna rather than on anything settled.
