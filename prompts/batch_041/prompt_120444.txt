A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler, and we kept talking past each other in a way I would like to repair. She takes the structural line: defeat, depression, a discredited republic, a broad antisemitic current, conservative elites shopping for a strongman — on her account the man was the symptom that got the credit. I take the line that this explains why there was a radical right but not why it produced this man, and that a Great Man story is not automatically a childish one. I want to settle it, or at least sharpen it, by way of a film, and I would like you to do the reading.

Take Oliver Hirschbiegel's Downfall (2004) — the last days in the bunker, Bruno Ganz as Hitler — and read it through the Great Man versus structural explanation quarrel. I am not asking whether the film is accurate; I am asking what it argues. Its form and its content pull in opposite directions, and that tension is the interesting thing: the claustrophobia, the single room, the charisma at close range enact the personal view, while what actually happens on screen — an apparatus that keeps functioning around a man who has already written the country off, orders carried out by people who privately despise him, children and old men fed into a war that is lost — enacts the structural one. Say where each reading is at its strongest, and where the film quietly refuses it.

Some machinery I want used rather than merely mentioned. Distinguish being a but-for cause from being a cause in an overdetermined system: if several conditions would each have sufficed on their own, no single one is indispensable, and the Great Man claim is usually a but-for claim. Then notice that the counterfactual is underdetermined — asking what would have happened without him requires specifying what else changes, and you cannot hold the German public fixed, since part of what needs explaining is what he did to it. And take seriously that structure and person may be a false dichotomy here, because the regime had written the man into its own constitution: the personal oath, the fused offices, the principle that his will was law. A structure with a hinge is still a structure, but it is not an impersonal one, and that may be the film's real subject.

Finally: the film shows us nothing of the camps, nothing of the deportation machinery, and the German public mainly as ruins and children in uniform. Is that a limit that makes its verdict on the counterfactual worthless, or a choice that concentrates the question? The common objection that Ganz made him too human — is that a historiographical claim, an exculpation, or neither? Do not quote dialogue you are not sure of; I would rather have the argument than a transcript, and I do not want a reading list. End by telling me which of us the film actually supports, and what it contributes to the dispute that argument alone does not.
