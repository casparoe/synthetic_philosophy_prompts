I have been defending a view I will call the gradient view of moral status, and I want the strongest honest map of its exposure — what would count against it, ranked by how decisive it would be.

The view has four claims. (1) Phenomenal consciousness, and in particular the capacity for valenced experience — pleasure, pain, and their kin — admits of degrees rather than being all-or-nothing. (2) An entity's moral status is a strictly increasing function of its degree of sentience: more sentience, more status, without exception. (3) The relevant degree is breadth as much as intensity — roughly, the number of independent yet integrated streams of valenced experience an entity can sustain at once — so that, other things equal, a creature capable of suffering in many channels simultaneously has proportionally greater moral status than one capable of suffering in only a single channel. (4) There is no threshold and no discontinuity anywhere along the scale: an insect, a fish, a dog, a human being, and — sitting at or near the floor rather than outside the scale — a thermostat all lie on one continuous gradient, and every increment of sentience, however small, contributes some increment of status.

Give me the defeaters. I want the empirical findings, conceptual arguments, formal results, and intuitions that would tell against each of the four claims, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Which of these would a defender of the view have to concede as genuinely counting against them, rather than dismissing as a misunderstanding? What does the gradient view predict that its rivals — a sharp-threshold view, a species-membership view, a view on which only certain kinds of experience ground status — do not, and what observations would embarrass those predictions? Where does the multiplicative claim in (3) get into trouble: aggregation problems, the apparent incommensurability of different creatures' interests, or the suspicion that moral status may not be the kind of thing that can be summed at all?

I would also like an honest verdict on insulation. Has the gradient view been qualified into unfalsifiability — for instance, by retreating to degrees so fine that no observation could ever count against it? If so, is that a defect for a view of this kind, or an acceptable feature of any view about a continuum? Finally, tell me what I, as someone already inclined toward it, should find most persuasive against it, and whether there is any single result or argument that ought to move me off the view entirely.
