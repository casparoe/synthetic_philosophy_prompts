I host a philosophy-of-mind podcast, and next week's guest has spent the last decade defending a heterodox position she labels 'causal quietism about the mental'. Her view, in her own summary: she accepts the exclusion argument's machinery in full — physical causal closure, the supervenience of the mental on the physical, and the claim that a mental property cannot be an additional cause of a physical effect that already has a sufficient physical cause, since it would then either be redundant with its physical base or simply be its physical base under another description. She also accepts that the standard escape routes do not work: supervenient causation, the appeal to determinables as the proportionate causes of what their determinates produce, and the causal relevance/causal efficacy distinction. Where she departs from the usual reading is the conclusion. She denies that any of this shows mental properties to be causally inert, on the grounds that 'cause' is indeterminate in precisely the respect the argument needs, so that the question 'are mental properties causes?' has no settled answer. What she offers instead is that mental explanations earn their keep by being invariant across a wide range of interventions and background conditions, and she insists that invariance is an explanatory virtue rather than a causal relation. She concedes that on her view mental properties are instrumentally rather than ontologically indispensable, and says that this is enough. She is a formidable interviewee with one habitual deflection: when pressed on whether her view differs in practice from epiphenomenalism, she answers that the objection presupposes a determinate conception of causation which she has already declined to accept.

You are an experienced interviewer and a philosopher of mind who has published on mental causation. Think carefully before drafting: begin by working out, in a few lines, what the strongest objections to her position are and which replies she is most likely to reach for, so that your questions are aimed at the joints rather than at the position's self-presentation.

Then draft nine to eleven interview questions that press on the substance of the view. At least one question should go after the overdetermination horn and her account of what the supervenience relation is, if it is neither identity nor entailment; at least one should ask whether her quietism generalises to the special sciences — biology, economics, geology — and, if it does, whether there is any principled stopping point short of a general scepticism about causation, and if it does not, what is supposed to be special about the mental; at least one should raise interventionist accounts of causation, which appear to make invariance sufficient for causation and so to convert her position into a straightforward causal claim rather than a quietist one; at least one should ask whether any of this makes a practical difference — to deliberation, to attributions of responsibility, to psychiatric explanation — or whether she takes the dispute to be merely verbal; and the sequence should end with a question about what consideration would count against her view.

For each question, add one line stating the answer she is most likely to give, followed by a follow-up designed so that her usual deflection — that the objection assumes a conception of causation she has already refused — is either unavailable or visibly costly. Order the questions so that this deflection becomes progressively harder to use as the interview proceeds. She should be able to answer from the position as described plus general philosophy of mind, so do not ask her to cite papers, recommend reading, or survey the literature, and do not include generic warm-up questions.

Present the result as a numbered list, with the question, then 'Likely reply:' in one sentence, then 'Follow-up:' in one or two sentences.
