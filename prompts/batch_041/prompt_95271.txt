I teach a ninety-minute session on the archive as an instrument of power to first-year PhD students in history, most of whom will spend the next three years in record offices and arrive believing that the archive is a neutral warehouse whose gaps are simply boxes nobody has sorted yet. I want the session built on a distinction of my own devising rather than on any named theorist, so please do not rest anything on a scholar I would then have to go and read: between contingent silence, where a record could have existed and was lost, discarded, or never made, and constitutive silence, where the recording apparatus could only ever register what fitted its own categories, so that the absence is produced by the act of recording rather than by a failure of it. Plan the session for me rather than explaining the distinction. What has to come first and what depends on what; whether a single page from an asylum admissions register — columns for name, age, occupation, diagnosis, and cause of insanity — should carry the whole session, and if not, what should carry it and what the carrying case must have and must lack; the misconceptions I should expect and the order in which they will arrive, my guess being that the students will treat every silence as a gap that sufficient diligence could fill, and precisely where in the sequence each should be headed off; the one question to put at the end that would show the distinction has landed rather than been memorised; and what I should refuse to cover at this level, saying so explicitly. The session succeeds if they leave able to say what the distinction changes about what they will do in the reading room, not merely willing to agree that archives encode power.
