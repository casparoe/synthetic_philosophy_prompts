I'm a journalist — science and ideas, mostly — and I'm working on a piece about how people decide when there's a small chance of something terrible, and I keep getting stuck on my friend Devon. He's 44, two kids, a mortgage, and two wrecked discs in his back. He can't sit through a movie anymore and his job is a desk job. His surgeon says there's a fix — a fusion — and put it at roughly 85% that he comes out basically normal. The other 15% is a spread: alot of it is "no better than now," some of it is worse, and a couple of percent is properly disabled, wheelchair-and-aid territory. The alternative is to keep living like this, which is survivable and boring and he's been doing it for six years. Everybody around him says take the surgery. His wife says take it. When I went through the numbers with him over beers — and I was being pretty grim about the bad outcomes — the surgery came out ahead, and not by a whisker. He still won't do it, and here's what makes me nuts: it's not because he thinks the bad outcomes are worse than I think they are. He says he agrees with me about every single outcome. His objection is that he isn't willing to let a 2% chance decide the rest of his life. And then he says something I can't tell is profound or nonsense: "if you told me it was 50/50 I'd probably do it. At least then it's a real fork and I could live with either side. It's the 2% I can't do." Our friend Ruthie, who underwrites for a living and thinks in probabilities all day, says that's incoherent — you don't get to treat a 2% as if it were secretly bigger than 2% and not be inconsistent somewhere.

Except he isn't a guy who avoids risk. He rides a motorbike to work and climbs on weekends and I've never once heard him mention the odds on either. When I brought that up he said it's different, because those risks aren't something he's deciding to take, they're just the price of the life, and he never has to look at them. The surgery is him putting a number on the table and picking it. I dont know what to do with that answer either.

So: is Devon being irrational, or is there a defensible way of thinking on which his refusal holds together — and if it does hold together, is it holding together because of how he's weighing the odds, or because of something else entirely, like the fact that he'd be the one doing the choosing? Ruthie thinks those are the same question and I have a feeling they're not. And then the practical part: what do I tell him, and is that even the right thing to be asking? I'm not after a reading list or "everyones risk tolerance is different," I want to understand where the actual disagreement lives, because I have to file this and I'd rather not hand-wave.
