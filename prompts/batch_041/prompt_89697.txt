I'm a doctoral student working on Jain logic and Nyāya realism, and I want to test my reading of the anekāntavāda/Nyāya exchange by having it argued out rather than summarized. Please write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers: one a Jain defending anekāntavāda and the many-sided predication of syādvāda, the other a Naiyāyika realist.

Stage it on a single concrete, ordinary case: a clay pot that has just been smashed. The Naiyāyika holds that the pot was a product generated from its parts and is now simply destroyed — it is nothing, though the clay remains — so there is no one thing of which we may say both that it exists and that it does not. The Jain holds that substance, quality and mode are not one story: the mode perishes while the substance persists, so the same real thing is at once permanent and impermanent. Neither character should be a caricature of their school.

Requirements:

- Real back-and-forth: each speaker must answer the other's actual argument and must concede or restate a claim under pressure at least once. No talking past each other, and no trading slogans about tolerance or pluralism. The point at issue is whether many-sided predication is a claim about how things are or merely a policy of courtesy among disputants.
- The Naiyāyika must press two objections and not let them go. First, that syādvāda is either trivial or incoherent: trivial if "from this standpoint" quietly specifies different respects and so never licenses a contradiction, incoherent if it does not. Second, the self-application problem: if every predicate holds only from a standpoint, what is the status of the claim that every predicate holds only from a standpoint? The Jain must answer both without equivocating, and the answers must be the strongest available.
- The Jain must in turn press at least one serious difficulty for the Naiyāyika: for instance, what it can mean for a product to be produced out of what did not previously exist, or the cost of the relation supposed to tie a whole to its parts and a universal to its instances.
- No names of historical authors, no titles, no citations; every argument must stand on its own. Gloss any technical term in plain English the first time it appears.
- Roughly 1,200 words.

Before the dialogue ends, each speaker should name the consideration that would actually move them — the argument or case that would make them abandon their position. Then, in a short paragraph outside the dialogue, say which position the exchange leaves weaker, what distinction or argument would be most likely to settle the matter, and where your own handling of the debate is most likely to be wrong.
