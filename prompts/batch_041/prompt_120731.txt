I'm a TA for an intro behavioral genetics course, mostly psych undergrads with a few phil students mixed in, and this Thursdays discussion section is the one where the whole room quietly decides that heritability means mostly genes. You know the misreadings I mean: that .8 means 80% of a person is genetic, that it's a fixed property of the trait, that heritable means you can't do anything about it. The lecture before mine, a student asked, perfectly reasonably, so if height is really that heritable, why was everyone shorter a hundred years ago, and the lecturer sort of waved at environment and moved on. I want that question to land in the room before I open my mouth. So what I'm after is a short poem to open the section with, read aloud, something like sixteen lines, a minute at most.

The form I want is a pantoum, and the reason is the recycling: each stanza takes the previous stanza's 2nd and 4th lines and makes them its 1st and 3rd, and the last stanza loops back so the poem ends on lines the reader first heard at the start. Here's the thing I actually care about. In a pantoum some lines keep coming back and some are brand new every time. I want the recurring lines to read like the fixed thing, the blood, the family, whatever keeps returning no matter what, and the fresh lines to read like circumstance. Then I want the fresh lines to get stranger and more various as the poem goes on, while the recurring lines stay word for word identical, so that the same lines that seemed to explain everything in stanza one are explaining almost nothing by the end. Nothing about the recurring lines changed. Only the company they keep. Thats the whole lesson and I want it in the readers body before I put anything on the board.

Couple of constraints so it doesn't turn into the lecture I'm trying to replace. Please don't use the words heritability, gene, genetic, DNA, innate, inborn, nature, nurture, I want people to get it from the structure rather than the vocabulary. No ending on it's both nature and nurture, thats exactly the mush I'm trying to get past, and skip the seed and soil thing, that analogy is in every lecture already. It doesn't have to be about twins either, in fact I'd rather it wasn't, the twin thing is what everyone reaches for first. It obviously doesn't need numbers or studies, no citations, I'm not putting a poem with a bibliography on a slide, just make it true. After the poem, give me three or four sentences on which piece of the machinery is doing the work and what would break if I moved it, then end with one line that starts Summary: and gives a single sentence naming the one structural feature that carries the point.
