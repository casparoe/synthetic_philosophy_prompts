Okay, I spent 35 years doing crystallography and the last two reading philosophy, and I've got one I can't get passed. A guy in my reading group is a big Penrose fan and he laid this out for me:

(1) For any consistent formal system F strong enough for arithmetic, there's a sentence G_F that in effect says "F cannot prove me" — F can't prove it, but it's true as long as F is consistent.
(2) A human mathematician, stepping outside F, can just see that G_F is true. She runs the reasoning.
(3) Any machine is a formal system of that kind.
(4) So for any machine there's a truth it can't prove and a human can.
(5) So the human mind isn't a machine.

The thing is, (5) isn't the only conclusion this seems to force on me. If I really can always step outside and see the truth of the Gödel sentence of any system, then let H be the system that captures everything I can prove about arithmetic. By (2) I can see that G_H is true. But that's just to say I can prove my own consistency, and Gödel says a consistent system can't do that. So this argument seems to show I'm either inconsistent or not a system at all, which is alot to swallow. I'd rather keep (1), the math part feels rock solid, and find the problem upstream. So which step do I give up, and if I give it up, what else does it cost me? Tell me how confident you are in your diagnosis, and flag the one piece of it your least sure about.
