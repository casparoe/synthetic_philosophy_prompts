I cover AI for a magazine, so I spend alot of my week watching very smart people reach for the same three comparison points the moment the conversation turns to whether this stuff is actually a big deal: the bomb, the printing press, and the Industrial Revolution. I've started keeping a tally in the back of my notebook. What gets me is that the bomb analogy gets used to argue both ways in the same afternoon — we got through the Cold War, so everybody breathe; we came within a hair of ending civilisation at least twice, so absolutely do not breathe — and nobody ever says which structural feature of the situation is supposed to be carrying the inference. It's all vibes with a citation.

Anyway, a friend of mine run's a graduate seminar on technology and catastrophe (history of science-ish, cross-listed with philosophy) and has to set the final, and he's asked me to draft the essay question because he wants one that a student can't get through by listing analogies and shrugging. Most of the seminar have read the standard AI-risk material but almost no history of technology, so that's the asymmetry to exploit. Here's what I want the question to force: pick the analogy they think is strongest, spell out the mapping explicitly — which feature of the source case is supposed to correspond to which feature of the AI case, and which features are stipulated to be irrelevant — and then locate the disanalogy that actually does damage, meaning the one that if you grant it the inference breaks rather than just wobbles. Then I want them to say what kind of claim that disanalogy is: an empirical one about timelines and actors, a conceptual one about whether the two things are even the same kind of thing, or a normative one about what we're entitled to infer from the past. My hunch is that half the seminar is arguing past each other on exactly that last point, and I'd love a question that makes that visible. Two things to head off: please don't let them hide behind the line about history rhyming rather than repeating, that's the exact move I want the question to make unavailable; and it's a closed-book 90 minute exam in a room with no internet, so nothing can depend on them remembering who wrote what.

Give me the question itself, a model answer of roughly 600-700 words, and a marking scheme out of 20. I'd rather have five named must-hit points with marks attached and a one-liner on how students typically blow each one than a rubric full of words like clarity and engagement. The model answer should lean on the reasoning, not on bibliography — and if it helps, feel free to have the question hand them one worked mini-example so they can see what an explicit mapping even looks like before they have to build their own.
