For a unit I teach in an undergraduate political philosophy course, provisionally titled "What Is My Vote For?", I am putting together a one-page handout that sorts the positions one can hold about the ethics of voting, and I would like you to build the sorting instrument rather than summarise any literature on the subject. Please give me a taxonomy in the form of a tree: every branch node is labelled with the question that separates that branch from its siblings, and every leaf is a position, carrying a name (or the word unnamed where nobody defends it) plus one sentence identifying its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the actual positions within it, noting the unoccupied combinations and briefly diagnosing each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Where the tree cannot separate two leaves, flag the pair and say why. The unit follows a seminar in which two students nearly came to blows over whether staying home on election day is a betrayal of everyone who fought for the franchise, so the handout has to be usable by people who arrive with convictions rather than with vocabularies.

Every branch node must be a question my students can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, so no node may take the form "do you accept the fair-play account of political obligation?"; each should instead be a concrete case or a claim about a case. Use at least these cases: a voter who knows her single ballot cannot change the outcome in her safely held district; a two-candidate contest in which one is merely less bad, alongside a third candidate who matches her views exactly but cannot win; a spoiled or blank ballot cast deliberately as protest; a voter tempted to stay home because voting would cost her an unpaid shift; a voter whose preferred candidate will win overwhelmingly with or without her; and a voter in a gerrymandered district whose preferred party cannot take the seat. I need the following distinctions to do real work in the tree, because they are what the handout's second page will discuss: whether the vote is treated as a lever, whose point is the difference it makes to the outcome; as a token, whose point is what it expresses or the stance it takes; or as a constitutive act, whose point is that it helps compose a collective decision-maker to which the voter will afterwards owe something. Also, whether any duty to vote is owed to other citizens, to the voter's own integrity, or to no one; and whether sincerity is a constraint on how one may vote or a sentimentality to be traded away.

Please make sure the following appear as leaves: a fair-play or reciprocity duty to vote; a no-duty view on which voting is morally optional; a sincerity-constrained expressive view; a lesser-evil instrumentalism; a complicity view on which voting for the lesser evil implicates one in what the winner goes on to do; a view on which one's vote is evidence rather than a cause, so that its point is what it tells others like oneself; and a civic-republican view on which voting matters as participation in self-rule irrespective of its effects. If you judge that one of those seven collapses into another, say so at the leaf rather than padding the tree with a distinction that does no work.

Begin the response with the tree itself — the top-level split first, then each branch indented beneath the question that produces it — with no preface, no restatement of this request, and no account of what you are about to do. Put the diagnoses of unoccupied branches and the list of unseparable pairs in short notes after the tree. Keep the whole thing to ten leaves at most.
