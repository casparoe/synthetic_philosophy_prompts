Ok so in my seminar we just did the Gödel, Escher, Bach stuff about typogenetics and self-replication, and now I keep classifying random things as self-replicating or not and I want to know what rule I'm actually using, cause I can't tell anymore.

My gut verdicts: a cell replicating is the real deal. A virus hijacking a host cell to crank out more viruses is self-replicating too, even though it's totally dependent on the host. A prion converting other proteins into its own misfoldded shape — NOT self-replication, just contagion, even though structurally it's spreading its own form around. A crystal seeding more crystal out of solution: not self-replicating. A flame crawling across a field: not self-replicating. A program that prints out its own source code: self-replicating, definitely. A book that contains instructions for building a machine that prints more copies of the book: self-replicating. And the typogenetic strands that code for enzymes which then act back on the strands: self-replicating.

The pattern I *think* I see is that it has to carry a description of itself that gets used both to build the thing and to be copied — that would exclude the prion and the flame, which fits. But then the self-printing program has no separate description-versus-build step, the code just copies itself, so doesn't that break it? And a photocopier copies descriptions all day long and isn't self-replicating. So what principle actually gets all my verdicts, what does it say about a case I haven't thought about — like a molecule that catalyzes its own synthesis from smaller parts, I think those exist — and which of my verdicts do I have to give up? I have a feeling it's either the virus one or the program one.
