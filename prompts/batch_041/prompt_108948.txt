I'm a fourth-year PhD student (philosophy, but I spend half my time lurking in a psych lab), and I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about moral worth — going by gut on cases instead of by what the literature says I should think. I'm sick of the shopkeeper and Huck Finn, so these are all mine. Verdicts, as honestly as I can report them:

- Locksmith. A locksmith I know charges pensioners and broke students less, landlords full price. Asked why, he says he couldn't stand to be the sort of person who gouges a pensioner. He doesn't seem to care about the pensioners as such and wouldn't cross the street for one otherwise. I give this full credit. Genuinely good, whatever the worth amounts to.
- Donor. Someone gives away most of her savings to a famine charity, and when you press her she says she does it because it makes her feel like a good person. No audience, no reputation, no glow she can point to beyond that. This strikes me as hollow. Right act, something missing. And I can't say what, because the locksmith is also in it for the self-image.
- Late-night drive. I drove a neighbour to the emergency room at 3am because she asked and it was obviously needed, and I'd have done it for anyone. But if she'd asked me for a lift to the airport at 3am I'd have said no. I think the hospital drive has full moral worth, and that the airport lift (had I given it) would have had none, even though in both the thing moving me is "she asked, and it's a big deal for her".
- Promise. A manager honours a promise to a departing employee at real cost to the company, purely because she promised, and she'd much rather not. Full credit.
- Paperwork manager. Same action, same cost, but he honours it because breaking it means a legal mess he doesn't want. No credit. The employee can't tell the difference and gets the same thing either way.
- Two motives. A student hands in a wallet she found. She's partly honest and partly afraid of the cameras, and either motive alone would have sufficed. I want to say she gets most but not all of the credit — which feels like it can't be right, since the honest motive was there at full strength.
- Convert. A man stops cheating on his taxes after a religious experience. Asked why, he says God would know, and he can't really say more; he has no view about why cheating is wrong. I want to give him credit, and I notice I'm giving it for the wrong thing, since God's knowing isn't the fact that makes cheating wrong.
- Fragile virtue. Someone donates a kidney to a stranger, moved entirely by the stranger's need. But if the surgery had been booked for a day with a big work deadline, she'd have backed out. I feel this is worth less than the same donation from someone who'd have gone through with it, even though the motive in the moment is identical.

The principle I keep reaching for: an action has full moral worth when the feature that makes the act right is the feature doing the motivating — the act wouldn't have happened if the agent hadn't registered that feature. Call it the operative-motive view. Test it against the above. I already know it dies on the locksmith and the convert, but I don't know whether the repair is to loosen "operative motive" (say, the motive has to be *responsive* to the right-making feature rather than identical with it) or to give up on the single-condition idea and go for two or three clauses (motive, plus counterfactual robustness, plus the agent's own grasp of why it's right). If it needs clauses, fine, I'd rather have the ugly truth then a tidy falsehood — but tell me which clause is doing the real work and which is decoration.

Then tell me what your principle says about a case I haven't listed: someone does the right thing from a decent motive, but the motive rests on a false belief. She stays with a dying friend because she thinks the friend has nobody else, when in fact the family is already on the way. Right act, decent motive, wrong picture of the world. Credit or not?

And please say how confident you are in each thing you claim, and single out the one point you're least sure of. I'd rather have your uncertainty than a clean verdict.
