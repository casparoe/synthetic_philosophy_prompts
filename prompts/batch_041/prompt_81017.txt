I run a small removals firm with my mate Col — nine of us all in, two vans, house moves and a bit of office stuff. Twice a year I sit down and do the pay reviews, then theres the christmas bonus which is at my discretion, and in between theres overtime and who gets the training budget and who drives the new van. Call it a grand a head on the bonus and 3-6% on the rises, so it's not nothing to these lads and it's not nothing to me either.

I've got no theory background, I did A-level sociology and thats about it, but I keep chewing on the word 'deserves', because I use it about four times a day and I've realised it's doing at least three jobs in my mouth and they don't agree with each other:

1. Earned it. Backwards. They did the graft, so the money's theirs by right. Nothing to do with whether paying them gets me anything.
2. Worth it. Forwards. They'll do the most with it, or they're the one who'll bring the work in next year. Nothing to do with what they've already done.
3. A fair shout. They were in the running, and nobody can say they got passed over for a reason that wasn't about the work.

And I know 'owed' isn't the same word as 'deserves' — I owe Aaron his hours whether he's earnt them that week or not, thats just the contract.

Then theres a fourth thing that keeps getting muddled in with it: how big the pot is. Nobody deserves the pot being the size it is, thats just what the jobs pay and what we can afford. But the second the pot's fixed, 'deserves' suddenly means 'deserves more of it than her', which feels like a different sentence to me. I don't know if thats a real difference or me being thick.

And then the thing that really nags me. Aaron's built like a shed and can carry a piano on his own, and he didn't earn that, he just is. Priya's got a way with panicking customers I couldn't teach her if I tried. I didn't earn the fact I can read a balance sheet and Col didn't earn his dad lending us the deposit for the first van. My sister-in-law says if you take that seriously nobody deserves anything ever and the whole idea's a nonsense. I don't think it's a nonsense, I think it just means desert runs out somewhere and past that point it's just rules we've agreed on, and I'd like to know where that point is.

So what I want is a procedure I can actually run. Not an essay, not a reading list (I wont read it), not a lecture on what some dead bloke said. A set of questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the thing stops with a straight no, and where it hands the decision back to me because its a values call and not a fact. I've got two weeks and about an hour a head before the reviews, its me and Col who sign off, and the accountant needs the numbers by the 15th, so it has to be quick and it has to be something I can explain to Col without him thinking I've lost it.

Three cases to run it on:
- Aaron: strongest pair of hands we've got, six years in, doesn't say much, and half of what he does is just his build.
- Priya: four months off with stress last year, but when she's in she holds the diary together and does the horrible phone calls nobody else will do.
- Me and Col and our cut at the end. I suspect the procedure is going to have something to say about that and I'm not going to like it, so say it anyway.

Also please check the little tests I've been using and tell me which ones are rubbish: (a) would I still say this if the same work had been done by someone I can't stand? (b) if the firm had a bad year, would I still say I owed it to them? (c) if I could keep the money and nobody would ever find out, does the claim still stand? (d) am I handing them the same thing they're owed — if what they deserve is credit and what I've got is cash, am I settling it or buying it off?

And say what the procedure can't do, so I know when to stop asking it things. British English please, I'm in Leeds and 'gotten' does my head in.
