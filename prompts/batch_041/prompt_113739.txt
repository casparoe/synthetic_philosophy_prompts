I chair the public memory commission in a mid-sized river city. Five members, a 90-day statutory clock on every petition, 75-minute monthly meetings, one half-time staff researcher, and a city council that can override us by simple majority and has done so twice. Our docket mixes requests to remove or relocate monuments, requests to designate buildings that have no architectural distinction but some other claim on us, and proposals for new commemorative works, including several that indict the city's own founding. Every finding enters the public record, gets quoted in the paper, and has already been cited once in litigation. Right now we decide these cases one at a time, on the strength of whoever speaks most confidently, and I want that to stop.

Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life strikes me as the right skeleton: three ways of treating the past — monumental, antiquarian, critical — each necessary, each with a characteristic way of going bad, and a single criterion, whether the history serves life, that is either the whole point or an open invitation to do whatever the majority already wanted. Three of my five commissioners will never read the essay, and I do not want a procedure that depends on their reading it.

So please write the procedure. The questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to tell which mode a petition is really asking for when it asks for two at once, what to do when the mode that fits is precisely the mode whose corruption is already in play — the hero-worship monumental history drifts into, the reverent mummification antiquarian history drifts into, the self-righteous condemnation critical history drifts into — the point at which the procedure returns a flat no, and the point at which it must stop and hand the question to a vote because what remains is a conflict of values rather than a question of fact. It has to be runnable in under an hour per petition by people with no theory, and specific enough that a dissenting commissioner can point to the step where the majority went wrong. Tell me which of Nietzsche's distinctions must survive the translation and which can be dropped, and be candid about where the procedure will misfire, above all about any test that would let a confident majority dress its preference up as a finding about what serves life.

Then run it on three petitions now on our docket. First, removal of a bronze of the mill owner who gave the city its park, and whose mill worked children and broke a strike with hired guns; I want him gone and I want to know whether the procedure gets me there. Second, designation of the last surviving building of a settlement drowned by the reservoir — a 1904 hall of no architectural merit, which the descendants cannot pay for and the county would have to; I think it is a money pit and I suspect the procedure will overrule me. Third, a proposal to rename a plaza for a local civil rights organizer and to include, on the same plaque, a line about his later conviction for embezzling from the organization he led, a request that seems to be monumental and critical at once. For each, give the verdict, the step it turns on, and the strongest objection to that verdict. Finish with what the procedure cannot do.
