I have been discussing Molyneux-style cases in a reading group, and we keep talking past each other, so I want to pin down my own verdicts and then ask what principle, if any, can hold them together. In the classic case, a person blind from birth learns to distinguish a cube from a sphere by touch and then gains sight: I think that, at the first moment of seeing the two objects, she cannot tell which is the cube and which is the sphere. But I also think that a blind person who has used a tactile sensory-substitution device for years, and who has learned to read the device’s vibration patterns as a cube or a sphere, would recognize the cube visually right away after gaining sight, or at least far sooner than the classic subject. I am less sure about a third case: a person with only light perception, enough to see shadows and movement, who learns the cube and sphere by touch; after surgery I think she can distinguish them visually, but only by moving her head and watching how the shadows shift, not in a single glance. A fourth case troubles me: a person who lost sight in infancy, learned the shapes by touch, and then regained sight as an adult—I think she can recognize the cube immediately, because she has a faded visual memory of shapes. Yet I do not think a person who has never had sight and who has only learned to match a felt cube to a seen cube while both are present can, after the seen cube is removed, say which of two seen objects is the cube.

I am trying to find a principle that yields all of these verdicts, or else tells me which one I should give up. I suspect the answer will need to distinguish immediate perceptual recognition from inference or from learned cross-modal matching, and perhaps to say something about active exploration and the role of a prior mapping between modalities. What principle, or small set of competing principles, best captures the pattern? Please state it precisely, say what it predicts for a case I have not described—say, a person who gains sight and can identify the cube only when allowed to touch it simultaneously—and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
