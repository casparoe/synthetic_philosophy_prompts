I am the vice-chair of my country's parliamentary defense commission, and I have to circulate a written opinion before we vote on the Defense Reform Bill. Some background you will not have, since the country is small: we belong to no alliance, we sit between larger powers, and for a century our defense has rested on a universal militia. Every able-bodied young man is conscripted for a year, then returns to civilian life keeping his equipment and reporting for refresher training. The system is expensive and increasingly resented; a growing share of each cohort sees it as an arbitrary interruption to education and careers, and the general staff says the training period is too short to produce specialists for modern systems.

The bill would abolish conscription and replace it with a professional force roughly a third the size, recruited on the open market and concentrated in technical roles. Two amendments are also live, and I must address each. The first would retain the militia but select inductees by lottery rather than universal obligation, which its sponsors say is cheaper and more honest, since only a fraction of conscripts are ever needed. The second would attach a commutation clause to that lottery: a selected person could discharge the obligation by paying a levy into a defense endowment instead of serving, at a rate set to fund two professional soldiers for the term he would have served.

The commission is split. Some members argue that a volunteer force is a blood tax collected from the poor and the patriotic, since those who sign up will be the ones with the fewest options while the rest of us free-ride on their service, and that this is a graver injustice than an obligation that falls on everyone alike. Others argue the reverse: that forcing an unwilling citizen to serve, and perhaps to kill and die in a quarrel he opposes, is a far worse wrong than paying someone who chooses the risk, and that a militia whose members are hostile to their own service is a paper force in any case. A third group cares less about fairness than about what a professional force does to the republic. They claim that a standing army loyal to the government, paired with a citizenry that no longer holds its own arms, removes the last structural check on the government's use of force at home, and that our neutrality is credible only because an occupier would confront an entire population rather than a few brigades.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend one concrete course of action — the bill as it stands, one of the amendments, or rejection with a specified alternative — and give the commission reasons it can actually argue with, including a fair statement of the strongest case against my recommendation. Do not hedge into a survey of the options; I need a bottom line and an honest reckoning with the objection to it.
