I write about science and ideas for a UK magazine, and for the past few months I've been sitting in on the ethics panel of a behavioural insights consultancy — the sort of place that designs nudges for government departments, charities and a couple of supermarket chains. They keep a log of past rulings, and I'm trying to work out what they'll do with next week's case, because it's the first one that's really split the room.

Their form so far. They waved through: a supermarket moving milk to the back of the store so shoppers walk past more aisles; a government switching organ donation to opt-out after a public consultation; a charity letter that truthfully says a neighbour has already donated; a charity presenting its success rate as 90 per cent of children helped rather than 10 per cent not helped; a shop playing slow music to make people linger; a canvasser who mirrors a voter's values back at them before asking for their vote; and a doctor presenting a operation as having a 90 per cent survival rate rather than a 10 per cent mortality rate, when both are accurate, to a patient who asked for the numbers. They blocked: a casino app with near-miss animations and variable-ratio rewards; a pharma company paying a patient group to campaign for its drug without disclosing the funding; a salesman inventing 'only two left' to an elderly customer when it wasn't true; a dating app hiding some matches to keep people swiping; a timeshare salesman who feigns romantic interest in a lonely customer; and a political campaign that implies its ad is from a grassroots group when it's actually party-funded.

Next week: a fertility clinic's app. It uses cycle-tracking data to send notifications at the moment a user is most likely to conceive, saying things like 'This is your best window this month — don't let it pass'. Nothing in the message is false; the data is accurate and the user consented to cycle tracking. But the notifications are timed and worded to produce anxiety, and the app's premium subscription converts much better among users who get them. The panel has to decide whether this is legitimate persuasion or manipulation.

I want you to predict what they'll do, and more importantly what rule they're actually running. Set out the two or three principles that fit all their past rulings equally well — something like 'manipulation is deception', 'manipulation is exploiting a vulnerability', 'manipulation is bypassing rational deliberation', 'manipulation is influence that wouldn't survive transparency', whatever you think is really doing the work — say what each predicts about the fertility app, give me your best guess and why, and name the further case that would best tell those principles apart. If their past rulings genuinely underdetermine it, tell me what principle they'd be adopting with each possible decision. And please write it in British English.
