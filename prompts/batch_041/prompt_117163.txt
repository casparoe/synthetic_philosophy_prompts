A friend ended an argument the other day with a proverb — 'there is no disputing about tastes' — and I have not been able to decide how much he was entitled to get out of it. The argument was this. I told a mutual friend that a novel was beautiful and that she should read it; she asked how she could be in a position to believe that without having read it; and he closed the matter with the proverb, meaning by it that no one can be told what is beautiful, that my recommendation could carry no weight with her, and that she might as well consult her own preferences and have done. I suspect the proverb says nothing so strong, and that the claim he needed from it is not the claim it makes.

I want the distinct things that sentence could be asserting, laid out as a menu and ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical. For each reading I want: the claim stated precisely in its own terms; whether it is a claim about the meaning of aesthetic words, the metaphysics of beauty, the psychology of disagreement, or the norms of aesthetic argument; whether it is true, plausible, or false; and what it implies about whether one person can come to believe justifiably, on another's word alone, that some particular thing is beautiful. I have also heard the variant 'there is no accounting for taste'; say whether it is the same claim differently worded or a different claim altogether, and where the difference lies.

Two cases should be kept apart throughout, since I believe his use of the proverb depends on running them together: testimony about a work's beauty and testimony about its non-aesthetic features — that it is narrated in the first person, that it moves every reader to tears, that a critic of proved reliability ranks it above its rivals. Where the readings divide on that contrast, say so.

I would also like one pressure test applied to your menu. Consider a painting destroyed by fire, of which there survives a detailed contemporary description, the unanimous praise of everyone who saw it, and no original — a work no one can now experience. Which of your readings allows a justified belief that it was beautiful, and which forbids one?

I do not want names, dates, or a reading list; I want the claims themselves and your verdicts on them. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} naming the reading that actually supports the use he made of the proverb, or \answer{none} if no single reading does.
