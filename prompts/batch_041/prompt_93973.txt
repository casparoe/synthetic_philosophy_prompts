I spent thirty-five years as a marine biologist and I've been reading philosophy seriously since I retired, so forgive me if I've got the vocabulary half-wrong. What I want is to be told what I actually think, because I keep noticing that my gut verdicts about emotions don't line up with any of the three stories I keep hearing — that they're judgments, or that they're perceptions, or that they're just feelings. Here are the cases as I actually call them.

Turbulence over the Atlantic. I'm white-knuckled, heart going, genuinely frightened — and if you asked me at that moment I'd tell you flying is the safest way to travel and I believe it. I still call that fear, not just a body-state. So fear isn't the judgment that I'm in danger.

My brother-in-law gets quietly furious at slow drivers. Ask him and he's not angry at all, he's just "concentrating." His knuckles say otherwise. I think he really is angry and simply has no access to it. So anger isn't the feeling he can report either.

My wife's chair. Years on, I'll catch sight of it and get the throat thing, and I'd call that grief, though no judgment I could name goes through my head while it's happening.

A horror film. Palms sweating, genuinely scared, and I know perfectly well it's a film and nothing is coming for me. I still say that's fear, real fear.

My grandson's fear of the dark. He says there's a monster. As he grows out of the belief the fear fades with it, and I think that was a different sort of thing from my turbulence fear — his fear just was the belief, mine isn't.

I got angry at the referee in a novel last month. There's nobody there to be angry at. Still anger, I'd say.

A friend can't eat soup from a bowl she knows was washed, because she once saw it holding something foul. She knows it's spotless. Still disgust, and I don't think she's being irrational in a way that makes it not disgust.

A colleague after losing his father says he feels nothing, he just can't sleep and keeps working. I think he's grieving even though he can't name it.

But: someone tells me, flatly, like reading a thermometer, that they're sad. No feeling to it at all. There I'm tempted to say that isn't sadness — it's a report, a thought about themselves, not the thing itself.

Here's my problem. Cases three through eight push me toward saying emotions are a kind of perception — a way of taking in how things stand with me, not a belief and not a bare sensation. That handles the turbulence and the film nicely, and the disgust, and even the grief I can't put into words. But it leaves my grandson's case and the flat reporter sitting awkwardly, and I can't tell whether those two are the same kind of case or opposite ones. His fear was the belief; her sadness, on my verdict, is the absence of any feeling. Those look like they should fall on the same side and they don't.

So: give me the principle, or the smallest set of principles, that gets my verdicts — and if something has to go, tell me which verdict to drop rather than leaving it to me. I want you to actually pick, commit to it, and defend the choice, not hand me a menu. Also say what your principle predicts for a case I haven't listed, something like being amused at a joke you don't find funny, or being ashamed of something you don't think was wrong.

Last line, so I can file it: Verdict: <the principle in one sentence>.
