I'm a third-year phil/politics student and I'm taking a module on distributive justice this term — we've just done Nozick, the entitlement view and the Wilt Chamberlain example, and honestly I walked out of the lecture fairly convinced. Then my flatmate, whos in the same module and argues for sport, gave me the following and now I can't unsee it. I'd like you to tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs, because Ive been going round in circles about this for two days.

His argument, tidied up: (1) Whether what you hold is just depends on how you came to hold it — just acquisition, just transfer — and not on the shape of the total distribution. (2) Any pattern you name (equality, need, desert) gets knocked out of shape by voluntary transfers: Chamberlain's gate receipts do it on their own, nobody forced anyone to buy a ticket. (3) So keeping a pattern in place means the state repeatedly moving holdings back, i.e. taking from people who came by what they have by perfectly just steps. (4) Taking what someone came by justly, against their will, in order to serve someone else's purposes, isn't merely *like* being made to work for another person — it *is* being made to work for another person; that's the whole force of Nozick's line that taxing the wages you earn is on a par with forced labour. (5) So patterned principles could only be sustained by something in the neighbourhood of forced labour, and historical entitlement wins. Now the turn: (6) notice that (4) never mentions patterns anywhere. Read literally it says that any non-consensual taking from just holdings is forced labour, full stop. (7) Whatever pays for the police, the courts and the defence of the realm — call the payments fees, call them taxes — is money people earned and would mostly rather keep, and there's no moment at which each payer individually consented to handing it over. So (4) condemns the funding of the minimal state too. (8) If the reply is 'ah, but those takings are different, because without them nobody's rights are enforceable at all, and any reasonable person would agree to them', then the egalitarian says the same thing about a tax for schools or a basic income, and (4) stops doing any work. (9) So either the minimal state is unjust, which is the opposite of what Nozick set out to show, or (4) is false and the forced-labour rhetoric can't condemn redistribution.

My problem is that I want to keep (1), (2) and Nozick's conclusion, and I'm willing to drop (4) if I have to — but every time I try to say what's wrong with (4) I end up saying 'well, the difference is that one of them interferes with rights and the other doesn't', which is just the conclusion again. And when I try instead to defend the minimal state's funding as voluntary, I can't see how it stays voluntary once there are people living in the territory who simply won't pay. So: which step do you reject — (4), (7), the 'reasonable person would agree' move in (8), or something else entirely — and, more importantly, what was that step doing for the argument before you gave it up, and what do you have to pay to do without it? If you think the argument is actually sound and it's Nozick who's in trouble, say that too, and tell me what he'd have to concede.
