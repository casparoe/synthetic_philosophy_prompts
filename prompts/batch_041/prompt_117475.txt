My landlord says I caused the mould in the bathroom by not running the extractor fan, which annoyed me, so a friend and I tried to build him a real argument that not doing something can cause a thing. Now I think the argument eats everything and I cant find the bad step. (1) A caused B just in case A came before B and, if A hadn't happened, B wouldn't have. (2) My not running the fan is a thing that happened — the flat spent six months being a flat with the fan off. (3) If I'd run it, the mould wouldn't have grown. So the omission caused the mould, landlord wins. (4) Same test, bigger case: if I'd spent my spare cash on famine relief instead of coffee, some people who died wouldn't have, so my not donating caused their deaths, and likewise for every other thing I never did that would have saved somebody, which is an enormous list. (5) Nothing in the test draws a line between the fan, the famine, and my not having invented a cure for anything: all of them are absences whose corresponding action would have prevented some harm. So I caused the famine deaths, and the earthquake deaths, and everything else, and so did you, on exactly the footing of the guy who set the fire. (6) But that can't be right — causation is supposed to do work in sorting out who's responsible, and a relation that makes everyone the cause of everything does no work at all. So one of (1)-(3) has to go. Which one, and what do I pay for dropping it? I'd rather give up the landlord's verdict than (1), but I don't know if that's affordable.
