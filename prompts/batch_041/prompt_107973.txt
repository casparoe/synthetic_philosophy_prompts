I never got past school mathematics, but for the last few months a friend who is taking a statistics course and I have been arguing about a family of puzzles that he says all come down to something called the principle of indifference, and I have reached the point where I want an answer from someone who has actually thought hard about the foundations of probability rather than from him. So please take the role of a philosopher of probability who knows why these puzzles are hard, start by brainstorming the cases below before you commit to anything, and then answer me carefully. The puzzle that started it is the one he says is called Bertrand's paradox: you pick a chord of a circle at random, and depending on how you do the picking you get the answer that the chord is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle one third of the time, or one quarter of the time, or one half of the time. What bothers me is not that I cannot do the arithmetic; it is that each of the three methods feels obviously right while I am doing it, and then when I set them side by side I can see they cannot all be right.

But the same thing happens to me in much humbler places, and there my reactions are not uniform at all. The ones that sit perfectly well with me: a die I have never handled, with no reason to suspect it is loaded — one in six for each face. A coin from a country I have never visited, with no reason to suspect it is unbalanced — one in two for heads. A jar of marbles where all I have been told is that each marble is either red or blue — one in two that the first one out is red. A machine that has chosen a point somewhere on a circular dartboard, with no detail about the machine — one in two that the point is in the left half, one in four that it is in the top-left quarter. The ones that do not sit right: a book I have not seen. Is its cover red? One in two. Is it blue? One in two. Green? One in two. That is plainly nonsense, and I can feel that it is nonsense, yet I cannot say what is different about the marble jar, since in both cases I am supposed to know nothing about the item in question. Then the ones that feel forced but contradictory. A glass holds a mixture of wine and water, and I am told only that the wine is somewhere between one and two times the water; what is the chance the wine is less than one and a half times the water? If I spread my ignorance evenly over the wine-to-water ratio I get one in two; if I spread it evenly over the reverse ratio, water to wine, I get two in three; and I cannot see why one of those ways of spreading my ignorance should be the permitted one. A factory makes cubes, and I am told only that every edge is between one and three centimetres; what is the chance a cube's edge is under two centimetres? Evenly over the edge gives one in two, evenly over the volume gives about seven in twenty-six, evenly over the surface area gives three in eight. And the doctor case: my GP gave me a figure for how often a procedure like mine goes well, taken from people like me, and my friend pointed out that I am also like plenty of other groups whose figures differ — I am a man, I am in my fifties, I had a different operation ten years ago, I live in a particular town — and I could not tell him which grouping was the one that was supposed to count, or why. Meanwhile the two-envelope business, where swapping and then swapping back each seem to improve your expectation, leaves me with no verdict at all: I am sure the reasoning is broken somewhere, but I cannot find the break.

Here is the best principle I have managed on my own, and I would like you to test and repair it rather than simply tell me it is naive: indifference is allowed when I can describe the situation in a way that treats the possibilities I am spreading my ignorance over as genuinely the same kind of thing, and it is not allowed when my description hides a further choice I could have made differently, so that the rival answers are answers to different questions rather than the same one. I suspect that is either circular or false. So please state the principle you think actually captures my pattern of verdicts, or the two or three competing principles that each capture most of it, precisely enough that I could apply them myself; for each clause, tell me which of my verdicts it exists to save; tell me what each principle implies about cases I have not mentioned, and add one case of your own that you think is the sharpest test of it; and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I would rather have a simple principle that overturns one of my reactions than a complicated one that saves them all by fiat. Finally, tell me where your reasoning is weakest — which single step, if it went the other way, would change your answer — and what sort of argument or example would genuinely move you, so that I know how much of this to trust.
