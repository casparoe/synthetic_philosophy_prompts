I have been trying to pin down a puzzle that keeps surfacing in my teaching, and I cannot decide whether it already has a name or whether I am describing something the literature has never isolated. The puzzle concerns what happens when I try to make sense of a speaker whose framework is genuinely remote from mine — a historical figure, say, or someone from a culture whose categories do not map onto my own. I find that I can almost always render such a speaker largely right about the world, provided I am willing to adjust what I take their words to mean, and I can almost always render them badly mistaken, provided I hold their words to mean what mine do. The only evidence I have is what they say and do, and that evidence seems to fit both readings equally well; so at every difficult point I must choose between charging the speaker with error and revising the meaning of their words, and the choice seems to turn on where I begin rather than on anything in the speaker. I suspect that two equally careful interpreters could reach different and equally defensible readings of the same person. I know the familiar advice to interpret others generously, but that is not what I am after; I want to know whether the stronger claim has a name — the claim that substantial agreement is a precondition of interpretation, and that meaning and belief cannot be fixed independently of each other. If it has a name, what is it, and how does it stand to the nearest ideas that are not quite it? If it has none, name it and mark its edges: is the necessity it asserts a methodological rule, a constraint on what counts as interpretation at all, or a claim about meaning itself?
