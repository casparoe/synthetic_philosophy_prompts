I work as a software engineer, and my training is in engineering rather than statistics or philosophy, so I want to be explicit about what I am asking for and what I am not. I have spent the last few years reading about the reproducibility problems in empirical psychology and, more recently, about analogous problems in machine-learning benchmarks, and the debate keeps invoking Ronald Fisher (1890–1962) — usually as the man who handed the sciences a testing procedure that is now blamed for the trouble. What I want is a careful, reasoned prediction of what Fisher himself would have said about a set of developments that all postdate his death, built out of his own commitments rather than out of the received story about him. His views on race and eugenics are not what I am asking about; I am asking about the statistical and methodological question.

Please do not give me citations, reading lists, or references to particular papers, books, or archives — I cannot check them and I am not asking for them. Reason from what is known about his positions, and be explicit when you are extrapolating rather than reporting. Assume I will read your answer without looking anything up, so anything I need to understand must be in the answer itself.

The developments I have in mind are these. For each, assume only the plain description I give here; do not assume I know the literature on it.

1. Preregistration and the registered report: authors deposit their hypotheses, design, and analysis plan before collecting data, and some journals review the plan in advance and commit to publishing the result whatever it turns out to be.

2. Proposals to move the conventional significance threshold from 0.05 to 0.005, and a separate proposal to retire the phrase statistically significant from journal articles altogether, on the ground that it is used as a decision rule rather than as a report of evidence.

3. The argument that a large fraction of published findings in some fields are false, made by reasoning about how plausible the tested hypothesis was before the study ran, how much power the study had, and how often null results go unpublished; on this argument, what a significant result tells you depends on how many hypotheses were worth testing in the first place.

4. Multi-laboratory replication consortia: the same protocol run by dozens of laboratories, with the pooled estimate rather than any single laboratory's result treated as the finding.

5. Meta-analysis as the highest form of evidence in medicine and psychology, including statistical corrections for the tendency of small studies with null results to go missing.

6. Bayesian replacements for testing: priors over hypotheses, Bayes factors, posterior distributions, with the stated aim of saying how much the data should move a rational agent's belief rather than how often a procedure would mislead in the long run.

7. Statistical practice driven by prediction: models selected because they predict held-out data well, with no null hypothesis, no significance level, and no pre-specified test at all, together with the claim that in many applied settings prediction was what was wanted all along.

8. The diagnosis that the problem is not statistical technique but incentive structure — careers, hiring, and journal prestige reward novel positive results — so that the remedy is institutional (registered reports, badges for open data and open code, publication of null results) rather than mathematical.

9. The claim, made about ordinary honest research rather than fraud, that the flexibility of analysis decisions — which covariates to include, which observations to drop, where to split a continuous variable — makes the reported p-value uninterpretable even when the researcher runs exactly one test and reports it truthfully.
