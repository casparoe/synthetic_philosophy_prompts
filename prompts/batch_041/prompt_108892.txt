I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years now, and there's a move I keep meeting in comment threads that I've never been able to either fully embrace or fully shrug off. Call it the no-difference principle: if two descriptions of the world agree on every observation anyone could ever make, they describe one situation, and whatever difference remains between them lives in the description rather than in the world. It gets deployed against metaphysical extravangance of all kinds, and most of the time I nod along. But I recently went back to the hole argument in general relativity, and now I suspect the principle is doing more work there than anyone admits, and possibly question-begging work. I want to see it fought out.

Here's the setup I want the dialogue to work with, in case it's needed. A theory like general relativity is built from a manifold of events plus fields defined on those events, the metric field above all. Take a region of the manifold in which the matter fields vanish, and suppose everything outside that region is held fixed. Now smoothly reshuffle the points inside the region among themselves, dragging the metric along with them, so that the new assignment still satisfies the field equations, still agrees with the original assignment everywhere outside the region, and inside it disagrees about which point carries which metric value. If the points of the manifold are real individuals with identities of their own, independent of the fields, then those two assignments are two distinct physical situations that agree on all the data outside the region and diverge inside it. That looks like a failure of determinism, and it also looks like a fact about the world that no observer could ever get at, since the difference is invisible from outside. The standard relationist response is that the two assignments are the same physical situation described twice, so there is no indeterminism and no unknowable fact. The standard substantivalist reply is that this identification buys the conclusion by denying the points the very independent identities that made them substantival in the first place. A third position worth having in the room holds that the points are real but have no identities over and above the pattern of fields on them, so diffeomorphic assignments are one world without any retreat to relationism.

Write a dialogue between two people arguing about this: one a substantivalist, one a relationist. Requirements:

- Both are equally competent. Neither is a strawman, and neither wins by being handed an obviously bad version of their view. Each must respond to the other's actual last point rather than to the point they wish had been made.
- The relationist may not simply invoke the no-difference principle and declare victory. The substantivalist should press on why possible observation is the right test for what exists, and should point out that in this context the principle is the disputed premise rather than a neutral arbiter.
- The substantivalist should get to make the third-position move above. The relationist must then say whether that move is relationism in disguise, a genuine third option, or an unstable halfway house, and the substantivalist must answer the charge.
- At some point one of them should reach for the analogy with surplus structure in gauge theories, and the other should say precisely where the analogy holds and where it breaks, rather than gesturing at it.
- They should argue about whether the alleged indeterminism is a reductio at all, or merely a feature a theory can have, and about what it would take for that to be a real objection.
- No citations, no quotations, no appeals to who said what. If either of them uses a label for a position, they should gloss it in a sentence. Argue it from first principles.
- Neither should concede anything they wouldn't really concede, and neither should be converted by rhetoric. If one side seems to have the better of it, let the other say exactly what they would and would not give up.
- End with each of them naming the single premise they would need the other to abandon, and stating whether they think the dispute is substantive or merely verbal, and whether they find the other's verdict on that convincing.

Roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words. No mathematics beyond the sketch above. I'm not looking for a resolution so much as for a clean view of whether the no-difference principle, applied here, is a discovery or a presupposition.
