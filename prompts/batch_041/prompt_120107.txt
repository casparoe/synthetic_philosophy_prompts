I'm putting together questions for a small podcast episode, and next week's guest is an epistemologist who's spent a lot of time on Williamson's anti-luminosity argument and what it means for the KK principle. I've read the standard stuff but I want to push him past the usual recapp. Can you draft eight to ten interview questions that actually get into the weeds?

A few things I specifically want covered, and for each I want a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give:

First, the margin-for-error principle. I want a question that forces him to say what exactly the principle is doing in the argument -- is it the real engine, or is the sorites-style series of small changes the thing that does the work? Then a follow-up for when he says it's the margin principle: ask whether the argument then just reduces to a safety condition on knowledge, and whether someone could accept safety while rejecting the specific margin-for-error formulation.

Second, the bridge from feeling cold to KK. The anti-luminosity argument is usually run on phenomenal states -- feeling cold, or a slow series of temperature changes. But KK is about knowledge, not about phenomenology. Does the argument actually refute KK, or does it only refute the luminosity of phenomenal states, leaving KK untouched? I want him to either draw the connection explicitly or admit there's a gap. Follow-up: if he says knowledge is itself a mental condition and so subject to the same argument, ask whether that assumes a controversial internalist or mentalist view of knowledge.

Third, a new angle I've been kicking around. Instead of the standard luminosity thesis -- if you feel cold, you're in a position to know you feel cold -- I want to ask about a knowledge-luminosity thesis: if you know p, you're in a position to know that you know p. That's basically the positive-introspection half of KK, but stated as a luminosity claim. Can the anti-luminosity argument be adapted to that thesis directly, without going through phenomenal states? What would the sorites series even look like for knowledge -- a series of cases where you know something, then know slightly less? And does the argument need an extra premise that knowledge comes in degrees or has a margin? Follow-up: if he says the series can be built from cases of knowing with decreasing evidence, ask whether that just assumes that knowledge is not binary.

Fourth, the KK principle itself. Some people treat KK as a consequence of luminosity of knowledge; others treat it as a separate thesis in epistemic logic. Ask him whether he thinks the anti-luminosity argument gives us a reason to reject KK, or whether KK could be true even if no condition is luminous. Follow-up: if he says KK could survive, ask what independent motivation there is for KK once you've given up luminosity.

Fifth, a question about the dialectic. The anti-luminosity argument is often presented as a refutation of a certain Cartesian or internalist picture. But I want to ask whether it's actually a refutation of KK or just a refutation of the idea that knowledge is transparent to the knower. Is there a version of KK that doesn't require transparency -- say, a purely formal principle in epistemic logic that doesn't make any claim about a subject's access to her own epistemic state? Follow-up: if he says yes, ask whether that formal version is still philosophically interesting or just a theorem in some model logic.

Sixth, higher-order knowledge and the iteration. If KK is true, then knowing p implies knowing that you know p, which implies knowing that you know that you know p, and so on. I want a question about whether the anti-luminosity argument can be blocked by rejecting the iteration rather than rejecting the first step. Follow-up: if he says the argument doesn't need full iteration, ask what minimal version of positive introspection it does need.

Seventh, a methodological question. The anti-luminosity argument uses a sorites series of very small changes. Ask him whether the argument is really a sorites argument in disguise, and if so, whether it inherits all the controversies about vagueness and higher-order vagueness. Follow-up: if he says it's not a sorites argument because the margin principle is doing the work, ask whether the margin principle itself is motivated by vagueness considerations.

Eighth, a question about the feels-cold example specifically. Why does the argument use a phenomenal state rather than a propositional attitude? Is that just for vividness, or does the choice of example matter? If you ran the argument on a belief -- say, a belief that slowly becomes less justified -- would it still work? Follow-up: if he says it would, ask what happens to the distinction between knowledge and mere belief.

Ninth, a so-what question. If the anti-luminosity argument succeeds against KK, what's the biggest casualty? Does it undermine the idea that we can know our own epistemic standing? Does it have implications for epistemic injustice, or for the idea that people should be able to give an account of what they know? Follow-up: if he says it doesn't have those implications, ask why not.

Tenth, a closing question that ties it together: does he think the KK principle is false, or just unproven? And does the anti-luminosity argument give us knowledge that KK is false, or just knowledge that it's not luminous? I want the irony there to land.

Keep the questions concrete and make them build on each other so the interview has an arc. No generic what-drew-you-to-philosophy stuff. At the end, give me a one-sentence summary of the through-line as the last line, in the form "Through-line: ..."
