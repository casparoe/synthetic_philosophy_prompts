I run a political philosophy reading group and next month I want to do Shirley Jackson's 'The Lottery' as a communitarian-versus-liberal text, rather than the usual mob-violence reading. Can you give me a reading that takes the communitarian side seriously in its strongest form -- the self is constituted by its community, practices carry goods you can't reconstruct from outside them, the appeal to neutral principles is itself just another tradition -- and then asks what the story actually does to that position? Old Man Warner is the obvious communitarian voice, and what's interesting is that he can't give a reason for the lottery beyond its being theirs, and he treats the villages that have dropped it as corrupted rather then as evidence. But the detail I keep coming back to is that they've forgotten the ritual: they keep the box, they've lost whatever the ceremony was for, and nobody can remember it -- which seems to cut against the strongest defence, since that needs the practice to be embodying some shared good rather than just reproducing itself. Then there's Tessie, whose objection when her name comes up is an appeal to fairness, i.e. to exactly the neutral-principle vocabulary the communitarian says is a hollow abstraction, and she's the one who was joking with her neighbour moments before. So is the story a reductio of communitarianism, or only of a caricature of it, or does it land on the reader instead -- it's told in the villagers' own flat cheerful register, so you don't even twig what's happening until the end. I'd also like something on exit: the village is the whole world here, and the one person who objects is the one who's stoned, so is the story deliberately foreclosing the liberal escape route? Be faithful to the story and to the actual debate, including where the story resists the reading, and say what it shows that the arguments on their own can't. Please write it in British English.
