I run a reading group that mixes cognitive-science graduate students with working programmers, and next month I have ninety minutes to teach the notion of conceptual slippage: the way an analogy-maker, when the reading of its source case that it started with cannot be carried across to the target, quietly re-describes the source instead of failing, and produces an answer that is neither derivable from the rules as written nor arbitrary. I do not want the idea explained to me. I want a plan for teaching it.

The material is a microdomain I built myself, so nobody in the room has seen it. A shelf holds three stones in a row, written left to right. Stones come in four grades in ascending order: pebble, stone, rock, boulder. The domain licenses exactly four moves: promote one stone by one grade, demote one stone by one grade, swap two adjacent stones, and reverse the row. A puzzle gives a source shelf and its result, then a target shelf, and asks what the target becomes. My carrying puzzle is: pebble-stone-rock becomes pebble-stone-boulder, so what does boulder-rock-stone become? I chose that source pair deliberately — the stone that changes is both the rightmost and the largest — and I need to know whether that choice is doing the work I think it is.

Plan the session. Specifically: the order in which the ideas must arrive and the dependencies among them, that is, what the room has to believe, or be able to do, before slippage can be felt as a phenomenon rather than heard as a mistake, and what must be withheld until after they have produced a slip of their own; whether my shelf puzzle can carry the whole session or whether it buckles under the weight I am putting on it, and if it does, what a carrying example needs that mine lacks, and whether I would be better off with the familiar letter-string puzzle of the kind in which the string abc becomes abd and one is then asked what ijk becomes; the readings of my source pair that the room will actually generate, in the order they will generate them, which of those readings the session should endorse as a principled slip and which it should mark as an error or as no answer at all, and exactly where in the sequence each must be handled; the misconceptions I should expect — I am anticipating that they will hear slippage as sloppiness, that they will assume one answer is correct, and that once they have granted two answers they will conclude that anything goes — with the point in the sequence at which each of those has to be defused rather than merely corrected; the trade-off I must make them feel between a system that never slips and one that slips at will, together with what licenses a slip rather than merely permitting it; and one question to put to them at the end that would show whether they can distinguish a licensed slip from an arbitrary answer rather than merely recite the distinction. Finally, tell me what to leave out at this level and to say out loud that I am leaving it out.
