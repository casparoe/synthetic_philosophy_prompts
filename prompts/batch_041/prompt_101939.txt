I convene a graduate seminar, cross-listed between philosophy of science and machine learning, on the epistemology of measurement under optimization. I am assembling the take-home final and need a single essay question on Goodhart's law and specification gaming — one that cannot be answered by reproducing the familiar inventory of ways a proxy can drift from its target (regression to the mean under selection, extremal tails, causal back-channels, adversarial gaming). The question should force candidates to say what kind of claim Goodhart's law is and to defend a diagnosis, not to list categories.

To give the question its teeth, build it around the following two pieces of apparatus, which I have been developing in lecture and which I want treated as the substance of the problem. Elaborate them as you see fit, but keep their core.

First, the counterfactually locked proxy. Suppose an engineer could construct a proxy P for a target T that is not merely correlated with T in the observed data but locked to it counterfactually: across every world reachable by any action the agent might take, P moves if and only if T moves, by a fixed monotone relation. The intuition is that such a proxy is ungameable, since no action can move the number without moving the thing. Candidates must decide whether this construction shows that Goodharting is entirely a matter of finite data and selection — the proxy fails only because our evidence about the P–T relation was thin — or whether a residual gap survives perfect counterfactual coupling, namely the gap between a representation and what it represents.

Second, the invariance test. A colleague proposes that a proxy is safe to target just in case it is invariant under the agent's full action set — that is, the agent cannot move it without moving the target. Candidates should assess whether this test is circular (the action set and the metric each seem to presuppose the other), too strong (no real metric survives, because action sets are open-ended and creativity expands them), or too weak (it certifies a proxy that a sufficiently inventive agent will later defeat), and they should connect their verdict to the locked-proxy case, which is its limiting instance.

The crux I want the question to reach: distinguish three diagnoses of Goodharting — a statistical diagnosis (the proxy's warrant was always an artifact of how the data were selected), a strategic or causal diagnosis (the agent finds a back-channel that moves the proxy without the target), and a semantic or representational diagnosis (once a measure becomes a target, its meaning becomes endogenous, so the same numeral no longer denotes the same quantity) — and ask which of these, if any, survives the locked-proxy thought experiment. The payoff should be a verdict on whether specification gaming is a remediable artifact of poor metric design or an inescapable feature of optimizing against any representation whatsoever.

Please produce, in this order: (1) the essay question itself, worded so that a well-prepared candidate could answer it in sixty minutes of writing and so that merely naming the standard categories earns almost nothing; (2) a model answer of roughly six hundred words that takes a definite position, works through the locked proxy and the invariance test, and shows where the three diagnoses come apart rather than merely defining them; (3) a marking scheme out of twenty marks, organized around five must-hit moves, with the marks for each and a one-line description of how candidates typically get that move wrong. The scheme must draw a line between an A answer and a B answer by naming the specific move — for instance, a treatment of the locked proxy that notices it is not an empirical possibility but a device for isolating the semantic question — that a B answer will miss. Include at least one false friend: a move that sounds sophisticated, such as declaring that the locked proxy simply dissolves the problem, and say why it should cost marks. The question must be fully self-contained: assume the candidate has no internet access, so it must not require citations, reading lists, or the names of particular published works, and it should not depend on any specific empirical study.
