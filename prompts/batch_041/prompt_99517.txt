I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, and the utility monster is the one staple of that world I have never been able to settle. I would like to set out my gut verdicts on a series of cases and ask what principle, if any, they add up to, which of the verdicts it forces me to abandon, and what it predicts about cases I have not considered.

(1) A being of otherwise ordinary psychology whose consumption of a human being produces in it more pleasure than any human life has ever contained. Its pleasure is caused by the victim's terror, and the human is used as the means. Do we feed it? No.

(2) The same being, except that its great pleasure comes from an activity that has a human death as an unavoidable side effect. It takes no interest in the death and would spare the human if it could have both. Do we still forbid the activity? Yes, but with markedly less confidence.

(3) A billion people each receive a brief, barely noticeable pleasure from a policy that subjects one person to a year of torture — a descendant of the old forum chestnut about torture and dust specks. No.

(4) One person is tortured for a year so that a billion people are each spared a year of torture. Yes — torture the one.

(5) A being that suffers more from any given loss than others do: losing a finger costs it a thousand times what it costs an ordinary person. It is about to lose a finger, and a hundred ordinary people are each about to lose a finger. I would relieve the hundred.

(6) A hive mind whose welfare is the sum of a trillion sub-agents of wholly ordinary capacities, so that it is a monster only in the arithmetic sense that its total dwarfs any individual's. Feed it a human? No; treating it as a single locus of utility feels like counting the same goods twice.

(7) A being whose good is defined over experiences of a kind we cannot have and cannot rank against our own, so that calling its utility larger presupposes a common scale that the case itself denies. I take the scenario to be ill-posed, with the default being not to sacrifice a person.

What I can extract on my own is this, and I doubt it survives contact: the numbers count, but only among goods of a comparable kind; a severe harm may be traded against preventing many severe harms, but never against any quantity of trivial goods; a being's sheer capacity for utility is irrelevant in itself; and a good constituted by another's suffering counts for nothing.

That principle seems to save (1), (3), (4), (6), and (7), but it appears to force me to drop (5) unless I add a clause I cannot formulate — something about which differences in sensitivity are admissible and which are not. Case (2) is the one I keep returning to, and I cannot tell whether my hesitation there marks a real distinction between intending a harm and merely foreseeing it or is just squeamishness dressed up as principle.

Please tell me what principle actually captures the pattern, or the smallest set of candidates if no single one does. State each clause precisely, identify the case that motivates it, say what it implies for cases I have not listed — for instance a monster whose utility is spent creating many future happy beings, or one that would rewrite itself into a non-monster if it expected to be fed — and say honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate.
