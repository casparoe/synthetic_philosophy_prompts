I am a third-year student enrolled in a course on classical Indian epistemology, and for the past year I have also been a student editor at our undergraduate research journal. During the submission cycle I read every manuscript and mark each empirical or interpretive claim with one of three verdicts — let stand, hedge, or strike — and then send the author a short note explaining each call. I see roughly eight manuscripts a month and have about fifteen minutes for each, I am not allowed to consult anything outside the text in front of me, and the faculty editor-in-chief has to ratify any strike. Right now I have no principle at all: I flag whatever sounds overstated, which is inconsistent from paper to paper and tells authors nothing about how to fix it.

The course has given me the raw material for something better, and I would like you to turn it into a procedure I can actually run. The pieces are these. Knowledge is said to arise from four sources (pramāṇas): perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. A cognition is presumed to be knowledge until something defeats it — a defeater (bādhaka) can overturn a claim that would otherwise stand, and the school's working default is closer to innocent until reasonably challenged than to guilty until proved. Doubt (saṃśaya) is a state in its own right, distinct from acceptance and from rejection, and it is meant to be worked through to ascertainment (nirṇaya) rather than left standing. A justification is properly set out in five parts: thesis, reason, example, application, conclusion. Inference fails in five characteristic ways — the reason may be inconclusive, because it turns up in cases where the conclusion fails as well (savyabhicāra); contradictory, because it establishes the opposite of what is wanted (viruddha); unproved, because the reason itself has not been established (asiddha); counterbalanced, because a reason of equal strength points the other way (satpratipakṣa); or nullified, because some other means of knowledge already contradicts the conclusion (bādhita). An inference can also be defeated by an upādhi, a further condition that was never stated and whose presence would make the reason fail to establish the conclusion. And hypothetical reasoning (tarka) — assuming the denial of a claim and seeing whether it collapses into absurdity — is the standard device for shaking a claim out of doubt.

Please write the procedure itself, in the order I would run it, with each step phrased as a question I can answer from the manuscript alone: which of the four sources the claim rests on, what has to hold for that source to deliver knowledge here, what would count as a defeater for it, where the procedure stops and I write strike, where it stops and I write hedge, and where it has to hand the decision to the faculty editor because what is at issue is a value or a disciplinary norm and not a fact. Be explicit about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, and about what I should do when a step cannot be answered at all.

Then run the procedure on three claims sitting on my desk.

(1) First-generation students in our sample report a weaker sense of belonging than continuing-generation students — from a paper reporting the authors' own survey: n = 47, convenience sample, a single self-report scale.
(2) Stereotype threat, as the literature has established, accounts for the persistence of the achievement gap — offered with no data of the authors' own.
(3) Average GPA fell in the two semesters after the mentoring program was introduced, so the program caused the decline — from a paper with no comparison group.

I believe (2) myself, and a friend of mine wrote the manuscript containing (3), so if the procedure condemns either of them I want to be told plainly rather than let off the hook.

Finally, I want one procedure with the thresholds set where you think they should be set, and defended, rather than a survey of the defensible options with the choice left to me. And where the Nyāya apparatus does not carry over — it was built for a solitary knower rather than a journal's collective verdict, and for perceptual contact rather than for statistics — say where the analogy breaks and what I should do at that point instead of pretending the translation went through.
