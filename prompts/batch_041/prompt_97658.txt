Last night a friend and I argued, for far longer than either of us intended, about a problem we could both feel but neither could name: that whenever we try to determine which of our actions does the most good, our reasoning gives out after a step or two, because the long-run consequences of what we do are effectively opaque to us. My friend treated this as a decisive objection to any morality built around doing the most good. If we cannot trace consequences, he argued, then the whole project is guesswork dressed up as rigor, and we would be better off falling back on ordinary rules — do not lie, do not harm, keep your promises — that do not require us to see around corners. I suspect his objection proves too much, but I could not say why, and I have been turning it over ever since.

The puzzle in this form, I gather, only came to be discussed long after the classical utilitarians were writing, which is precisely why I want to put it to one of them. Tell me how John Stuart Mill would most likely have answered my friend. Would he have conceded that our ignorance of remote consequences cripples the utilitarian project, or would he have found a way to absorb the objection — and if the latter, on what grounds? Please reason from what Mill actually held, rather than from a generic utilitarian template, and flag clearly where you are extrapolating past anything he said.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to predict the opposite about his view? And where is your reasoning most likely to fail? Keep the whole reply under 350 words.
