A stray cat has been hanging around the entrance of the flat I share with my flatmate, Dean, and the building manager has finally given us a deadline to sort it out. The lease allows us at most one pet, so at most one of us can take it in; otherwise it goes to a shelter. I have spent two weeks trying to work out the fair way to decide this, and I keep getting tangled up in my own reasoning. My own ranking of the three possibilities is: the cat goes to the shelter; then, if someone must keep it, I keep it; and last, Dean keeps it. The reason for that last place is not spite — Dean travels most weeks and is generally chaotic, and I am convinced the animal would end up neglected. Dean's ranking is nearly the reverse. He would most like me to take it in (he thinks a cat would do me good, that I work too much and could use the company), then he would take it himself, and he is genuinely upset by the idea of it going to the shelter.

When I laid this out for him, Dean made an argument that has been nagging at me. He said that we clearly agree on two things: first, that each of us should decide for himself or herself whether he or she takes the cat in, and second, that if both of us prefer one outcome to another, we should go with the outcome we both prefer. He then claimed that these two commitments, taken together with our actual preferences, are simply inconsistent — that no rule whatsoever for settling the question can honour both, and that this is a general result about collective decisions rather than a quirk of our cat. He is not a philosopher and he produced no argument beyond the assertion, so I cannot tell whether he has spotted something deep or just tied himself in knots. He is usually wrong about things like this, but this time the claim feels uncomfortably precise.

I would like you to tell me what is actually going on here. Is Dean right that the two commitments cannot both be kept? If so, which of them is doing the damage, and is there a principled way to decide the cat that does not simply amount to one of us overruling the other — or do we just have to accept that something has to give, and if so, what would a sensible person give up? I am also curious about a variation: I am not indifferent here, but suppose I truly did not care either way about whether Dean kept the cat, so that my only real feeling was about my own choice. Would the problem he describes still arise, or would it dissolve?
