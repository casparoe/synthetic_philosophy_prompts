I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on Maxwell's demon. We've all read the standard stuff (Szilard, Landauer, Bennett) and I don't want another summary of it. What I want is a decision tree that sorts the *resolutions* of the demon by which step of the argument they give up, not by who said them. The root should be a question a reader can answer without knowing the literature, e.g. "do you think the demon's own memory has to be treated as a physical system with an entropy of its own?" Then branch on the real fault lines: whether measurement itself is what costs (and whether that cost is physical or just bookkeeping); whether the demon has to end up uncorrelated with the gas, and what decorrelation costs; whether erasure is the essential step or whether you can keep deferring it (hand the record to a second demon, who hands it to a third...) until eventually somebody has to pay; and — the one I care most about — whether the resolution needs the demon's memory to be incompressible/random rather than merely finite. Add a branch for the minority who think Landauer's principle is a derived idealization rather than a law, and a leaf for "the demon is a red herring, the second law is statistical and fluctuations let you cheat for a while." Use the one-molecule Szilard engine and the two-bath demon as the concrete cases each question is asked against, and phrase every node so you can answer it without already knowing where it leads. Aim for about six branch nodes and six to eight leaves; name each leaf by the position (with a well-known label where one exists, 'unnamed' otherwise) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. At the end, flag any two leaves your tree can't actually separate — I suspect the erasure-resolution and the fluctuation-theorem resolution quietly collapse into each other, but tell me. And mark the combinations of answers that nobody occupies, with a guess at whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.
