I'm a novelist. My last book was about a family running a dying cinema in Ohio; the new one is about a woman in her mid fifties who gets an early-onset Alzheimer's diagnosis and, while she's still completely sharp, sits down and writes out instructions for what should happen to her later on. Not a legal form, more a long letter to her daughter plus something stiffer that she gets witnessed, because she watched her own mother go and she is not going to be parked in a home for a decade being spoon fed.

What I need from you is an example. A concrete case, the kind of thing I could actually stage in a chapter, where it's genuinely unclear whether the earlier self has any authority over the later one. Not the usual one — not the content demented lady in the garden who's forgotten she ever wrote anything and is now perfectly happy. I want a case where the later self can still register a want, can still say no to something and mean it, so the reader can't just wave her away as a body we're guessing about.

Here's the knot I keep writing toward. My character writes the directive knowing full well that the woman who has to read it will be a stranger to her, and she puts in a line that anticipates exactly this — she tells them, in advance, dont believe her later, dont listen when she begs. Which is either the most generous thing in the book or a kind of self-kidnapping, one self taking the other hostage. So I want an example where that structure actually bites, where following the letter of the earlier instruction and respecting the person actually sitting in the room pull in opposite directions, and where the conflict doesnt dissolve just by saying she's not really a self anymore or not competent, because in my version she is competent enough to refuse something and mean it.

Then tell me which way the example cuts, if it cuts at all. I keep hitting the same fork alot. Ground the directive in it being the same person all the way through and the later self has just as much standing as the earlier one, so its a tie and I've got no scene. Ground it in autonomy and only the earlier self was autonomous, so she wins — but she wins by disqualifying the only person still there to be asked. Is there a third route that isn't a fudge, something about the shape of a life taken as a whole, or about the directive being a promise to other people rather than to yourself?

And the thing that actually keeps me up: if the later self can revoke, then the directive never binds anyone and my whole book is a woman writing into the void. If she can't, she's a puppet and there's no story either. Where does the authority actually live in that standoff?

Don't bother with case law or a reading list, I'm not going to cite anything — one sharp example and your best shot at the philosophy is what I'm after.
