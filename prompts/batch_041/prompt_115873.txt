I'm putting together a small online interview series in philosophy of biology, mostly for grad students and a couple of postdocs, and next months guest is a philosopher who works on biological function. I'm not a philosopher by training—I just host the thing—so I might be mangling her view, but here's the gist. She defends a view she calls the stabilized contribution account. Roughly: a trait's function is the effect that explains why the trait is retained in a lineage, but retention needn't be natural selection—it can be developmental canalization or reliable social transmission, and she thinks drift-retained traits have no function. The effect also has to be a contribution to a larger system's persistence, not just any effect. She thinks this keeps the etiological account's historical condition while answering the causal-role theorist's complaint that functions are often just capacities in a system, and she claims it gives a better account of malfunction than either: a trait can malfunction when it stops making its stabilized contribution, even if it was never selected for. She also draws a distinction between a trait's retention profile (what actually kept it around) and its contribution profile (what it does now), and says function ascriptions can come apart from both.

Can you draft eight to ten interview questions for me? I want them to go beyond letting her restate the view. Please include at least one question each on: (1) what stabilized adds to selected, whether canalization- or transmission-based retention can really ground functions and malfunctions, and whether excluding drift is principled; (2) how her account handles a byproduct that later becomes useful, and whether a trait's function changes when the environment changes but the trait doesn't; (3) the indeterminacy problem—lots of effects are stabilized, so how does she pick out the function without circularity or a designer's intentions; (4) how she individuates the lineage and the trait across generations, especially for symbiotic or cultural inheritance; (5) the standard causal-role objection that her history is just evidence for a capacity, not constitutive of function; and (6) what empirical findings or cases would make her give up the view. For each question, add a follow-up that's keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and make the later questions harder to deflect with that's a different project or that's an empirical question. Also, if you can, include one question about a one-off somatic mutation that helps a cell survive but isn't inherited, one about whether synthetic organisms or first replicators can have functions on her view, and one about whether the account extends to ecological or ecosystem-level functions, where lineage retention gets murky. I'd like the questions to sound like a real interviewer who's read her work, not a textbook. No need for reading suggestions or citations—just the questions.
