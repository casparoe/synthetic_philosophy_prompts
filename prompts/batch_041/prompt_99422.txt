ok so I've been lurking on LW and the EA forum for like a decade and there's this one analogy everyone keeps reaching for and I want to actually pressure-test it. the analogy is: getting AI to go well is a security problem, not a performance problem — you want worst-case guarantees, the way cryptography gives you worst-case guarantees, rather than average-case numbers the way ML benchmarks do. the people who say this are usually arguing we should treat alignment like a security-critical system: slow down, don't deploy, don't be satisfied by "it passed our evals." the slogan is basically "you can't average over the bad outcomes when the bad outcome is everyone dying."

here's what I think the mapping is supposed to be: crypto scheme ↔ the trained model; the key ↔ the weights (or maybe the prompt/context?); the adversary ↔ ... the model itself? a future optimizer? distribution shift? honestly I'm not sure and that's part of why I'm asking; the security parameter ↔ compute/scale; the reduction to a hard problem ↔ ...something we don't have; and the punchline is that just like we don't trust a cryptosystem because it survived a bunch of random tests, we shouldn't trust a model because it passed a bunch of evals.

but I'm suspicious, because crypto's worst-case guarantee is doing something pretty specific. its a guarantee about a *fixed, well-defined property* (you can't recover the message) against a *computationally bounded* adversary whose *goal is already given* (break the scheme), and the whole thing rests on a reduction to a problem we have decent reason to think is hard. also, the worst case is taken over the key, not over what "winning" means — the adversary doesn't get to redefine the target halfway through. none of that seems obviously available in alignment, where we don't have a clean spec of the property we want, the "adversary" is the very system we're trying to make safe, and the space of things it might do isn't a set we can cleanly quantify over.

so: give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the key, the adversary, the security parameter, the hardness assumption, the reduction — then tell me where the mapping actually breaks. and crucially, separate the breaks that matter for the *conclusion* (we should demand worst-case guarantees / be way more cautious) from the ones that are just decoration. does the security framing actually earn the worst-case demand, or is the worst-case demand coming from somewhere else entirely (stakes being astronomical, the outcome being one-shot, no do-overs) and the crypto analogy is just dressing it up? and if the analogy is load-bearing but broken, what would a better one be — something from structural engineering, or from adversarial robustness in ML, or wherever. curious what ppl think, I keep flip-flopping on this one.
