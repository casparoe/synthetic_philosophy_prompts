I design capability-based operating systems, and my working vocabulary is processes, address spaces, unforgeable handles, sandboxes, interrupts, and audit logs. The design principle I care most about is the elimination of ambient authority: no process should be able to exercise a power over another's resources unless that power has been explicitly and revocably delegated to it. When I try to read political philosophy, freedom looks to me like the same idea — the set of state transitions an agent can actually cause, together with the set of handles that could stop it — and I suspect that at least some of the debate is about whether that idea is enough. I have no formal training in philosophy and cannot fill gaps from the literature, so work entirely inside what you can say in my terms.

I would like you to restate, one at a time and as faithfully as you can, the three conceptions of liberty that political philosophers distinguish: negative liberty as the absence of interference by other agents; positive liberty as self-mastery or self-realization, being governed by one's own will rather than by alien desires or external forces; and the republican or neo-Roman conception, on which freedom is non-domination — not being subject to another's arbitrary power whether or not that power is ever exercised.

For each conception, give the translation concretely in my vocabulary (name the objects, permissions, events, and authority relations you are mapping onto), then mark every point where the translation required a distortion or a stipulation I would not have made on my own, and finally state what cannot be expressed in my terms at all. Keep the translation honest: do not quietly recast a claim about what may be done to a person as a claim about what can be done to a process.

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want. Take the cases the philosophers use to pry the conceptions apart — the servant or slave whose master never in fact interferes; the person who is unobstructed but lives under a power that could remove his options at any moment and need not answer for doing so; and the divided self, said to be unfree because its own desires rule it — and say for each what my vocabulary can and cannot register. My own hunch is that republican non-domination is simply the moral analogue of my no-ambient-authority rule. Test that hunch rather than endorsing it: if it holds, say what is added by saying it of persons; if it fails, say precisely where the analogy breaks.

End with a verdict on the question I have been circling. If freedom is just the transitions an agent can cause plus the handles that could stop it, is anything lost? Is the residue a defect of my framework — a blindness to something real about standing, relationship, and what one agent owes another — or evidence that the philosophers have fused a status claim and a power claim into a single word and then mistaken the fusion for a distinction? Say which of the three conceptions survives translation most nearly intact, and which turns out to be doing no work that the other two do not already do.
