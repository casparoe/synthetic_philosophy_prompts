I write about philosophy and the mind for a general readership, and I have reached the point in a long piece where I have to commit to a thesis instead of surveying the field. The thesis is this. The Buddhist doctrine of no-self is not, at bottom, the claim that persons do not exist. It is the claim that the person is a construction — a designation laid over a causally connected stream of physical and mental events, in the way that a chariot is nothing over and above its parts in the right arrangement — and that once the stream and its continuity have been described, there is nothing left over for personal identity to consist in. On this reading the doctrine is not exotic metaphysics but a version of the reductionist position in the Western debate about personal identity, and its real bite is the further claim that identity is not what matters in survival.

I would rather find out now than in print. What would count against the thesis? Rank the objections from the ones that should sink it to the ones that are merely awkward, and say plainly which of them a committed defender would concede rather than deflect. I am especially interested in whether the reading collapses into a rival — into straightforward eliminativism about persons, which the tradition would presumably reject, or into a reductionism with nothing distinctively Buddhist left in it.

I also want to know whether the thesis predicts anything its rivals do not. If the self is a construction, the sense of self should be disruptible and variable: vulnerable to meditation, to depersonalization, to dissociation, to whatever rearranges the relevant processes. A substantial self would predict the opposite — an invariant core that no circumstance could loosen. Does that give the construction view a genuine empirical edge, or is every result from the clinic and the lab compatible with both? And consider a case I keep returning to: suppose a future neuroscience identified a single, indivisible, persisting entity whose continuity was necessary and sufficient for personal identity, and whose removal ended experience while leaving the causal stream otherwise intact. Would that refute the no-self reading, or would the tradition simply absorb it as one more aggregate? If the latter, what does that tell me about what kind of claim I am making?

One more thing I want you to sort out, because it looks like a problem for my version. The tradition's own analysis, as I understand it, does not stop at dissolving the person; it goes on to treat the momentary constituents of experience as ultimately real. That looks less like eliminating the self than relocating it. If the reductionist reading is correct, why did the tradition do that, and does it damage the thesis I want to defend?

Finally, the question I am most afraid of: is this thesis falsifiable at all, or does the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth let it survive any evidence whatever? If it turns out to be insulated, tell me whether that is a defect in the thesis or a feature of the kind of claim it is. What would move me most is a case in which the reductionist verdict collides with a moral or prudential judgment I cannot give up — something like the conviction that I have a special reason to care about my own future that no mere continuity of processes could ground. I do not need a literature review; I need the map of where this position is exposed.
