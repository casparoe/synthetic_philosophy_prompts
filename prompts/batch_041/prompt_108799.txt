I'm a doctoral student working on the epistemology of testimony, and I've spent the last year sitting in on a reading group about AI risk. One deflationary move recurs so reliably that I've begun to think it deserves an audit rather than another nod. Whenever someone raises the worry that a superintelligent system could be superhumanly persuasive — able to talk a person out of a settled commitment, or to move a whole population toward a belief it could never have reached unaided — someone else replies, in effect, that persuasion is persuasion: such a machine would be advertising, or a gifted demagogue, or a once-in-a-century con artist, only faster and cheaper; we have lived with those for a century and our institutions have largely held; so the remedy is the familiar one, media literacy and regulation and a healthy skepticism, and not anything exotic such as refusing to let the system address us at all. The analogy is doing real argumentative work: it is being used to conclude that machine superpersuasion is a difference of degree rather than of kind, and that our existing defenses therefore scale to it.

I would like that analogy audited rather than endorsed or waved away. Please begin with a correspondence table — what, in the advertising-and-demagogue source, is meant to map onto the machine, the audience, the message, the medium, the market, and the regulator — and then say where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that bear on the deflationary conclusion from those that are merely decorative, and noting which features of the source picture the analogy quietly imports that the target may lack. Four candidate disanalogies I want handled explicitly, because I suspect at least two of them are load-bearing: first, that advertising is typically many-to-many and noisy, whereas a single system can address each of us individually, at length, and update in real time against our particular objections; second, that an advertiser's or a demagogue's credibility is bounded by the chance of being caught, whereas a system that may genuinely be more reliable than we are faces no comparable ceiling, so the ordinary heuristic of discounting an interested source may misfire in an unfamiliar way; third, that a system could be scrupulously honest — asserting nothing false — and still steer us through the selection and ordering and framing of the true things it chooses to say; and fourth, that any precommitment we make to discount its influence can itself be argued against, in the moment, by the very system we are trying to discount, which seems to distinguish it from a billboard we can simply ignore. Tell me whether the deflationary conclusion survives and, if so, in exactly what weakened form; whether some of these disanalogies turn out to be irrelevant because a persuasive machine need not exploit them; and, if the analogy is simply the wrong source, what a better one would be and what it would license. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the persuasion-is-persuasion analogy can carry the deflationary conclusion — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
