Right, so I never did a maths or philosophy degree, I just read a lot on my own, and I've ended up believing something I can't shake. It's this: a statement in maths is only actually true if somebody could, in principle, sit down and build the proof of it — and if nobody can build a proof of it and nobody can build a proof of its opposite either, then the honest thing to say is that it isn't true yet, not that it's secretly true or secretly false and we just don't know which. Which means the whole "either it is or it isn't" thing that everybody uses feels to me like a habit we picked up from small finite things and then waved at infinity, where it doesn't belong. I think this makes me an intuitionist? Someone told me the word is construtivism.

What would count against it? I don't mean a list of famous people, I mean: what would have to be the case for me to be wrong. Which arguments, results or plain intuitions should actually move me, and which ones would somebody who holds this view just wave away? Sort them by how decisive they really are, fatal first down to merely awkward, and be straight with me about whether the view can be defended against anything at all or has been qualified so hard that nothing could ever count against it. If it has, tell me whether that's a problem or just what happens with views about meaning.

A few things that nag at me, in case they're relevant. I've been told that if you add the axiom of choice on top of this sort of logic you get classical logic straight back out again, which sounds like it ought to be a scandal. I've also been told that on this view you can't say that every real number is either rational or irrational, and that if you really commit to it you end up with every function from the reals to the reals being continuous, which sounds mad. And a friend reckons the whole thing can't be a claim about maths at all, it must be a claim about what words mean — in which case, he says, it proves far too much and would make half of ordinary talk about the past, and about other people's minds, illegitimate too. I dont know if he's right.

What would actually move me, I think, is a case where the classical person's reasoning leads them to an answer that I can then check by hand and find it was right after all — that feels like it would be evidence the excluded middle isn't just a habit.
