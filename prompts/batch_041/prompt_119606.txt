I spent thirty-three years as a medical physicist — magnetic resonance and radiotherapy dosimetry — and retired four years ago. Since then I have been reading moral philosophy with more appetite than discipline, and for the past year I have sat as the scientist member on the AI oversight committee of the eight-hospital system where I did my clinical work. Our meeting is on the fourteenth and I must circulate a written opinion beforehand, so I am asking you to draft it.

The question before us is narrow, and I am told it is urgent. The system is deploying a machine-learning agent that will allocate intensive-care beds across its hospitals during surges and, in the same workflow, draft the messages sent to patients' families. The vendor offers two configurations, and we must choose one before the next surge, which our epidemiologists expect within about six weeks.

The outcome configuration scores every candidate for a bed by expected benefit — predicted survival, predicted function at discharge, and a few other terms — and assigns beds so as to maximize the total. It may prefer a patient with better prospects to one with worse, may move a bed already promised if a stronger candidate appears, and may soften the clinical picture in a family's message when the notes suggest that the plain account would prompt a refusal of care that would predictably end badly.

The duty configuration obliges us to write a short list of rules the agent may not violate: no consideration of disability or of ability to pay; first come, first served within a severity band; no revocation of an allocation once granted; and a complete account to families, whatever follows. Within those rules it optimizes as before.

What I cannot get past is something the vendor's engineer told us, almost as an aside, when I asked how the second configuration differs from the first in the code: a hard constraint, he said, is a weight you are not willing to trade, so the duty configuration is the outcome configuration with very large penalty terms attached to the forbidden outcomes. If that is right, my colleagues who describe this vote as a choice between building a utilitarian agent and building a deontological one are describing something that is not there, and we are really only choosing a set of numbers. My colleague in critical care, who has worked two surges, disagrees. She says the difference lies not in the arithmetic but in what the agent will and will not lay before a human being: a rule held as a wall means the agent never surfaces the case in which the wall costs, say, a dozen lives, while a consequentialist agent surfaces it and lets a person decide. She may have dissolved my question, or she may only have renamed it.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend one configuration, or a hybrid if a hybrid can be specified precisely enough that a night-shift attending could apply it without convening anyone, and it should state the strongest case for whatever I end up not recommending. I would like it to take two things seriously rather than gesture at them. First, whether anything of the distinction between the two configurations survives its implementation — if every duty can be written as a price, is the choice anything more than a choice of exchange rates? Second, whether a system whose stated reasons are composed after the fact can be said to reason in either framework at all, given that our clinicians will read its explanations and, I expect, come to trust them.

The memo goes to eleven other members: three clinicians, our general counsel, and four community members, none of whom will read past the first page unless the first page earns it. No citations, please — I will not be able to check them, and neither, in the relevant sense, will the committee. I want your argument, and a bottom line we can vote on.
