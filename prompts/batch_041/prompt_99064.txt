I have recently begun sitting with a small Buddhist study group, largely because of a difficult year, and I have found myself trying to reverse-engineer what one of the older members — a woman I will call R., whose judgments I have come to trust — actually holds about karma as a matter of desert. What she has said about the cases we have discussed:

- A surgeon performs a technically flawless operation, intending only to heal, and the patient dies of a condition the operation did not cause: R. says the surgeon incurs no karmic debt, though the death is a misfortune.
- A driver glances at a phone, means no harm, and kills a pedestrian: R. says this does incur karmic debt; carelessness counts even where malice is absent.
- Someone privately wishes a rival dead but does nothing: R. says no debt attaches to the wish alone.
- Someone gives anonymously to a charity that is in fact a front for harm, when a few minutes of checking would have shown this: R. says the giver incurs debt, because they failed to find out what they were supporting.

The case I now turn over is my own. Last spring I urged my mother to move into a care home, having looked into it carefully and with nothing but her good in view. She fell there, broke a hip, and died within the year. Nothing about the fall was foreseeable from where I stood.

What I want from you is not really a yes or no. I want the rule R. appears to be running; the other rules that fit her four verdicts just as well; what each of those rules predicts about my case; which rule you would bet she is actually running, and why; and the further case that would best separate the rules that survive. Please also be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who genuinely know the Buddhist traditions and which are contested — whether karma is best understood as a causal law or as a ledger, whether what matters is the agent's knowledge or their causal contribution, whether intention is required at all — and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
