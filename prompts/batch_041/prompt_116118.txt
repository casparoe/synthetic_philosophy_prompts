I spent thirty-five years as a physical chemist and have spent the decade since my retirement teaching myself epistemology. Next month I am presenting a paper at a philosophy department colloquium, and what I need from you is a question-and-answer preparation sheet, not encouragement.

The position I will defend is this. Nyāya epistemology draws a distinction that contemporary analytic epistemology has no counterpart for. It separates the warrant for a particular inference — the reason's genuinely belonging to the subject under discussion, pakṣadharmatā — from the warrant for the general connection the inference runs on, vyāpti. It then insists that vyāpti is never established by inference alone, and that what establishes it is tarka: hypothetical reasoning to the effect that if the connection failed, something impossible would follow. And it classifies tarka as not a pramāṇa at all. My claim is that this is not a taxonomic embarrassment but a considered solution to the rule-circularity problem. Tarka confers no warrant of its own, yet it is what makes the warrant-conferring relation available for use. I argue that this occupies a space neither reliabilism nor evidentialism can describe, and that Nyāya's requirement that a cognition be unsublated, abādhita, is a fourth condition of knowledge that gets right what the Gettier literature got wrong. Along the way I lean on two further Nyāya commitments: that inference has five members rather than two, and that perception has a non-conceptual stage, nirvikalpaka, prior to any predication. Nyāya recognizes four means of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony — and my paper concerns inference alone.

The audience is known to me. Roughly eight analytic epistemologists, mostly reliabilists, with one virtue epistemologist; two social epistemologists who work on testimony and disagree with each other about whether testimony is a basic source of knowledge; one Bayesian; one philosopher of science; and a single historian of Indian philosophy who reads Navya-Nyāya in Sanskrit and considers analytic reconstruction of it a category mistake. My host warns me that the department is skeptical of comparative philosophy done by outsiders.

I want the following. First, the eight objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they do rather than by the order in which they will be raised. Second, each phrased in the vocabulary of the person who will raise it, attributed to that person, and not softened into something more tractable. Third, for each objection, a two-minute spoken reply and one sentence on where that reply is weakest. Fourth, a short list of what I should concede at the outset, before anyone asks. Fifth, your judgment on which objections are fatal and which are merely parochial. Sixth, the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and what I would say if it comes.

Three cautions. The historian's objection is unlike the others: it will be about anachronism, and no additional argument of mine can answer it, so tell me what does. The social epistemologists will notice that my thesis is silent about testimony, which Nyāya counts as a full pramāṇa in its own right, and the historian will notice that I say nothing about upamāna, the means of knowledge that runs through comparison with a known paradigm; decide whether those silences are gaps I should fill or scope limits I should declare, and tell me which. And do not send me reading suggestions — I have two weeks and a paper to give.
