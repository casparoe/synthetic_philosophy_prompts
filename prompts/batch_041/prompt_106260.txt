Alfred North Whitehead described education as a rhythm that moves through romance, precision, and generalization, and he reserved his sharpest criticism for what he called inert ideas—knowledge received but never put to use, tested, or recombined. Suppose he were alive today and asked to pass judgment on a now-fashionable aim: teaching critical thinking, creativity, and similar generic skills as subjects in their own right, detached from any particular discipline. Write the position he would defend. Take a definite side and give your reasons, rather than cataloguing the verdicts he might have reached; ground each step in his own doctrines—the rhythm of learning, his attack on inert ideas, the demand that knowledge be utilized, and his insistence that mental training cannot be pried apart from the content being learned. Where those doctrines pull against one another, name the tension and then still commit to a verdict.
