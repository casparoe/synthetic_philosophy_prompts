I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about a decade now, and I've drafted a post on accuracy-first epistemology that I want torn apart before I put it anywhere public. The target is the package that says the sole fundamental epistemic good is accuracy, meaning the closeness of one's credences to the truth, and that the familiar norms (probabilism, conditionalization, and their relatives) are vindicated because credence functions that violate them are accuracy-dominated, or because conforming to them maximizes expected accuracy. My draft grants every formal result in that literature and tries to press a distinction I can't shake. This started as an argument in a group chat with a friend who thinks any credence you would have endorsed in advance is fine no matter how it got there, and I've been unable to settle whether he's right or whether I'm just attached to a picture. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of epistemology with a working knowledge of formal epistemology and epistemic utility theory, read the draft the way a referee would, and then tell me where it is confused, where it is unoriginal, and what would have to be true for it to land. Please think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming the two or three strongest replies an accuracy-firster would make, so that your comments aren't just the first objection that comes to mind.

The draft, roughly as it stands:

Start with two agents sharing an agenda, a time, and a body of evidence. Call them Careful and Coin. Both assign credence 0.7 to the proposition that it will rain tomorrow, and it does rain. Careful arrived at 0.7 by integrating everything she knows. Coin arrived at 0.7 by consulting a device she has no reason to trust and that would have said 0.7 whatever the evidence had been. Their credence functions are numerically identical, so every strictly proper scoring rule awards them identical scores. Accuracy-first epistemology, taken at face value, says they are epistemically on a par: same credences, same accuracy, same standing. But Coin's credence seems defective in a way no score can register, because she isn't tracking anything. Now generalize. If what we evaluate is an occurrent credence state, then an agent whose credences are arbitrary but numerically lucky is epistemically flawless at every moment, and the difference between Careful and Coin appears nowhere in the theory. If instead what we evaluate is the disposition or policy that produces credences, then the fundamental object of epistemic evaluation is a rule rather than a state, and the accuracy of a rule can only be its accuracy across the circumstances it might have faced, which requires a measure over those circumstances. That is a prior over evidence streams. So here is the dilemma I want to put to the accuracy-firster: evaluate states and you cannot see responsiveness to evidence at all; evaluate policies and you need precisely the privileged prior, or privileged class of priors, that accuracy-first epistemology was advertised as letting us do without. I take the second horn to be the interesting one, but I would rather keep the first horn's verdict than give up the thought that Coin is doing something worse than Careful, and I can't tell whether that preference is principled or just squeamishness.

What I want from you: first, is the dilemma exhaustive, or is there a third option that locates the difference between Careful and Coin somewhere other than the state and the policy, and if so, what does it cost? Second, if the second horn is right, does the standard move of showing that one policy accuracy-dominates another relative to every prior rescue accuracy-first epistemology from needing a privileged prior, or does that move only work for the norms it was built for and not for the responsiveness intuition I'm trying to capture? Third, is my thought experiment rigged — for instance, is the numerical identity of the two credence functions doing work a fairer version wouldn't allow, and how would you rebuild it so that Careful and Coin are indistinguishable at every moment yet differ in counterfactual robustness? Fourth, assuming the core survives, is this a post or a comment: which parts are doing real work and which should be cut? Be blunt about the parts that are just the state-versus-rule distinction with extra steps, since I would rather find that out now than in the replies.
