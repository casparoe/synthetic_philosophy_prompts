I am preparing an essay for an edited volume on the boundary problem in democratic theory, and I would like your comments on the argument rather than on the prose. Since you have no access to the literature, let me be explicit about what I am asking for: not citations, not reading suggestions, not an inventory of who has said what. I know the field. What I need is an interlocutor who will argue with the substance, including telling me if the substance is thin.

The thesis is that what we call the boundary problem is really three problems run together, and that nearly every proposed solution answers one of them while quietly presupposing answers to the other two.

1. The constitutive problem. There is no demos prior to the decision that establishes it, so any founding vote already presupposes the membership it is supposed to create. This is the version that gets all the attention, usually as a paradox to be admired rather than solved.

2. The delimitation problem. Given a territory, an institution, or a decision, which persons are inside? Here the candidates are familiar: those affected by the decision, those subjected to its enforcement, those born into the jurisdiction, those who share a political culture, those who will live with the consequences. I argue that these criteria are not rival answers to one question but answers to different questions, and that the debate has been confused by treating them as competitors on a single axis.

3. The revision problem. Once a demos exists, by what right may it alter its own membership, given that the initial exclusion it inherits was almost certainly arbitrary? Naturalization rules, denaturalization, secession, the admission of new member states, and the enfranchisement of long-resident non-citizens are all instances of this problem, and I claim it is neither the constitutive nor the delimitation problem in disguise.

Two consequences I draw from the distinction. First, the standard objections misfire. The unboundedness objection — that if anyone affected counts, everyone counts, since any decision ripples somewhere — is fatal only to a delimitation answer that refuses to specify a threshold; it leaves the constitutive problem untouched. The arbitrariness objection — that birthright jurisdiction is a morally arbitrary line — is fatal only to an answer to the revision problem that treats inherited membership as self-justifying. Second, the European case, where it is often said that no transnational demos exists and therefore the question of a democratic deficit cannot arise, looks different once the three problems are separated: it is a failure to answer the delimitation problem, presented as a solution to the constitutive one.

The constructive proposal, which is the part I am least sure of, is what I call domain-indexed membership. Instead of one demos per territory, the demos is constituted per decision and contains (i) everyone subject to that decision's enforcement and (ii) everyone bearing a material share of its costs or benefits, where material share is fixed by a stipulated threshold — for instance, a burden exceeding what the median resident of the affected area bears. Membership is therefore revisable as decisions change, and there is no single body that is the demos for all purposes. My illustration is a cross-border metropolitan area with one labor market, one commuter railway, and one environmental regulator, in which a decision about a smelter falls on residents of three municipalities in two countries. I argue the demos for that decision is the set satisfying (i) and (ii), that it is not the same set as the municipal electorates, and that this is a virtue rather than an embarrassment.

I already attempt replies to four objections: that the threshold merely relocates the arbitrariness it was meant to remove; that an ephemeral demos destroys the accountability of representatives, who will never face the same electorate twice; that the proposal makes every decision a constitutional moment; and that a shifting demos cannot sustain the solidarity that redistribution requires.

What I want from you:

(a) Is the threefold distinction exhaustive and non-overlapping, or is the revision problem simply the delimitation problem considered over time, and the constitutive problem not a distinct problem at all but a rhetorical point about founding moments?

(b) Does domain-indexed membership collapse into the all-affected-interests principle as soon as the threshold is specified — that is, have I merely relabeled the position I spend the first half of the essay criticizing?

(c) Is the smelter case doing real work, or is it decoration that could be swapped for any other case without loss? If the latter, tell me what a case would have to do to earn its place.

(d) Of my four objections, which is strongest, and is my reply to it a reply or a restatement in different words?

(e) The strongest objection I have not considered, phrased as the person who would make it would phrase it — someone whose commitments are not mine.

(f) Bluntly, which section is the least interesting, and would the essay be better if I cut it and let the remaining argument stand on fewer moving parts?

If you think the whole framing is misguided, say so and tell me what you would put in its place; a reasoned rejection is more useful to me than a list of refinements.
