I report on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and much of what I write about now falls under the loose heading of cancel culture: a scientist dropped from a conference after a petition, a writer's invitation rescinded, a scholar's fellowship quietly not renewed. I have read a great deal of argument about whether this or that episode was fair, and almost none about what kind of act the response was. Next month I am leading a ninety-minute session at a professional workshop for working journalists — fifteen people, none of them trained in philosophy, all of them wary of being lectured to — and I want them to feel, rather than be told, that a diffuse social sanction is a punishment, and that its diffuseness is precisely what makes it hard to justify.

Design the activity. It should be a staged deliberation or simulation with real choices and small stakes rather than a debate, and it should specify roles, materials, the sequence of rounds within the ninety minutes, and the debrief. No framing talk at the top: the point should arrive through the mechanics, and I would rather not name it until the debrief.

The mechanic I care about most is this — the sanction must not be chosen by anyone. Each participant should make a run of small, individually defensible decisions (whether to sign, forward, reply, attend, withdraw a name), and only at the end should the group see that their separate choices have composed into a real penalty that no one authored and no one could have prevented alone. They should leave that moment feeling both that they did something and that none of them did it.

The debrief should map particular moments in the activity onto the distinctions I want them to carry away: between the censure a sanction expresses and the hard treatment it inflicts; between withdrawing association and inflicting a penalty; the requirement that punishment have an author, a procedure, and a route of appeal, and what becomes of a penalty that has none of these; and the problem of proportionality when no one is empowered to set the scale.

Tell me what will predictably misfire the first time I run it — I expect someone to insist that this is only criticism and not punishment, and someone else to say I have stacked the deck — and how to handle both without lapsing into a lecture. Then be frank about which part of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply myself. My own suspicion is that it can establish that a penalty was imposed but not that it was undeserved, since a punishment can be deserved and still lack a legitimate author; tell me if that is right, and what I have to say aloud to close the gap.

Keep the whole design to a maximum of 850 words.
