For years now I've been the sort of person who reads decision-theory and free-will threads on the usual blogs and forums well past the point of diminishing returns, and the puzzle I keep circling back to is the old one usually credited to Buridan: a donkey set exactly between two identical bales of hay, equally hungry, equally far from each, and — this is the part everyone skips — perfectly rational, who starves because nothing in his situation gives him a reason to prefer one bale to the other. What I find genuinely in the weeds here isn't the starving; everyone knows the starving. It's the argument underneath it. One reading treats the ass as a reductio of choice without preference: a preference is just what a choice expresses, so choosing when nothing is preferred is incoherent, and the poor creature is simply obeying logic. A second reading — the one I've come to think is stronger, and the one I'd like the poem to take seriously — says the death indicts not reason but the missing tie-breaker: under an exact tie no option is better than any other, so taking either one, or delegating the pick to a coin or to whichever bale sits a hair nearer his left eye, is rationally permitted, and what fails is the breaking of symmetry, not the canons of rationality. And the medievals, as I understand it, had a third thing in view entirely — whether the will can act at all when the intellect presents two options as exactly equal. I'd like all three of those pressures to be felt in the poem, without its settling on any one of them.

So here is what I want. A villanelle, spoken in the ass's voice, in which the two refrains are mirror images of each other — the same proposition with the two bales swapped — so that the whole poem is invariant under exchanging left for right, and a reader who knows the problem can see that the two refrains are not two claims but one symmetry wearing two coats. Let the form do what the situation does: the refrains circling, trading places, and finally meeting side by side in the closing quatrain, which should be the spot where the symmetry either holds (and he dies of it) or breaks (and something arbitrary — a coin, a twitch, a breath — does what no reason could). I don't want a tidy moral at the end; I want the reader to feel why a creature with nothing to prefer might still be unable to move, and why that might be a defect in the situation rather than in the creature.

Keep it accurate enough that someone who has actually thought about indifference and tie-breaking won't wince — no invented history, no fake quotations, no leaning on names or dates — and let the verse stand on its own for a reader who has never heard of the donkey. Please close with a single line beginning "Summary:" that states in one sentence what the poem's form is doing with the symmetry.
