A colleague who teaches a philosophy of biology unit on information in biology has asked me to help grade midterms, and I would like a second opinion on one script. Below I have reproduced the lecture handout the students were given, the exam question, and the student's answer. Please grade the answer out of 12 and give me the specific comments that justify the grade, rather than a general impression of its quality.

Lecture handout (reproduced as the students received it)

The handout distinguished three claims that the slogan that DNA is a code can be used to make, each with its own success condition.

(1) The cipher claim: there is an arbitrary mapping between symbols (codons) and what they stand for (amino acids), so that a sequence can be said to stand for a product in something like the way a coded message stands for its decoded content. Success condition: the mapping is not fixed by physical necessity, and the symbol neither resembles nor physically necessitates what it stands for.

(2) The instruction claim: a sequence specifies a procedure, as a recipe specifies a procedure to a cook. Success condition: the sequence together with the reading machinery determines an ordered process, and the order of the steps, and not only the final product, is carried by the representation.

(3) The blueprint claim: the representation stands in a structural correspondence to the thing represented — parts of the representation correspond to parts of the product and preserve their relative arrangement, so that the product can be read off the representation.

The handout added two warnings: causal specificity, the degree to which a factor makes a difference to an outcome, is not by itself information in any semantic sense; and an arbitrary mapping need not be a conventional one, nor is arbitrariness settled by how widely the mapping happens to be shared.

Exam question (12 points)

Assess the claim that DNA is a code. Say which of (1), (2), and (3) the genetic system satisfies, and on what grounds, and then say whether either of the following follows from your answer: (i) that genes carry information about the proteins and traits they produce, in a sense strong enough to make the phrase 'gene for X' more than shorthand for a causal contribution; (ii) that genes are the locus of developmental information, so that other causal factors supply materials and triggers but not information. [3 points for (1), 3 points for (2), 2 points for (3), 4 points for the application to (i) and (ii).]

Student's answer

The three claims come apart, and I will argue that the genetic system satisfies the first only in a weakened form and fails the second and the third.

(1) The cipher claim. The mapping from codons to amino acids is not forced by chemistry. Nothing about the physical structure of the codon UUU makes it necessary that it specify phenylalanine, and several different codons specify the same amino acid. So the mapping is arbitrary in the sense the handout requires, and the codon is a symbol rather than a physical cause of the amino acid it stands for. One might object that the code is nearly universal across living things and therefore cannot be arbitrary, but universality is a fact about shared descent, not about necessity, so the objection fails. The complication is degeneracy. Because several codons map to the same amino acid, the mapping cannot be run backwards: nobody could recover the sequence from the protein. A symbol system in which one message admits many encodings, and in which the encoding cannot be recovered from the message, is not a cipher in the strict sense. I therefore treat (1) as satisfied only in a weakened form — enough to license talk of codons as symbols, not enough to license talk of sequences standing for products.

(2) The instruction claim. This is where the slogan does its real work, and it fails. A recipe specifies an ordered procedure; the sequence specifies a product. Nothing in the DNA says that the chain should fold this way rather than that, or that the steps of synthesis should occur in one order rather than another; that ordering is supplied by the ribosome, by chaperones, and by the surrounding cell. So the sequence is a template, not a set of instructions, and (2) fails.

(3) The blueprint claim. This fails for the familiar reasons. Proteins are not assembled from a map, there is no part-for-part correspondence between stretches of DNA and parts of the folded protein, and one and the same sequence can yield different structures in different cellular contexts.

(i) and (ii). Not much follows. If a sequence carries no representational content of its own, then 'gene for X' is at best shorthand for a causal contribution, and there is no distinctively informational role left for genes to play. The same reasoning disposes of (ii): if the sequence carries no information, then no factor is the locus of developmental information, and the question simply lapses. One might reach for the central dogma here, on the grounds that it makes genes the sole source of sequence information, but that would be a mistake: the central dogma is a claim about the direction in which sequence information can flow between nucleic acid and protein, not a claim about where developmental information resides. So neither (i) nor (ii) follows, and the slogan that DNA is a code should be retired in favor of talk about causal contributions.
