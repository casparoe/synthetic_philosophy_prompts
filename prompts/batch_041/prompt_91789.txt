So I've been reading LW and SSC and the various rationalist-adjacent blogs for like fifteen years at this point, and my favorite move is still when someone takes a question that sounds dumb — what makes every 'a' an 'a'? — and it turns out to have six or seven genuinely different answers hiding under it, and the disagreement is real rather than verbal. I want to make a quiz out of that for my blog, the good kind of quiz, where it sorts you by what you say about concrete cases instead of by which label you'd pick off a shelf.

Some background so you know what I'm chasing: there was this old Hofstadter project, Letter Spirit, where a program designs all 26 lowercase letters in one coherent style, and the whole thing hinges on a tension between two pressures — each letter has to still read as the letter it's supposed to be, and it has to feel like it belongs to the same family as the others. I half-remember it designing a 't' with the crossbar suppressed, getting something that reads as an 'l', and then compromising. That tension is exactly the 'a' question: is the a-ness in the shape, in the system of contrasts, in our visual system, in the community of readers, in the generative rule, or nowhere at all. Also, I'm using this quiz on people who've never heard of Hofstadter, so nothing can depend on them knowing any of that.

What I want: eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto named positions. At minimum the following have to come out as leaves: letter identity as a geometric/topological property of the figure itself; identity as typicality or graded closeness to a prototype; the family-resemblance/no-definition view where there are no necessary and sufficient features; identity as the slot a shape occupies in a system of mutually distinguishing letters; identity as whatever a community of readers reads it as; identity as what a coherent style-generator produces, so that the letter and the style get fixed together; and the deflationary option where there's no fact of the matter and the category is just a tool. Build the tree from the logical space first — figure out what questions actually split the positions, then drop the names into the slots. If a combination of answers has nobody in it, say so and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If two leaves come out indistinguishable on your questions, flag that instead of papering over it.

Cases I'd like in there, use these and add your own if they earn their place: a Futura-style single-storey 'a' next to a double-storey one from some old serif face, which share basically no geometry; a mirrored and a 180-rotated 'a'; a glyph halfway between 'a' and 'o'; the exact same shape sitting in two different fonts, one of which has an 'o' designed close enough that the shape is only unambiguous by contrast; a font where the designer swaps the roles of the 'a' and 'e' shapes; a Cyrillic 'а' or a shape used as a variable in an equation; a squiggle nobody can identify alone that everyone reads as 'a' inside the word hand; an 'a' that has only ever been specified by a style rule and never drawn; and a case where a classifier says 99% 'a' and every human reader says 'e'. Each leaf should get one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Two constraints. No question may be answerable by asking whether the person thinks letters are arbitrary — that tells me nothing. And no citations needed, I don't care who said what, I care about the diagnostic questions.
