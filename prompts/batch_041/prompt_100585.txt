Last night a friend and I spent several hours arguing about Aristotle on natural slavery, and I have come away with a question I cannot settle on my own. My friend's position is that the doctrine is not worth the trouble of refuting. Aristotle, he says, defines the natural slave by a deficiency of reason; no human population lacks reason; therefore the doctrine is the prejudice of its time dressed in teleological language. He then offered a thought experiment that he clearly took to be decisive. Imagine, he said, a person who cannot deliberate about his own good — who cannot form and pursue a plan of his own, or weigh one way of life against another — but who understands instructions perfectly, carries them out reliably, and is content under direction. Call him the steerable man. My friend's claim is that Aristotle is committed to calling this man a natural slave, and that this is a reductio: the only figure who fits the definition is a figure constructed to fit it, so the appeal to nature is doing no work at all. He summed up by saying that Aristotle had described a role with nobody to fill it.

I tried two replies and neither landed. First I said the doctrine is simply false — the people actually held in slavery in Aristotle's world were not like that, and the institution he was defending was not populated by his supposed natural slaves. He said that this concedes his point. Then I said the doctrine is incoherent rather than false: a slave has to understand and obey commands, and understanding a command is already a use of reason, so a being defined as lacking reason could never be a slave in the first place. He said this is the same objection in different clothing, and that between the two the doctrine is finished. I also mentioned that Aristotle appears to separate slavery by nature from slavery by law or by conquest, and even to allow that the two come apart in practice, so that those actually enslaved need not be natural slaves; he replied, not unreasonably, that this only shows the natural slave to be a role nobody occupies.

So my question is this. I take the options to be these: either the natural slave lacks reason, in which case nobody answers to the description and the doctrine is empty; or he does not lack reason, in which case he is not a slave in Aristotle's sense at all. Is there a third possibility? If the deficiency is supposed to be something narrower than a lack of reason, does my friend's steerable man refute the doctrine or illustrate it — because if he illustrates it, I do not see what is left to object to, and if he refutes it, I do not see how, given that he seems to satisfy the description exactly. What I most want to know is where the real weakness lies: in the descriptive claim about what some people are like, in the step from being unable to deliberate for oneself to being justly owned by another, or somewhere else in the argument entirely.
