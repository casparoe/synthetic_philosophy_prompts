For a second-year epistemology seminar (fourteen students, one ninety-minute session) I want an in-class game that makes the class feel the dogmatism paradox in their bones rather than merely hear it stated. The paradox I have in mind, which I won't name until the debrief, turns on three moves: you know that P; you also know that if P is true then any evidence appearing to show not-P must be misleading; so when such evidence arrives you may conclude it is misleading and go on believing P. The sting is that nothing in the reasoning is special to P, so it threatens to make every item of knowledge permanently immune to revision — a conclusion no one accepts.

Please design the activity so that students execute this reasoning themselves and feel its pull: ideally the rules should reward the dismissive move, so that it feels not merely permitted but positively sensible, and only afterwards should they see that the same move, applied without favour, would shield any belief whatever. Give me the setting and how the room is arranged; the group size and roles; the materials; the rules and the scoring scheme (no real money — points or tokens of credit are fine); a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; and a debrief that maps what happened at each stage onto the steps of the paradox. One concrete suggestion you may use or discard: give each student a proposition they have established for themselves and can therefore be said to know, and hand them a charter or rulebook containing an innocent-looking permission to treat counter-evidence to a known proposition as misleading; then have evidence arrive, some of it planted and misleading, at least one piece of it genuine. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it, and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce — I take it the game can dramatise the felt reasonableness of the dismissal and the dogmatic cost of applying it consistently, but cannot by itself settle whether the paradox's principle really follows from the nature of knowledge, or adjudicate the standard responses to it (denying that we know the counter-evidence is misleading; distinguishing knowledge from certainty; holding that knowledge is defeasible). Please write the answer in British English.
