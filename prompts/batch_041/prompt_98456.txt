I am trying to state the principle behind my own judgments about a parliamentary monarchy, and every formulation I attempt either over- or under-generates. Here are the cases and my verdicts; I would like to know what principle, if any, captures them.

The system is a hereditary monarchy with a single elected chamber. There is no codified constitution. Instead a body of established practice is treated as binding by everyone involved, although no court will enforce it. The head of state appoints the head of government, assents to legislation, and may dissolve the chamber before its term expires; by long practice each of these is exercised on the advice of the head of government. I will call the binding-but-unenforceable rules 'conventions', and I reserve 'unconstitutional' for the gravest of their breaches — the ones I would describe as a constitutional crisis rather than a mere impropriety.

My verdicts on particular cases are these.

- The monarch withholds assent from an ordinary bill that has passed the chamber. I call this unconstitutional, yet I do not think the bill thereby becomes law, nor that a court should order the monarch to sign. The wrong is a breach of convention and nothing more, and still I rank it above most breaches.
- The monarch refuses assent to a bill that would extend the chamber's own maximum term by several years without an intervening election. I call this lawful and proper — I think the monarch would be right to refuse, and perhaps obliged to.
- The head of government loses a confidence vote and declines to resign or to advise a dissolution. Unconstitutional.
- The head of government loses a confidence vote but remains in office for a fortnight to pass an emergency budget, then resigns. Lawful and proper; a caretaker is entitled to that.
- The monarch dismisses a head of government who still commands the chamber's confidence. Unconstitutional.
- The monarch commissions a head of government who plainly does not yet command the chamber's confidence, because the election has left no majority and negotiations are under way. Lawful and proper; someone must hold the seals while confidence is tested.
- The monarch dissolves the chamber on the advice of a head of government who still commands confidence, timing the election for partisan advantage. Lawful and proper — the monarch is bound to follow advice.
- The monarch refuses a dissolution when the head of government has lost confidence and an alternative majority is in view. Lawful and proper.
- A minister misleads the chamber and does not resign. A breach of convention, but not unconstitutional in the sense of the first case.
- The chamber, by simple majority, rewrites its own standing orders so that a policy cannot be reversed by a future majority without a supermajority. I am genuinely torn, and my verdict flips depending on the day.
- The monarch refers a bill that has passed the chamber to a standing commission, which may delay it for a year but cannot amend it. I call this lawful and proper as a safety valve, though I am uneasy when the bill carries a clear electoral mandate.

A tentative principle of my own: an act is unconstitutional when it attacks the capacity of the chamber or the electorate to hold the government to account; it is a mere breach of convention when it offends a norm without disabling that accountability; and it is lawful and proper when it either follows the norms or departs from them for a reason the norms themselves serve, as the caretaker cases do.

Please test that against the cases. It seems to me that cases one and two are the hard pair — refusing assent is unconstitutional in one and required in the other — and so are cases seven and eight, where following advice and refusing it are each proper. If the principle survives, say what it predicts for the case I keep circling: the monarch refuses assent to a bill that would abolish the monarchy. If it does not survive, say which of my verdicts has to go, and whether a simpler principle that overturns one of them is preferable to a more complex one that saves them all.
