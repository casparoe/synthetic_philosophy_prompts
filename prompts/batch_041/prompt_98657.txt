I'm a postdoc in philosophy of science, and next month I'm covering a session of a cross-departmental reading group that usually works through formal epistemology: about twelve people, mostly philosophy PhD students but with two statistics students and one cognitive scientist, all of them comfortable with Bayes' rule and basic conditional probability, none of whom has ever worked through confirmation theory as such. The topic I've been handed is the problem of old evidence, and I have seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, and no assigned reading, since the group is between books. I don't want you to explain the problem to me; I want you to plan how I should teach it. Lay out the order in which the pieces have to come and, for each step, what it depends on, so that by the time the puzzle actually appears the room feels the tension rather than being told that a tension exists.

Tell me which single example should carry the whole session. My instinct is to reach for Mercury's perihelion, but I suspect it has been done to death and that its familiarity lets people nod along without feeling the problem; if you agree, give me a better case and say precisely what it must have and lack — it has to be one where the evidence is genuinely old, where the confirmation is nonetheless something we would want to grant, and where this particular audience has no independent stake in the science. I would also like the session to end by opening onto the neighbouring worry about new theories, so tell me where in the sequence that door should be left ajar and how much to say through it. One further thing I want the plan to make room for, if it earns its place: the thought that the real difficulty concerns what a probability function is indexed to — a particular agent's credences at a moment, or a body of evidence — and that the puzzle may look different depending on which of those we mean. Decide for me whether that belongs in the sequence at all, and if so, exactly where.

Then give me the misconception map: the wrong turns this particular audience will take, in the order they will take them — I am already expecting at least one person to conclude that since the probability of old evidence is one, the problem is a triviality, and another to think the fix is simply to conditionalize on the fact that the hypothesis entails the evidence — and for each, the precise point in the sequence where I should head it off, with the one sentence I should use. Finish with a question I can put to the room at the end that would genuinely reveal whether they have grasped what the problem is, rather than whether they can recite a solution, and with a short list of things I should deliberately leave out at this level and flag as out of scope. Keep it a teaching plan; don't hand me the explanation itself.
