I teach Weil's case against political parties in a graduate seminar, but my own formation lies elsewhere: my training and nearly all of my published work are in social choice and mechanism design, and her essay is the one text each year that obliges me to argue in a vocabulary I do not really command. I would like to know how much of her argument is doing work that my vocabulary can see. Please restate her position, as faithfully as you can, in the terms I actually use, and then give me a residue report: what carried over intact, what carried over only under distortion, and what did not carry at all.

Take the position to be the following, which is my own reconstruction, and correct it only if some part of it is a genuine misreading. The criterion for judging political arrangements is truth, justice, and the public interest; democracy and majority rule are means to these and not goods in themselves. A political party has three defining features: it manufactures collective passion, it exerts a standing collective pressure on the minds of its individual members, and its own unlimited growth is both its first objective and its ultimate end. Together these make every party totalitarian in aspiration, so that a party which is not in fact totalitarian is merely held in check by the rival parties surrounding it. The underlying mechanism is a reversal of ends and means, in which something that is by nature a means — the party, like money or the state or national pride — comes to be treated as an absolute good; the corruption is therefore structural rather than a matter of bad actors. The harm falls not on interests or welfare but on the relation between a person and the truth. The remedy is not better or more parties but the general suppression of parties, together with some non-partisan organ for the expression of public opinion. Note also that she takes herself to be describing the Continental European party specifically, and treats the Anglo-Saxon party as a different institution with a different history; do not let that distinction dissolve.

The vocabulary I have is this: individual preferences and their aggregation, social choice rules and Arrow-type impossibility results, incentive compatibility and the revelation principle, principal-agent problems, agenda control, information aggregation of the Condorcet kind, costly signaling and commitment devices, common knowledge, information cascades, preference falsification, coalition formation, equilibrium selection. I can follow a formal statement, and I am reasonably good at noticing a loose one.

The residue report is the part I care about. For each claim above, say what the translation preserves, what it must distort to fit, and what has no counterpart whatsoever — and for each residue give a verdict: is this something my vocabulary is constitutively unable to express, or is it a sign that the original claim was doing rhetorical or metaphysical work that has no cash value? Three things interest me in particular. First, whether a framework built out of incentives and equilibria can state any claim of the form that an institution of a given kind will necessarily do such-and-such, or whether its results are invariably of the form that under such-and-such conditions these equilibria exist; if the latter, say what becomes of her necessity claim. Second, whether collective passion and the pressure on minds survive as anything more than preference falsification, and whether that substitution alters the content rather than merely the idiom. Third, whether the translated argument still supports the conclusion; if the translation makes her remedy look like the destruction of the only available technology for aggregating dispersed information, tell me whether that is her problem or mine. Do not quietly replace her terms with mine and present the result as a translation — where the replacement changes the truth value, that belongs in the residue report. No citations or bibliography, please; I know the secondary literature, and what I want from you is the translation.
