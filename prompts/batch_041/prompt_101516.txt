I host a small philosophy podcast whose listeners are mostly graduate students and serious hobbyists, and for next week's episode I am interviewing a philosopher who has just completed a manuscript on the Sleeping Beauty problem and the nature of self-locating credence. Her line is deflationary. She argues that there is no single fact of the matter about what Beauty's credence in heads ought to be, because a self-locating credence is defined only relative to a partition of the space of possibilities, and the halfer and the thirder are therefore not disagreeing about a number but about which partition is in play — roughly, whether the relevant possibilities are whole world-histories or individual awakenings. She further holds that the choice between partitions is not settled by anything purely epistemic but by the agent's deliberative situation, so that the puzzle dissolves into a question about the norms of practical reasoning rather than about belief.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for me, none of them generic, and please write them so that each one turns on the actual content of her position rather than inviting her to restate it. I want at least one question each on the following four threads: first, the halfer's no-new-information argument — specifically how her view can allow an agent's Monday credence to diverge from her Sunday prior when the agent has acquired no evidence, and what her view says about the case in which Beauty writes her Sunday credence on a slip of paper and reads it upon waking; second, the fair-odds argument for the thirder, together with the objection that the odds at which a bet is fair reflect the bookmaker's whole book rather than Beauty's epistemic state, and the related Dutch-book pressure that halfers are said to incur; third, the consequences for anthropic reasoning — whether her view implies that self-locating credence can never support arguments to the effect that we should expect to be typical observers, and how she handles the reductio in which a single indifference principle over observers licenses confident belief that vastly many observers exist; and fourth, the extremal variants, in which the tails branch contains a great many awakenings rather than two, or the coin is biased, or Beauty is told which day it is, and in which the two candidate partitions seem to come apart in ways the ordinary case conceals.

For each of these four threads, please pair the question with the reply she is most likely to give — the standard move being to say that the objection has already helped itself to a partition — and then add a follow-up that forces her to say something more than that, ideally something that pins down whether her view retains any substantive thesis or merely relocates the disagreement. Please end with a question about what evidence or argument could count against her position, and one about where she draws the line between cases in which a partition is genuinely privileged (ordinary self-location, such as not knowing which of two rooms one is in) and the cases in which she claims it is not.
