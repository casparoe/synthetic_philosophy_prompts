I am drafting a short position statement on decision theory for a reading group and would like it audited for coherence before I circulate it. I set out my commitments below as flatly as I can.

1. **Causal decision theory.** An act is rational insofar as its causal consequences are better than those of its alternatives; whatever evidence the act provides about other matters — including about what I would otherwise choose — is irrelevant to its choiceworthiness.

2. **The absent-minded driver.** Take the problem in this form: a driver must leave the motorway at the second of two exits; at neither exit can she tell whether it is the first or the second, since her memory does not distinguish the two decision points; exiting at the first yields 0, exiting at the second yields 4, and overshooting both yields 1. I hold that the rational driver continues with probability 2/3 at each decision point. I further hold that she needs no mnemonic device and no precommitment: at the 2/3 mix she is exactly indifferent, at the moment of decision, between exiting and continuing, so the policy is self-enforcing.

3. **Locality of act-evaluation.** Whether an act is rational at a decision point is a local matter: it depends on the acts then available, their causal consequences, and her credences about which point she is at — not on the fact that the act is one component of a plan adopted earlier.

4. **Strategy-consistent credences.** Her credence about which point she is at is not an independent given; it is fixed by her own policy, much as a mixed strategy fixes the probability of reaching each information state. She should act on that credence.

5. **Randomisation.** A rational chooser randomises only in order to keep an adversary uncertain about what she will do; a solitary chooser, with no opponent, never has reason to randomise.

6. **Policy-first evaluation.** The general lesson I want to draw is that decision theory should evaluate policies — mappings from information states to acts — rather than individual acts, and that this stance is a genuine third option, neither causal nor evidential.

I would like a consistency audit, not a literature survey. Which of these can be held together, and which pull against one another? Distinguish the pairs that are genuinely in tension from those that only appear to be, and explain what dissolves the latter. For each genuine tension, say how severe it is, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs; where a tension admits more than one repair, say which is least damaging. Order the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad, and conclude by naming the single revision that would clear up the most at once.

End with a final line in exactly this form: Verdict: <the one commitment to drop> — <the single revision that fixes the most>.
