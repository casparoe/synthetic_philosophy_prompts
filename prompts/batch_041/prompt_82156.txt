I am preparing a short critical note on Berkeley's economic writings and have run into a problem with a colleague's summary of them. She and I are both in a reading group on the history of monetary thought, and her draft — which the group has provisionally adopted as the framing for our session on The Querist — reads as follows:

"Berkeley's economics is usually reduced to the slogan that money is only a ticket, but the argument is more interesting than the slogan. His central thesis is that the true source of national wealth is the labour and industry of the people, not the stock of gold and silver; money is useful only insofar as it sets that industry going and lets men share the fruits of one another's labour. From this he concludes that money has no intrinsic value — it is a ticket or counter, a device for reckoning and transferring claims — so the quantity of money in a country is in itself a matter of indifference, and what matters is only the briskness with which it circulates. He therefore proposed a national bank that would issue paper currency for Ireland, on the principle that credit rather than coin is the real medium of commerce, and that a state which can create credit can thereby create the means of industry. In this he anticipates the modern state theory of money, on which money is whatever the sovereign declares to be money. And the whole performance is of a piece with his metaphysics: just as the material substance supposed to lie behind our ideas is a fiction, so the value supposed to lie behind the coin is a fiction — the book is immaterialism applied to the coinage."

I have the text in front of me, and I can see that parts of her summary sit very close to what Berkeley actually wrote. Some of the queries she is drawing on:

- "Whether the four elements, and man's labour therein, be not the true source of wealth?"
- "Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?"
- "Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating? Or, whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser?"
- "Whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?"
- "Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?"

I suspect the summary is right in substance and wrong in ways that matter for how the group will read the book, but I cannot tell where the line falls. What I would like from you is an accuracy audit I can act on, in the following order.

First, go claim by claim through her summary and sort each into three buckets: what a historian of early-modern monetary thought would accept as a fair statement of Berkeley's position, what such a reader would wince at as an overstatement or a conflation, and what is simply wrong. Be specific about which sentence does which work, and where a sentence is a mixture.

Second, separate what she has restated from what she has inferred, and say for each inference whether it follows from anything true in the material she is summarizing. Her conclusion about what a state can do by issuing credit is the one I am most suspicious of, but I would rather be told why it does or does not follow than be reassured.

Third, a difficulty about attribution that the group has not faced. The Querist is written entirely as a numbered series of questions rather than as assertions. Does that form affect which of her claims may be attributed to Berkeley at all, and if so, which ones? If you think a query can nevertheless commit its author, say what distinguishes a query that carries a commitment from one that is genuinely exploratory or rhetorical, and apply that test to the queries quoted above.

Fourth, on the economics itself: does the ticket-or-counter view, properly understood, entail anything about how much money there should be? Is the velocity query best read as supporting her claim that quantity is "a matter of indifference," or as something weaker? And how do the queries in which Berkeley asks whether raising the denominations of the coinage can bring money into the kingdom, or whether shifting the proportions among the metals does anything but move money from one metal to another, bear on that reading?

Fifth, on the genealogy: is the state-theory reading an anachronism, and where does the text locate the source of money's usefulness — in the sovereign's declaration, or somewhere else? Say what the text actually makes money depend on.

Sixth, on the metaphysics: is there anything in the economic argument that depends on immaterialism? If not, say what her closing sentence costs the summary as a reading of Berkeley, as distinct from what it costs it as intellectual history.

Please close by telling me which sentences of her draft can stand in my note unaltered, which must be rewritten, and what a corrected version of her thesis sentence would look like. I am not asking for a reading list — I have the primary text and the group has the session scheduled — and I would rather not be told that interpreters differ. I want to know which reading the text best supports, where the real interpretive fault lines are, and which of her sentences cross them.
