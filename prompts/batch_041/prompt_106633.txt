ok so i've been going back and forth on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox and i want you to write me a proper essay that takes a side, not a survey. here's the setup as i understand it so you know where i'm coming from, though correct me if i'm mangling it — in the lottery case you know for certain that exactly one ticket wins, and for each individual ticket it seems rational to believe that that ticket loses (the odds are overwhelming), so by and large you beleive of every single ticket that it's a loser, and if you put all those beliefs together you've concluded that no ticket wins, which flatly contradicts what you started with. the preface case feels like the same disease with better manners: an author has gone through every claim in her book, she'd defend any one of them if challenged, yet she writes in the preface that some error has probably slipped in somewhere, and that modest sentence looks just as reasonable as the rest, except it's the negation of the conjunction of everything else she believes. so in both cases you've got three things that all look true of a rational believer and can't all be: that she can believe each of a big set of claims, that her beliefs have to fit together, and that it's rational to believe what's overwhelmingly probable.

where i want the essay to actually live, though, is a version i don't think gets enough airtime. take the preface and iterate it. someone writes a foreword to the book observing that the preface probably contains a mistake too; the foreword is itself a chapter, so it gets a foreword, and on down. or strip it to the bone: imagine an editor whose whole job is to append, to any document she's handed, the sentence "some sentence in this document is false." she appends it, someone hands her the result, she appends it again, forever. does the paradox get worse as you iterate, does it evaporate, or does it just move house? i suspect the answer tells you which of the three principles you should actually give up, and i want you to commit to one.

so write the essay. lay out the strongest case for each of the three escape routes — give up closure (or the idea that believing each of a set amounts to believing the set), give up consistency, or give up the rule that high probability is enough for belief, say by splitting acceptance off from credence — and then argue for the one you think a rational agent should actually abandon, and show that your choice handles the iterated version and not just the tidy original. dont wimp out at the end with "each has its costs"; pick one, say what it costs, and defend paying that price. a few hundred words is fine, a bit more if you need it. oh and please don't just tell me what some famous philosopher said about it, i want the argument itself.
