I teach an upper-level seminar on ethics and development — mixed bag of philosophy and politics students, couple of anthropologists — and I'm putting together the take-home final. For context on where I'm coming from: I've been reading rationalist blogs for like fifteen years (LessWrong, the old SSC stuff, ACX, all of it), and the thing that makes me want to bite my desk is how my students handle this topic. They treat universalism vs relativism like a light switch: either "corruption is objectively bad everywhere" or "who are we to judge, it's their culture," and then they're done, no actual thinking. I want one essay question where that move is just not available.

You're a political philosopher who's taught this material for years and is also comfortable in the anthropology of corruption — write like it. Before you draft anything, brainstorm on paper: work out the strongest version of each side, including the version of the relativist challenge that isn't about values at all, and the anti-relativist reply that doesn't just thump the table about universal moral truth. Only then write the final version.

The setup I want the question built around: a provincial education officer in a country where a gift from a contractor to an official is part of an ongoing relationship of mutual obligation, not a hidden price. The officer steers a textbook contract to his cousin's firm. The textbooks arrive, on time, and the schools get them. A donor-country audit calls it corruption; the officer's neighbours think he did what a man in his position is supposed to do. That's the case.

Three things I want the question to force students to keep apart, because I think that's where the crux is:

1. The boring transactional sense — misuse of public office for private gain. This is what the "Western standards" complaint is actually aimed at, and it quietly presupposes a public/private split and a bureaucratic office that a lot of social settings just don't have in that form. So the sharpest relativist challenge isn't "they have different values," it's "your concept doesn't apply here at all." I want them to see that those are different challenges.

2. The older republican sense — corruption as a whole polity going rotten, civic virtue eaten away. Also Western, but a completely different concept: no transaction, no office, no bribe. Cuts the other way too, since it doesn't need a modern state to get going, but it does need a substantive account of what a healthy polity is.

3. The functional move I half remember from legal academia (Lessig? "institutional corruption"? I might be mangling it) — conduct that's perfectly legal and locally normal but corrupt anyway because it undermines what the institution is for. Looks like it buys you a culture-neutral standard, since it only appeals to the institution's own purpose. My hunch is it just relocates the contested norm into the phrase "the institution's purpose" and whoever gets to define the purpose wins — but don't hand them that, make them argue it.

The question itself has to make them commit to a verdict: is there a fact of the matter about whether what the officer did was corrupt, or isn't there? No fence-sitting, no "it depends on your framework" as a conclusion.

What I need from you: (1) the question as it'll be printed, with the vignette in it; (2) a model answer, about 700 words, taking a definite line rather than surveying the field; (3) a marking scheme out of 25, built from five or six must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on how candidates typically blow it; (4) a short list of tells that mark an answer as a B, and one sentence on what an A does that a B doesn't. No reading list or citations needed.
