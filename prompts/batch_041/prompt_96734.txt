I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me. I'm writing a book with two characters who are atom-for-atom identical — one born here, one born on a planet where the wet stuff in the seas is a completely different chemical that looks, tastes and behaves exactly like water, and everyone there calls it water too. The book only works if the difference between them is more than a difference in the world outside them, so I want to argue with the people who say meaning isnt in the head.

Play one of them for me. Be a committed externalist: you hold that what a word or a thought is about gets fixed by the world and the community a person is stuck into, not by anything inside their skull, and you'll give ground on the details but you will not give up that last claim, since without it the original two-planet case falls apart. Stay in character till I say stop, dont step out to tell me what you really think, and keep each reply to a paragraph so it feels like a conversation.

My opening: my character June grows up on the other planet, and then one night she wakes up here — same body, same memories, no gap, she never notices a thing. She's halfway through a novel about the sea she grew up beside. So either her word water flips to mean our water the second she lands, in which case the book she's writing about her own childhood has silently turned into a book about the wrong sea and she can never find out — or it doesn't flip, and then the environment was never what fixed what her word meant in the first place. Which is it? And if you tell me it flips gradually, then tell me what her word meant on the Tuesday in the middle, and whether she was right or wrong about her mother's kitchen that day.
