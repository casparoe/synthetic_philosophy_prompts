Suppose the universe consists of an astronomically large number of causally isolated cells, each containing exactly one agent. No agent can influence any other agent's actions, beliefs, or payoffs through any causal channel; the cells never interact, now or at any future time. Yet every agent was produced by the same generative process and consequently runs the same decision procedure, so that the action taken by any one agent is evidence — perhaps very weak evidence, since the shared procedure is noisy — about the action taken by every other. Each agent faces the following one-shot decision, once and for all: she may incur a personal cost c, in which case every other agent in the universe receives a benefit b; or she may incur nothing, in which case no one receives anything. Receiving a benefit imposes no burden on the recipient. Assume b > c, and assume the population is astronomically large.

Please argue for the following claim: an agent in this situation can be rationally required to incur the cost, and this requirement can survive even when the evidential correlation among agents is very weak — a sufficiently large population can compensate for a weak correlation, so that the reason to cooperate may be decisive even though the evidence linking one agent's choice to another's is slight. As you develop the argument, engage directly with the most natural objection: that a merely evidential connection, with no causal pathway running from one agent's choice to another's benefit, cannot by itself generate a reason to act, and that any appearance to the contrary arises from treating one's own choice both as an action and as evidence about what one will receive. Say what you take the objector to be getting right, and where the objection fails. Argue the case on its merits rather than resting it on the verdict of any named decision theory.
