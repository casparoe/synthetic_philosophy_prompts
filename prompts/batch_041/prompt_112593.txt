Write an essay of about 1,800 words, in American English, on a controversy about aggregated and disaggregated evidence. Assume your reader has no library and no internet, and write accordingly: include no citations, no bibliography, no reading list, and no attribution of any claim to a named author, experiment, or published work. Treat the numbered items below as the agenda for the essay rather than as its format; the result should be continuous prose that reaches and defends conclusions.

I have constructed two pairs of tables. In each, an outcome — recovery from a condition, say — is recorded for two arms, A and B, in each of two strata. All counts are invented for the purpose.

Table 1

Stratum 1: A, 9 recoveries out of 10 (90%); B, 72 out of 90 (80%).
Stratum 2: A, 18 out of 90 (20%); B, 1 out of 10 (10%).
Pooled: A, 27 out of 100 (27%); B, 73 out of 100 (73%).

Table 2

Stratum 1: A, 20 recoveries out of 30 (66.7%); B, 12 out of 30 (40%).
Stratum 2: A, 15 out of 35 (42.9%); B, 7 out of 35 (20%).
Pooled: A, 35 out of 65 (53.8%); B, 19 out of 65 (29.2%).

The two tables are meant to be read against one another. They differ in one structural respect only: in Table 1 the two arms are distributed very unevenly across the strata, whereas in Table 2 each arm contributes identical numbers of cases to each stratum.

1. Table 1 is commonly said to exhibit a reversal of association. Verify the arithmetic, then state exactly what is reversed and what is not. Explain why the reversal is neither a contradiction nor an error of computation, and say precisely what the "paradox" consists in — what has to be true of the world, or of the description of it, for the two comparisons to disagree.

2. Say whether the reversal in Table 1 survives a change of measure: compute the risk difference, the risk ratio, and the odds ratio, stratum by stratum and pooled, and report which measures reverse and which do not. Do the same for Table 2, where I have arranged the counts so that the stratum-specific odds ratio is the same in both strata. Is anything reversed in Table 2? Is the pooled odds ratio equal to the stratum-specific odds ratio, or to any weighted average of the two stratum-specific odds ratios? Does the pooled risk ratio behave in the same way? Then say what this contrast shows about the common phrase that "the same data" can be summarised in a single number.

3. Work out, from the arithmetic alone, whether a reversal of the Table 1 kind can occur when the two arms are allocated in identical proportions within every stratum, and prove your answer for the recovery proportions rather than merely asserting it. Say whether anything comparable holds for the odds ratio, or whether only the magnitude and not the direction of that measure can shift. Then say what follows about whether randomisation by itself protects an investigation from the phenomenon, and identify the single feature of Table 1 that does the work in producing the reversal.

4. Say what must be true of the causal relations among arm, stratum, and outcome for the stratified comparison in Table 1 to be the right one, and then construct a plausible story under which the pooled comparison is the right one instead. Consider in particular what changes if the stratifying variable is a downstream consequence of the arm rather than a common cause of arm and outcome, and say whether the tables alone could ever tell you which of your stories holds.

5. Consider the pooled table as evidence. Its counts are recoverable from the stratified counts, so in that sense it carries no information the stratified tables do not already carry. Argue about whether the pooled and stratified tables can nevertheless be "the same evidence" for two conflicting verdicts, and whether either can be evidence for something the other cannot support at all.

6. Close with two verdicts, each defended rather than hedged.

First: in Table 1, is there a fact of the matter about whether arm A outperforms arm B, or is the answer merely relative to a choice of partition and of measure? A conditional verdict is acceptable — "there is a fact of the matter if and only if ..." — but the condition must be stated exactly, and you must say which side of it Table 1 falls on.

Second: does the phenomenon show that statistical evidence is inescapably relative to the analyst's choices, or does it show only that a question left vague has no answer? Defend whichever verdict you reach, and say what a careful investigator should report when the data look like Table 1, and why reporting only one of the two tables would be a way of misleading a reader.
