I teach torts, and the causation rules we actually apply dont hang together and it bugs me. The default is the but-for test: no liability unless the harm wouldn't have happened without the defendant's act. But then we turn around and hold each of two polluters fully liable when either one's discharge alone would have killed the fish — neither is a but-for cause. And in medical malpractice, where a patient had maybe a 40% chance of surviving and the doctor's negligence cut it to 20%, some courts award damages for the lost chance while others throw the claim out precisely because you can't say the patient would have survived. So what theory of causation does this package presuppose? Does the but-for test commit us to a counterfactual-dependence view of causation, and is the overdetermination exception a quiet slide into something more like an interventionist picture, or the old 'insufficient but necessary part of a sufficient set' idea? Or is the whole thing better read as a doctrine about proof and error costs rather than about what causation really is? Which rival theories does the practice actually rule out versus merely sit awkwardly with, and are the parts even consistent with each other? And if we rebuilt doctrine around a fully interventionist account, what specifically would change in the lost-chance and overdetermination cases — would they get easier, or would the hard part just move somewhere else? I'm less interested in what judges say theyre doing than in what the structure of the rules requires.
