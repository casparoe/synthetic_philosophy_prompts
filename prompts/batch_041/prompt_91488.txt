I teach a 200-level seminar on philosophy of action, about 18 students, one 75-minute session, and I want them to feel the difference between akrasia as philosophers describe it and the time-inconsistency story decision theorists tell. Heres the setup I have in mind, tell me if it works and fix the details: at the start of class everyone gets a card with two boxes, one asking which option they think is better for them overall and one asking which they actually want to take. The options are something small and close versus something bigger and later -- two candies at the break, five at the end of class, that kind of thing, no real money. Half the room gets a card they can make binding at the start (signed, handed to me, and I'll honor it), half doesn't. Everyone also predicts what they'll choose at the break, in writing. Then just before the break I re-offer the same choice, and we see who reverses. Then the debrief: the ones whose judgment stayed put but whose hand moved are the classic incontinent; the ones whose judgment flipped too are a different animal, and I want to press whether anything went wrong for them at all or whether there just isnt a single preference to fail. The naive versus sophisticated thing should fall out of who pre-committed and who mispredicted themselves. Don't let the word hyperbolic discounting in until the debrief. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, how to fix it, and which part of the real phenomenon this staging can't reproduce -- I suspect it's the felt struggle and the question of whether the later self is even the same agent, but I want your honest read on that.
