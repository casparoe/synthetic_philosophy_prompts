I'm a retired physiologist — thirty-odd years on kidney and blood-pressure stuff — and I've spent the last few years reading philosophy in a very undisciplined way. Self-deception is the topic that's got under my skin and I want to settle it properly, so before I read anything else I want the spec sheet. What does a theory of self-deception actually have to deliver to be worth the name? Give me the requirements, each with the case or the consideration that puts it on the list, and tell me which of them are real requirements and which are just things the intro books have drilled into me.

Stuff I'd expect to be on the list, though don't take my word for it:

- it has to explain how one and the same person can be both the deceiver and the deceived, without literally installing two people in there and without smuggling in a little homunculus who knows the truth and hides it from the rest of us
- it has to keep self-deception apart from its neighbours: wishful thinking, weakness of will, plain error, changing your mind, deliberately lying to yourself, and simply never thinking about the thing
- it has to say something about the direction of the thing — the person seems to steer away from the evidence, and the steering is the wierd part, because steering looks like a strategy and a strategy looks like it has to be known about
- and it mustn't need any part of the mind smarter than the whole person, which I suspect is what kills most of the folk versions

Here's a case I keep coming back to, because it happened to a colleague and I think its harder than the textbook examples. He had a mouse colony on a diet that was supposed to extend lifespan, and for two years he read those survival curves in the most generous possible way — every early death in the treatment arm was "not attributable", every control death was "expected", and he'd have passed a polygraph on any of it. He wasn't lying and he was a careful man. Then someone re-ran it and the effect evaporated. The twist I want you to take seriously: while the study was running he needed *not* to know the result, because a man who knew it was failing would have changed the protocol, and then there'd be no clean answer either way. So the ignorance was doing work. Is that self-deception, or is it a separate box the theory has to build — and if it's a separate box, what stops the box from swallowing everything?

Then the part I most want: are any of these requirements impossible to hold at once? I suspect at least one pair is flatly inconsistent, and I'd rather learn that now then after reading four books about it. And a short pass at the main families of answers — the ones that posit two beliefs, the ones that posit one belief plus motivated attention, the ones that say it isn't really a belief at all — scored against the list. Keep the scoring brief, the criteria are the deliverable.

One thing on what the theory is *for*, since that changes the answers: I want something a working scientist could actually use, so it has to hook up to attention, memory, and what we know about how people handle evidence, not just to armchair puzzles. If a requirement only makes sense for a disembodied reasoner, say so and I'll treat it as a preference rather than a requirement.
