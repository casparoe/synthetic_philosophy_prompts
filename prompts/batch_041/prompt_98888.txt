The introspection-reliability debate — you know the confabulation results, the split-brain stuff, the 'we are strangers to ourselves' line — seems stuck to me because both camps treat 'reliable' as a single thing. I think there are at least two things you could mean by it: whether introspection reliably tells you *what* you're currently thinking, feeling or experiencing, and whether it reliably tells you *why* — the causal basis of the state. My hunch is the famous results only really bite on the second, and even there only under a reading where 'why' means 'the actual cause' rather than 'the reason I would cite if pressed'. Once you pull those seperate, the sceptical conclusion stops looking like a general verdict on introspection and starts looking like a claim about one specific inferential task — and that task might be one where we're bad for boring Bayesian reasons rather than spooky ones (we're inferring a hidden cause from noisy, and possibly self-contaminating, internal evidence). There's also a wrinkle I can't shake: for other people we have behaviour plus a theory of mind; for ourselves we have all that *plus* privileged internal evidence, so if anything the self-case should be the easy one, which is the opposite of what the sceptic says.
