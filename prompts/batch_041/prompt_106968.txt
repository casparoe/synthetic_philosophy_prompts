I am a novelist at work on a counterfactual in which William James does not die in 1910 but lives on into the present, and the book turns on a chapter in which he is asked for counsel on a decision of a kind that could not have arisen in his lifetime. I have drafted the scene but do not yet trust it: I cannot tell whether the answer I have put in his mouth is the one he would actually have reached, or merely the one I find dramatically convenient. I want you to predict his view — not to summarize what he wrote, but to reconstruct his moral thinking closely enough that you can say what it would do when pressed against a case he never imagined. I would rather have your reasoning than a list of sources, and there is no need to cite anything.

The case is this. A low-lying coastal town of some four thousand people has been the continuous home of the same families for three centuries; it has its own dialect, its own festival, its own burial ground, and its residents describe their attachment to it not as a preference but as the ground of who they are. The engineers are unambiguous: within thirty years the sea will make the site uninhabitable, and the process will not be gentle. The state offers a collective buyout. Every household would be compensated, the graves moved, the church rebuilt on higher land, the town reconstituted, in name at least, some miles inland; in exchange the site is surrendered and no one may return. Those who refuse keep their town for as long as it lasts, and accept a rising annual probability of drowning deaths among their own. The choice, as the town experiences it, is between a future in which no one dies in the water and a future in which the form of life that makes their lives worth living continues a while longer and then does not. Residents have begun to speak of a grief that is not quite nostalgia, since it is felt while still at home — a distress at a place altering under one's feet that some now call solastalgia. There is no common currency in which the safety of persons and the survival of a way of life can be weighed, and yet the town must choose.

I want you to reconstruct the parts of James's ethical thought that would actually bear on this — his pluralism; his conviction that each genuine demand is real and refuses to be traded against a single scale; his distrust of any system that pretends to a final hierarchy of goods; his notion of the strenuous mood; his picture of the moral philosopher as one who tries to satisfy the most demands, and to invent new ideals, rather than to deduce from a rule — and then tell me where he would come down and, more important, by what road.

Specifically, I need:

1. Whether he would accept the framing at all — that these goods are incommensurable, that the choice is forced, that someone must choose — or whether he would try to dissolve the dilemma, and if so how.
2. If he must choose, which way, and what he would say about the value that is sacrificed: whether he would admit a remainder that no compensation can clear, or whether his pragmatism would let him call the lesser loss simply the lesser loss.
3. The point at which he would refuse to give me a clean answer, and how I should stage that refusal so that it reads as integrity rather than evasion.
4. What would move him the other way — what fact or argument would turn him.
5. Where the prediction is genuinely underdetermined by what he actually thought, together with your best guess and your confidence in it.

Tell me how he would have argued it, in what order, and what he would have refused to concede.
