The question I set:

"Expected-value (EV) maximization says: choose the act with the greatest expected value. Consider a choice between

S: 1 unit of welfare for certain.
L: 10^100 units of welfare with probability 1 in 10^98, and nothing otherwise.

(a) Work out what EV maximization says about this choice, and explain how the case can be 'sweetened' so that it isn't a one-off.
(b) State the fanaticism objection as sharply as you can, and say what exactly it is an objection to.
(c) Consider two proposed replies: (i) don't take probabilities below some threshold seriously — round them down to zero; (ii) assume utility is bounded above. For each, say whether it answers the objection, and why."

The student's answer:

"(a) EV(L) = (1/10^98)(10^100) = 10^2 = 100, and EV(S) = 1. So EV maximization takes L. And it isn't a special case: for any sure amount s, I can pick a probability p small enough and a payoff v large enough that pv > s, so the EV maximizer is always dragged into the lottery. That's what makes it structural rather than a quirk of these particular numbers.

(b) The fanaticism objection says EV maximization is committed to trading any sure, modest good for a gamble with an arbitrarily small chance of an arbitrarily large good — and since the sweetening in (a) always works, it's committed to this no matter how modest the sure thing is or how small the chance. The objection isn't that the arithmetic is wrong (it isn't), and it isn't that the rule is inconsistent (it gives a single answer each time). It's that the rule's verdicts look insane. I think the deepest version of the problem is that expected values can come out infinite or undefined, which is what really breaks the framework.

(c) Reply (i) fails. If you round tiny probabilities to zero you'd reject L, but the sweetener just re-runs with a probability above your threshold and a payoff big enough to clear the sure thing, so you're pushed back into a lottery. And there's no non-arbitrary place to draw the threshold. So (i) relocates the problem rather than solving it.

Reply (ii) is the better one. If utility is bounded above, there's a best outcome, and no lottery can have a higher expected value than simply getting that best outcome for sure. So the sweetener can't beat the sure thing, and the fanaticism is defused. The catch is that bounded utility isn't handed to you by the axioms of decision theory — it's a substantive extra assumption — but it's the reply I'd go with.

Overall: the objection is serious, but I'd keep EV maximization and accept that we sometimes have to bite the bullet on tiny probabilities."
