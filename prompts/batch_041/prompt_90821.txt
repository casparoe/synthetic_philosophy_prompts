Right, bit of an odd one. I'm a novelist — four books, mostly first-person narrators who are not, shall we say, reliable — and I keep running into the philosophical literature on self-deception and feeling like were talking past each other. The philosophers seem to treat it as a paradox: to deceive yourself you'd have to both believe the thing and not believe it, or intend to conceal the truth from someone who already knows it, namely you. Which sounds mad. But I've spent twenty years writing people who do it, and it doesn't feel mad at all.

Here's what I'd like. Restate the main philosophical accounts of self-deception — the ones that take it to be literally holding contradictory beliefs, the ones that split the mind into a deceiver part and a deceived part, the ones that say it isn't really belief at all but something more like motivated avoidance or pretence — in the vocabulary I actually use. Voice, free indirect discourse, the gap between what a character says and what the narration lets slip, subtext, dramatic irony, the difference between what a narrator reports and what the reader can see. As faithfully as you can, one account at a time.

Then the bit I really care about: what doesn't survive the translation. Which parts of the philosophers' problem have no counterpart in how a novel works — and when something won't translate, is that because my craft vocabulary is missing something real, or because the philosophers have smuggled in an assumption (that there's a single unified believer, say, or that deceiving is always something you do on purpose) that the fiction never needed? Don't be polite about it. I half suspect one of their puzzles is just an artefact of how they describe the case, and I'd like you to tell me if I'm wrong.

You're a philosopher of mind who actually reads novels, so I want the philosophy to be right and the fiction vocabulary used properly — no vague hand-waving about unreliable narrators as if that's a magic word that dissolves the problem. Think it through carefully before you start writing; maybe sketch the mapping out for yourself first. Keep the whole thing under 900 words, and lead with a one-sentence verdict on whether the paradox is real or a description-artefact. British spelling, please — I'm not American.
