I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for years — the Sequences, the long-running arguments about objective Bayesianism and whether priors can be derived rather than merely chosen, the periodic revivals of the maximum-entropy-versus-indifference debate — and I keep running into Bertrand's chord problem as the supposedly decisive objection to the principle of indifference. A few weeks ago I read an essay on this by a writer whose math I usually trust (I no longer have the link, and I'd rather not misattribute it), and it summarized the whole business roughly as follows. Bertrand's 1889 example shows that a chord drawn "at random" in a circle can be assigned at least three mutually inconsistent probabilities — 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 — each of which follows from an "obviously" legitimate application of indifference, and this proves the principle of indifference is simply incoherent and cannot be repaired. The standard modern fix, the essay continues, is Jaynes's: in the 1970s he showed that the principle of maximum entropy uniquely picks out the 1/2 answer, so the problem is now regarded as solved; the broader moral is that anyone who wants to spread a probability measure over an infinite possibility space should reach for the entropy apparatus instead of naive indifference, since entropy maximization and symmetry arguments ultimately amount to the same thing.

That account is convenient for the essay's polemic, and I suspect it is too convenient, but I know just enough to be dangerous rather than enough to adjudicate. I have a vague and possibly garbled memory that Jaynes's actual treatment of the chord problem turned on invariance — that he argued a well-posed answer has to be indifferent to the position and scale of the circle — and that this is a different instrument from entropy maximization even though the same author championed both; I genuinely can't reconstruct which of those two he used where. I've likewise seen it complained, in the same corner of the internet, that any invariance argument begs the question, because you have to decide in advance which transformations the answer must respect, and a different choice would crown a different method as the unique solution; and the cube-factory puzzle, where indifference over a cube's side and indifference over its volume yield different answers, tends to get wheeled out in the same breath, which makes me suspect the two cases aren't as parallel as people imply.

Before I go repeating any of this at people who know more than I do, I'd like an honest audit of the essay's summary.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. They should collectively cover which of the essay's claims are solid, which are subtle distortions rather than outright errors, how someone sympathetic to the essay (or to Jaynes) would defend each contested step, and whether the essay's bottom-line moral about indifference, entropy, and symmetry survives once the distortions are corrected.
