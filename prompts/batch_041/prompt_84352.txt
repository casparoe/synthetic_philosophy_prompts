I work in the provost's office at a mid-size university, and a big chunk of my job is dealing with people who say the university promised them something. A professor told a prospective PhD student she'd have funding for four years, nothing in writing, the student turned down another offer, then the grant fell through. A chair told a lecturer her contract would be renewed as long as enrollment held, enrollment dropped eight percent, and the chair retired. A community group says someone in facilities gave them the gym every Tuesday indefinitely, and they've since bought equipment. I get maybe forty of these a term, I've got about a week on each, I have no legal training and no philosophy background, and anything involving money or a multi-year commitment goes to the provost to sign off. I've been putting this off for months becuase I keep going in circles.

What I want is a procedure my two staff and I can actually run on one of these, in order, and then explain out loud to the person complaining without hiding behind policy language. Not an essay on promising. Questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where it stops with a flat no, and where it stops because the remaining question is a value call that a human has to make. Don't send me to the literature, I won't read it.

Two things I've noticed from reading around that I want built in. First, I keep conflating three different questions: whether a commitment was actually made (what did these two people understand themselves to be doing?), whether it binds (what makes a promise stick in the first place -- the other person's reliance on it, the speaker's will, or something the two of them made between them that neither can just cancel?), and whether it beats everything else we might owe people. Those come apart and my office keeps smashing them together. I want them seperate in the procedure. Second, I need a test for the line between a promise and a prediction -- when somebody says you'll be fine, or we'll always have room for you, is that an undertaking or a forecast? Does it matter whether the listener was meant to rely on it, or just happened to? And related: does a promise need to be accepted to bind, or is it enough that it was made and heard?

Also, I don't think binds is a yes/no. I want the output to be one of at least four: binds, we do it; binds but we can renegotiate; doesn't bind, but we owe them a straight explanation and maybe something anyway; and binds but not the way you think it does -- the obligation lands on someone else, or takes a different form than the complainant is asking for.

Run it on three: (1) the four years of funding, where I suspect the answer is one I won't like, since nobody with budget authority made the promise; (2) the renewal, where the person who said it is gone and the condition she attached has failed; (3) the community group, where the promise is vague, open-ended, and was made by someone who has retired. Include one case where the procedure says no, and one where it genuinely can't decide because the parties disagree about what was said and there's no record.

Tell me where the procedure will misfire and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as questions. And at the end, say how confident you are in the procedure as a whole and which single step you're least sure about -- I'd rather know where it's shaky than get something that sounds tidy.
