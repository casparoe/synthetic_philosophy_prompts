ok so I'm in this reading group that's half historians and half philosophers and we keep having the same fight about moral revolutions, and I've been told to turn it into something we can actually look at, so I want you to build me a decision tree (or a taxonomy, whichever is cleaner) of the positions on how moral revolutions happen. Our two test cases are the abolition of the Atlantic slave trade and slavery in the British world, and the collapse of dueling as an honorable practice in Britain and the US. Here's the commitment I want the tree to start from, because I think it's the one thing everyone in the group actually shares: I accept that these were moral revolutions — that is, big durable changes in what people thought it was ok to do, not just changes in what the law said. So don't spend any nodes arguing about whether they happened.

The split I care most about, and the reason I don't want just another list of "arguments vs interests vs honor" (we've all read those), is that I keep catching people sliding between three very different engines without noticing: the practice became dishonorable, the practice became unprofitable, and the practice became unjust. I want the tree to force those apart. So the first branch node should be something like: is the change primarily in what people believed about the practice, or primarily in the conditions/behavior, with belief trailing behind? And if belief-first, what moved the belief — reasons a person could actually state, or shame/honor/standing, or the practice just coming to mean something different (getting attached to a despised class or a rival nation)? And if conditions-first, whose interests are doing the work — the economic decline story (the old Eric Williams line about the West Indies and the money), geopolitics/empire, or an elite reputation/signaling story that I suspect secretly collapses back into the honor branch.

A few constraints. I want the leaves to include, at minimum: the rationalist/progress view (it ended because the arguements won), Appiah's honor-is-the-engine view, the Williams-style economic-decline view, a deflationary or genealogical view where the "moral revolution" framing is itself part of what needs explaining, and a contingency / moral luck view where it could just as easily have gone the other way and nothing about the reasons made it inevitable. Build the tree from the questions first and the names second — work out the combinations the questions allow and then locate the real positions in that space, and where a combination has nobody in it, mark it "unnamed" and give me one line on whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also flag any two leaves your tree can't really seperate (I have a suspicion the honor view and the elite-signaling interest view will be hard to pull apart, and maybe the economic and geopolitical ones too — tell me if so rather than pretending the branches cut clean). Aim for eight to ten leaves, and pitch it so a grad student in either field could answer every node without already knowing which position it leads to — no node should just be "are you a materialist or an idealist?". For each leaf add one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and keep the whole thing to positions and cases, not citations, since none of us can look anything up.
