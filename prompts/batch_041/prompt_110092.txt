I write features on science and ideas, and I'm most of the way through a long piece on Vera Lindqvist, the philosopher who chairs the standing committee that advises our government on which very long-horizon projects to fund. She publishes almost nothing in journals; her output is committee reports and the occasional lecture, so you will not have read her, and everything I have is what follows. The committee has a decision coming up that I want to write about in advance, and she has said she will not discuss it before the report is published. So I need to reconstruct the rule she is actually running from the rulings she has already made, and then predict her.

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on intergenerational ethics and who also knows the cost-benefit machinery governments actually use — the two halves of the field that usually don't talk to each other. Think this through carefully before you commit to anything, and show your reasoning as you go.

Her record, as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Climate policy. She has defended a consumption discount rate of about 2% a year for long-horizon climate policy, and has rejected a rate of zero on the ground that it would license demands on the present generation that no government could survive politically. She insists the 2% is not a claim that future people matter less. It registers the expectation that they will be richer, so that an extra dollar does less for them.

2. Very long horizons. She has said that beyond a century or two the rate should step down — her committee's guidance runs from about 2% in the first decades to well below 1% after two centuries — because uncertainty about future growth compounds, and that no single number is a decent approximation across those spans. She denies that the step-down is a concession to pure time preference.

3. A contractual liability. Asked whether a public body should set money aside now against a fixed real payment due in 2125 — a decommissioning obligation, in that instance — she said the right rate is the rate at which money actually grows when it is invested, meaning the government's own cost of borrowing, and that using the climate rate for a contractual payment is a category mistake: one rate is for welfare, the other is for money.

4. Conditional beneficiaries. On a scheme whose payouts are conditional on a particular population still existing in 2200, she said it is legitimate to multiply the benefit by the probability that anyone is there to receive it, and that this is not discounting the future but arithmetic.

5. Extinction risk. She supports large present spending on detecting and deflecting a large asteroid, and has said that the beneficiaries' distance in time does not reduce what is owed to them: deaths averted in 2125 count as deaths.

6. Prudence and institutions. She has written that an individual may legitimately prefer a smaller good now to a larger good later — a life is not a portfolio — while a state or an institution may not make the analogous move, because it has no single life to live.

Now the case in front of her. A programme for a coastal delta would cost $60 billion, spent over the next ten years. Beginning in 2150 and running for fifty years, it would deliver a flow of real resources worth about $2 billion a year in today's prices — $100 billion undiscounted — to the roughly four million people who, on the government's own projections, will be living there. Those projections are bleak: the regional economy is expected to contract, and real consumption per head in the delta in 2150 is put at something like 40% below today's national average. Nationally, consumption per head is expected to be roughly flat for the next century and to decline after that. The government can borrow at 1.5% real, and the economy-wide return on capital is expected to average around 4.5%. Demographers put the chance that the delta is still inhabited in 2150 at about four in five.

Here is what I need. Start by brainstorming: set out every rule for weighting benefits that arrive in the future that could have produced those six rulings, including rules she has never stated and rules you would expect her to disown. Then:

1. Say which of those rules survive all six rulings, which are killed by a specific ruling, and what the survivors disagree about.
2. For each surviving rule, give its verdict on the delta programme — approve or reject — with the arithmetic wherever the numbers decide the matter, and flag any point at which a rule needs an extra assumption before it yields a verdict at all.
3. Give me your best guess at what she will recommend, with your reasons and your confidence; tell me which of her six earlier rulings is the closest analog to this case, what makes the analogy hold and where it breaks; and give the strongest case against your own guess.
4. Name the single further case that would best separate the surviving rules, described concretely enough that the committee could plausibly take it up.

If her record genuinely underdetermines the verdict, say so rather than manufacturing a prediction, and tell me which one further fact about her, or which one further ruling, would settle it. I don't need references — I need the reconstruction.
