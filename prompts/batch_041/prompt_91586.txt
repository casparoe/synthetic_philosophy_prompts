I'm prepping a seminar on Moore's ethics and I've been rereading the beauty-and-friendship parts of Principia, which has got me poking at my own position. Before I say any of this out loud to grad students, tell me if it hangs together. Here's what I hold:

(1) Value is objective — whether something is good is a fact about it, not a fact about anyone's attitude toward it.
(2) The bearers of intrinsic value are states of consciousness, not objects. A sunset isn't good; the enjoyment of it is. Take away all the minds and there's nothing left that's worth anything.
(3) The greatest goods are the enjoyment of beauty and personal affection, and the very best of these are organic unities: the love of a person together with the appreciation of their actual good qualities.
(4) Organic unities aren't additive. A whole can be better than the sum of its parts, and the same parts can make a better or worse whole depending on how they're related.
(5) To get at the intrinsic value of something you consider it on its own, in isolation from whatever else it happens to be attached to.
(6) Two verdicts I'm committed to: a friendship resting on a flattering but false picture of the friend is worth less than one resting on an accurate picture, even when the affection feels identical from the inside; and the delight someone takes in a perfect forgery is worth less than the identical delight taken in the original.

My worry is (6). If the two experiences are indistinguishable from the inside, and value lives in consciousness (2), how can the value come apart? Tell me which of these actually conflict, which only look like they do (I have a hunch there's at least one fake fight in the list), and rank the real ones by how much damage they do. For the worst one, give me the cheapest repair and tell me what it costs — I'd rather drop a commitment then keep a story that only holds up if nobody asks.
