Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about how a rational agent ought to respond to conditional testimony, and we broke off with neither of us conceding anything. I would like you to write the dialogue we should have had.

The argument began with a variant of the Judy Benjamin problem. The variant is the whole point, so let me set it out in full.

Judy is dropped into a territory divided into four regions. She knows only this much about where she is: the chance that she is in the Red half is 1/2, the chance that she is in the Blue half is 1/2; if she is in Red, the chance that she is in a defended part is 1/2; if she is in Blue, the chance that she is in a defended part is 1/4. Her prior credences over the four regions are therefore Red-and-defended 1/4, Red-and-undefended 1/4, Blue-and-defended 1/8, Blue-and-undefended 3/8.

She carries a radio. The officer at the other end has an accurate map of where Judy is, and is under orders to transmit exactly one of two pre-written sentences, and to transmit one only if it is true:

(i) If you are in the Red territory, then you are in the defended part.
(ii) If you are in the defended part, then you are in the Red territory.

Judy knows all of this. What the orders leave open is which of the two true sentences the officer sends when she has a choice, and Judy does not know how she exercises that discretion. Take three candidate rules. Under R1 the officer sends (i) if it is true, and otherwise sends (ii). Under R2 she sends (ii) if it is true, and otherwise sends (i). Under R3, when both are true she flips a fair coin, and when only one is true she sends that one. In all three cases the sentence Judy receives is (i).

Here is what we could not settle. I hold that what Judy learns is the content of the message — that she is not in the Red-and-undefended region — and that the rational response is to move her credences as little as that constraint allows, adopting the credence function that satisfies it and lies closest to her prior (closest in the relative-entropy sense). On that account her credence that she is in Red is 1/3, and it is 1/3 whichever rule the officer was following; the officer's discretion is not part of the evidence. My friend holds that a message is an event rather than a proposition: what Judy learns is that this sentence was transmitted, and how much that tells her depends on how likely the officer was to transmit it in each of the four regions, which is settled by the rule and not by the words. On her account the very same sentence can leave Judy at quite different credences under different rules, under one of them it would tell her exactly where she is, and where the rule is unknown the words alone underdetermine the posterior — so anyone who reports a single answer is quietly assuming a rule.

Write the dialogue between the two of us, each holding one of these positions. Make it a genuine exchange rather than two monologues. Neither of us may be handed a weak version of our case, each of us should have to concede a point the other has earned, and neither may win merely by declaring the other's question ill-posed. Both of us should work through the arithmetic for at least two of the three rules and turn the numbers against each other, and we should finish by saying exactly what the remaining disagreement turns on, whether or not we settle it. No citations and no appeals to authority — neither of us may lean on a book the other has not read — and the dialogue should be intelligible to someone who has never met the original puzzle.
