"That is why the soul is the first grade of actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it. The body so described is a body which is organized. The parts of plants in spite of their extreme simplicity are 'organs'; e.g. the leaf serves to shelter the pericarp, the pericarp to shelter the fruit, while the roots of plants are analogous to the mouth of animals, both serving for the absorption of food. If, then, we have to give a general formula applicable to all kinds of soul, we must describe it as the first grade of actuality of a natural organized body. What I am trying to understand is how the distinction between first and second actuality—between the possession of a capacity and its exercise—is supposed to do work in the definition of life. In my seminar I have been using a distinction between what I call the reservoir reading and the exercise reading of "having life potentially." On the reservoir reading, a thing can be alive in the relevant sense merely by retaining the organized capacity for life, even if no vital activity is occurring. On the exercise reading, the capacity must be grounded in some ongoing vital activity or function; otherwise the thing is only potentially alive in a weaker sense. I want to know which reading the passage supports, and why.
