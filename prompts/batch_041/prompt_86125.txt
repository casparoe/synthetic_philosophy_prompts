I am a third-year doctoral student working in the philosophy of mathematics, and I am drafting a chapter that defends the Lucas–Penrose argument as a decisive refutation of mechanism. Before I commit to the line of argument, I would like an assessment of whether it is in good order, since I am anxious about having overlooked something at the technical level. The reconstruction runs as follows. Suppose that some consistent, effectively axiomatised formal system, rich enough to express arithmetic, is proposed as a model of my mathematical reasoning. By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, that system cannot prove its own consistency. I, by contrast, can establish the consistency of my mathematical reasoning: I have worked for years with axioms I am able to survey directly, and I have never once been led into a contradiction, nor do I expect to be. Having established this, I can recognise the truth of the system's Gödel sentence, which the system itself cannot prove; and since the same manoeuvre can be repeated for whatever system is proposed in its place, it follows that no such system captures my reasoning. I am aware that mechanists reply that the system might be inconsistent, so that the incompleteness theorem would not apply to it, but this strikes me as a desperate expedient: an inconsistent reasoner would be committed to every proposition whatever, and it is evident that I am not such a reasoner. I also note that certain writers insist the argument requires the system to be sound and not merely consistent, and recommend Rosser sentences in place of Gödel sentences; but since the Rosser sentence is independent of the system in the same way, this concession appears to cost the anti-mechanist nothing. I take it that the matter is settled. Is there any step here that a mechanist could reasonably resist?
