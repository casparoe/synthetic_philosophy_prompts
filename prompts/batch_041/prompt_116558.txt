I would like help drafting a satirical piece, and I would rather have it written than be advised on how to write it. The subject is the toy system of "typographical genetics" I came across in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach: strands that are simply strings of letters, and enzymes that are themselves strings, each reading off a sequence of instructions — cut the strand at a named spot, delete a base, copy the strand in one direction or the other, switch to the strand's opposite, step along without touching anything — until the enzyme runs off the end or is itself cut. Some strands, given the right enzymes, build copies of themselves, and the moral usually drawn is a cheerful one: life is nothing but information reproducing itself, self-replication is just copying, nothing mysterious left over. What I want to write is a deadpan consumer-safety recall notice from a fictional agency about a mail-order kit that lets hobbyists grow these strands, and the agency's difficulty is that the same string, read through the coding that turns these letter-strings into arithmetic, is also a claim about its own copying — so the inspectors cannot decide whether an escaped copy is a defective product, a new organism, or a true sentence, and therefore cannot decide whether the agency has any jurisdiction at all. Hold the flat, bureaucratic register from beginning to end: no winking, no narrator who knows it is funny. Let the humour come out of the mechanics rather than out of jokes about them — the direction in which a copy is made, a strand that deletes itself versus one that merely spells out its own deletion, copies that are almost but not exactly identical, the unit that will not stop reproducing, a department receiving a complaint from one copy about another. It should be legible to someone who has never heard of the system and worth a wince of recognition from someone who has. Around 500–600 words, laid out as a real notice would be: product and batch, nature of the hazard, affected units, instructions to consumers, contact details. Put no quotation from any real text in it, and cite no book or author inside the notice itself. Then add a short, clearly separated note on where the piece takes liberties with the ideas — in particular whether treating the self-copying strand as a statement about itself is a fair use of the isomorphism or an overreach, and which of my premises, if it turned out false, would make the whole conceit collapse. I would rather be told that the central joke is confused than be told that it is clever.
