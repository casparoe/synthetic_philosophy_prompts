Ok so I'm teaching a grad seminar on vagueness this term, and separately I got roped onto the state health department's guideline comittee — we're the ones who have to put actual numbers on things like "hearing loss that warrants a subsidy" and "blood lead level that triggers home remediation." It's been eye-opening. Every meeting ends with somebody saying "well, the line has to go somewhere," and nobody can say what's wrong with that, even though everyone can feel that somthing is wrong with it. We meet monthly, the chief medical officer has to sign off on whatever we publish, and the minutes are public, so I can't just wave my hands.

I want to hand my students — and honestly the comittee — an actual procedure for this: a step-by-step thing a group of non-philosophers can run when they have to fix a threshold in a vague predicate. Not a summary of the sorites paradox. A procedure. So give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, when the procedure stops and says "this cutoff is defensible, put it in the guidelines," and when it has to hand the question back to the committee as a value call that no amount of evidence will settle.

Things it has to cope with, because they come up every single time:

- The underlying quantity is usually smooth. Risk climbs gradually with the number; there's no joint in nature where you can say "here the kind stops." So the procedure can't just say "find the natural boundary" — for these predicates there often isn't one. But if there's no joint, what makes a stipulated cutoff more than arbitrary? And notice that "more than arbitrary" can't mean "not a stipulation." I want the procedure to be upfront that some arbitrariness is unavoidable, and then to say what makes the remainder acceptable: transparency, revisability, robustness (the choice shouldn't flip if we nudge the input numbers or swap the model), the size and distribution of the errors on each side, and who absorbs the cost of a wrong call, especially people who aren't in the room.
- The tolerance intuition. If 30 dB counts as a loss, then 29.9 has to count too, since nobody can hear the difference. Push that and nothing is a loss. Any procedure has to say what it does with that argument rather than pretending it goes away. My instinct is it should force the committee to state, for each adjacent pair of categories, what difference between them is actually worth treating differently — like a difference-that-makes-a-difference ledger. If the answer is "nothing," they don't get two categories there. If it's "a tenth of a decibel," they have to own that in writing. But I don't know if that survives contact with the actual accounts of vagueness, so I want the procedure to stay neutral between them: the tolerance intuition itself; the view that there really is a fact of the matter about where the line falls and we just can't ever know it; the view that the boundary is fixed by context and legitimately shifts with what's at stake; and the view that borderline cases are neither true nor false. Include the version of that last one where "definitely" and "borderline" have vague boundaries of their own — that's the one that really worries me, because a gray zone with fuzzy edges looks like it just relocates the problem. If it does, I want the procedure to say why relocating it is or isn't progress.
- Gray zones. Committees love them because they feel like a way out. Say when a gray zone is a genuine improvement and when it's the same problem with a smaller gap, and what to do about the fact that the edges of the gray zone are themselves lines.
- Context and stakes. The same word does different work in a screening program (cheap, low harm, we want to catch everyone) and in a surgery guideline (expensive, irreversible, we want to be sure). Should stakes move the line? The procedure should say where that's legitimate and where it's just the loudest stakeholder redrawing it.
- Appeals. What does the procedure tell the person who lands one unit below the line? They aren't irrational and they aren't making a philosophical mistake — their complaint is real. The procedure has to say what the committee owes them, and what it can honestly refuse.

Then run the procedure on two cases and show me the output: (a) the threshold for subsidizing hearing aids, and (b) the blood lead level that triggers remediation. Make one of them come out in a way I won't like — my bet is the hearing case ends up saying the subsidy line is a budget decision wearing a lab coat, and the committee should stop pretending otherwise.

Finish by saying what the procedure can't do. I don't want my students thinking they've been handed a decision machine. And keep the whole thing to something we could actually get through in two meetings — no forty-page framework.
