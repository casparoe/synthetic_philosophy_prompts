ok so I'm a grad student in political theory and I keep losing an argument I should be winning, and I want to understand the shape of the thing rather than get a reading list (please no bibliography, I know the lit exists, I just can't think straight about it).

Here's the scene. Public consultation on a big housing development in my city. Local advisory vote came back about 60/40 in favour. Afterwards a guy stood up and said "the will of the people has to prevail" — his point being that the council had no business sending it to a planning committee, that further scrutiny, expert review, legal challenge, whatever, all amounted to "overriding the will of the people." A few people nodded. I wanted to object and realized I couldn't say exactly what was wrong, because I wasn't sure what he'd even claimed. Maybe nothing was wrong with the sentence and something was wrong with the inference.

I'd like you to take the role of a political philosopher who works on democratic theory and popular sovereignty — someone careful and precise who thinks things through from scratch rather than reciting the field. Take your time. I'd actually like you to start by brainstorming freely: generate as many candidate readings of that one sentence as you can before you start pruning, then sort them.

What I want back is a menu, not a verdict. The distinct precise claims "the will of the people" could be making in a situation like that one, ordered from the trivial to the radical, and for each one: what exactly is being asserted; what it presupposes about who "the people" are and whether they can be said to have one will at all; whether it's trivial, plausible-but-contentious, or just false; and crucially whether it would actually do the work my guy needed done — i.e. license "no committee, no review, no court, the vote settles it."

Some of the distinctions I half-see but can't get clean, and I'd like you to adjudicate rather than just list them:

- the flat descriptive reading (most people here want this) vs the normative one (it ought to happen because most want it) — and whether the second needs anything like a further premise;
- "the people" as the sum of actual individual wills vs. a single corporate agent with one will of its own, the way a company or a person has a will — and if it's the latter, whether that thing exists, or is a useful fiction, or is just a category error dressed up;
- the counterfactual reading: the will of the people is what they would will if informed and undistorted, so the actual vote is only evidence of it, possibly bad evidence — and what that does to the authority of the vote;
- who counts: the majority of those who turned out, all residents, dissenters included or excluded, future people who have to live there;
- the idea that the phrase isn't a report at all but a move in a game — that saying it is a way of authorizing yourself to speak for a "people" you're simultaneously bringing into being, so the question becomes who gets to do that and on what warrant;
- and the possibility that the whole thing is a truism that eats itself: if the will of the people is by definition whatever comes out of the legitimate procedure, then the planning committee is part of the procedure too, and he's said nothing.

End with the two or three readings most worth pressing him on, and for each, the one-sentence follow-up that would actually corner him. If some reading supports his conclusion but is obviously rotten, say so plainly instead of being diplomatic.
