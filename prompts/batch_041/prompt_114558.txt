I am a third-year undergraduate in an upper-level Ancient Philosophy seminar, and I would like you to write me an exam question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. Our instructor has asked everyone in the tutorial group to contribute one draft question to the final-exam revision packet, together with a model answer and a rubric, so that we can mark one another's practice attempts; mine has to come from the unit on Plato's Laws.

Here is what that unit covered, so that you can pitch the question correctly. The Athenian proposes the supervised drinking party as an institution the lawgiver should legislate rather than merely tolerate. Wine is put forward as, in the translation we use, "a cheap and comparatively harmless test" of the conditions that master a man — anger, lust, insolence, covetousness, extravagance — and also as a means of "affording practice in them" (649d–e). He draws the comparison with courage explicitly: no one becomes perfect without being tested and practised in contests against fear, and temperance likewise cannot be acquired without a fight against the pleasures and lusts that entice towards shamelessness (647c–d). He regulates the practice by age: nothing before eighteen, since that is adding fire to fire; moderation without intoxication for the young man under thirty; and from forty onward, convivial gatherings and the invocation of Dionysus, wine being a medicine against the sourness of old age (666a–b). Sober, sedate men are to preside over the drinkers, so that modesty and shame — which he calls the most noble of fears — can do their work (671d). Against the popular view that wine was given to mankind as a punishment to make us mad, the Athenian insists it is a medicine for modesty of soul and for health and strength of body (672d). The whole discussion sits under the image of the human being as a puppet worked by several cords, with law and reason as the golden cord set against the iron cords of pleasure and fear.

In the last lecture the instructor wrote two readings on the board and said the exam question would turn on them. On the diagnostic reading, the drinking party is an instrument for discovering what a person is really like once the restraints come off; on the formative reading, it is a gymnasium in which the disposition is built by repeated supervised practice. Her warning was that the two cannot simply be added together: a test that the tested party knows about, and can prepare for, is no longer a test, and a practice that alters the disposition is no longer a measurement of it.

What I need:

1. One essay question for a 90-minute closed-book final, worth 20 marks. It must not be answerable by summarising the passage or by reciting what the Laws says about wine. It should require the candidate to take a position on whether a single institution can be both a test and a training, and to say what would have to be true of the institution, or of the Athenian's conception of virtue, for the double function to be coherent — while leaving room for a candidate to argue that the tension is fatal to his proposal. Give the question a short stem naming the textual anchors a candidate would need.
2. A model answer of roughly 650 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 20, organised around five must-hit points, with the marks for each, a one-line note per point on the mistake that typically costs candidates those marks, and a closing sentence saying what specifically separates an A answer from a B answer.

Two constraints. We have been assigned no secondary literature and the exam is closed-book, so nothing in the model answer may depend on a named commentator or on citations; it has to be defensible from the text as we discussed it in class. And please make the question discriminate against the standard move of saying that Plato is simply suspicious of pleasure, which several of us would otherwise reach for.
