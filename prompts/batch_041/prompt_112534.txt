A working paper that circulated in our department last month studies a river that is now an international border and that, from the 1890s until independence, separated two colonial administrative districts. On the eastern bank the administration governed through appointed chiefs with discretionary authority over land and labour and collected customary tribute; on the western bank it installed a salaried district administration, a head tax, statutory courts, and registered land titles. The authors find that villages on the western bank today have household consumption about a quarter higher, more years of schooling, and many more registered firms, and they conclude that the difference in institutions accounts for a substantial share of the income gap between the two present-day countries. Two colleagues argued about the paper afterwards at some length. I was in the room, had no stake in the dispute, and could not decide who had the better of it. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

1. Dara: a river is the worst possible place to look for a discontinuity. Rivers are where the ports, the floodplain farms, and the fisheries are; and the same ethnic groups live on both banks, with marriages and trade crossing the water. The comparison therefore mixes the legal regime with geography and with culture, and the villages on the two sides were never comparable.

2. Ines: those are pre-treatment differences, and they are observable. The paper's balance table shows elevation, rainfall, soil type, and distance to the nearest pre-colonial trading town moving smoothly across the border, while the legal regime jumps at the line. That is precisely what an identification strategy is for. Dara: a balance table is a table of means. What the design needs is that nothing unmeasured jumps at the border, and unmeasured things are exactly what a river sorts — garrison towns, mission stations, smuggling routes.

3. Dara: grant the design, and the treatment is still a package: chiefly discretion against statutory law, tribute against a head tax, customary tenure against registration, forced labour, mission schooling, cash-crop regulation. Because these move together perfectly, no estimate from this design can separate them, and calling the result the effect of inclusive institutions is a label, not a finding.

4. Ines: every historical natural experiment estimates a package. If that were disqualifying we should give up on learning anything from history and fall back on cross-country regressions with thirty observations and instruments nobody believes. And a package estimate is still a policy quantity: it is what happens when a whole legal order is replaced. Dara: agreed — but the paper does not report a package estimate. It reports a contribution to the gap in national income, which is a different claim.

5. Ines: the authors also offer a second design for persistence: villages where the post-independence state did or did not recognise customary chiefly authority, along another old boundary, show the same pattern. Dara: then the two designs do not estimate the same thing. The first compares two colonial legal orders; the second compares two post-independence arrangements, and that second treatment was chosen by politicians who could see which villages were already prospering.

6. Dara: and the obvious alternative explanation is culture. Ines: culture as it is measured today — survey items about trust, kinship obligation, and willingness to use courts — is downstream of the legal order, so controlling for it would block the very channel in dispute. Dara: not necessarily. If the groups were sorted across the river before the colonial administration arrived, culture is a pre-treatment confounder, and the paper's crude measures of pre-colonial settlement cannot settle that.

Please work through this move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, and justify the rating briefly, flagging any move that turns out to be right for a reason its speaker did not give. Then say who has the better of the argument overall, and identify what both of them are missing — in particular anything about what quantity the paper actually estimates, what the comparison group is a counterfactual for, and how the outcomes are measured. I am not asking for a reading list; I want the reasoning. Finally, tell me what this exchange implies for the larger question of whether we can ever know why some nations are rich: does it show that the question is unanswerable, that it can be answered only in fragments, or neither?
