So I'm a novelist, but once a year I get roped into teaching a seminar — it's called "The Individual and the Social in the Novel," and the gimmick is we read novels alongside bits of social theory, stuff about whether characters make their own history or whether the social structure is doing the writing all along. It's a good time but I'm not a natural grader, and I've got a stack of exam answers to get through before grades are due. Here's the question I set and one student's answer. Can you grade it? I need a number (we do it out of 20) plus the actual comments I'd hand back, so point by point — what's right, what's wrong, what's half-right — not just a general impression, because I have to justify the number if the student shows up at office hours. And flag anything a tired grader skimming at 1am would definately let slide. Don't worry about references or citations, the exam was closed-book.

The question:

"Social theory often separates three things that all get called 'individualism.'

(a) Distinguish methodological individualism, ontological individualism, and normative individualism. For each, say what it claims and what it does not claim.

(b) Here is an explanation of a local event: 'The town's annual festival died out in the years after 1970 because the reciprocal obligations that had bound the two founding families into a working alliance broke down; once those obligations lapsed, no single person could have kept the festival going by deciding to, since each person's willingness to take part depended on whether the others would.' Is this explanation committed to holism? Can a methodological individualist accept it? Explain.

(c) Does methodological individualism carry any implications for how a society ought to be organized?"

The student's answer:

"(a) These three get run together constantly, so let me seperate them. Ontological individualism is a claim about what there is: only individual people exist, and 'society,' 'the market,' 'the family' are just shorthand for collections of individuals and their relations. Normative individualism is a claim about value: the individual person is the basic unit of moral and political concern, and institutions have to be justified by what they do for individuals. Methodological individualism is a claim about explanation: to explain a social outcome you have to show how it falls out of the actions and attitudes of individual people. The three are logically independent — you can be a methodological individualist and a socialist, or an ontological holist and a small-c conservative.

Holism, by contrast, is the view that groups are agents in their own right — that a society or a class can have beliefs and wants and can be the sort of thing that acts. Durkheim is the classic case: he held that social facts have to be treated as things, external to and constraining the individual, and on that basis he explained things like suicide rates by properties of the collectivity rather than of the people in it.

(b) The festival explanation is holistic. It explains the outcome by appealing to a supra-individual structure, the network of reciprocal obligations between the two families, and treats that structure as the thing doing the explaining. A methodological individualist would insist on rewriting it so that the obligations are just facts about what particular people believed and expected of one another, at which point the explanation is really about individuals after all. But as it stands the structure is doing the causal work, which is what holism does.

That said, the interdependence point is important and isn't itself holistic. Even a thorough individualist can grant that what it's rational for me to do depends on what you do, so participation can unravel even when everyone privately wanted the festival to keep going. So the mechanism is fine; it's the framing that's holistic.

(c) Methodological individualism has no direct implications for politics. It's a thesis about explanation, not about how society should be run, so it doesn't by itself tell you to favor markets or anything else. It's true that it's often been used to argue for free markets — the thought being that social order is the unintended result of individual choices and so can't be centrally planned — but that's an application of the method, not a consequence of it."

That's the whole answer. Thanks.
