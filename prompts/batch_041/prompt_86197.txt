A friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake, and the argument left me with a pattern I keep running into and can't name. I'd like you to tell me whether it has a name, and more importantly to draw its boundaries, because I've been burned before by thinking I'd found a concept when I'd only found a new label for an old one.

His case, in short, was that foragers ate better, worked less, and were healthier, and that what came after was a slow catastrophe for the people living through it. What interested me wasn't the verdict but the shape of the thing, which I'll try to state without any vocabulary that would prejudge it.

A group is doing well enough under an old arrangement. A new arrangement comes along that is better for whoever takes it up first — more food per acre, more food per hour, whatever the local version is — so it spreads household by household. No one ever decides anything on behalf of the group; there is no moment at which anyone weighs the whole thing. The new arrangement feeds more people. And that is what closes the trap: once there are enough people, the old arrangement can no longer support them, so going back is not a sacrifice someone can choose to make but a mass death sentence. The way out isn't forbidden or guarded; it has been eaten by the success. Meanwhile, by the measures anyone living inside it would recognize — health, hours, how equal people are to one another — the people inside are worse off than their great-grandparents were, even though the group is larger and more powerful than it has ever been.

I've seen the same shape on a smaller scale, which is part of why I don't think it's just history. Think of an industry where a cost-cutting technology becomes mandatory once everyone has it: the early adopters win, and then the price of the product resets, so anyone who didn't adopt is now selling at a loss. Again, nobody punished them. The option they'd be retreating to simply no longer exists at that price.

I keep calling the big case a tragedy of the commons in my head, and I know that's wrong — nothing is being depleted by free riders, and there's no shared resource that would recover if everyone restrained themselves. I also know Malthus is somewhere in the vicinity, but I think he sits on the other side of the problem: a ceiling that pushes back, rather than a floor that gets removed.

Two features I can't decide whether to treat as essential. First, no one is at fault; every step was taken by someone better off for taking it, and the harm is a byproduct of arithmetic rather than of anyone's indifference. Second, the only cases I know of in which the trap has opened again involve catastrophe — a plague that kills a third of the population, after which land is suddenly cheap and labor suddenly scarce. If that's the only way out, then perhaps the phenomenon is really about numbers rather than about choice, and a case with a stable population and a ruined option would have to be called something else. I'd like to know where you'd draw that line.

So: does this have a name? If it does, tell me which of the relatives it is actually closest to and why the others are wrong — I'm sure there are several, and I'd rather be corrected than humored. If it doesn't, coin a term, define it, and tell me what it excludes: what must be true for a case to count, what merely tends to happen, and what would make it a different phenomenon altogether. I'm not after a reading list; I can't look anything up from here, and pointers would just be a way of not answering. Take a position. If you think I've simply rediscovered something with a well-worn name, say so plainly and show me why the fit is tighter than I think it is.
