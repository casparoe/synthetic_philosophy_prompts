I do the interview slot on a small podcast my friends and I run, and our audience is basically the same crowd that hung around the rationalist blogs in the old days, so if I pitch soft questions they'll notice and I'll hear about it for a month. Next month's guest is a philosopher who has spent a long time defending a very hedged version of the idea that national character is a real thing you can say true things about. Her line is that claims about a people are claims about a distribution of traits in a population, that some of those claims are both true and useful, and that the reflex of calling any such claim a stereotype is just a category error. She calls her own view "thin realism about peoples" and she's careful to say it commits her to nothing about essences, souls, or destiny. She also has a move she uses whenever anyone brings up the ecological fallacy: she says the burden is on the critic to show the between-group difference isn't just noise plus confounds, and if the critic can't, the claim stands as a legitimate prior.

Draft me 8-10 questions. I want at least one each on: (1) what makes a national character claim well-formed at all — i.e. how you fix the reference class without just picking the one that flatters the conclusion, and whether "the French" or "the Japanese" is even the right grain; (2) the feedback loop, since institutions, schooling and other people's expectations seem to be able to make group-level traits come true, and I want to know whether she thinks that helps her or hollows the view out; (3) the within/between variance point, where I want her to say out loud how big a between-group difference would have to be before it did any work for anyone; (4) the ethics of assertion rather than the ethics of belief — whether a true generalization may be voiced when you can predict how it'll get used, and whether she thinks that's a different question from whether it's true; and (5) what evidence would actually move her, since I suspect she'll say nothing could falsify it and I want to see her squirm. For each of those, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, not the answer I want. Push where the actual literature pushes. No "what got you into this" filler and nothing that needs me to have read her book.
