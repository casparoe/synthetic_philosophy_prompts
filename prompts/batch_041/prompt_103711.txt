After a career in pharmacology, I have lately been reading Plato and have become interested in his scattered remarks on drunkenness — above all the Athenian's proposal in the Laws that a properly supervised drinking party can be a test and a training of moderation, and the Symposium's use of a drunken Alcibiades to expose something about Socrates that the sober speeches miss. I have also been rereading Euripides' Bacchae, in which Dionysus comes to Thebes, Pentheus refuses him and is torn apart, and Agave comes on stage carrying her son's head. I would like a reading of the Bacchae through Plato's views on drunkenness. Does the play vindicate the Athenian's hope that wine, if controlled, can educate desire, or does it show that the god cannot be supervised? Is Pentheus's refusal to drink and to see the god a failure of the very moderation Plato praises, or is he right that some ecstasy cannot be admitted into the city? And what should I make of the fact that Plato elsewhere treats certain forms of madness as divine, while the Bacchae shows madness destroying a family? I am not asking for a survey of scholarship or for citations; I want an argument about what the play shows that Plato's discussion misses, and where Plato's distinctions nonetheless help make sense of the tragedy. Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
