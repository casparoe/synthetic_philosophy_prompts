I teach a mid-level epistemology course and have just finished a first pass over my final exams. One question is giving me trouble, and I would rather have it assessed on the merits than by how fluent the answer sounds. The night before I began marking, a friend and I spent a long evening arguing about the second part of it, and we ended up disagreeing about whether the position the student takes there is defensible; I would like that question settled before I write the comments.

Question 3 (20 marks: 8 for (a), 8 for (b), 4 for (c)).

Ravi has spent a decade working on a technical problem and is now firmly convinced that theory T is correct; his conviction rests on a long argument he has constructed and checked many times. He then reads a well-conducted study of his subfield, whose methods he has no reason to doubt, reporting that specialists with track records like his, who have held a single theory as correct for a decade or more, turn out to be mistaken about that theory in roughly 70 per cent of cases. Nothing in Ravi's own data or argument has changed: he has the same evidence and the same argument he had before he read the study.

(a) Does the study give Ravi evidence bearing on whether T is true, or only on whether his belief that T is true is justified? Explain what makes it evidence and how it is supposed to bear.

(b) Suppose Ravi goes on believing T while also believing that, in the light of the study, he is not justified in believing T. Is that epistemic akrasia? Is such a state ever rationally permissible? Set out the case for thinking it cannot be rational and the case for thinking it can, then say which you accept.

(c) Ravi objects: the study's conclusion is itself an expert judgment in his subfield, so by the study's own finding it is about 70 per cent likely to be mistaken, and he can therefore set it aside. Assess this objection.

Student's answer:

(a) The study is higher-order evidence: it is evidence about the process by which Ravi formed his belief, not evidence about T. It nevertheless bears on the truth of T, because the reliability of a process is a guide to the reliability of what it produces. If a process that issues convictions of this kind gets them right only about 30 per cent of the time, then a conviction it issues has about a 30 per cent chance of being correct, so Ravi should now be about 30 per cent confident that T. The study is therefore not merely about Ravi's justification; it is a reason to think that T is probably false.

(b) Ravi believes T, and he believes that he is not justified in believing T. That is epistemic akrasia — a belief that condemns itself. One can argue that the state cannot be rational: if Ravi really accepts that the study has undermined his entitlement to T, then he should suspend judgment about T, since to keep believing something that he judges himself unjustified in believing is simply not to be moved by his own epistemic standards. I think the state can be rational all the same. Rationality is permissive: the first-order evidence and the higher-order evidence point in different directions, and there is no single correct way of weighing them against each other, so Ravi may legitimately hold on to T while conceding that the study has undermined his entitlement to it. The contrary view looks like a coherence requirement imposed on beliefs rather than an evidential one, and it is not obvious that beliefs answer to any such requirement.

(c) The objection fails. The study's finding concerns specialists who have held a conviction for a decade or more; the study's conclusion is a statistical result about a population, and is not itself a decade-long conviction about a theory, so the 70 per cent figure does not apply to it. Ravi would be applying the study outside the class of cases it is about.

Please give me a mark out of 20 and then the comments I should write on the script: for each part, what is right, what is wrong, and for anything wrong exactly what the error is and why it matters; where the student takes a defensible but contestable line, say so, and say what a fully satisfactory answer would have had to do instead. I need the assessment keyed to the parts rather than a general impression, since the mark has to be defensible if the student appeals it.
