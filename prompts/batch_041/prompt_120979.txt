I've been hosting a little philosophy podcast since I retired — I spent thirty-odd years as a geophysicist and now I finally read the books I never had time for — and next month's guest is a philosopher who's just brought out a book on Pascal's politics. Her line, as she gave it to me on the phone, is that the famous justice-and-force fragment isn't the bit of worldly cynicism everyone quotes (a friend of mine calls it Hobbes with a crucifix) but a claim about the fallen will, and that on her reading Pascal is neither a Hobbesian nor a natural-law thinker: "justice" in his mouth never names a standard the law has to meet, it just names whatever custom has already settled, and the justice/force pairing belongs to the order of the body rather than the order of mind or the order of charity. She also thinks this makes him a forerunner of legal positivism, which strikes me as a stretch. I've read the Pensées twice in translation now and I'm definately unable to tell whether this is a real reading of the text or a clever way of making Pascal say something a modern can respect, so I want to press her on it, politely, on air.

Could you draft me eight to ten interview questions that build — the ones she'll enjoy answering first, the ones that make the position work for a living last — with at least one each on (a) the fragments on custom, and the Pyrenees remark, and her claim that custom rather than content is what does the justifying; (b) whether her reading can survive Pascal's insistence that we're obliged to obey even unjust force, and what he thinks that obligation actually is — penance, prudence, or just the way concupiscence works on us; (c) the orders of things, body and mind and charity (or whatever my translation should be calling them), and whether it even makes sense to park justice in one of them; and (d) Montaigne and Port-Royal, i.e. whether her Pascal is doing anything Montaigne hadn't already done before the Jansenists got hold of him. For every question, add a one-line follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not left nodding at the microphone.

Two things about the shape of the answer. First, I'll be the one out there saying this stuff, so keep what's actually settled among people who work on Pascal separate from what's genuinely contested — I don't want to go on air and present one school's reading as though it were just what the text says. Second, structure it as three named sections, in this order: "What I'd stake my name on" (a hundred words or so — the handful of things almost everyone who knows this material agrees about, and the handful they don't); then "The questions", the bulk of the reply, numbered, each with its follow-up; and last "If she stonewalls" (eighty words or so) on what to do if she answers everything with "that's a research programme" or starts quoting French I can't check.
