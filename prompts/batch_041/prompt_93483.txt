I am assembling materials for a graduate seminar on how canonical political philosophers would have handled problems that arose only after their deaths, and I need a careful reconstruction for John Stuart Mill. The problem is the social-choice result usually called the liberal paradox, also known as Sen's paradox, due to Amartya Sen. Stated abstractly: on an unrestricted domain of individual preference orderings, no social ordering can satisfy both the weak Pareto principle and a minimal liberalism condition, where minimal liberalism requires that at least two individuals are each decisive over at least one pair of alternatives (in the intended reading, a pair differing only in what that individual himself does). In the standard proof, if person 1 is decisive over the pair {x, y}, person 2 is decisive over the pair {z, w}, and everyone strictly prefers y to z and w to x, the conditions jointly generate the cycle x over y over z over w over x. The upshot, as I understand it, is that a liberal who also wants to honour unanimity must give up one of three things: the unrestricted domain, the Pareto principle, or individual decisiveness.
