I'm a journalist who writes about science and ideas, and I'm embedded for a few weeks in a seminar on democratic innovation while I report a feature on sortition. The professor asked me to give a blind first read on a sample exam answer from a student, since she wants an outside perspective before she grades it. I don't know the student, and I don't want to just wave it through or nitpick it to death, so I'm asking you to grade it instead.

The exam question was: "The Civic Lottery Bill proposes a 300-member Citizens' Assembly chosen by lot from a 5,000-person pool drawn from the electoral register. The pool is stratified by age, gender, and region; those selected from the pool must serve a one-year term, are paid, and can hear expert witnesses but cannot delegate their votes. Drawing on the arguments for sortition/lottocracy, explain what normative problem this design is supposed to solve and assess whether it actually avoids elite capture. In your answer, be clear about what sortition is and is not."

Student's answer: "Sortition is selection by lot rather than election, and it was central to Athenian democracy, where a lot of political offices were filled by random draw. The Civic Lottery Bill is trying to solve the problem of unequal voice: elections tend to favor wealthy, educated, and well-connected candidates, and even when they don't, campaigns are dominated by donors and media. A random sample of the population is descriptively representative: it looks like the population in terms of age, gender, and region, so the assembly can speak for the people in a way that a parliament of lawyers and party hacks cannot. Paying members and making service mandatory also reduce the class bias that would come from a volunteer assembly, because low-income people can afford to take the year off and nobody can buy their way out. The two-stage lottery is also important: drawing 5,000 first and then 300 gives more people a chance to be chosen and makes it harder for a faction to rig the final result.

The deeper justification for sortition is epistemic. According to the Condorcet Jury Theorem, if each person is more likely than not to get the right answer, then the probability that a majority gets the right answer increases toward certainty as the group gets larger. That is why a randomly selected assembly of 300 will make better decisions than an elected legislature: it is a statistically representative sample of the whole population's judgment, and the law of large numbers does the rest. Expert witnesses help with information, but the point is that ordinary people, when they deliberate, can pool their perspectives and correct each other's biases.

Does it avoid elite capture? Mostly yes. Because there are no elections, there are no campaign donors, no parties, and no professional politicians, so the main channels of elite influence are closed. The experts who testify are only witnesses, so they can inform the assembly without controlling it. The one remaining worry is that the people who run the lottery could try to bias the pool, but if the draw is public and stratified, that seems like a minor technical problem. So I would say the bill largely succeeds in creating a lottocratic body that is both representative and resistant to elite capture."
