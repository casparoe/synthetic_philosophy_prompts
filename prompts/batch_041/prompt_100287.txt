Construct an example of the impasse that Amartya Sen's liberal paradox describes, without reusing his well-known reading scenario. Set up a case with two or more people in which each person holds a decisively protected say over some pair of alternatives that concerns only their own life, and yet a Pareto-style judgment appears to recommend an outcome that overrides one of those protected choices. Lay the example out as a table of outcomes with each person's ranking, and show precisely where the conflict surfaces. Then put your example to a further test: stipulate that no one's ranking may depend on what another person does within their own protected domain, so that every preference is strictly self-regarding. Does the conflict survive this restriction? If it does, explain the mechanism that sustains it; if it does not, explain whether the paradox can be generated at all without preferences that reach into other people's private affairs, or whether the appearance of a purely self-regarding case is an artifact of how the alternatives are individuated. I am less interested in a verdict on Sen's own formulation than in your identifying exactly what does the work in your example.
