I'm teaching a unit on the timing of conscious experience in my senior seminar this fall, and a former student of mine — sharp kid, he writes for one of the big popular-science outlets now — sent me the draft of a piece he's doing on the color phi phenomenon. I'd like to use it as a reading, but I need to know what I'd actually be signing off on. Here's the heart of what he wrote:

"In the mid-1970s a couple of psychologists ran a now-famous experiment. A blue dot is flashed at the top of a screen; a fraction of a second later, a red dot is flashed at the bottom. At the right timing, subjects don't report two dots — they report a single dot traveling from top to bottom, and, remarkably, they report seeing it *change color* partway along its path. But the color change can't have been seen when it seemed to be seen, since the red dot hadn't been shown yet. So the brain must have waited for the second flash, assembled the whole experience — motion and color change together — and then served it up as though it had happened in real time. This is what Daniel Dennett called the Stalinesque hypothesis: like Stalin's show trials, the verdict is decided in advance and the trial is just theater. The rival, Orwellian hypothesis would be that you really do experience the blue dot moving and then the brain goes back and rewrites the memory, 1984-style, so you merely misremember seeing it turn red. Dennett's own view, worked out in Consciousness Explained, is the Stalinesque one, and color phi is his central piece of evidence for it: our conscious experience of the world is a construction that trails behind the events it represents, and the feeling of living in the present moment is a trick the brain keeps pulling on us. It's the same basic story as the flash-lag effect and the Libet experiments on the readiness potential. What color phi doesn't tell us is how long the lag is — that's the open question the field is still chasing. If you slow the presentation down and ask subjects to mark the moment the color changed, you can read the delay straight off their reports, and a few labs are doing exactly that."

Go through it and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's flat wrong. I'm especially interested in whether his framing of the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction is right, and whether that follow-up experiment he proposes could possibly do what he says it does. My instict is that he's got the big picture right and is just fuzzy on the details, but something about it is nagging at me.
