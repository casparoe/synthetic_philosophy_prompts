I am a high school student writing a paper for my school's philosophy and debate society, and I keep running into one phrase that different people ask to do very different amounts of work. Last month, when our group presentation fell apart because two members stopped contributing, a classmate said it was the tragedy of the commons. A week later the same phrase was offered to explain why the bicycles in our school's free loaner program were all damaged within a semester, and someone argued that the program could only work if each bicycle were assigned to one student. Our economics teacher then complicated matters by remarking that the tragedy is not a law of nature, since some communities have governed shared pastures and irrigation systems for centuries without privatizing them, and that people often call something a commons when it is really an unmanaged free-for-all.

Please separate out the distinct precise claims that 'the tragedy of the commons' could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the very strong, and give a verdict on each: trivial, contentious but defensible, or false. For each reading, I want to know what exactly it asserts -- whether it is a claim about a model, about how people actually behave, about inevitability, or about the remedy -- and whether it would genuinely support the conclusions that were drawn in the three cases above. I am especially curious whether the group-presentation case is even the same problem as the loaner bicycles, since one concerns a benefit that one person's enjoyment does not subtract from anyone else's and the other concerns a resource that each user wears down, and whether the phrase ever does explanatory work that a plain description of the local incentives would not do. Please also say which reading people most likely mean when they use the phrase casually to settle an argument.

Lay the readings out as a numbered menu I can paste into my paper. Be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the one point in your answer about which you are least sure.
