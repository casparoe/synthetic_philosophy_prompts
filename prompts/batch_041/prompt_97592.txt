Consider the two-envelope paradox in its standard form. A benefactor places two sums in sealed envelopes, one exactly twice the other, and you pick one at random. Before opening, you may reason as follows: let the amount in your envelope be X; the other envelope then holds either 2X or X/2; since the envelopes were assigned at random, these two cases are equally likely; so the expected value of the other envelope is (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = 5X/4, which exceeds X; therefore you should switch. Having switched, the same reasoning would send you back, indefinitely.

Now consider a variant. The benefactor fixes a positive integer M and a pair (a, 2a), where a is drawn uniformly from {1, 2, ..., M}; the two amounts are placed in the envelopes, and the envelopes are handed to you at random. You may open your envelope before deciding whether to switch.

Below are five candidate diagnoses of where the standard reasoning goes wrong. They are stated in full and are to be treated as the complete list of options; do not import others.

(D1) No prior exists. The reasoning presupposes that X is, in effect, equally likely to be the smaller or the larger amount no matter what X turns out to be. No probability distribution over the amounts has this property: making the two branches equiprobable for every observed X would require a scale-invariant uniform distribution over an unbounded domain, which does not exist. The argument therefore rests on an impossible assumption.

(D2) Conditional-expectation error. For any genuine distribution over the pair, the conditional expectation of the other envelope given that you observe X is not 5X/4. The calculation assigns the two branches probabilities of 1/2 each without conditioning on the observed value, which is a mistake for any fixed prior.

(D3) Equivocation on the value in hand. In the branch where the other envelope holds 2X, the amount X is the smaller of the two sums; in the branch where it holds X/2, X is the larger. A smaller amount and a larger amount have different distributions, and writing both branches with the single symbol X conceals that the two branches concern different random variables.

(D4) Ill-defined expectation. In the standard setup both envelopes have infinite expected value, and an infinite expectation cannot be used in a comparison of the form 5X/4 > X. The paradox is an artifact of comparing quantities that are not well-defined.

(D5) Symmetry suffices. The two envelopes are symmetric from the outset, so no argument in favor of switching can be sound. Once the conclusion is rejected on symmetry grounds, nothing further needs to be explained; the reasoning must contain an error somewhere, but locating it is optional.

Your task: rank D1–D5 from most to least adequate as a resolution of the standard paradox, assigning each a score from 0 to 100 for how much of the paradox it actually explains. A strong diagnosis should both identify a specific step that fails and explain why that step is not a mere slip but is forced by the setup; a diagnosis that merely restates the correct conclusion, or that locates an error only in some versions of the puzzle, should be scored accordingly. For each item, give a short justification of its rank and score. Then, for each item, state whether it still applies to the capped variant described above, and where it does not, explain what the capped variant reveals about that diagnosis.
