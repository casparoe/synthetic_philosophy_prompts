I'm putting together questions for a Q&A with a visiting game theorist next month, and I want to actually pin her down rather than let her do the polished version of her own pitch. Her whole career is basically one argument: the real engine of game theory is iterated elimination of strategies that are strictly dominated once you let mixtures do the work, and Nash equilibrium gets credit that belongs to dominance reasoning. The audiance will be econ and philosophy grad students, so technical is fine, but no softballs.

I'm going to open by putting this symmetric three-by-three game on the board — both players have A, B, C, the numbers are the row player's payoffs, and the three columns are the other player's A, B, C (the game's symmetric, so the column player's payoffs are just the transpose):

A: 1 1 1
B: 3 0 0
C: 0 3 3

What I like about it: A is strictly dominated by the half-and-half coin flip over B and C, which pays 1.5 against every column, but A isn't dominated by B alone or by C alone — and if you only allow dominance by pure strategies, nothing gets eliminated at all and the game just sits there. Once A is gone, though, C strictly dominates B in what's left, so the iterated version with mixtures walks all the way to (C,C).

Write me 8 to 10 questions, each with one follow-up attached, keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I need at least one on each of the following, in whatever order makes for a good arc:

- what the dominance argument here silently assumes about the numbers. Against column A the coin flip is a lottery between 3 and 0 while A is a sure 1, so a player whose utility is the square root of the payoff comes out ahead with A in every column — 1 beats 0.866 — and now it's the flip that looks dominated, not A. Does the argument need the payoffs to already be von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, and if so isn't it circular as a defense of dominance reasoning?
- the fact that B is what does the justifing work in killing A, and then B itself dies the next round. Does that matter for how the elimination is supposed to be justified?
- the epistemic side. B is the unique best reply to the belief that the opponent plays A, so killing B looks like it needs a belief about the opponent, not just rationality. Press her on whether "each player keeps every opponent strategy in play" is a rationality assumption or an extra one, and what happens if I've got good evidence you're irrational.
- correlation. If her action is correlated with mine in the wrong way — she plays A exactly when I play C, and B when I play B — the coin flip pays 0 while A pays 1, and the dominance argument is dead. Does she think dominance reasoning needs an independence assumption, and does that dent the belief-free selling point?

Somewhere in there also work in whether the mixture is something the player literally flips a coin for or is just the opponent's uncertainty about her (the argument sounds like it needs an actual coin), and whether she sees dominance-solvability as an argument for Nash equilibrium or a replacement for it. And if it fits, ask whether the order-independence of the strict version is a lucky accident, given that the weak version's survivor set depends on the order you eliminate in — I'm pretty sure the strict version is order-independent, but I want her to say it out loud.

Keep each question to one or two sentences and make the follow-ups genuinely follow up (if she says X, come back with Y). No requests for reading lists or who-else-worked-on-this, she's been asked that a hundred times and it burns the hour.
