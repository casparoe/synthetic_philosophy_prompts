I teach a 200-level course on the Scottish Enlightenment and I'm supposed to run one active-learning session this term, and I've been arguing with a friend about Hume's money essays for like two weeks, so I figure I might as well make the students suffer through it too. Here's the fight: she says Hume's whole point in the essay on money is that money is a veil, just oil on the wheels — more of it only raises prices, it can't make a country richer, end of story. I said yes, but then there's this other stretch where he says that during the interval before prices catch up, an influx of money actually stimulates industry, because the new money hasn't spread around yet and the people who get it first are still buying at the old prices, so there's a real effect on output and employment in the meantime. She said that's just the quantity theory plus a lag. I said no, it's the interesting part, because it means the path the money takes through the economy is doing real work, and who gets it when is not a footnote. We went back and forth for hours and I still can't tell if I'm right or just restating her point in fancier words.

Anyway. What I want is a 90-minute in-class activity for about 20 undergrads, no pre-reading, because they won't do it, that makes them feel the difference between the endpoint claim and the transition claim. I want them to run into it: ideally they play some kind of market for a few rounds, prices are sticky or slow to move, then a chunk of new money enters at one specific point in the network — handed to a few players, not everyone — and the early receivers get a real windfall while the latecomers get squeezed, and by the final round everyone is nominally richer and nobody is actually better off. Materials should be cheap (index cards, poker chips, candy as the payoff, whatever) and the payoff scheme shouldn't need real money. If you can build in a second stage where two teams are two countries trading, and one of them tries to hoard all the coins and then discovers the flow reversing on its own, even better — that's the part of Hume's trade essays I actually care about, the thought that the money stock self-corrects so the fear of being drained is overblown. But if that makes it too crowded, cut it, and tell me what you cut and why. The activity has to carry the idea in its rules, not in me explaining it at the end. The debrief should map the specific moments (who got coins when, when did sellers reprice, when did wages move) onto the specific parts of Hume's argument, and it should be honest about what a 90-minute game can't reproduce — I assume it can't reproduce the long-run neutrality claim or the actual empirical proportionality of prices to money, but tell me which feature of the idea I have to supply myself with a mini-lecture. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it; I'm already worried the early receivers will refuse to exploit their advantage because it feels unfair, or that people will just haggle instead of using posted prices.

Give me one definite design — pick the mechanic, pick the round structure, pick actual numbers for the money injection and the starting prices, don't hand me a menu of options and tell me to choose. Take a position on which part of Hume's view the game is really testing and defend it. And dont ask me to assign any readings; they need to be able to play it cold.
