I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology, and next week we turn to the species problem. I have assigned the second chapter of the first edition of Darwin's *Origin* — the one on variation under nature — and I have been rereading its opening pages in the hope of saying something useful about them, but I keep finding that I am reading through a screen of later debates rather than attending to what is actually on the page. The sentences that stop me are these, from the first few pages:

"Nor shall I here discuss the various definitions which have been given of the term species. No one definition has as yet satisfied all naturalists; yet every naturalist knows vaguely what he means when he speaks of a species. Generally the term includes the unknown element of a distinct act of creation."

"No one supposes that all the individuals of the same species are cast in the very same mould."

and, where he is describing what taxonomists actually do when they are uncertain:

"Practically, when a naturalist can unite two forms together by others having intermediate characters, he treats the one as a variety of the other, ranking the most common, but sometimes the one first described, as the species, and the other as the variety."

A few lines earlier he adds that monstrosities "graduate into varieties," which seems to me of a piece with the rest, though I could not say exactly what the piece is.

What I want from you is a close reading of these sentences, the sort you would give a bright student who has the text in front of her but no background in nineteenth-century taxonomy. What is Darwin asserting about the words "species" and "variety," what is he merely reporting about how other naturalists use them, and what is he asserting about the organisms themselves? Is "vaguely" doing semantic work — the term's content is indeterminate — or epistemic work — the naturalist's grasp of a perfectly determinate term is inexact? Is the remark that the term "includes the unknown element of a distinct act of creation" a claim that species-talk is theory-laden in a way that ought to worry him, a claim that the term is simply gappy, or something else again? And if it makes sense to ask whether the indeterminacy he is describing is in our words or in the world, say which of the two his examples actually illustrate.

Two further matters I would like you to sort out, because I suspect I am importing them rather than finding them. First, a colleague of mine — more deflationary than I am, and usually right — tells me the chapter ends by treating "species" as no more than a convenient label, not essentially different in kind from "variety," and that this is Darwin's real view of the matter. I want to know how much of that the sentences above actually support, and where the deflationary reading has to be supplied from elsewhere in the argument. Second, I have always taught the standard story, the one associated with Mayr, that pre-Darwinian naturalists were essentialists who thought species had fixed and knowable essences and that Darwin's contribution was to replace essentialism with population thinking. The sentence about the very same mould appears to attribute to his contemporaries the very denial of type-uniformity that the story says they lacked. Does the passage embarrass the story, or only the crude version of it? I do not need references, a reading list, or a survey of the secondary literature — I know it, and I would rather see what these sentences will bear. Where my paraphrases have quietly added something to the text, say so; where the text is genuinely underdetermined, say that too, and tell me what would have to be true of the rest of the chapter for each of the competing readings to be the right one.
