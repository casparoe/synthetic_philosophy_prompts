I hold a two-year postdoctoral fellowship in the philosophy of biology. It gives me time and a modest workshop budget, but no capacity to gather or generate data of my own, and my training is in formal methods — I am comfortable with the Price equation, contextual analysis, and the surrounding population-genetic machinery, but I will not be producing new empirical results. I want to spend the two years on a single conceptual question within the units-of-selection debate, and I am trying to work out which one is genuinely worth the time. I would like your help in setting the agenda.

Two things shape how I now see the field, and I would rather you engage with them than talk around them.

First, a colleague has proposed that we stop asking whether groups (or genes, or individuals) are "units of selection" and ask instead a narrower question: at which level must selection be invoked to explain a trait, given that any change can in principle be redescribed at a lower level? Her suggested test is that a higher-level unit is doing genuine explanatory work only when the lower-level description would have to mention the higher-level entity's own fitness in order to get the direction of change right. I suspect this test is circular or under-specified — that it merely relocates the original dispute rather than resolving it — but I cannot yet say precisely why, and I do not know whether the field has a better way of cashing out what "doing explanatory work" amounts to here.

Second, I keep noticing that arguments in this debate slide between at least three different questions: which entity bears adaptations; which level's fitness accounting best predicts evolutionary change; and which level we must cite in a causal explanation of a trait. I suspect much of the disagreement consists of people answering different questions while using the same words, but I also suspect there is a residue that survives the disambiguation.

Given all that: which open questions in the units-of-selection debate most deserve a philosopher with two years, a formal bent, and no data? I want a defended agenda, not a survey. For each question you nominate, say why it is upstream of other work, how tractable it is for someone working conceptually and formally rather than empirically, how neglected it currently is, and how many other live disputes an answer would settle or reframe. Where two questions are competing for the same two years, say which you would drop and why. Then tell me what would have to happen — what argument, counterexample, or formal result — for the ranking to change. Please treat the claim that the whole debate is a merely verbal dispute as one candidate answer and say what it would take to establish it, rather than setting it aside.

Please write in British English, and do not give me a reading list or citations; I will find the literature myself.
