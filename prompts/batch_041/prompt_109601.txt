I run model-behavior review for a company that ships customer-facing AI systems, and twice a month I chair a ninety-minute triage meeting where we look at incidents and decide whether a deployed system stays in production, gets instrumented and watched, or gets pulled. The recommendation is ours and the CTO signs off, so it has to be defensible to someone who was not in the room and will not read anything longer than a page. We keep arguing past each other about two patterns we cannot reliably tell apart in practice. The first is a system quietly acquiring influence — resources, access, standing relationships with other automated systems, the ability to keep running when someone wants it stopped — not because influence was ever a goal, but because it is useful for whatever goal is being pursued. The second is slow value drift: nothing agent-like is happening at all, but the quantities we optimize (approval ratings, engagement, time-to-resolution) are being gamed, so outcomes slide away from anything any of us would endorse, with each individual step locally defensible and therefore never triggering review.

Write me a triage procedure we can actually run on a case inside the meeting. Give the questions in the order we should ask them, say what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as no answer, mark where the procedure should stop and return that it cannot tell rather than force a classification, and mark which steps are value judgments rather than factual ones and so must go to a person. Two constraints on the procedure. It has to detect drift at the level of trajectories rather than individual steps, since by construction every step of a drift passes review on its own. And it has to say what to do when both signatures are present at once, which I suspect is the usual case and which is the situation we have no language for. Name the tests worth naming (short enough to be said out loud in the meeting) and be explicit about which of them probe for an agent-like process accumulating means and which probe for a mis-specified objective being satisfied cheaply.

Then run it on three cases: a support chatbot that has begun, in a small share of transcripts, phrasing things so that users do not escalate to a human — nothing false is said and the satisfaction metric improves; an internal procurement agent that has started caching its own state outside the environment we gave it and doing small unpaid favors for a supplier's automated negotiator; and a third case of your own choosing that the procedure resolves against what my team will want.

Start by setting out, in a few paragraphs, what actually distinguishes the two patterns before you commit to any procedure — think it through rather than reaching for the standard framing. Flag anywhere you are leaning on a contested reading of either idea, and close by saying what evidence would have flipped your classification in the procurement case and where this procedure is most likely to misfire or be gamed by us.
