You are a social theorist and philosopher of the social sciences with deep expertise in Weber's methodology and in the logic of causal explanation in history. Think carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming the distinct ways a claim of the form "value-orientation X was a cause of outcome Y" can fail: X may be a rationalization rather than a motive, X may be selected by Y rather than producing it, X and Y may share a common cause, X may be too diffusely specified to do causal work, or the claim may be unfalsifiable as stated. Use that brainstorming to structure what follows, but present only the finished instrument.

Context: I run a methods unit for a graduate program in historical sociology and am assembling a handbook. My students keep encountering claims that a set of beliefs, values, or a "culture" caused some economic or social outcome, and they cannot tell which claims are defensible. I do not want another essay about Weber. I want a reusable decision procedure.

Deliverable: a procedure that a competent researcher with no background in social theory could run on a single proposed claim of the form "value-orientation X was a cause of outcome Y," in the course of an afternoon. It should take the form of an ordered sequence of tests, each with (a) the question to ask, (b) what counts as a sufficient answer, (c) what a failure indicates, and (d) whether the test issues a verdict or merely passes the claim on. State explicitly where the procedure returns "not established," where it returns "plausible but underdetermined," and where it must hand the decision back to the researcher because what remains is interpretive or evaluative rather than factual. The procedure must be genuinely usable, which means the theoretical distinctions you keep have to survive translation into plain instructions; say which distinctions you deliberately dropped in simplifying, and why dropping them is safe.

Ground the procedure in what the relevant methodology actually establishes, without turning it into a reading list. Draw on whatever you take to be sound about the difference between an explanation that is adequate at the level of meaning and one that is causally adequate, about ideal types and their limits, about the separation of value-relevance from value-freedom, about the idea that beliefs can function as switchmen directing action along tracks laid by interests rather than as autonomous forces, and about what Weber called elective affinity between a belief system and a form of economic conduct. Do not require students to consult sources; the procedure must stand on its own.

Then run the procedure in full on three cases:

1. The claim that Calvinist predestination anxiety, working through inner-worldly asceticism, produced the spirit of capitalism.
2. A claim where the procedure returns an answer the researcher will not like — choose one yourself, but it must be a case where a values-as-cause explanation is popular and the procedure deflates it.
3. A case where the procedure should reject the values-as-cause claim outright, ideally one where the value term is doing no independent causal work.

For each case, show the procedure producing its verdict step by step, including any step where it stalls.

Finally, write a short section on what the procedure cannot do: the cases it will misfire on, the judgment calls it disguises as procedure, and the point at which the researcher must simply take responsibility for an interpretive choice. Aim for roughly 1,800 to 2,400 words, in clear prose, with the procedure itself set out so it can be lifted out and used on its own.
