"At bottom the frame problem is the problem of how a system that reasons about a changing world can avoid having to state, one by one, everything that stays the same. When an action is performed, most facts about the world are unaffected, and a representation that recorded each of them as an axiom of its own would be unmanageable; but a representation that leaves them out will license inferences to their falsity. McCarthy and Hayes identified the difficulty in their original treatment of the situation calculus, and it is not merely one of storage: it is a question about what a system is entitled to infer, not only about what it stores.

Following the distinction drawn in lecture, two questions should be separated that the phrase 'frame problem' tends to run together. The first is inertia bookkeeping: representing and deriving the persistence of facts across actions without enumerating frame axioms. The second is salience triage: selecting, from the indefinitely many facts the system knows, those that bear on the task in hand. The first is a representational problem with formal solutions; the second is a problem about relevance.

Three further problems are often lumped in with these, and it is worth keeping them apart. The frame problem concerns what does not change. The ramification problem concerns the indirect effects of an action — moving a table moves the things on it — and the difficulty of representing those effects without listing them. The qualification problem concerns the conditions under which an action succeeds at all, since any ordinary action has indefinitely many defeaters. These three are related but not identical, and treating one as a version of another obscures what is distinctive about each.

The standard formal responses to inertia bookkeeping include successor-state axioms, which build persistence into the representation of the action itself, and nonmonotonic approaches, which minimize change by default. Both have real costs. The Yale Shooting Problem is the standard illustration: on the natural minimization policy, a system that has loaded a gun and then merely waited can be forced to conclude that the turkey dies anyway, since the minimal model prefers an unexplained death to an unexplained persistence. So nonmonotonic logics cannot, even in principle, solve the frame problem; the difficulty is intrinsic to any system that must reason about change.

Once inertia bookkeeping is solved, salience triage is largely a matter of computational resources rather than a further representational problem. A system that represents persistence correctly can be given better search heuristics, and the wide problem dissolves into engineering. This is why I do not think the wide problem is a problem for any finite reasoner: it is a problem for symbol systems that carry too much explicit knowledge. A connectionist system, which has no axioms to search, is not obviously exposed to it. The philosophical interest of the frame problem lies in the second question rather than the first, even though the second is usually the one that gets dismissed as an engineering matter."
