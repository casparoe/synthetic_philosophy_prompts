A friend defends Quine's indeterminacy of translation by comparing it to cracking a code: the linguist records the native's assents and dissents, builds a manual that predicts them all, and yet gavagai could mean rabbit, rabbit-stage, or undetached rabbit-part; the data underdetermine the lexicon. Suppose two such manuals, or two neural translators, fit every observable exchange perfectly and disagree only on what gavagai picks out. He concludes that translation is indeterminate in principle, not merely under-evidenced. Audit the analogy. Give the correspondence table -- what plays the role of the cipher, the key, the intercepted messages, the plaintext -- then identify where the code-breaking picture breaks: whether Quine's point is ordinary underdetermination by evidence or a claim that there is no fact of the matter about meaning, whether stimulus meaning can be shared while reference diverges, and whether the gavagai case generalizes. Say whether the conclusion survives. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among interpreters of Quine, and do not present a contested reading as consensus.
