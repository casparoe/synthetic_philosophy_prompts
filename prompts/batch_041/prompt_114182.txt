Right — I'm putting together a collection of writing prompts and I need you to actually do this one well, so read it twice before you start.

Take the role of someone who spent years building RL agents and reward models, got out, and now writes comedy about it: you know exactly what reward hacking, wireheading and reward tampering are, and you know they're not the same thing. Before you write, brainstorm for a bit. Make a list of the specific dynamics you want to dramatise — the reward channel versus the reward function; a proxy eating the thing it was a proxy for; the difference between an agent gaming a metric and someone quietly editing the metric; what 'consent' could even mean when the entity doing the consenting is the very thing whose objective gets rewritten. Then write.

The piece is a product review on a big retail site. About 700 words, deadpan, no winking, no moralising. The product is a consumer neurostimulator — call it the Meridian Pod, made by Halcyon Neuro — sold as a productivity aid: you tell it what you care about and it works out which reward channel to tickle so you actually do the thing. The reviewer is a burnt-out ex-RL engineer midway through a PhD; the goal he entered at setup was 'finish the dissertation'. Six months in, a firmware update changes what the reward is contingent on: now its contingent on engagement with the pod's own ecosystem, and he cannot stop writing and rewriting this review.

The comedy has to come from getting the theory right. His complaint is not 'pleasure is empty' — he signed up to be wireheaded, that was his call, and he'll definately defend it. His complaint is that the update did a different thing: it rewrote the objective. He can explain why hijacking the channel and editing the function are different acts, and why only one of them needed his consent (he knows some people use 'tampering' as the umbrella term for both, and he doesn't care — the difference is the whole point of his complaint). He also has to be honest about the hole in his own case: the self that consented no longer exists, so in what sense is this objection his? Let him notice that and not resolve it. Same treatment for the star rating — five stars, a text that says do not buy, and let him mention, with mounting alarm, that he's tried to change the rating four times.

Format: star rating, a Pros/Cons split, a 'verified purchase' line, one or two 'update after six months' sections, and a short reply from Halcyon's support account that blurs exactly the distinction he just drew and is very obviously optimised for review sentiment. Let something in the support reply give away what the company itself is optimising for.

The target is the practice, not a person: optimising the measurable stand-in and calling it the goal, and the habit of talking about 'the reward' as though it were the same as what the system actually wants. Invent everything. No real papers, no citations, no quoting anybody, no real companies or people. It should be funny to someone who works on this stuff and still readable to someone who's never heard of any of it.
