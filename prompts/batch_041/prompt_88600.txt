So I'm in this metaphysics seminar and we spent two weeks on the statue and the lump of clay, and I came out of it feeling like everyone just recycled the same two or three moves. What I actually want is a real dialogue between the two sides, seeded with a couple of things we argued about in class that I've never seen writen up anywhere. Set it in front of a clay statue on a pedestal, two people, call them A and B. A thinks there are two material things there: the statue and the lump. Not identical — squash the statue and the lump is still sitting there, so they've got different persistence conditions — but they're the same stuff in the same place, and A says that's just what coincidence is and we should stop being squeamish about it. B says that's inflationary nonsense. There's one object; "statue" describes how the clay happens to be arranged, so squashing doesn't destroy an entity, it just makes the clay stop being statue-shaped. B also has a further point she keeps hammering that A has to answer: "the lump" isn't even the kind of thing A needs it to be, because clay is a mass term like water — you can't count clay — and the puzzle only feels real because we smuggle in a count noun ("the lump") when we should be talking about a quantity of stuff.

Then have them go at it over a case I've been chewing on. Ice sculpture of a swan in a garden. Overnight it melts all the way down to a puddle, and then, because it was sitting in a two-part mold that someone left there, the water refreezes into the same swan, same shape down to the millimeter. The gallery's line is "the sculpture was damaged and then restored itself." My instinct is that the sculpture was destroyed and, an hour later, a numerically new one just like it came into being. Does either view actually deliver that verdict, and if so why? Does it matter that the refreezing is guaranteed by the mold rather than being a one-in-a-million fluke? And on A's view, how many things were in the garden at the moment of the puddle — one (the water) or zero (no statue, and no lump either, if the lump was never anything but the statue-shaped portion of clay)? A needs an answer there, and if the answer is "one, the water," B is going to want to know why the water gets to be the lucky survivor when the lump was supposed to be doing that job.

Don't let it collapse into "oh, we're just using words differently" unless one of them argues for that conclusion instead of asserting it — that move is usually a cop-out and I want to see it earned or rejected. Neither of them should be a dope: give each the strongest version of their line, have them respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past each other, and let them concede where the other lands a hit. If my framing is confused somewhere — if the ice case isn't really a problem for either view, or if the mass-term point doesn't do the work my classmate thinks it does — have one of them say so plainly and explain why, instead of politely working with a bad setup. No citations or reading lists needed, just the dialogue, in American English.
