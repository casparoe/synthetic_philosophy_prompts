My friend in the decision theory reading group keeps writing these little proofs on napkins and I can never tell if they're brilliant or garbage. This is last week's. We'd been going back and forth about whether prices can 'contain' information, and he said fine, watch this.

The setup: one share of a small company. It pays $100 if the merger goes through and $10 if it falls apart. Priya and Marco both start out thinking it's 50/50. Each has done homework the other hasn't — Priya went through the filings, Marco called around to suppliers. They both know the other is rational, and neither thinks the other is a mark. Then:

(1) Priya offers to buy Marco's share at some price p.
(2) If Priya is rational she must think the share is worth more than p, else why offer.
(3) If Marco is rational and accepts, he must think it's worth less than p, else why sell.
(4) They're equally good at reasoning from the public stuff.
(5) So the only way they end up on opposite sides of the same price is if one of them knows something the other doesn't — private information.
(6) But Marco knows Priya offering is evidence she got good news, so he marks his own estimate up. Priya knows Marco will do that, so she marks hers down. Marco sees that and marks up again, and so on.
(7) The only place that regress can stop is where no gap is left — where neither of them thinks the trade is a good deal anymore.
(8) So two rational people starting from the same beliefs never trade on private information.
(9) And since any disagreement between them is ultimately just a difference in information, it scales: rational people with the same priors don't make speculative trades.
(10) But theres a stock market and people trade on it constantly. So one of these steps is broken.

He says (10) just shows my priors about priors are wrong. I think there's alot going on in there but I keep losing the thread. Which step do I cut, and what does cutting it cost me? I'd like to keep (8) if I can, since that feels like the actual meat, but I don't want to be the guy insisting the stock market isn't real.

Don't just pick whichever step makes me feel best — if the argument is actually sound and my gut is the confused part, or if I've even set it up wrong, say so straight. And tell me how confident you actually are that the step you name is the right one to cut, not just the tidiest.
