I am a novelist, about two-thirds of the way through a book whose protagonist is a marine biologist who has spent her working life on octopuses, and I have run into a philosophical difficulty that I would rather resolve properly than paper over with atmosphere. The novel turns on a real episode I have been reading about with some care: the decision in Britain, a few years ago, to bring cephalopods and decapods — octopuses and lobsters among them — inside the scope of animal welfare law alongside the vertebrates, on the ground that they are sentient. The change followed a scientific review which found the evidence for sentience in octopuses to be strong, and the legislation was drafted in terms of sentience rather than of intelligence, reasoning or self-consciousness; my book is set partly in a research aquarium and partly in a committee room, which is why the wording of the statute matters to me. What I want from you is a case analysis rather than a summary. Take Descartes' account of animals — the animal machine, and the two tests he proposed for distinguishing a creature that thinks from a creature that merely works, one of them being genuine language and the other the capacity to act with general adaptability across novel situations — and use it as a lens on what those reviewers and legislators were actually claiming. I am aware of the common caricature and want to avoid it: on the more careful reading, Descartes did not deny animals sensation as such, and allowed them fear, hunger, anger and the rest, denying them thought — an immaterial mind — rather than feeling. My protagonist has a distinction she keeps pressing on people, between a creature that suffers and a creature that can mind that it suffers, and I suspect Descartes' line runs exactly between those two. So: does it? Which parts of the modern case would the framework endorse, which would it call a category error, and what does the framework imply was really at stake in drafting the statute around sentience rather than around intelligence?

And then turn it round, because I need the novel's argument to cut both ways. What does the episode do to the position? The awkward fact, as I understand it, is that much of the behavioural evidence for cephalopod sentience — flexible problem-solving, tool use, apparent play, apparent boredom — is evidence of precisely the adaptability that Descartes treated as a mark of thought rather than of feeling; so does the case for protecting the octopus undercut the Cartesian line, or merely miss it? Is there a version of Cartesianism that survives conceding sentience to an octopus while withholding thought, or does the concession that the creature has a point of view of its own do the framework in? I am not asking for a survey of the literature on animal minds, and please give me no reading list — I want the mapping done carefully, with the players and the claims kept distinct from one another, and with a candid account of the places where the framework simply has nothing to say rather than being either confirmed or refuted. Write it in British English.
