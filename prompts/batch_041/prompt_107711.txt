I spent most of last night arguing with a close friend who is a psychiatric nurse, and we kept circling back to Thomas Szasz's old claim that mental illness is a myth—not because we wanted to settle whether he was right, but because we could not agree on what our legal and medical institutions would even be committed to if he were. Here is the practice that puzzles me. In most jurisdictions, a person can be locked up against their will, without having committed any crime, if a court or clinician finds that a mental disorder makes them dangerous to themselves or others; and in most jurisdictions, a person who commits an otherwise criminal act can be acquitted or punished less severely if a mental disorder is found to have deprived them of the capacity to know what they were doing was wrong or to control their conduct. The first practice treats mental disorder as a condition that justifies paternalistic confinement and treatment; the second treats it as a condition that excuses conduct and diminishes responsibility. I want to know what philosophical commitments about mental illness, disease, mind, and responsibility these two practices presuppose.

Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Does the arrangement require a realist view on which mental illness is a genuine medical disease, perhaps a brain disease, or could it be licensed by a thoroughly social or normative account of psychiatric categories? Does it require that mental disorder undermine free will or agency in a way ordinary physical illness does not, or can it be explained by a narrower claim about rationality, information-processing, or the capacity to respond to reasons? Are the two practices' commitments consistent with one another, or does the excuse practice presuppose something—about the self, about the will, about the difference between compulsion and choice—that the confinement practice denies? And which parts of the arrangement are strictly presupposed by the practices, as opposed to merely fitting comfortably with a particular theory?

Finally, suppose we replace the presupposed view with the Szaszian alternative: mental illness is not a disease in the literal medical sense but a metaphor, a way of describing problems in living, and the proper response is not medical coercion but something closer to moral, social, or legal judgment. What exactly would have to change in the two practices? Would involuntary commitment have to be abolished, recast as a form of police power justified by dangerousness alone, or replaced by something else? Would the insanity defense have to be abandoned, retained on non-medical grounds, or transformed? I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I want the conceptual reconstruction and the consequences. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
