Two rival accounts of why a pain is bad for the one who feels it are on the table, and I want them compared. The first locates the badness in the felt unpleasantness itself: the awfulness of the experience is the whole of what is bad, so remove it and no harm remains. The second locates the badness in what the pain presents and commands — it registers a bodily condition as a threat or a defect and presses you to act — so the badness can survive the loss of the felt awfulness. The test case is pain asymbolia, in which some patients with particular brain injuries report feeling pain, sometimes severe, yet are untroubled by it and show no drive to escape it. Compare the two accounts on that case: which does it support, and which can accommodate it only by redescribing the evidence? As you go, distinguish a pain's sensory quality, its felt awfulness, and its motivational pull, and say which of the three each account treats as the bearer of the badness — and keep clear the difference between what is bad for a subject and what merely feels bad to one. Then press both accounts with a case the clinic does not supply: a subject whose pain keeps its quality and its pull to act but loses every trace of felt unpleasantness. Can either account make that state intelligible, and what would it imply about whether such a pain is bad for its subject?
