I work in contracts for a small construction firm — no philosophy training, just a habit of arguing about this stuff with a friend who did a minor in it — and I have arrived at a set of views about lying and misleading that I would like audited before I stake anything on them. Here is where I have landed. (1) I am convinced there is a real moral difference between lying and merely misleading: misleading someone can be a small fault, a bit of social grease, while lying is a serious betrayal. (2) I think the reason lying is wrong is not that the words are false but that you are turning a person's trust in you against them; falsity is just the usual mechanism. (3) I think a statement can be a lie even when every word of it is true — if I pick my true words so that you will draw a false conclusion, I have lied to you, and the fact that a suspicious listener could have parsed me correctly is no defense. (The case that convinced me: a landlord tells a prospective tenant that the plumbing was overhauled in 2019 when in fact one pipe was replaced, counting on the tenant to hear that all of it was. I call that a lie.) (4) I think what ultimately matters, in the end, is whether the hearer is left with a false picture; the particular words are only a means to that end. (5) In the case of the murderer at the door, I think you may say whatever works, a flat lie included, and that there is no lingering moral remainder, because the person has forfeited any claim on your truthfulness.

I hold all five of these, and my worry is that I have assembled a package that cannot be true at once. I would like you to tell me which of these commitments are genuinely in tension with each other, which pairs only appear to conflict but are actually compatible once the terms are pinned down, and how serious each real tension is. Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad; for each, say precisely which commitment would have to give, what the minimal revision would be, and what I would lose by making it. Then tell me the single change that would clear up the most trouble at once. I would rather swallow one uncomfortable concession than paper over everything with five hedges, so be blunt about where the pressure actually falls and resist the urge to declare the whole set consistent.
