ok so I'm putting together a handout for the first meeting of a grad seminar on knowledge, and I want to do it backwards from usual — before anyone gets to defend a theory, I want the spec nailed down. So write me the desiderata: what does any theory of knowledge have to deliver before it counts as adequate? Not a survey of the field — the actual requirments, each one with the case or consideration that forces it.

And I don't want just the four or five that everyone already knows. I want the list to bite. Some things I'd like on the table:

- the Gettier-style cases, sure, but also the fact that they can be manufactured by the thousand — you can write a little program that takes a true belief and swaps the good grounds for lucky ones, so a theory that needs a separate patch per case is already in trouble
- cases where the only thing going for the belief is a base rate. A guy sues a bus company because their buses are 80% of the buses on that route, and everyone agrees he doesn't *know* it was their bus, even though 80% is a strong ground. Whatever the theory says, it has to say something about that, and I suspect its the requirement that kills the most contenders
- the fact that we ascribe knowledge to things that don't obviously have beliefs at all — a dog knows where the food is, a lab "knows" the results are bad, a trained classifier knows there's a cat in the picture. Does the theory have to cover them, or is it allowed to say knowledge is a human thing? Either answer is fine, but it has to be a principled one
- the fact that "knows" does work for us: we let people act on what they know, we hold them to it, we stop asking once someone knows. A theory that ignores what the word is for is probably describing a different word
- and the awkward one — does it have to match what ordinary people say when you actually run the vignettes on them? Because if yes, and the answers come out inconsistent, or vary by culture, or flip depending on how the question is worded, then "get the cases right" is itself in trouble as a requirement, and I want you to say so if it is.

For each requirement give me the case or consideration behind it, and one line on why it's a requirement rather than a preference of one camp. Then flag which are negotiable and which aren't, and — this is the part I actually want — any pair or triple you think can't be satisfied together, with the reason. And tell me which single requirement does the most excluding work.

If it helps to end with a few lines on how the usual families (grounding-and-defeaters style, reliabilist, virtue-theoretic, knowledge-first) fare against the list, fine, but keep it short — the list is the deliverable, I'll do the arguing in class.
