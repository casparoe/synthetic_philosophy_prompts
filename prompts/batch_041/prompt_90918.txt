Ok this is a weird one. My flatemate just started pricing annuities at an insurance company - she looks up a table of how many 65 year olds die each year, uses it to work out what to charge someone for an income that runs until they die, and the whole thing works because you pool thousands of people and the deaths come out roughly like the table says. Separately I've been reading about quantum suicide: if many worlds is right, shooting yourself with a quantum-random trigger never kills you in the first-person sense, because there's always a branch where it jams and you wake up, and you only ever experience the branches where you survive. Death stops being something that happens to you, even though the total weight of the branches containing you keeps shrinking.

I can't tell if these two things can be held together, or where the clash actually is. So:

Does the insurance practice presuppose that a given person's death in a given year is a single, determinate future event with an objective probability attached? Or does it only need something weaker - a stable frequency, or a stable measure, over a cohort? Which of those is doing the real work, and which pictures of chance and of death does the practice quietly rule out?

What about personal identity? The insurer needs the person cashing the cheque next year to be the same person who signed the contract. Under branching, is that still the same person, and does "my death" become branch-relative? If it does, is there still a determinate fact that the contract is tracking?

And if quantum immortality were true and I believed it, would it be rational for me to drop life insurance (I never collect it - some successor does) and buy an annuity instead (I keep collecting)? Does that break the pricing, since the practice assumes the policyholder cares about the payout the way an ordinary mortal does? What would actually have to change - the contract wording, the pricing, the solvency rules - if the whole industry priced death the many-worlds way?

Bottom line I'm after: what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it's philosophically underdetermined. Also flag it if the actuary needs a fact about me and a fact about the crowd, and those two don't sit together.
