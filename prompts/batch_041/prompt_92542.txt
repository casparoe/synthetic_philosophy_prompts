Construct a case in which two forecasters who are both committed to the outside view — to forecasting by reference class rather than by narrative — reach opposite verdicts about one question, and the disagreement is settled not by retreating to the inside view but by a fact about how one of the reference classes is constituted. The setting should be a fictional polity of empire-like scope, historically plausible enough that a student of empires would recognize the type, but invented outright: no real states, dynasties, wars, or datasets, so that nothing in the case can be resolved by recalling what actually happened. The forecasters' question is whether this polity will still meet some stated survival criterion at a stated future date; choose the criterion and the horizon yourself, but fix them before any numbers are computed.

Engineer the case so that three things hold at once. First, reference class A is broad and dull — the widest class of comparable polities over a long window — and its base rate makes continued existence look unlikely. Second, reference class B is narrower and, on its face, more apt: polities that have just come through a shock closely resembling the one our polity has just survived. Its base rate makes continued existence look likely. Third, both classes are defensible by ordinary standards of reference-class selection, so the choice between them cannot be dismissed by declaring one obviously gerrymandered. The two rates must be genuinely in tension, not two ways of stating the same number.

Then show that the case does the work I am asking of it:

1. State each class's membership rule, its base rate, and the forecast it yields, and show the arithmetic.
2. Identify the feature that makes class B's base rate untrustworthy. It must be a feature of how membership in B is assigned — a fact about when the information used to assign the label becomes available relative to the outcome — and not extra inside-view information about our particular polity. The correction must be available to a forecaster who knows nothing about this polity beyond what is needed to place it in a class.
3. Give the corrected forecast and say which class, if either, it coincides with.
4. Run the check: if that feature were absent — if the relevant information were equally available regardless of outcome — say precisely how the two base rates would move and whether they would converge. If your case in fact depends on some other feature doing the real work, say so.
5. State in one sentence what is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed.
6. Finally, speak for someone who would defend the reference class that comes out badly, and give the strongest attack on your diagnosis. Then say whether the case survives that attack, and if it does not, how you would rebuild it.

Constraints: no real historical examples and no reliance on any specific author, book, or study — a reader who has never encountered the forecasting literature should be able to follow the whole thing — and no appeal to the inside view to break the tie. Keep the scenario to a few paragraphs and the analysis tight.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: say plainly which forecast is correct and why. Then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my question.
