Ok so this is a weird one, but I've gone down a rabbit hole and I need help. I do exhibit design at a natural history museum — I make the little pictogram panels, the ones with the fork, the bed, the no-flash-camera symbol — and my museum sent me to a workshop where someone brought up Otto Neurath, this guy from back between the wars who wanted to build an entire international language out of pictures. Not decoration, an actual language, so that science and public information could get across without any words at all. His team made these giant charts of little stick figures and factories and houses, and he really seemed to think it could work as a universal language.

Anyway, now two of my coworkers wont shut up about it. One of them, the Believer, thinks Neurath basically had it right and that a well-built picture language can be a full language — she says a picture carries meaning that words can't, that a picture means more than whatever it literally depicts, and that we're just not clever enough yet with our conventions. The other one, the Skeptic, thinks that's a fantasy: pictures are fine for man, house, five apples, but the second you need "not," or "if...then," or "every," or "probably," or "ought," or a thing that doesn't even exist, you have to smuggle in words or made-up symbols, and then it's not a picture language anymore, it's a rebus.

They've been going in circles for two months and I want to make them write the one document they could both actually sign — an adversarial-collaboration thing. Can you draft it for them? I'd definately want:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — what pictures can and can't do, what a "language" even has to be able to do to count as one, what Neurath actually claimed versus what people now say he claimed, and the cases where both of them agree the picture really does carry the meaning.

2. The real disagreements, each phrased so that both of them would say "yeah, that's more or less my view" — the big one being whether a picture language can be a complete language or only a notation for a restricted job.

3. The cruxes, i.e. the sub-questions where settling it would actually move one of them. My guess is one is whether logical relations — negation, conditionals, "all," "some" — have to be carried by a dedicated symbol at all, or whether absence, crossing-out, ordering, and repetition can do the work; and another is whether the point of a picture language is to say everything or just to be unambiguous for one specific audience. Tell me if I've got the cruxes wrong.

4. For each crux, something they'd both agree counts as evidence — a test, a case, an example — plus what each side predicts it will show. For instance I keep coming back to my own panels: if you draw an empty birdcage, does a viewer read "no birds," or "birds," or nothing? Both of them should be able to agree on what a viewer would actually take away, and on whether that settles anything.

And one more thing, maybe the most important: if part of their fight is really just about words — like they mean different things by "language" or by "picture" — say so, and tell me which part.
