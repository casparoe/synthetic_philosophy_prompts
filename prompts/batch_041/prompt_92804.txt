In arguments about evidence-gathering, a move I keep running into in my circles is the claim that information is a free option: to learn a fact is, on this picture, to be handed an option you need never exercise, so it cannot leave you worse off, since you can always ignore what you have learned and act as though you had never received it. The move is deployed to support a fairly strong conclusion — that there is never a reason to prefer ignorance, that regret at having learned something is always irrational, and that the rational policy is simply to gather evidence whenever it is free. It is, I take it, a folk rendering of Good's result on the value of information, and it is doing a lot of argumentative work for how casually it gets asserted.

I would like you to audit the analogy. Begin with an explicit table of correspondences: what in the source domain plays the role of the underlying asset, the strike, the premium, the holder's exercise decision, the expiry, and the counterparty, and what each of these maps onto in the target domain of acquiring a piece of information and then deciding what to do. Then set out where the mapping breaks down, being careful to separate the disanalogies that are merely picturesque from those that actually bear on the conclusion the analogy is being used to license. I am particularly interested in the following: whether the "you can always ignore it" step survives when the agent cannot bind their future informed self to act as though ignorant; what becomes of the free-option picture once the agent is not an expected-utility maximiser, or holds fixed preferences, or cannot compute the optimal action at no cost; whether the arrival of information can be genuinely irreversible in a way an option is not; and whether information has effects on the agent or on third parties that a private option would not. Finish by telling me whether the strong conclusion — that ignorance is never rational and that regret at learning is always a mistake — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion all along, and if you think a better analogy is available, propose one and say what it buys.
