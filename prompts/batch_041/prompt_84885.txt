I was up til 2am arguing with my friend Dana about the ring of Gyges — you know, the shepherd who finds the ring, turns invisible, and then does whatever he wants — and the annoying part is that we agreed on all the facts and still ended up nowhere. Her line was that the question "why be moral" has no real answer, that asking it is like asking why the rules of chess obligate you, and that everyone who gives an answer just smuggles in a desire to be moral that the person asking doesn't have. My line was that there has to be *something* to say, and I kept saying "because it's the right thing to do," which she correctly pointed out is just repeating the question louder. So before I read another thing about this, I want the spec sheet. Not the answers — what an answer would even have to do to count.

So: write me the desiderata for a theory of why anyone should be moral (or of morality's authority, or whatever you want to call it — the thing Glaucon is pressing on). The cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the results or constraints it has to be consistent with, and the case or consideration behind each requirement. To be clear about what I'm after, here's the pile I want it to handle, and if I'm missing something obvious, add it: the ring case as given, and also a variant I thought of — a ring that hides the agent but not the effects, so the world keeps a perfect record of what happened and never of who did it (does the answer change? if it does, I want to know whether the theory was ever about the act or only about being blamed); the person who is already decent and asks why keep going, versus the person who isn't and wants a reason to start (are those the same question? I suspect most theories quietly answer one and ignore the other, and if so I want that listed as a requirement or split into two); the free rider who agrees with every factual claim and simply doesn't care; the suspicious case where someone says morality is a con the weak run on the strong and wants to know why it binds *them*; and the requirement that the answer distinguish moral reasons from the rules of a club or the expectations of your neighbors, otherwise "why be moral" and "why be polite" come out identical. Also the demandingness thing — any answer that says you have to be impartial all the way down makes being moral look like it eats your life, and any answer that lets you always favor yourself collapses into prudence, so it has to hold both ends without one swallowing the other. And I'm curious whether the answer has to survive being broadcast: if the real reason is that being moral makes you happier, what happens when everybody believes that?

Keep the weight on the criteria — the cases and why each one is a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one side. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just something one camp happens to want, and please tell me if some pair of them can't both be satisfied, because that's the part I actually care about; if the list itself has a contradiction in it, that's a result and I want it said plainly. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out, and a couple of sentences on how the usual suspects fare against the list — but skip the survey and skip the reading list, I'm on a train with no internet and I don't need names, I need the standard. Oh, and fix the purpose for me: I want the standard for an answer that would satisfy an intelligent skeptic at a dinner table who can't be fobbed off with "because it's right" and who is definately not going to read any philosophy. If no theory could clear that bar, say so and tell me which requirement is the one doing the killing.
