I'm putting together a six-session workshop for a mix of grad students and two people from the city heritage office, and the theme is the 'man of his time' defence — the move where someone says you can't judge Jefferson or Churchill or Columbus by our standards, because nobody back then thought it was wrong, or nobody who mattered did. Everyone in the room already knows the standard back-and-forth, so I don't want a rehash of it. What I want from you is a ranking of the open questions in this area by how much they'd actually matter if someone settled them. Which ones are upstream of the others, which are tractable for someone with a term to spare, and which look important but wouldn't change anything downstream — downstream for commemoration, for school curricula, for who gets a statue or a building named after them, for reparations arguments, and for whether it even makes sense for me to resent my own great-grandfather.

To keep this from turning into a survey, here are the threads I'd want you to sort rather than summarise:

Is the defence a claim about what was knowable at the time — an excuse in the ordinary sense — or a claim that moral facts are time-indexed, so the act wasn't wrong yet? Does anything practical hinge on which one someone means?

Is it a claim about his character's counterfactual robustness, i.e. would he have acted differently if he'd been raised here and now? And does that question matter at all for blameworthiness, or is it a red herring?

Does the defence work better for what people did in their private lives than for what they did with state power, or the reverse? Is there a principled version of 'he was a man of his time about X but not about Y', or does that always collapse?

Is the individual even the right unit — should we be reforming institutions and structures instead, in which case the defence is beside the point?

And the one I keep tripping over: blame as an attitude versus blame as a practice. A statue is an institution's speech act, not one person's resentment. Does the defence have different force for each, and does that split do any real work?

Order these — plus anything I've missed that deserves to be on the list — by how much is downstream of them, defend the ordering, tell me which you'd drop entirely, and say what would move one up or down the list. Please skip reading lists and citations; I don't have library access and half the room won't read them anyway.

Also, say how confident you are in each part, and single out the claim you're least sure about.
