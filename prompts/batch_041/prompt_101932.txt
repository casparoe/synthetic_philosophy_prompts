You are grading a short-answer exam in an upper-level philosophy of mind course. The exam question was:

"Explain what is supposed to be absurd about Moore-paradoxical sentences, taking as your example 'It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining.' (a) Distinguish the omissive and the commissive forms of the paradox. (b) Is the absurdity semantic or pragmatic? (c) Does the paradox tell us anything about the nature of belief, or is it only a fact about assertion? Defend your answer to (c)."

One student's answer reads as follows:

"Moore's sentence is famous because it is absurd to assert but not self-contradictory. Since it may well be raining without my believing that it is, the content of the sentence can be true, so the absurdity cannot be logical; it must be pragmatic. There are two forms. The omissive form runs 'It is raining, but I do not believe that it is raining'; the commissive form runs 'It is raining, but I believe that it is not raining.' Both are absurd in exactly the same way: in each, the speaker asserts something while denying, in the same breath, that she believes it. Following Grice, asserting that p carries a conversational implicature that one believes p; the second conjunct cancels that implicature, and the result is a pragmatic contradiction. Because the trouble is confined to assertion, the paradox tells us nothing about belief itself — belief is simply a propositional attitude, and my belief about my own belief is no more problematic than my belief about the weather. The only real lesson is that assertion and belief answer to different norms, which is why the sentence can be true yet unassertable. Wittgenstein, who gave the paradox its name, treated it as a minor logical curiosity."
