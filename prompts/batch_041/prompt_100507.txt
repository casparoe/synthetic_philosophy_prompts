I'm assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of causation, and I keep running into the same problem with omission cases: every example I can find is either the drowning child or the negligent gardener, and my students latch onto the verdict long before we have done any of the analytical work. I would like a fresh case built to order rather than an existing one described, so please construct it yourself.

Here is the specification. The setting should be realistic — a hospital, a workplace, a construction site, some ordinary context with ordinary agents and no exotic machinery. The case must contain a harm and two candidate causes: one a positive action by one agent, the other an omission by a different agent, arranged so that the two are matched in their counterfactual structure, in the sense that had the acting agent not acted the harm would not have occurred and had the omitting agent acted the harm likewise would not have occurred, and yet ordinary judgment, I expect, will treat the two very differently. Under a difference-making or counterfactual-dependence account of causation, the omission should count as a cause; under an account that requires a genuine causal connection or a productive process running from cause to effect — the kind of view on which omissions are at most quasi-causal and never genuinely causal — it should not. After presenting the scenario, do the work of showing that it satisfies the specification: identify the harm, the two candidates, the relevant counterfactuals, and walk through the verdict each account gives and the reasoning behind it.

I also want the case stress-tested. First, identify precisely which feature of the scenario is doing the work — is it the presence or absence of a process, the deviation from a norm or expectation, or something else? — and then alter that feature while holding the counterfactual structure fixed: give me a control case in which the omission is counterfactually identical but conforms to what was expected of the agent, and say whether either account's verdict shifts. Second, give me a variant in which the positive action is replaced by a second omission, so that the harm depends counterfactually on two omissions and no one does anything at all; say whether the difference-making account is then committed to two causes, whether the production account is committed to none, and whether that strikes you as a reductio of either position. Finally, take the side of whichever account fares worse: what is the strongest thing a defender of the causal-connection view can say to resist counting the omission as a cause, and what would a defender of difference-making say in reply? Keep the whole thing self-contained and reasoned from first principles — no appeals to the literature, no names, no citations — I want only the case and the argument.
