Compare two readings of the axiom of choice, and tell me which one better locates the real disagreement between the mathematicians who accept it and the ones who don't.

On the first reading, AC is a claim about what agents can do: from any nonempty set you can extract a member, and that ability iterates, so a choice function is nothing more than a licence to pick. Since picking from a single nonempty set is trivially possible, AC is just the unremarkable extension of the trivial case to families of arbitrary size, and the only thing that makes it feel strong is our habit of expecting existence claims to arrive with a recipe attached.

On the second reading, AC says nothing about us and everything about the universe: the set-theoretic world is closed under a certain operation — every surjection has a right inverse, every product of nonempty sets is nonempty — so no family of sets is so anonymous that it resists being indexed. On this reading AC is not permission to do anything; it rules out a kind of structure.

Here is a case I keep coming back to and would like you to use as a test. A demon will truthfully answer yes whenever you present it with a nonempty set, but will never, under any circumstances, hand you a member. Is the demon's world one in which AC fails, or one in which AC holds and the demon is merely unhelpful?

Address three things: which reading makes the familiar unease — non-measurable sets, Banach–Tarski, well-ordering the reals — intelligible as a single objection rather than a grab bag; whether the two readings can be kept apart once we press on what a choice function is supposed to be; and whether the disagreement is substantive, about the universe, or really about what counts as an existence proof. Steelman both before you commit.
