For my jurisprudence essay I've got to take a real episode and run it through the Hart/Fuller thing, and I want to do the East German border guards rather than the grudge informer that everyone else in the seminar is doing. Facts as I understand them, so you dont have to guess: after reunification the German courts convicted guards who'd shot people trying to cross the wall, and the defence was that the GDR's border law plus the standing shoot-to-kill order made it lawful at the time. The Bundesgerichtshof did two things at once -- read the border law down so that it never authorised shooting unarmed people (pulling a proportionality principle out of the GDR's own constitution), and then, in the alternative, said that if it did authorise it, it was so unbearably unjust that it wasn't law at all. The Constitutional Court and then Strasbourg both held this wasn't retrospective punishment.

What I want is the mapping done properly, in both directions. Where does a positivist actually get stuck here -- is Hart really forced to say the guards did nothing illegal, or does his minimum content of natural law give him somewhere to stand? And does Fuller help at all, given the GDR was rather good at the formal side (publically promulgated, prospective, general rules, real courts)? If the eight desiderata are satisfied, is the Radbruch move even open to him, or is that a seperate substantive argument he'd have to disown? And on retroactivity: Strasbourg got to no breach partly by reading the GDR's own law in the light of the human rights treaties it had signed, which looks like a positivist-friendly route -- does that make the natural law route idle?

Then finish by saying what the episode shows about the debate itself: does it vindicate either side, or just show that both can reach the same verdict and only disagree about the description? And if it's the latter, say where the disagreement would actually bite.
