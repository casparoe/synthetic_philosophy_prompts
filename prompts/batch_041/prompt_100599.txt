For most of my career I did empirical work in soil and crop science, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science — Popper first, then the literature on how research programmes actually survive their failed predictions — which I had assumed would stay a private pleasure. It has not, because I sit on the scientific advisory council of a small foundation that funds long-horizon modelling of food and resource systems, and the reading has become a live problem. One of our grantees has spent a decade on a model whose core mechanism is essentially Malthusian: population and demand growth outrun the land and water that can be brought under cultivation, and the system overshoots into a collapse whose timing is set by the gap between the two curves. The model's central prediction — that a specified set of regions would cross a scarcity threshold by a date the team itself fixed in advance — has now come and gone. Several intermediate indicators did move in the predicted direction; the collapse did not occur; and the team's explanation is that it has been deferred by factors the model treats as exogenous (trade, fertiliser, and a fertility decline they say the model anticipates in the long run). They are asking for a five-year renewal and, more awkwardly, for the council to describe their framework in our next annual report as broadly vindicated. The council is split, and I notice that everyone reaches for the same phrase — that the theory was right but early — without anyone saying what would count as too late.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. Recommend a concrete course of action — renewal as it stands, renewal on stated conditions, or decline — with a time frame and the reasoning behind it, and engage the right-but-early defence at its strongest rather than merely repeating that a prediction which fails is a failed prediction. In particular, I would like you to say whether the distinction between a programme that stays progressive by generating new and genuinely risky predictions and one that persists only by patching its protective belt can be made operational for a funding decision, and if so how: what the team would have to commit to in advance, and what observations over the coming five years ought to count against them. Take a definite position and defend it rather than laying the options out and leaving the choice to us, and keep the whole thing to 650 words or fewer.
