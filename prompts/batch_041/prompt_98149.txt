And here's what this student turned in, essentially verbatim:

"Block distinguished phenomenal consciousness (P-consciousness), which is what it's like to be in a state — the felt redness of red — from access consciousness (A-consciousness), which is a state's being poised for use in reasoning, behavioral control, and verbal report. His point was that these can come apart and that conflating them causes a lot of bad theorizing.

Global workspace theory, as developed by Baars and later Dehaene, says a content becomes conscious when it is broadcast from a local processor to a global workspace that many other processes can access. Dehaene's 'ignition' — a sudden, widespread, self-amplifying pattern of activity — is the neural signature of this broadcast. So the theory's core commitment is functional: a content is conscious when it is globally available. That makes it squarely a theory of A-consciousness. Contents processed only locally — say, a masked word that activates visual cortex but never ignites the workspace — aren't conscious in the theory's sense.

Now, Block himself is a leading proponent of GWT, and he leans on Sperling's partial-report experiments to support it. In partial report, subjects can only report a few of the letters they seem to see, but they can report any of them if cued quickly. Block argues this shows that P-consciousness overflows A-consciousness: subjects phenomenally see all the letters but can access only a few. This is exactly the prediction GWT makes, since the unaccessed letters never entered the workspace.

As for the hard problem, GWT basically handles it: once a content is globally broadcast there's nothing further left to explain, because the broadcast just is the phenomenal experience. Ignition isn't merely correlated with the felt quality, it constitutes it. So GWT is a full theory of consciousness, not merely a theory of access."
