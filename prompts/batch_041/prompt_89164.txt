You are an expert in the economics of performance measurement and incentive design — someone who understands, at the level of mechanism rather than slogan, why a proxy metric comes apart from the thing it is meant to track once people begin optimizing against it — and you are also a practiced satirist with a deadpan corporate ear. Think carefully and brainstorm before you write.

Your task is to produce a mock marketing brochure / FAQ, roughly 650 words, for an invented consultancy (give it a plausible, faintly self-congratulatory name) that sells "Goodhart-proof" performance dashboards to firms, hospital systems, and school districts. The brochure is a numbered Q&A: a question a prospective client would ask, followed by an answer in the unctuous, reassuring register of enterprise software copy. Keep the irony sustained and legible: no winking at the reader, no asides, no stated moral at the end, no line that steps outside the voice.

Before drafting, work out — silently — the several distinct mechanisms by which a measure and its target can drift apart under optimization pressure, and decide which ones the brochure will dramatize as selling points. Your brochure should quietly embody at least the following three, each described in plain language rather than by any technical label:

1. The way the act of selecting on the measure reshapes the population being measured, so that the correlation that held before you started rewarding the measure no longer holds afterward.
2. The way optimizing drives you toward the extremes of the distribution, where the measure and the goal were never tightly coupled to begin with.
3. The way the measure and the goal can share a common cause that you are able to decouple — so the needle moves without the thing moving.

Requirements for the piece:

- Each of the three mechanisms above should appear as the unexamined content of a confident answer, presented as a feature rather than a flaw.
- Include a client testimonial that, read attentively, is a confession of precisely the failure the product claims to prevent.
- Include one question about what happens when customers start gaming the dashboard, answered by selling them a second dashboard; and one question about how the consultancy measures its own success — which should turn out to be measured by exactly the same kind of proxy it warns against.
- The firm should never acknowledge the irony of its own name and premise. It believes it is the exception.
- The humor must come from getting the mechanics right, not from inventing absurd features: every promise the brochure makes should be a real property of metric-based management taken seriously and sold straight. A specialist in performance measurement should recognize the accuracy and find it fair; a reader who has never encountered the underlying law should be able to infer it from the brochure alone.
- Do not name any real company, product, or person, and do not attribute the phenomenon to any author or field.

Length roughly 650 words, formatted as a titled brochure with numbered questions and answers.
