An objection to Aumann's agreement theorem was put to me recently, and I want your credence that it is sound — the number first, then the derivation.

The objection runs: Aumann's theorem says that agents who share a prior and whose posterior credences are common knowledge cannot disagree. But common knowledge is an infinite hierarchy — I know that you know that I know, without end — and no finite agent ever completes an infinite hierarchy. So the theorem's antecedent is never satisfied by actual people, and a theorem whose antecedent is never satisfied has no bearing on why informed, honest people disagree.

Please give your credence that this objection is sound. Then decompose it: (i) the probability that common knowledge genuinely requires an infinite hierarchy rather than some weaker condition; (ii) the probability that such a hierarchy is unattainable for finite agents; (iii) the probability that the antecedent is therefore never in fact satisfied; and (iv) the probability that the closing inference — never satisfied, so no bearing on reality — is good even if (i)–(iii) all hold. One line of justification for each, how they combine, and which term your number is most sensitive to. I put the objection at about 0.35; a colleague puts it near 0.7, so tell me which sub-question most likely carries that disagreement.
