I have been thinking on my own about a game that seems to break the usual way of calculating expected value. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time. If it lands heads on the nth toss, the payoff is 2^n divided by n dollars, and the sign alternates: you win that amount when n is odd and lose it when n is even. Since the probability of heads on the nth toss is 1/2^n, the expected value is the sum 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to about 0.693. But the convergence is only conditional: by rearranging the order in which the outcomes are added, the same terms can be made to sum to any number at all, or to diverge to positive or negative infinity. So the game seems to have no well-defined expected value, even though every individual outcome is perfectly definite and the probabilities are perfectly definite. I am told this is called the Pasadena game, and that it is a serious problem for decision theory.

Please describe the most important implications of this for the way I should think about expected value and rational choice. I am particularly interested in what it implies about the following: (1) the idea that every uncertain prospect has a definite expected value; (2) the idea that a rational person should always choose the option with the highest expected value; (3) the idea that a decision problem is well-defined only if it specifies a complete ranking of the options; and (4) how I should actually decide whether to play such a game if I am offered it, given that the expected value is undefined. I am not looking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I want the conceptual implications explained clearly, with the reasoning behind them, and with attention to how this game differs from the St. Petersburg game, where the expected value is infinite rather than undefined. Please end with a single sentence, in the form \answer{...}, that states what you take to be the single most important implication.
