I'm running a session for a philosophy of mind reading group next week — all grad students, they've read a bit of Dennett and Chalmers, but none of them have ever looked at an EEG in their lives. Topic is Libet-style experiments and what they do and dont show about free will, and I want a plan for the session, not the explanation itself.

What I need: the order the ideas have to come in so that by the time we hit the actual result the room feels the pinch rather than the punchline, the one experiment to hang the whole thing on and what it has to have and lack to carry that weight, the misconceptions they'll walk in with or form during the session — in the order they'll actually form them — and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off, plus one question to ask at the end that tells me whether it landed, and what I should openly say we're not getting into at this level.

A few things I care about. First, I don't want the session to collapse into "Libet showed the brain decides before we do, therefore no free will" — I want the structure to make it obvious why that's a non sequitur, and I want to know where in the order that move has to be pre-empted. Second, I want the carrying example to be the readiness potential itself and the clock-report method, not the veto, and I want you to tell me why the veto is a bad thing to build the session on — and whether that's the same reason the external-trigger version (tap when the light comes on) won't work either. Third, they'll have read somewhere that later decoder studies predict a choice seconds before it's conscious; I want to know how to slot that in so it doesn't just become a bigger version of the same mistake.

The weedy stuff I'd like worked in somewhere, if the order can bear it: the difference between the readiness potential and its lateralized version; the fact that the readiness potential is an average and what that averaging does to what we're entitled to say about any single trial; the accumulator-style reinterpretation in which the pre-movement signal is just neural noise that happens to cross a threshold, and what that does to the "the brain already decided" reading; and whether reporting the time of an urge is even a reliable measurement in the first place. I don't want those as asides — I want the sequence to make each of them feel necessary.

Also tell me what to cut. I'd rather drop something than bluff, and I want to name the cut out loud so they know it's a deliberate boundary and not me not knowing.

Keep it practical: fifty minutes, a whiteboard, no slides.
