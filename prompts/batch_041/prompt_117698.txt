I am in my final year of high school and have spent the past few months reading about scalable oversight for an independent project, and the version of it that interests me most is debate: two systems are made to argue opposite sides of a question whose answer no human is in a position to check directly — whether a long computation really did what it was supposed to, say, or what a long and technical document actually means — and a judge, who is assumed to be weaker or less informed than either debater, decides which of them won, on the thought that it is easier to notice a flaw in a bad argument than to produce the good answer oneself. Before I read any further into particular proposals, I would like the specification written down first: what does a debate-based oversight protocol have to be able to do in order to count as a genuine solution to the problem it is advertised as solving? I mean the requirements with their reasons attached — which failure cases it must survive (a deceptive debater, a judge who is systematically biased, debaters who are copies of the same model and can therefore coordinate, a protocol that must eventually be checked by humans who cannot follow the whole exchange), which distinctions it must not collapse (between the judge being unpersuaded and the judge being wrong; between a protocol that returns true verdicts on the distribution it was tuned on and one that stays reliable as it is used to generate its own future training data), and which features of the setting it must be consistent with (the judge really is less capable than the debaters; no human ever inspects the full transcript; the honest answer is sometimes the less plausible-sounding one). Where two of the requirements pull against each other, I would like you to say so and explain the trade-off, because I suspect the interesting result here is that some of the standard demands cannot be met at once.

So that this does not float free of examples, here is a case I invented to test proposals against, and I would like you to use it in working out the list. Suppose the judge is itself a model trained on verdicts that earlier runs of the very same protocol produced, so that any bias in the judge is fed back into the next generation of judges; suppose the debaters are permitted to be identical copies of one another, which makes coordination between them trivially available; and suppose the honest debater's case has to rest on a genuine but rare exception to a pattern that the deceptive debater can describe in a far more natural and general way. Which desiderata does that case bring out, and does putting it on the table create a conflict between requirements that looked independent beforehand? I am not after a reading list or a survey of who has proposed what; I want the criteria themselves, marked as to which are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of one camp, together with a closing remark on which single requirement does the most to rule out otherwise attractive protocols.
