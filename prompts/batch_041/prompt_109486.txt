I'm recording a long-form interview next month for a small series I run, and my guest is a philosopher who defends a view she calls the attribution criterion. Roughly: a computation matters morally only if the system performing it also represents that computation as its own — some second-order state has to pick out the first-order computation and tag it as belonging to the system. So two systems can run the identical input-output function and still differ in moral status, and she'll happily say that a big feedforward net that classifies pain-behaviour isn't in the running while a much simpler system that loops back on its own processing might be. I want questions that force her to defend the seams of this rather than restate it. At minimum I need: (a) the regress worry — if attribution is what confers status, what has to be true of the attributing state itself, and does the criterion bottom out anywhere; (b) the misattribution case — a system that represents itself as computing something it isnt, or as being in a state it isn't in; (c) whether she takes attribution to be necessary, sufficient, or both, and what that does to dreaming and to non-reflective experience on the necessary reading; (d) the grain question — one system, two true computational descriptions at different levels, and which one the attribution is supposed to latch onto. For each, give me the follow-up that lands if she gives the answer I'd expect, and add one closing question that wont let her retreat to 'that's a further empirical question.' And commit: where the regress and the grain problems admit several lines, pick the one you think the view should take and defend it — don't just lay the options out for me.
