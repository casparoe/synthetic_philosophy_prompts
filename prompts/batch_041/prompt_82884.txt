I've been asked to help a small foundation design a three-year research programme in moral philosophy. The programme is to be purely conceptual — no empirical studies, no surveys, no experimental work — and its topic is supererogation: actions that are morally admirable but not required. The board is philosophically literate and wants a defended agenda rather than a survey, and since it intends to fund a coherent programme rather than a scatter of puzzles, questions that other questions depend on should outrank isolated ones. Which open questions should it fund, in what order, and why?

Here is the board's shortlist of candidates. Evaluate these, but feel free to add, merge, demote, or reject items, and to say if any of them is a pseudo-question.

1. The coherence of the category. If an act is morally better than every alternative open to the agent, doesn't it follow that the agent ought to do it? If so, what exactly does "supererogatory" name — a genuine deontic status, or a loose label for acts we admire but do not require?

2. Act-types versus act-tokens. Is supererogation a property of particular performances (this donation, made now, by her) or of act-types (donating a kidney to a stranger)? Does the answer change what counts as an example, and does it matter whether the agent could have done otherwise?

3. The mirror-image category. Are there acts that are permitted — nothing forbids them, you are within your rights — yet criticizable, the sort of thing it is fitting to resent or to think less of someone for? (This is sometimes called the suberogatory.) If such a category is real, does it show that deontic status and the fittingness of blame can come apart, and what would that do to the usual picture on which "optional" and "blameworthy" line up?

4. Scalar pictures. On a view that ranks acts by how good they are and treats "required" as a threshold (or dispenses with it altogether), does supererogation survive as a distinct phenomenon, or is the whole category an artefact of a threshold conception of duty?

5. Collective and institutional agents. Can states, firms, or universities act beyond what is required of them — grant asylum to more people than the law demands, forgo a legally available advantage — or is the category restricted to individual agents with individual projects?

6. Role-relative supererogation. In some roles (clinician, rescuer, fiduciary) acts that would be supererogatory for an ordinary person appear to fall inside the role's duties. Does this show the required/optional line is partly conventional, and if so, what fixes it — the profession's own norms, reasonable expectations, or something else?

7. Fitting attitudes. Is the phenomenon best captured in deontic vocabulary at all, or in the vocabulary of praise, admiration, and esteem — that is, is the interesting question not "was it permitted?" but "was it fitting to admire?"

For each item, tell me how much downstream work depends on it, how tractable it is with the tools of analytic moral philosophy rather than decision theory or psychology, how neglected it is, and what a decisive result would look like. Then give the ordering and defend it against the obvious rival orderings — for instance, someone who says the coherence question must be settled first because everything else is downstream of it, and someone who says the coherence question is largely verbal and the real work lies in the applied and role-relative cases. Say explicitly what argument or finding over the next couple of years should move an item up or down the list. Finally, if the board can start only three of these, which three, and what would you drop from the shortlist entirely?

Be explicit about which of your claims are settled in the literature and which are genuinely contested among people who know this area; don't present a contested position as the consensus, and flag any point where you are giving your own view rather than reporting the state of play.
