Consider what makes a letterform an instance of a particular letter: why every 'a' — across typefaces, handwriting, and distortion — is still an 'a', while a small change of the same kind can turn a 'b' into a 'd' or a 'p'. Write the desiderata any adequate theory of letter identity must meet. Include the cases it has to get right (the same letter rendered in radically different styles, single-storey versus double-storey forms, glyphs one perturbation away from being a different letter, malformed or ambiguous shapes); the distinctions it must preserve (a letter's category versus its style, shape similarity versus letter identity, a glyph in isolation versus a glyph as one member of a mutually constraining alphabet); and the facts it must be consistent with (that letters are learned and recognized as members of a system, so that what counts as an 'a' can depend on the other letters it is drawn alongside). Give the case or consideration behind each requirement, and say which requirements are genuine and which are artifacts of a particular way of theorizing. Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I expect the sharpest tension to lie between accounts that define a letter by its intrinsic shape and accounts that define it by its place in the alphabet — and identify the single requirement that excludes the most otherwise-popular theories. Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases rather than on scoring existing views. Separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
