I've been following the free energy principle for the better part of a decade — I came to it the way a lot of people in my corner of the internet did, through rationalist blogs and forums banging on about active inference and the Bayesian brain, and for a couple of years I was convinced it was the most important idea in the cognitive sciences. I got far enough in to try writing my own small active-inference model of a foraging agent, and it was in the debugging of that model, of all places, that I began to lose my grip on what the principle was actually claiming. The impasse I keep running into is this. On one side, the proponents: the free energy principle is a unifying law of self-organising systems; anything that persists must in some sense minimise variational free energy; perception, action, learning, development and evolution are the same story told at different timescales; and the Markov blanket — the statistical boundary that makes a thing a thing rather than a smear of interacting parts — is its technical core. On the other side, the critics: the principle is either vacuous or unfalsifiable; you can write down a variational description of a candle flame or a weather system, so nothing specifically about minds follows; and whenever the principle is tightened enough to forbid anything, what you get is a local result about one model rather than a law about the world. Both sides have been saying roughly these things at each other for years, and as far as I can tell nobody has updated.

What makes me suspicious is the way the disagreement changes shape depending on the question asked. Pressed on falsifiability, proponents retreat to what I think of as the identity reading — the principle as a mathematical fact about a broad class of dynamical systems, on which there is nothing to falsify because nothing empirical was ever claimed. Pressed for explanatory pay-off, they advance the optimisation reading — organisms are selected, over evolutionary and developmental time, to minimise free energy — which does claim something, and which is where the interesting active-inference work lives. My suspicion is that these are two different theses and that the row is partly a bait-and-switch between them, though I can't rule out that proponents would say I'm simply confusing the principle with the process theories built on it. Two friends crystallise the problem for me. A physicist friend says the giveaway is that the blanket is always findable — describe the system at a fine enough grain, or pick the right coarse-graining, and a Markov blanket appears — so the boundary belongs to the modeller's choice rather than to the system. A friend who actually runs these models says that's beside the point, because the content was never in the principle but in the specific generative model, and nobody serious ever thought otherwise. I genuinely cannot tell whether those two are disagreeing or talking past each other. The distinction I keep reaching for, and would like you to assess rather than adopt, is between a constitutive principle, one that tells you what it is to be a system of that kind, and a regulative one, which only tells you how to build a model of one; the FEP looks constitutive when it is being sold and regulative when it is being defended.

So diagnose this for me, and treat my hypothesis as a hypothesis. Is it a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'theory' (or 'explanation', or 'prediction'), or two groups answering different questions under a single heading — one asking what a science of mind must explain, the other asking what a model of a brain may posit? Is a hidden formal or empirical question doing the real work, something about coarse-graining, or about what accepting a Markov blanket commits you to, or about the difference between a principle that constrains a model and one that constrains the world? And if my reading of it as a disguised dispute about standards of explanation is wrong, tell me what the fault line actually is. I'm not after a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want the structure of the disagreement, with reasons, followed by an honest verdict on what — a theorem, a worked counterexample, a distinction, or simply a change of question — would count as progress here, and whether 'is the free energy principle a theory?' is ill-posed in a way that makes the whole row unwinnable. I'd like to turn this into a post for a forum I lurk on, so I would much rather be told I'm confused than flattered. Please write your answer in British English.
