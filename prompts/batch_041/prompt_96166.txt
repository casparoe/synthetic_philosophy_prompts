Hey — I run a reading group on formal epistemology and I'm trying to make a one-page flowchart that sorts people by what they actually think about uncertain evidence, rather than by which name they've decided they like. So a "which theory of uncertain evidence do you actually hold?" quiz, but built as a decision tree with named leaves.

Some background so you don't have to assume the reader has read anything (treat this as given, and dont bother citing anyone): the ordinary rule is that when you learn something for sure, you conditionalize. The awkward case is when what arrives isn't something you're certain of — a reading off a noisy instrument, a look that might be red or might be orange, a colleague saying "I'd put 0.8 on it." One rule for that case: hold fixed a list of the possible observations (red-ish / orange-ish, or test-positive / test-negative), and update by averaging your old conditional credences in the hypotheses across those observations, weighted by your new credences in the observations themselves. That leaves the conditional credences untouched — usually called rigidity. A second family of options says there's always something you *are* certain of: the reading, the look, the fact that the expert said 0.8. So conditionalize on that, and let your model of the instrument, or the expert, or your own eyes do the work. A third option is to shove the observation itself into the space as a new event and conditionalize on it. And a fourth says there's no rational rule for the transition at all — your credences just change, and the only real constraint is that the resulting set be coherent.

Now the tree. Start from "I'm a Bayesian and a signal arrives that I'm not certain about." Every branch node has to be a question I can answer without already knowing which rule it leads to — no node may be "do you accept probability kinematics?" Things I want it branching on, though you decide the order and wich of them earn their place: whether there's a proposition you're certain of when the signal arrives, and if so whether it's about the world / the source or about your own experience; whether you can list the possible observations in advance or the signal is continuous and you can't; whether rigidity is a constraint you accept or a bug; whether two signals in a row have to give the same answer in either order; and whether the fact that a bookie can exploit your shifting credences over time settles anything for you, or whether that style of argument only tells you that you need *some* coherent rule, not which one.

Leaves I want in there — name them where there's a standard name, otherwise mark them "unnamed": conditionalize on the certain fact about the world or the source; conditionalize on your own experience; the averaging rule with rigidity; the observation-as-new-event rule; a path-independent rule that fixes the order problem; the no-rule / brute-input view; and the "everything is a likelihood, there's no special phenomenon of uncertain evidence" view.

Three cases have to do diagnostic work, so make sure they show up as nodes or as the cases that seperate the leaves: (1) a dim light you'd call red with credence 0.7 and orange with 0.3; (2) a colleague who tells you they're 0.8 on H — this is where "take their credence as mine" and "condition on the fact that they said it" come apart; (3) two uncertain signals in a row, where you should say whether the order is allowed to matter.

Each leaf gets one line, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then tell me which leaves the tree can't really seperate from each other — I suspect a couple of them are the same rule wearing different hats, so say so if so. And for the combinations of answers that nobody occupies, say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Eight-ish nodes is fine, and skip the references.
