I run the near-miss and safety reporting program at a mid-size plant -- about 600 people across three shifts, injection molding and a paint line -- and I personally triage every report that comes in, 20-40 a week depending on how the line is running, maybe fifteen minutes a report. A few months ago my sister, whos doing a masters in philosophy, told me that half of what I do all day is something philosophers call epistemic injustice, and made me read a summary of it (Miranda Fricker, I think -- the split between testimonial injustice, where somebody's credibility gets knocked down because of who they are rather than what they said, and hermeneutical injustice, where the person doesn't have the words or the concept available for what's happening to them, so it can't even get into the conversation). That part I think I follow. What I'm stuck on is a third thing she started explaining and then had to go get her kid: apparently there's a difference between a worker not having the vocabulary for something and me having the vocabulary sitting right there on the shelf -- the ergonomics guidance, the ventilation standard, the whole existing category of heat strain -- and declining to reach for it for this particular worker. She never got to the name of that one, so don't take any label I use as gospel, and if my version of the idea is a distortion, say so up front.

What I want is a triage procedure I can actually run, in order, on each report: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, where it stops and says no action, where it stops and says this isn't mine to decide and hands off (to maintenance, to the plant manager, to the union rep, to an actual industrial hygienist), and -- the part I care about most -- where it says the problem is my form and not the reporter. It has to survive being written down, because every decision I make is auditable and the union reads them. Two things it has to handle: first, that the corrective is supposed to be a virtue rather than a rule, so it can't just be "believe everyone," and I have a real budget for false alarms -- pulling a line costs about eight grand an hour; second, that some of my best evidence is a report that never got filed, because people have learned we don't act on certain things, so a blank column isn't the same as nothing happening. And please don't hand me something that amounts to "trust the person with less power" -- I've got a guy on second shift, twenty-six years, everybody loves him, who keeps telling me the new agency hires are accidents waiting to happen, and I'm fairly sure a good procedure makes me discount him, which I will hate. End with what the procedure can't do.

Run it on three: (1) a cleaner on nights tells me the air by the paint booth "feels thick" -- no number, nothing on the form matches it, she's raised it twice; (2) the shift lead thing above; (3) a guy who has filed three reports about the same spot in his lower back, and my honest instinct is that he wants light duty. You're a philosopher who works on applied epistemology and institutional decision-making, so think this through carefully, and start by listing which distinctions actually have to survive the simplification and which you're willing to let go, before you write any steps. And be upfront about the line between what's settled among people who really work on this and what's genuinely contested -- including whether a virtue is even the kind of thing that can be turned into a checklist, and where you're just making a judgment call dressed up as a step.
