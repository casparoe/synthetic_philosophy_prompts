Colleagues in the human behavioural sciences keep invoking group selection, so I want to write a short briefing that clarifies what turns on a distinction that usually goes unstated: between cases in which a group is only the arena in which selection on individuals plays out, so that group composition matters only because it alters the fitness of the individuals in it, and cases in which the group is itself the entity whose differential survival and reproduction does the explanatory work. Please describe the most important implications of that distinction for how one should assess concrete claims that a trait — human warfare, say, or a pathogen's virulence — evolved because it benefits the group, including what follows for the genic restatement of such claims, in which nothing is tracked but gene frequencies. I want the implications that would change how a careful reader evaluates those claims, not a survey of the literature or its history, and where the distinction makes no difference to what the evidence can settle, I would rather you said so than manufactured one. Finally, tell me which of these implications you hold least firmly, and what argument or evidence would lead you to withdraw it.
