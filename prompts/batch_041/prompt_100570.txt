I am a novelist, and for several years now I have been writing — and living inside — a book about a forger. The research has pushed me toward a position I did not expect to hold, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it.

The position is this. The artistic value of a work is carried entirely by its appreciable surface — what can be perceived, together with the structural relations and the conventions of reading under which that surface is taken up. Everything we are tempted to call the value of authenticity turns out, on inspection, to be one of two other things. It is either evidentiary — the original is a more reliable bearer of the artist's intentions and of the correct text — or it is narrative — the value of the true story we tell about the object, its maker, and its passage through the world. And narrative value, I have come to think, is fungible: a story equally vivid and equally well-supported would do the same work whether or not it happened to be true. From which it follows that if a forgery were perfect and undetectable, and no one ever learned, nothing of artistic value would have been lost. What would have been lost, if anything, would be a fact, not a value.

Two ideas I keep chewing on, which I suspect are where the pressure lies. First, a friend who works on the philosophy of art tells me I have collapsed a distinction she believes is real: between an object's own past and our story about its past. She holds that the former can matter to appreciation even when we already possess the latter and can perceive no difference — that there is a kind of value in having been in contact with the thing itself, or with the hand that made it, which is not the value of any story we might tell. I cannot decide whether this is a genuine category or a verbal one, and I would like you to adjudicate that rather than assume it. Second, I have been struck by works whose history seems constitutive rather than merely evidentiary — where the doing of the thing, not the resulting object, appears to be what we value, so that a replica of the object would be a replica of the wrong thing. I do not know how far this reaches: whether it is confined to performance and conceptual work, or whether it quietly infects the ordinary case of a painting or a manuscript.

What I want from you is the following. Give me the considerations that should move me, ranked from those that would be decisive — the ones that would force me to abandon or seriously recast the position — down to those that would be merely awkward; where you can, say what my position predicts that its rivals do not. Tell me which of these a defender of my view would himself concede as counting against it, rather than only what critics would say. And give me an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated against refutation — whether narrative value can always be stretched to absorb any apparent counterexample, and if so, whether that is a fatal defect or merely the price of a view of this kind. Finally, tell me which single consideration you think I, in particular, should find hardest to answer, and why.
