My friend teaches semantics and she has extremely firm, extremely specific verdicts about sentences with definate descriptions that don't pick anything out, and I want to know what she'd say about a case she hasn't ruled on yet. Her record so far: "The current emperor of Brazil is left-handed" — false, flatly false, and she gets visibly annoyed if you suggest it's neither true nor false. "The tallest kid in the class is a chess prodigy," said when two kids are tied for tallest — false. "The man in the corner drinking a martini is my brother," said when the guy she's nodding at really is her brother but he's drinking water out of a martini glass — false, and she wont budge on it; what the speaker meant, she says, doesn't change what the sentence says. "The cat on the windowsill is asleep," one cat up there and it's wide awake — false. "The author of that anonymous pamphlet was a woman," exactly one author and she was a woman — true. And "The dodo is extinct" — true, and she thinks that one's obvious and not a puzzle at all.

Now the new case: "The current emperor of Brazil is not left-handed." What does she say?

I want the rule she's actually running across the whole list, at least one other rule that fits every one of her verdicts just as well, what each of those rules predicts about the negated sentence, your best guess at which one she's on and why, and one more sentence I could put to her that would settle it. No need to tell me who said what or when — just spell out the rules and do the reasoning.
