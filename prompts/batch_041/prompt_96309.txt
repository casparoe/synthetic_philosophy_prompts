I'm putting together a handout for a small reading group on testimony — the reductionism vs anti-reductionism thing — and I want it to be a quiz that sorts people by what they actually say about cases, not by which side they claim to be on. So: 8 to 10 concrete scenarios, each with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto positions.

Problem is, if you just ask people whether theyre reductionists they'll all say something vague. The questions have to be little cases where the positions genuinely come apart. Use things like: a stranger on a train platform tells you which platform your train leaves from; a used-car dealer tells you the car has never been in an accident; a six-year-old tells their teacher something happened at home; you read a company's own press release about how safe its product is; you ask an AI assistant a factual question and it answers confidently; a colleague tells you what was said at a meeting you missed; a doctor gives you a diagnosis; and a rumour that everyone in your building keeps repeating. Some of those aren't obviously testimony at all — I want the quiz to make the reader commit on whether the machine, the press release and the rumour count as somebody telling them something, and if not, what changes epistemically.

The distinctions I actually care about (build these in, don't just gesture at the classic labels):

- whether the default is that you may believe a speaker until you get a reason not to, versus needing some positive reason for this speaker or this occasion
- whether that default, if there is one, is an epistemic entitlement or only a practical licence — i.e. is believing without evidence justified, or merely permitted/excusable
- whether taking someones word for it differs in kind from treating their utterance as evidence of the fact (one makes their telling part of your reason, the other just treats the fact that they said it as an indicator), and if it does differ, which cases show it
- whether sincerity and competence need separate treatment (sincere but clueless vs competent liar) or one reliability question covers both
- whether the hearer must be able to reconstruct the speakers evidence, or whether it's enough that the speaker has it — and relatedly whether the hearer inherits the speakers knowledge or merely gets evidence and knows the fact independently
- whether the view is about individual acts of believing or about the social practice and institutions that produce the speaker
- whether moral or aesthetic testimony (someone tells you what's right, or that the film is good) gets the same answer as testimony about plain facts

Sort on those, and make sure at least a few questions pull apart pairs people normally run together — someone anti-reductionist about facts but reductionist about values, or someone who wants a default entitlement for people but not for institutions or machines.

Then the key: for each answer pattern, give the position name (global reductionism, local reductionism, non-reductionism or credulism, the assurance-style view where trusting is second-personal, transmission vs mere-generation views, whatever else falls out) and one sentence on what that position is most awkward about. Also tell me which positions the quiz can't actually separate given these cases — I'd rather you flag that than fake a distinction — and which combinations of answers are just empty: nobody holds them, and why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored).

Keep it in the register of a reading group, not a textbook, and no reading list obviously. Also, dont use the words reductionist or anti-reductionist anywhere in the questions themselves.
