I retired from the lab about six years ago and have spent most of that time reading philosophy, and I've ended up with a thesis I'm presenting at our earth sciences department colloquium in a couple of weeks, and I'm a bit nervous about the question period. Help me get ready.

Here's what I'm going to argue. Everyone in that room has spent the last decade fighting about the Anthropocene — is it a real geological epoch, where's the golden spike, should it be formalized, etc. My claim is they've been asking the wrong kind of question. The Anthropocene isn't (or isn't only) a discovery about the rock record; it's an invented tradition in the historians' sense — it manufactures a community ("humanity," a single agent with a single history) and hands it a founding date, the way nationalists manufacture a nation and hand it a founding date. And the thing I actually want to push is the analogy in both directions: if you think nations are modern inventions dressed up as ancient, then "humanity" is a much younger and much thinner invention, and the Anthropocene is its origin myth. So "how old is the Anthropocene" has the same shape as "how old is the nation," and the honest answer to both is: younger than it wants you to think, and the wanting is the interesting part. I also want to say that the fight over whether the golden spike should be 1610 or 1945 or 1950 is, structurally, a fight about the founding date of a nation — not a criticism of the geology, a description of what's happening.
