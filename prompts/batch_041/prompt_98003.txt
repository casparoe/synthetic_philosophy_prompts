I'm a high school junior taking a college-level seminar on early modern political thought, and I've just finished a short essay on Machiavelli that I'd like you to comment on. Please take the role of a political theorist who works on Renaissance republicanism and on the history of the concept of fortune — the kind of reader who is not impressed by a clever reframing unless it earns its keep, and who will tell me plainly where the argument breaks. What follows is the essay in summary; please treat it as the object of criticism rather than something to summarize back to me.

My thesis is that Machiavelli's virtù is best understood not as a bundle of character traits (boldness, prudence, decisiveness) but as a conversion rate: the capacity to turn unanticipated circumstances into durable structure. To make that precise I split what Machiavelli lumps together under fortune into two things. Fortune-as-noise consists of discrete, unpredictable shocks — a patron dies, an ally defects, a siege arrives early. Fortune-as-tide consists of slow, large-scale drift that quietly changes what counts as an opportunity at all: the decay of a military system, the diffusion of a new technology, the consolidation of a neighboring power. My claim is that virtù is quite effective against noise and nearly powerless against tides, and that this explains something odd about Machiavelli's admired figures: they mostly confront noise, which is precisely why they look so masterful. I lean on the famous image of fortune as a river in flood to argue that he has quietly conflated the two — a flood is a shock, but the river's course is a tide. The practical upshot is meant to be a reversal of the usual lesson: the relevant skill is not seizing the moment but building arrangements that do not require you to be present to seize anything, i.e., converting personal virtù into impersonal institutions. A corollary is that a republic can accumulate virtù in a way a prince cannot, yet is no safer from a tide — which, if right, makes the familiar republican boast about institutional superiority conditional in a way Machiavelli never quite admits.
