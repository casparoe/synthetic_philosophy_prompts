As a journalist who covers philosophy of mind for a general-readership science magazine, I've spent the past year following a running exchange between two philosophers who agree that mental properties supervene on physical ones but split over whether that leaves the mental any causal work to do. The first defends a non-reductive physicalism on which mental properties are genuinely efficacious because they are realized by, and inherit their causal powers from, the physical properties that realize them; in its most recent form the view is stated in powers-based terms, so that a mental property just is a cluster of powers forming a proper subset of the powers of its physical realizer, which lets it count as a difference-maker for behavior without duplicating the realizer's efficacy. The second thinks any such inheritance is a promissory note the exclusion argument never lets us cash: grant causal closure, the supervenience of the mental, and the denial of systematic overdetermination, and the physical realizer already suffices for the effect, so the mental property is at best a symptom of the causal action rather than a participant in it.

An edited volume has now asked them to do what they have never managed in print — produce a single joint statement that fixes what they agree on, states their disagreement in wording each side would sign, and pins down the cruxes their dispute actually turns on, together with a case both accept as probative and what each predicts about it. I'd like you to draft that statement, and where you judge some part of their quarrel to be merely verbal, say so inside the relevant clause rather than in commentary.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered clauses, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no closing paragraph: two clauses should be agreed claims both camps can sign; one should state the residual disagreement in neutral wording, with each side's position phrased as that side would accept it; one should isolate the crux on which the disagreement most depends; and one should record the case or test both agree bears on that crux, with each side's advance prediction written into the clause.
