I teach a graduate seminar in normative ethics, and one of my colleagues — a deontologist of long standing, though I have never once gotten her to state her principle in a form that survives contact with a counterexample — has now issued verdicts across enough cases that I think her implicit position can be reconstructed from them. She holds that it is permissible to divert a runaway trolley onto a single track worker in order to save five, on the ground that the worker's death is a side effect rather than a means; that it is impermissible to push a large bystander off a footbridge into the trolley's path, or to use him as the sole means of stopping it; that it is impermissible to torture one person to extract information that would save five, and impermissible again when the number is a hundred; that it is permissible to break a confidence in order to prevent a serious assault; and — this is the verdict that interests me — that it is permissible to kill one innocent bystander, someone who would otherwise survive, when doing so is the only way to stop a genocide that would otherwise kill many thousands. Throughout, she insists that she is not a consequentialist, that her constraints on harming are genuine constraints and not merely very heavy rules of thumb, and that numbers never by themselves settle what she may do.

I have been saving a case for the last meeting of the term, and I would like your prediction before I put it to her. A lethal contamination of a city's water supply will, on the best available evidence, kill exactly ten thousand people over the coming year through ordinary physiological processes; no one's agency is involved, and if it runs its course no one will have been wronged by anyone. The only way to prevent those deaths is to kill one unconsenting person, who would otherwise survive, and use material from her body to synthesize the antidote; there is no other option, the numbers are certain, and the person seized has no special relationship to anyone involved. Tell me what she will say, reconstruct the rule or rules she is most likely running, be explicit about which of her earlier verdicts are consistent with more than one such rule, and say which rule you would bet on and why. Then name the single further case — not this one — that would do the most to separate the surviving rules, and tell me where your reconstruction is likeliest to fail: which consideration about the grounding of constraints, or which of her earlier verdicts if I have misreported it, would lead you to reverse your prediction. I am less interested in a survey of the paradox of deontology than in a prediction I can actually test against her response, so please commit to a verdict. Write your answer in American English.
