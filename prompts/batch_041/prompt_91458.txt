I've been circling the no-trade theorem for weeks now — the result that if a group of agents begin from a Pareto-efficient allocation, share a common prior, and their rationality is common knowledge, then no one can profitably trade on private information, because the very fact that someone is offering you a trade is itself information, and you update until the offer dissolves. What grips me isn't the theorem so much as the epistemic picture it presupposes: prices as compressed knowledge, the gulf between a thing being known and its being common knowledge, and the way the whole result collapses the instant you drop the common prior — two agents who genuinely disagree about the odds can trade all day, and neither of them need be irrational for it. I'd like you to write me a short story that takes this seriously as speculative fiction rather than as an economics lecture.

Set it in a plausible near-future city where a public forecasting infrastructure has driven almost everyone's beliefs into convergence, so that the exchanges still stand but function as mausoleums — beautifully maintained, almost never used. Your protagonist is one of the last people who keeps a private model she refuses to publish, and she has worked out that she can make a quiet living precisely because the fact that she holds that model is not common knowledge. I want the story to dramatise, without ever explaining itself, the difference between a fact being known and being common knowledge; what it is like to depend on an edge that evaporates the moment anyone suspects it exists; and the unnerving possibility that the entire apparatus of prices was only ever a machine for converting private hunches into public facts. Build towards a moment where she must decide whether to publish her model, and make the reader feel why doing so would be at once the most honest and the most self-destructive act available to her — please let the plot carry the idea and keep the narration free of exposition about the economics.

Somewhere in the middle I'd like a second character who trades with her profitably for the opposite reason: not better information, but a genuinely different prior. I want the story to leave it unresolved whether his disagreement with the consensus is a rational difference of opinion or simply a man inhabiting a different world from everyone around him, and I'd rather you trusted the reader to notice the ambiguity than flagged it. A little texture about the rituals of the near-dead exchange — the custodians, the obsolete terminals, the strange etiquette of the few who still come — would be welcome, so long as it never crowds out the two of them and the decision she has to make.
