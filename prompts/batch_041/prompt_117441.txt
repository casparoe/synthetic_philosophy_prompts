I am writing a response to the work of a colleague — call her D. — who holds that the boundaries of a cognitive system need not coincide with the boundaries of a body. The awkward thing about writing on her is that she almost never states a criterion. She rules case by case, and the verdicts I have collected from her papers and her seminars are these.

1. A woman in the early stages of Alzheimer's disease keeps a paper notebook of names, dates and errands. She reads it without deliberation and treats what it says as she treats her own recollections. D.'s verdict: part of the woman's cognitive system.
2. A woman who has never been able to do arithmetic in her head has carried the same pocket calculator since she was at school. It is always in her bag, she reaches for it without thinking, and she treats its answers as she treats her own. Verdict: part of her cognitive system.
3. A man married for forty years asks his wife to remember things for him and never checks what she tells him; the two are together almost all day, and he does not distinguish her recollections from his own. Verdict: she is part of his cognitive system.
4. A nursing student must walk to the chart at the end of the ward to check a drug dose, and then compares what she reads there with the label on the vial. Verdict: not part of her cognitive system.
5. A tourist keeps a phrasebook in her hotel room, rehearses its sentences before going out, tries them on the concierge, and adjusts on the basis of the reply. Verdict: not part of her cognitive system.
6. A junior lawyer puts her questions to a senior colleague once a week and checks his answers against the statute book before relying on them. Verdict: the colleague is not part of her cognitive system.
7. A hiker carries map and compass in her pack, unfolds them at every junction, and confirms her position against landmarks. Verdict: not part of her cognitive system.

Next month D. will be asked about a new case, and I would like to know her answer in advance. A hospital has installed a machine-learning system that triages radiology images: it decides which images reach the radiologist's screen and in what order, and it drafts a preliminary reading of each. The screen stands beside her all day; she glances at its output as routinely as at the patient's notes, and she could not keep up with the department's workload without it. She does not, however, treat its drafts as her own. She reads them as fallible suggestions, examines the images herself, and reverses the system in about one case in five; she calls it "a colleague I have to babysit."

Do not give me her general position; I know it, and I would rather have criteria than a label for it. Tell me which criteria fit her seven verdicts equally well, and what each of them predicts about the radiologist; which criterion I should expect her to be running in fact, and what in the record tells for it; which of her earlier cases is the closest analog to the radiologist and where the analogy breaks down; and one case she has not yet ruled on whose outcome would most cleanly separate the criteria that survive, with the verdicts that fall out on either side.
