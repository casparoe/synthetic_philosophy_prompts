I am the senior program officer at a regional coastal restoration fund. Our board makes two kinds of grants: rapid-response grants after a discrete disturbance — a spill, a breach, a bad storm season, a disease outbreak in a shellfish bed — and long-horizon grants for land acquisition, hydrology, sediment management, and managed retreat. The board meets four times a year and commits a fixed sum each cycle; the acquisitions are permanent, and a rapid-response grant is usually spent before anyone can evaluate whether it worked. Every proposal we receive carries an implicit claim about the time-scale on which the problem lives, and we have no way to adjudicate those claims. In practice the most recent disturbance sets the agenda, and the long-horizon applications get read as requests to do nothing.

I want you to write us a decision procedure grounded in Fernand Braudel's stratification of historical time. Braudel distinguished three registers: the event, the short episodic occurrence that he treated as surface disturbance; the conjuncture, the cycle running over a decade or a generation — fishing effort, land prices, population, settlement, commodity markets; and the longue durée, the structures that persist across centuries and shape what is possible at all — coastlines, soils, prevailing wind and current, property regimes, and the habits of mind that outlive the people who formed them. His claim was not merely that some things change slowly. It was that the explanatory weight of a phenomenon depends on which register it belongs to, that the short register systematically misleads the observer, and that structures are at once enabling and constraining — the same durable arrangement that makes a way of life possible also forecloses alternatives.

Constraints on the procedure. It must be runnable by program staff with no historical training, in about an hour, using only the material already in a proposal file. It must not require anyone to consult outside scholarship, and I do not want a reading list; I want the procedure. Assume the evidence available is of the ordinary kinds: instrumental monitoring series, landing and catch records, cadastral and survey maps, aerial and satellite imagery, sediment cores, archaeological material, and the testimony of people who have lived in the place for decades.

The procedure must deliver, for any proposal: (1) a temporal signature, assigning the problem, the proposed intervention, and the expected effect each to a register, with the evidence supporting each assignment and the evidence that would overturn it; (2) explicit rules for the mismatches, both the event-scale intervention justified by a structure-scale claim and the structure-scale intervention offered to fix an event; (3) a test that separates a slow trend from a structure — persistence alone is not enough, so say what else has to be shown; (4) a safeguard against the abuse I worry about most, in which a recent and real harm is dismissed as a mere event, or as a mere fluctuation, and the file is closed; and (5) a short list of the points where the procedure cannot decide and must be escalated to the board, with the exact question to put to them.

One complication to build around. Our charter forbids funding an intervention whose expected effects are shorter-lived than the disturbance it addresses. That clause was written to stop us from patching symptoms, but it gives every applicant an incentive to describe their problem in the longest register available. The procedure has to hold up with that incentive pushing against it.

Then run it, showing your work at each step, on three proposals: (a) restoring a salt marsh to its historical extent, where a nineteenth-century survey, a sediment core, and sixty years of community memory of the marsh edge disagree about where the edge was; (b) an expensive rapid-response project after one severe storm season, argued on the grounds that the storm is the visible edge of a long-term shift; and (c) a proposal to take no action on a collapsing fishery because the collapse is said to belong to a natural cycle.

Finish by telling me which of the three verdicts I will dislike, and what the procedure cannot do.
