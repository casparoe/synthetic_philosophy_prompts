Hey — I'm a second-year PhD student in HPS and I'm supposed to write up a chunk of my qualifying exam on truthlikeness, except I keep stalling on the same thing, so I thought writing it out as a conversation might unstick me. Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers of science who disagree about whether "closer to the truth" is even a coherent thing to say about theories we know are false?

One of them should be a realist who thinks verisimilitude is the right way to make sense of scientific progress, and who treats the 1974 trivialization results (Tichý, Miller, Harris — the ones showing that on Popper's own definition no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other) as a technical glitch that got patched rather than a reason to give up the notion. The other should be a deflationist who thinks that any ranking of false theories by closeness to truth is an artefact of the vocabulary you happen to describe the world in and the weights you happen to assign to different respects of comparison, and that we should just talk about predictive success or problem-solving instead. Neither should be a pushover. I want the realist to have real replies — I know some people go for likeness accounts, where you compare the possible worlds in which the theory is true against the actual world, and others try to restrict which consequences of a theory get to count as relevant — and I want the deflationist to actually engage those replies rather than just repeating "but it's all relative". Bonus if they get into whether you can even specify the target, since I read somewhere that the truth is a single point and so any distance to it just reflects whatever coordinates you chose.

To keep it from being all abstract, could you have them fight over a concrete pair of false theories? The thing I keep bumping into in my own work is the case of two rivals where one is bold: it gets the central mechanism right but drags in an extra entity that isn't there, while the other is timid: it says almost nothing false but also almost nothing true. Intuitively the bold one is closer to the truth, but it also has more false consequences, so a content-based measure might well rank the timid one higher — I'd love the dialogue to do something with that. Setting can be a workshop coffee break, doesn't matter. Don't bother with references, I'm not citeing any of it, I just need to see the shape of the disagreement. And please don't have them converge on a happy middle at the end, I'd rather the disagreement get sharper.
