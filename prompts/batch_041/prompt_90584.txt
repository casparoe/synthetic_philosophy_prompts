I'm a TA for an upper-level seminar on philosophy of history and historiography, and I'm writing the take-home final for the unit on periodization. The core question is whether the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and the Scientific Revolution are real historical periods or retrospective inventions, and I want an essay question that can't be answered by the usual 'periods are useful heuristic labels, but they're also socially constructed' two-step. The question should force students to confront the tension between periods as causal-explanatory entities ('the Renaissance changed X', 'the Scientific Revolution was a rupture') and periods as selection effects: the labels were often coined later, by people with agendas, their boundaries are vague and contested, and the people inside them often didn't use the term. I want them to take a stance on what it would even mean for a period to be real rather than merely useful -- is it a natural kind, a conventional kind, an institutional fact, a narrative device, or something else? -- and to say what follows for historiography if we treat periods as instruments rather than objects. A specific trap I want the question to set: students should have to distinguish the claim that a period label is anachronistic from the claim that the period itself is unreal, and they should have to handle the boundary problem without either collapsing into 'it's all just language' or treating periods as obvious natural kinds.

Could you draft one in-class essay question suitable for 90 minutes, plus a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme. The marking scheme should total 20 points, built around 5 must-hit points, each worth 4 points, with a one-line note on how students typically miss each one. The A/B line should depend on whether they can articulate the anachronism/unreality distinction, use the boundary problem as a real philosophical constraint rather than a complaint, and evaluate the idea I've been kicking around that periodization is a kind of 'cognitive infrastructure': it doesn't just describe time, it makes certain comparisons and causal claims possible, and then those claims get read back as evidence for the period's existence. Make the question ask them to evaluate that idea, not just name it. No citations or reading lists needed -- I care alot about the conceptual moves, not who said them.
