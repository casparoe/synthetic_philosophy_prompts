I am setting the final examination for a graduate seminar in value theory, and I need a single essay question on the aggregation of tiny harms — the family of cases, sometimes filed under the rubric of torture versus dust specks, in which a single enormous harm is weighed against an arbitrarily large number of negligible ones. The question must be answerable by a candidate who can consult nothing, and it must not be answerable by reciting the standard literature. Its difficulty should lie in forcing a definite choice and in exposing what that choice costs.

Please build the question around the following case, which I have invented for this exam precisely so that no one can lift a prepared answer from a textbook or an encyclopaedia. Define a grain as a single just-noticeable flicker of mild bodily discomfort, lasting a fraction of a second, of a kind that no one would take any step to avoid and that leaves no trace. Define the wrack as fifty years of unremitting severe pain undergone by one person, with no relief and no compensation.

The question should put two things to the candidate. First: is there any finite number N such that an outcome in which N distinct people each undergo exactly one grain is worse, all things considered, than an outcome in which one person undergoes the wrack? Second: suppose the grains are so arranged that each person undergoes exactly one and no one's life, taken as a whole, is any the worse than it would otherwise have been — does that change the answer to the first question, and why?

Beyond a verdict on the first question, the candidate must (a) state the general principle that generates the verdict and explain what makes it a principle rather than a stipulation; (b) say what that principle forces them to accept elsewhere — in particular, whether it commits them to something like the repugnant conclusion, or to denying the transitivity or the continuity of the better-than relation, or to positing a discontinuity in value that stands in need of explanation; and (c) answer the second question in a way that is consistent with the principle given in (a). An answer that merely surveys the options without committing will not count as a good one.

Please write three things. First, the exam question itself, phrased so that a candidate can answer it in ninety minutes. Second, a model answer of about seven hundred words, written as a strong candidate's essay would be — and, as I say, committed: it must take a definite position and defend it rather than laying the options side by side. Where it rests on a controversial premise, it should flag the premise and argue for it. Third, a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks each is worth (twenty-five in total), and a one-line note on how candidates characteristically fail each point — naming the specific error, not merely insufficient depth.

End the whole response with a single final line of the form \answer{...}, enclosing a short label for the position the model answer finally defends.
