I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy writing on truth and self-reference, and there's a slogan floating around my reading group that I cannot get a grip on. A colleague of mine, defending a broadly deflationary line about truth, put it to us roughly this way: the word 'true' is nothing more than a disquotational convenience, whose whole function is to let us endorse a list of claims at once, or gesture at an infinite conjunction, without repeating ourselves; once you see that, he said, the Liar stops being a deep problem for the theory of truth and becomes a bookkeeping glitch. Everyone in the room nodded. I did not, mainly because the second half of what he said seems to be doing all the work and nobody explained how it follows from the first half.

What I want from you is a menu of the distinct precise claims that this slogan could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical, with a verdict on each: platitude, substantive but defensible, contentious, or false. At the trivial end I take it to include the claim that in simple cases, saying of a particular sentence that it is true is interchangeable with simply asserting it; somewhere in the middle, that a truth predicate is in principle eliminable in favour of an expressive device for generalizing; further out, that truth is not a property of anything, that it has no nature to investigate, or that there is nothing for a theory of truth to explain at all. Where a reading is only intelligible against a background commitment (to propositions, to a hierarchy of languages, to groundedness, or to the idea that some declarative sentences fail to express propositions), say which commitment that is, and whether the reading survives without it.

The part I really care about is the inference. For each reading, tell me whether it would actually license my colleague's move, that the Liar is a housekeeping problem rather than a genuine test of a theory of truth, or whether it makes the Liar harder to contain; and whether the reading survives the strengthened form of the paradox, which turns on the very notion of 'not true' that the reading has just invoked. I am particularly interested in which of the standard deflationary packages (redundancy, disquotationalism, minimalism, or any cousin you think belongs in the family) can consistently say of the Liar sentence that it is not true, what each of them has to add to the base slogan in order to say it, and whether that addition is a quiet concession to a non-deflationary account of truth. I would rather have the menu with the costs attached than a single recommendation, since I suspect my colleagues mean quite different things by the same sentence and I need to be able to tell them apart in my chapter.
