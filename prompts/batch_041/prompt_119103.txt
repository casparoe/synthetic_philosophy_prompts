After thirty years as a field entomologist I took early retirement, and for the past four years I have been working my way through philosophy — desultorily at first, now as a part-time MA student — with the result that I have attached myself to a reading group of graduate students in metaphysics and philosophy of mind whose biology stops somewhere around high school. In three weeks I am to lead the ninety-minute session on free-floating rationales, and since I am the only person in the room who has spent whole seasons watching the animals in question, the group has decided (perhaps unwisely) that the introduction should be mine. What I want from you is not an explanation of the idea but a plan for my explanation of it: the order in which the pieces must come so that the central claim is not merely announced but forced on the audience; the one example that should carry the whole session, together with what that example must have and must not have if it is to do that work; the misconceptions my philosophers will bring in or manufacture, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; and what I should say out loud that I am deliberately not attempting at this level.

I have a rough sequence in mind and would like it either endorsed or rearranged with reasons. It begins from the contrast between a reason that someone actually had and a pattern that behaves as though it were tracking a reason — a creature that avoids the trap without representing traps — and then moves to the claim that such a pattern can be genuinely explanatory even though no one ever entertained it. My worry is that if I put the paradigm case first, a trait whose apparent rationale is underwritten by selection, say the provisioning routine of a solitary wasp that is exquisitely sensitive to the right cues and blind to the wrong ones, the audience will quietly assimilate the rationale to a designer's rationale merely relocated, and will hear the whole thing as a dressed-up version of the argument from design. The alternative I am tempted by is to open with a case in which a rationale is real and explanatory but is not the reason the structure exists at all: I am thinking of the hexagonal cells of a honeycomb, where the least-wax-for-most-room rationale is genuinely available to us and genuinely true of the geometry, while the historical explanation may be a mechanical packing process that never had to solve anything — or, if that case is too contested to bear the weight, a structure held in a lineage by drift or by phylogenetic inertia, so that the rationale selection would have found was never in fact consulted. Is one of these the right carrying example, or should it instead be a case of deception or signalling, where the audience's instinct to hunt for an intender is at its strongest? And where do the thermostats and the immune systems belong — before the biology, as a warm-up along the gradient from mere mechanism to a rationale worth imputing, or after it, as a way of marking where the boundary lies?

I can already predict some of the trouble. Someone will hear "free-floating" as "fictional" or "merely heuristic" and conclude that the rationale is a manner of speaking we could drop without loss; someone else will hear it as an instrumentalist licence, so that any description predicting equally well is as good as any other; a third will take it as licensing purposes in nature and will accuse me of smuggling teleology back in under new vocabulary; a fourth, more sophisticated, will grant the rationale but insist it be reducible to the mechanism, and will want to know what the reason-talk does that the mechanism-talk does not. I would like these laid out as a map rather than a list — which are mistakes about what is being claimed, which are legitimate objections the view must answer, and which point to a genuinely unsettled question — with the moment in the session at which each is best raised, since I would rather raise them myself than have them raised against me. Finally, give me a single question to put at the end that would show whether the audience has grasped the distinction rather than merely acquired the vocabulary, something they must decide rather than define, and tell me honestly which parts of the topic I should announce that I am leaving out.
