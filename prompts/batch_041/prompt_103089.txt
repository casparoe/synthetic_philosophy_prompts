Consider two agents who disagree not about means but about what is intrinsically valuable. Assume each agent's values are internally coherent: each has a complete, transitive preference ordering over a common set of outcomes, but the two orderings are not reducible to a single scale, and neither agent treats the other's valuations as a constraint on her own. Add one further stipulation: each agent also has a floor — a set of outcomes she regards as impermissible to bring about, or to trade away, however much else improves. Now define the compromise thesis (CT): whenever two such agents face a status quo that neither regards as optimal, there exists a feasible alternative — possibly a lottery over outcomes, possibly a bundle spanning several separate issues — that both agents strictly prefer to the status quo. Treat CT as false if there is even one pair of coherent value systems, with floors, and one status quo, for which no such mutually preferable feasible alternative exists.

Give me your credence that CT is true, and decompose it. Separate at least the following terms, each with a number and one line of justification:

(a) the probability that the relevant agents' values can be represented in a way that makes 'strictly prefers to the status quo' well-defined at all, given that their orderings need not be interpersonally comparable;

(b) the probability that a lottery over outcomes can be strictly preferred by an agent whose values are lexicographic or otherwise discontinuous — that is, whether the mixture move survives once some goods are ranked infinitely above others;

(c) the probability that issue-bundling — trading concessions across separate questions — can manufacture an improvement in cases where no single question admits one;

(d) the probability that the two agents' floors do not jointly rule out every feasible improvement, so that the impermissible sets are not simply disjoint in the wrong way;

(e) the probability that feasibility constraints — commitment, enforcement, and the requirement that the compromise be stable against renegotiation — do not kill the trades that survive (a) through (d).

Then show how these terms combine into your final number, name the term your estimate is most sensitive to, and say what argument or concrete case would move you most.

To anchor it, use this pair if it helps: one agent's floor forbids ever coercively overriding an informed refusal, and she is otherwise indifferent to aggregate outcomes; the other's floor forbids any outcome that leaves a certain threshold of suffering unrelieved, and he is otherwise indifferent to how the relief is brought about. They face a status quo in which an informed refusal is being overridden and the suffering is being relieved.

Finally, I put my own credence in CT at about 0.4, while a colleague who works on bargaining puts it near 0.85. Which sub-question most likely accounts for the gap between us, and is there a version of CT, weaker than the one stated, that we could both accept?
