The question was: "Signalling is often described as a bargaining problem -- signallers want receivers to do something, receivers want to be told the truth. Using either the peacock's tail or stotting in gazelles, explain how a costly signal can stay honest in an evolutionarily stable population, and say what the cost is actually doing. Then say whether the handicap principle and the theory of costly signalling are the same idea, and why or why not."

Here's the script:

"The problem with any signal is that it's cheap to fake. If a male could grow a big tail for free, then every male would grow one, receivers would learn to ignore it, and the trait would stop meaning anything. So the signal has to be expensive, and this is Zahavi's handicap principle from 1975: the signal is a handicap that lowers the signaller's fitness, which is exactly why it can be trusted. A low-quality male simply can't afford the handicap, so only high-quality males produce it, and the receiver can take the signal at face value. Zahavi's idea was rejected for years and only became respectable once Grafen (1990) built an ESS model showing the handicap is evolutionarily stable as long as the cost is high enough that only the best males can pay it -- the same logic Spence had already used for education in the job market, where a degree is only worth getting if the weaker applicants find it too expensive.

Take stotting in gazelles. When a gazelle spots a cheetah it jumps up and down instead of running straight off. This is a handicap in Zahavi's sense: it wastes energy and makes the gazelle conspicuous, but it tells the predator 'I'm fit enough to waste time on this, so don't bother chasing me'. It's also what Maynard Smith and Harper would call an index, because it can't be faked -- a sick gazelle physically can't jump that high -- and that's really the same point as the handicap principle, since both say the signal is reliable because of the cost.

The peacock's tail is the classic case. Only a healthy male can grow and carry a tail that big, and the hens prefer the males with the biggest tails, so the preference and the trait evolve together. Since the signal is honest and the hens benefit by getting good genes for their sons, the long tail keeps spreading until every male has one, at which point the population is sitting at the honest equilibrium Zahavi predicted. So I'd say the handicap principle and costly signalling are basically the same theory: any costly signal that's honest is a handicap, and the handicap principle is just the biological version of Spence's signalling model."
