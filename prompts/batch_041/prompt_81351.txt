I've been running a small reading group on material constitution — the statue and the clay, the lump and the cat, the bronze that outlives the statue it was cast into — and we've hit the stage where each of us has a favored position and none of us can say why his position is worth the effort rather than the others. Partly this is my fault: I read too much rat-adjacent blogging and have picked up the habit of asking what a question is doing before asking whether its answer is true, so I keep wanting to reframe the question of which view is right as a question about which question is upstream. My working list of open questions, which I don't trust, runs roughly as follows: (a) whether there is genuine coincidence of numerically distinct material objects at all, or whether every alleged case is really identity, parthood, or confusion; (b) whether the relation between the statue and the clay should be called constitution, grounding, dependence, or something else, and whether any of those labels earns its keep — or whether the word constitution names the gap rather than bridging it; (c) whether the puzzle is just a special case of vagueness or of the problem of the many, so that a general solution to those would swallow it whole; (d) whether the whole debate presupposes a picture of objects as countable, bounded, matter-occupying things, and whether abandoning that picture — for a stuff-based or mass-term ontology, or for a hylomorphic one — dissolves the puzzle rather than solving it; (e) whether any of it is a verbal dispute, with the disputants agreeing on all the facts and quarreling only about how to talk; and (f) whether anything downstream really turns on it — the too-many-thinkers problem in personal identity, artifact categories in law and conservation, where a rebuilt cathedral or a relined painting sits in the ontology, the metaphysics of biological kinds.

What I want from you is a defended ordering, not a survey. Which of these — or something I haven't listed, if you think the real crux lies elsewhere — should be settled first, on the grounds that its answer changes what the others even look like? For each, tell me whether it is upstream or downstream of the rest, how tractable it is for someone with a couple of years, a taste for formal tools, and no appetite for empirical work, and whether its resolution would change anything for anyone outside the seminar room. I also want the trade-offs made explicit: where importance and tractability pull apart, say which you would sacrifice, and what over the next few years — an argument, a counterexample to a widely assumed principle, a shift in a neighboring field — would move a question up or down my list. If you think the framing itself is wrong, say so, and give me the framing you would use instead.
