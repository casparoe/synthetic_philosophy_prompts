I spent the better part of three decades as a field ecologist, mostly on foraging and mating systems in birds, and since retiring I have been learning the philosophy of biology properly, which has meant discovering how much of what I absorbed as a graduate student was slogan rather than argument. The handicap principle is my clearest example, and I want to build a single teaching session around the distance between the slogan and the theory.

The setting is the interdisciplinary reading group I convene: eighteen participants or so, roughly half biologists and half philosophers, meeting once for ninety minutes. Nearly all of them have heard of the handicap principle; almost none have asked what actually makes a signal honest, and I would rather they arrive at that by doing something than by hearing me say it.

Here is the idea I want them to feel. The familiar story is that a signal earns credence by being costly: the tail is a burden, so only a genuinely fit bird can carry it. But expense by itself accomplishes nothing. What does the work is that the cost be graded: a signal is honest at equilibrium when it is cheaper, at the margin, for a high-quality signaller than for a low-quality one, so that low-quality types cannot profitably imitate it. A signal that is equally costly to everyone separates no one; it only makes the whole group poorer, and the receivers, finding no information in it, stop attending to it. The corollary, and the part I think is most often lost, is that an honest signal need not be wasteful at all. The operative fact is the correlation between what the signal costs and what the signaller is worth, not the size of the cost. There is also a second route to honesty that the word handicap hides entirely: a trait can be an unfakeable index, causally bound to the quality it advertises, as the pitch of a call is fixed by the size of the animal that makes it, in which case it can be cheap or free and still perfectly reliable. And in either case the honesty belongs to the equilibrium rather than to the trait, so that the same display that informs in one setting would mislead in another, depending on what the receivers do with it.

Please design, for this group and these ninety minutes, a game that lets them meet these results in order rather than all at once: an opening in which communication is free and therefore worth nothing; a round in which a signal is costly but uniformly so, so that they feel the futility of mere expense; a round in which the cost is graded by quality, so that separation appears and they can see why; and, if the clock allows, a closing variant that isolates the index route, so that they see honesty without cost. I want hidden quality types, a payoff currency that need not be real money, and a structure in which receivers choose partners or partners choose each other, so that both the incentive to signal and the incentive to attend are live.

Give me the rules, the materials, the payoff scheme, and the running order. Then the debrief: map each round onto the corresponding piece of theory, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce. In particular, a classroom game can exhibit the incentive logic of an equilibrium but cannot show the evolutionary process by which a population finds it, so that part I will have to supply myself.
