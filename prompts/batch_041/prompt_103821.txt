Take the episode as given (I'm not asking for citations, I just want the argument): Nicolas Bernoulli poses the coin-flip game in a 1713 letter to Montmort; Cramer suggests a square-root utility in 1728; Daniel Bernoulli's 1738 paper swaps that for log utility and expected utility theory is born. For about two centuries that gets treated as the resolution — don't maximize expected money, maximize expected utility with diminishing marginal utility, and the infinity goes away. Somewhere along the line Menger points out that any unbounded utility function can be St. Petersburged: make the payoffs grow fast enough and expected utility blows up again, so the real fix has to be a bounded utility function.

Now the lens, and I'm spelling it out becuase it's easy to be fuzzy about: take a fair coin flipped until the first heads. If heads lands on toss n, the payoff is +2^n/n when n is odd and −2^n/n when n is even. The probability-weighted terms are then just (−1)^(n+1)/n. They converge to ln 2 in the natural order, but only conditionally — the payoff has infinite positive part and infinite negative part, so its expectation is undefined rather than infinite, and rearranging the terms gives you any real number you like. Nover and Hájek called this the Pasadena game; their claim is that it isn't an inconvenience for expected utility theory but a silence in it, because there's no number to maximize.
