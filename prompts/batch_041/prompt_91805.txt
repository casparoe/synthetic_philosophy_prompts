Our epistemology reading group has spent the term on the question of how practical stakes and conversational context bear on whether someone counts as knowing, and one member, Dana, has taken to keeping a notebook of verdicts on cases we invent, which she treats as data. Her format is to specify a subject, that subject's practical stakes, the grade of the subject's evidence, and then a second character — the person inside the vignette who actually voices a knowledge judgment — along with that character's own situation; Dana then rules on whether the voiced judgment is correct. Her rulings so far are these. (i) The pharmacy: Ravi wants to know whether the Elm Street pharmacy opens on Sundays, and if it does not he simply refills on Monday and loses nothing; his evidence is strong — he read the posted hours last week, they are printed on the door, and a neighbor corroborates — and the assessor is a friend idly curious over coffee with nothing riding on it, who says Ravi knows the pharmacy opens Sunday; Dana rules the assessor correct. (ii) The dose: Dev is deciding whether to give his toddler a second dose of fever medicine tonight, which he rightly takes to be high-stakes; his evidence is weak — a neighbor's offhand guess and an unreachable pharmacist — and the assessor is his sister, herself frightened about the same question, who says Dev does not know a second dose is safe; Dana rules her correct. (iii) The bus: Sam wonders whether the 7:40 stops at Maple and Vine, and if it does not he walks ten minutes with no harm; his evidence is weak — a stranger's vague gesture — and the assessor is a fellow passenger, equally casual, who says Sam does not know the bus stops there; Dana rules him correct.

This week we reach the job offer. Mira is deciding whether to accept a position across the country, uprooting her family; her stakes are as high as they come. Her evidence, though, is of exactly the same everyday grade as Ravi's — she has the written offer and a confirming phone call. The assessor is an old college friend chatting over coffee, with nothing at stake, who says Mira knows the offer is solid.

Tell me what Dana will rule, and then do the harder work. I want the set of rules that fit her three verdicts equally well, what each of those rules predicts for the job-offer case, which prediction you would actually bet on and on what grounds, and — since I suspect her notebook may not discriminate — precisely which further case, described as concretely as she describes hers, would force the surviving rules apart. I care less about the verdict than about whether her data does any real work, so be explicit about which features of her examples are load-bearing and which are merely correlated with the features that are.
