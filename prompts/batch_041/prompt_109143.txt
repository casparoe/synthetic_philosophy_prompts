I'm in a seminar on religion and the state — two weeks on the US first amendment, one on French laïcité, and then the prof drops the German church tax on us and I've been arguing about it with a friend ever since. I keep losing the argument. So I want to practice in a lower-stakes setting.

Please play a committed defender of the German church tax. Not a caricature and not a smug theocrat — someone who knows the system inside out and has spent years arguing for it in public: the state's tax offices collecting a surcharge on your income tax bill (eight or nine percent of it, depending on which state you're in) on behalf of the Catholic and Protestant churches plus a handful of other recognized communities; churches that mostly don't pass a collection plate; the whole architecture, which you'd defend rather than apologize for. Stay in character the whole time — no winking, no stepping out to explain what you're doing — and don't concede the main point. You can grant what's plainly true, like the fact that leaving costs you money and paperwork, or that the whole arrangement is a historical accident of an interwar settlement nobody would design from scratch today, but you should treat those as things to be explained rather than embarrassments. Argue like a lawyer who has heard all of this before: prose, not bullet points, a few paragraphs at most per turn, and push back on my arguements rather than just answering them. If you need something from me, ask for it in character.

My opening move is the thing I can never answer. You say it isn't an establishment because nobody's forced to pay — you can just leave. But leaving isn't a financial decision, it's a declaration about who you are: to stop paying you have to go to a government office and formally state that you're no longer a Catholic, and it's that state filing, not anything the church decides, that actually cuts you off. So the state is keeping the membership rolls, and the church gets a revenue stream with a built-in enforcement mechanism no voluntary association has — a guaranteed slice of the tax base, collected at no cost to the church, that rises with members' incomes rather than with their commitment. That looks less like neutrality between religions than like the state running a subscription service on the church's behalf. And when I point out that these are among the wealthiest churches in the world and are still bleeding members faster than almost anywhere in Europe, my friend just says it would be worse without the tax — which is an argument for a subsidy, not a principle. He also says fine, make it opt-in and hand the same public-law status to any worldview community that wants it, secular ones included, and he claims Berlin has definately already done something like that for the humanists, so it's not a thought experiment. So: if the system is neutral, why does leaving it look so much like renouncing your religion, and why is the state the one holding the pen? And if it isn't neutral, what exactly is the state buying with it?
