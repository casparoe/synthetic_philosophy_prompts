For most of my working life I was an experimental physicist, and I came to philosophy of science only after I retired; I am still working out where I stand, and I would like to use a short essay to do so. The topic I keep returning to is the pessimistic meta-induction against scientific realism, but I have found the standard discussions unsatisfying, because the argument is usually left as a slogan rather than set out in full. I have therefore tried to state it in what I take to be its strongest explicit form, and I would like the essay to be an assessment of that argument as stated, rather than a survey of the debate. The argument is this:

P1. A scientific theory is approximately true only if a substantial proportion of its central theoretical posits refer — that is, only if the entities it postulates, or something closely resembling them, really exist.
P2. Every past theory that was both empirically successful and later abandoned had at least some central posits that failed to refer.
P3. Empirical success is the best, and perhaps the only, evidence we can have for the approximate truth of a theory.
P4. The historical record shows that most empirically successful theories have eventually been abandoned.
Therefore: most of our present empirically successful theories are probably not approximately true, and we ought not to believe that they are.

I am aware that P4 is an empirical premise and that the whole argument stands or falls with how it is read; I want that made explicit rather than glossed over.

Please write the essay so that it does all of the following:

1. Sets out the argument fairly, in its strongest form, before criticising it.
2. Examines whether the inductive premise holds up once we are explicit about how theories and theoretical posits are to be counted — that is, whether the observed frequency of discarded theories changes depending on whether we individuate at the level of whole theories, of individual posits, or of the phenomena those posits were introduced to explain, and whether the conclusion survives each of these choices.
3. Considers whether our record of discarded theories is an unbiased sample of past theorising, or whether the way the history of science is written — which tends to favour dramatic revolutions over quiet continuity — inflates the apparent rate of discontinuity.
4. Assesses the reply that we should be realists only about those parts of a theory that earned their keep through novel predictive success, and about structure rather than entities, and says whether that reply genuinely answers the argument or merely concedes its conclusion in a narrower form.
5. Addresses a worry I cannot shake: if the argument is sound, does it not also undercut the inductive and abductive reasoning by which its own premises were established? Say whether this is a fatal self-undermining or a manageable tension.
6. Uses the following as its test case: a historian two hundred years hence classifies our present fundamental physics, and our best current theories of mind, much as we now classify phlogiston and the luminiferous aether. Asks whether that retrospective classification — unknowable to us now — is the right kind of fact to bear on what we should believe today, and lets the answer shape the verdict.
7. Reaches a verdict and defends it. Do not close on a non-committal note. A qualified verdict is acceptable, but it must be stated sharply, with its conditions spelled out.

I do not want a literature review, a list of references, or appeals to the authority of named philosophers; I want the argument assessed on its merits. Please write in British English.

After the essay, append a short structured summary in the form of a table with exactly these columns: Verdict (a single sentence stating whether the meta-induction succeeds and whether, and in what form, scientific realism survives); Premise rejected or restricted (which of the premises above you reject or narrow, and why, in one sentence; write none if you accept them all); Crux (the single consideration that does the decisive work); Strongest remaining objection (the best reply open to someone who rejects your verdict); Falsifier (the specific empirical or conceptual finding that would change your mind); and Confidence (low, medium, or high).
