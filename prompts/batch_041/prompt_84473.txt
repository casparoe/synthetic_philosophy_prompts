Second-year PhD student in a causal inference group here, and I keep running into the same one-liner. My stats professor says "no causation without manipulation" like it's a law of nature. Last week he used it to say that asking whether the Black Death caused the rise of real wages in medieval England isn't really a causal question at all, and earlier in the term to say a paper on whether race causes health disparities is "not even in the game." I have a vague sense that the slogan is doing three or four different jobs across those two sentences, and that the job doing all the real work is the one nobody would defend if you made them spell it out.

Can you lay out the precise claims it could be making, as a menu I can bring to office hours? I want the full range, from the boring methodological point about how you define a treatment in a potential-outcomes framework, up to the radical metaphysical claim that causation just is manipulability. For each reading, tell me what kind of manipulation is required and by whom — a human experimenter who can actually do it, any conceivable agent, a natural process that happens to occupy the right role, a merely hypothetical intervention — whether it has to be possible right now or only in principle, and whether it's a claim about token causes, about types of causes, or both. Then give me a verdict on each one: platitude nobody could object to, substantive and defensible, flatly false, or unfalsifiable. And say which reading actually gets him his conclusion in each case, because I suspect the reading that gets him the Black Death verdict and the reading that gets him the race verdict are not the same reading and he's sliding between them.

Also: I want to know what this adds over the plain counterfactual theory. Does "you'd need an intervention" say anything more than "if X hadn't happened, Y wouldn't have"? If a reading requires interventions to be actually performable, walk me through what breaks — I think it rules out historical causes, absences (a vitamin deficiency causing scurvy), and anything we can't touch for ethical reasons — and whether there's a way to state the view that doesn't have that consequence. And there's some circularity worry I can't articulate, about needing to already know the causal structure in order to tell whether something is an intervention.
