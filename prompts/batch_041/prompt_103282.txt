So I just got out of a three-hour argument with my friend about first-past-the-post vs. proportional representation and I'm still worked up about it. Here's where I landed: FPTP is basically indefensible for a national legislature, and any country that could switch to PR should. Not "it's a tradeoff," not "it depends on the country" — indefensible. I figure the burden is on the FPTP defensers at this point. But he landed alot of punches, and now I want to know what should actually move me, if anything.

So: what would count against the view that PR is simply the more defensible system? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to be clear about which ones PR's own advocates would concede are real problems vs. which they'd just wave off. I'd also like to know what the position predicts that FPTP doesn't, so I know what to even go look for.

Some stuff we went back and forth on that I want a straight answer on:

- His main move was that the real case for FPTP isn't fairness, it's accountability — you get one party in charge and you can throw the bums out, whereas PR gives you permanent coalitions where nobody's actually responsible for anything. Is that a genuine argument against my position, or just a rival preference? What would have to be true about coalition governments for it to count as a real defeater?

- He brought up the interwar Europe thing, that countries adopting PR made it easier for anti-democratic parties to get a foothold. I don't know how strong that is. If it holds up, does it count against PR as such, or only against PR under particular conditions you could design around?

- Duverger's law. I always assumed FPTP mechanically crushes third parties, but then India is FPTP and has a zillion parties. Does that falsify the law or just show it's a tendency? And if the tendency is that soft, does my "FPTP wastes votes" complaint lose its teeth?

- Is proportionality even the right yardstick? If someone says the thing we want is stable, responsive, accountable government and proportionality is just one input among several, does my view survive that reframing? What if PR turns out to be worse on the thing I claim to care about most?

- What are the strongest real cases that PR people treat as no big deal but should actually worry about? I keep hearing about countries where it takes months to form a government after an election.

And be honest with me on this part: has "PR is more democratic" been qualified into unfalsifiability? Like, every time PR produces a bad outcome, the answer is "that's the voters' fault, not the system's." If that's what's happening, is it a fatal problem or just the price of any structural claim? And is there any empirical result at all that would make me drop this, or is it the kind of view where only my underlying values could ever shift?

For what it's worth, what would move me most is a case where FPTP reliably does something PR structurally can't — not "it's simpler to explain," something real.
