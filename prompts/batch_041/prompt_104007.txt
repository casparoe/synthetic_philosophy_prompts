Construct a thought experiment in the form of a compact case study: a fictional but realistic society — no fantasy elements, no more than two paragraphs of setup — in which a widely practised, costly, and morally objectionable custom is abolished, engineered so that the three standard explanations of moral revolutions come apart in a specified pattern. Take the three explanations to be: (i) the argument explanation, on which the custom ends because moral entrepreneurs produce arguments that eventually convince enough people; (ii) the interest explanation, on which it ends because it stops paying, the payoffs to those who sustain it or to those with the power to suppress it having shifted; and (iii) the honor explanation, on which it ends because the code of honor that made the custom a source of standing is refigured, so that practitioners now lose standing by continuing rather than gain it by continuing, and abandon it without being persuaded of anything. This is the dispute between economic readings of British abolition (the reading associated with Eric Williams) and honor-centred readings of moral revolutions (the reading associated with Kwame Anthony Appiah).

Specifications for the case:
- The custom must be invented rather than one of the stock examples (slavery, dueling, footbinding, sati, or the like), and its material structure must be laid out explicitly enough for the interest explanation to get traction.
- At the moment of collapse, the argument explanation must predict persistence: the best arguments against the custom available in the society's own moral vocabulary must already be on the table and already have failed, and no new argument may appear.
- At the moment of collapse, the interest explanation must also predict persistence: the material payoffs must be unchanged, or must change in the direction that favours continuing. State which, and why.
- The honor explanation must predict collapse, and must do so without smuggling in either an argument or a payoff: the trigger may alter only whose esteem matters, or what the custom signifies to those whose esteem matters.
- The trigger must be cheap and attributable to a single actor or small group; no conquests, no technological revolutions, no charismatic figure who simply persuades everyone.

Then, in order: (a) present the case; (b) state what each of the three explanations predicts and why, making clear that the predictions are generated by the mechanisms rather than stipulated; (c) identify the single feature of the case that does the work, and say what happens if it is deleted — does the case collapse back into an interest or argument story?; (d) give the strongest available reply for a defender of the interest explanation, who will want to redescribe your trigger as an interest shift in disguise, and say whether the reply succeeds; and (e) sketch a companion case in the mirror-image direction, in which the interest explanation predicts collapse while the honor explanation predicts persistence, so that the two mechanisms are shown to be separable rather than two descriptions of one event.
