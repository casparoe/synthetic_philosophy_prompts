I'm a novelist — three books in, all of them domestic realism, nothing with a thesis — and lately I've gotten weirdly fixated on luck egalitarianism. My partner is finishing a PhD in philosophy and she feeds it to me over dinner in pieces: inequalities that trace back to brute luck (where you were born, what your body does on its own, what falls on you out of the sky) are unjust and ought to be corrected, while inequalities that trace back to option luck — gambles you knowingly took — are, on the strict version, your own problem. What hooked me wasn't the clean version, it was the ugly edge cases the papers seem to treat as embarrassments: the objection that a consistent luck egalitarian ends up abandoning the imprudent, and the nastier one where someone's genuinely voluntary bad choice turns out to be downstream of brute luck all the way down — bad schooling, a childhood that taught her that caution is for people with money, and so on.

So here's what I'd like: a short story, 2,000–3,000 words, third person, past tense, set in a near-future coastal town that has actually implemented the thing. There's a Reckoning Office, staffed by clerks who classify each resident's misfortunes as brute or option, plus a published schedule of what the town owes you when a loss comes out brute. My protagonist is one of the clerks, Della, mid-fifties, good at the job mostly because she's unsentimental, and the story turns on a single claim she has to rule on: a fisherman who turned down the town's storm insurance three years running — he could afford it, barely, and said he'd rather put the money into the boat — whose boat then went down in a storm, and whose teenage daughter now needs something expensive that the town will only cover if the father's loss is classified as brute. I don't want a lecture or a thought experiment with names stapled on. I want the machinery to sit in the background the way insurance paperwork sits in the background of a real life, and I want Della's ruling to feel like an actual administrative decision that costs somebody something. Ideally the reader ends up feeling the pull of the principle and the pull of the objection at once, and can't quite tell which one the story is for. One more thing: there's a younger clerk in the office who keeps arguing that the whole classification scheme is just a way of laundering contempt for the poor — I'd like that argument present but not settled, and I'd rather nobody wins it in dialogue. If the ending can land on Della doing something small and definately not heroic, that's the register I'm after.
