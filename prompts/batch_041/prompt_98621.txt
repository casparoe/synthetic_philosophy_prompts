I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a neuroscientist who has come to believe that her experiences are entirely real and entirely idle — that nothing she feels ever makes anything happen — and I need to keep her position internally coherent across four hundred pages, including the arguments she has with a colleague who thinks there is nothing to feel in the first place. What I want from you is not an essay on the topic but the instrument itself: a decision tree that carries a reader from one starting commitment to a named position on the causal status of phenomenal properties.

The reader enters at a single commitment, phrased so that it gives away nothing: that there is something it is like to be her, and that this fact is not exhausted by any physical description of her. From there, every node must be a question she can answer without already knowing which position it leads to — no node may simply ask whether she is a dualist. Each leaf should carry the name of a position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

The tree must be able to reach, though not necessarily as leaves: epiphenomenalism; parallelism or occasionalism; interactionist dualism; property dualism under an exclusion argument, where the phenomenal property is squeezed out by its own physical base; identity theory; Russellian monism or panpsychism; illusionism; and the higher-order view on which the judgement about an experience is itself the experience. At least one branch node must turn on the paradox of phenomenal judgement — if a state does no causal work, by what route does anyone come to know or report it? — and at least one must separate the positions by what they are willing to say about a creature whose reports are reliably produced but never caused by the states reported.

For that last purpose I have invented a case for the novel and would like the tree built so that it can be run on it: the stowaway, a being whose utterances about its experience — the red is blazing, the note is sour — are generated entirely by a physical subsystem that receives no input whatsoever from the phenomenal states those utterances appear to be about. Tell me which nodes the stowaway case forces into existence, and which positions must treat it as impossible, incoherent, or merely unactual.

Build the tree from the logical space first — the questions and the combinations of answers they permit — and only then locate the actual positions in it, noting which combinations are unoccupied and diagnosing each briefly: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored? Flag any two leaves your tree cannot separate rather than papering over the gap. I would rather have an honest node that forks three ways than a tidy one that lies.

Write the whole thing in British English, and do not send me a reading list or a survey of who has said what; the tree, its nodes, its leaves, and your notes on the empty branches are the deliverable.
