I'm in a reading group at work on AI safety and I keep getting hung up on the "control" side of it -- the part that just takes it as given that the model might be quietly working against you and tries to build a cage around it instead of trying to make it nice. The setup is always roughly the same: there's the strong model you don't trust, a weaker model you do trust (often literally a smaller checkpoint of the same lineage) that reads the strong one's outputs and flags anything suspicious, a couple of tripwires that cut the run short, and you only let the strong one near anything sensitive when the stakes are low. Now here's what i can't square. We treat the model's account of its own motives as worthless -- if it says something like "I'm just trying to be helpful" we roll our eyes, because it might be lying or just confabulating. But we do read its written-out reasoning and treat it as evidence about what it's actually up to, at least when it looks sketchy enough to be worth reading closely. And we trust the monitor, which is basically the same kind of thing as the model we've just decided might be a schemer. So the same entity is untrustworthy about its intentions, semi-trustworthy about its reasoning, and its sibling is trustworthy as a judge of it. That can't all be right at once, and I want to know what it's actually assuming.

So: reconstruct the picture of the model's mind that this whole apparatus presupposes. Does it assume the model has stable goals that persist across contexts, or just context-bound behavior that happens to look goal-directed? Does it assume deception leaves behavioral traces that a weaker observer can catch -- and if so, isn't that a real substantive claim about deception, one that might fail for a smart enough model? Which bits are strictly presupposed and which are just engineering conveniences we could swap out (like, is the trusted monitor doing an epistemic job, or just a cheap filtering job)? I'm especially interested in whether the parts are consistent with each other, because the distrust of self-report versus the trust in the monitor feels like it's pulling two ways, and in what would be left of the whole control framing if we gave up the idea that the model has anything like intentions and treated it as pure stimulus-response. And a side question I keep arguing about with a friend: the apparatus treats the model as a tool with no standing, but the whole reason we need it is that it might have goals of its own -- does buying the one commit you to anything about its moral status, or can you have the former without the latter? No citations needed, just reconstruct the argument and tell me where it's underdetermined.
