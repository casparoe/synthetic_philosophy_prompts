Bit of an odd one, but I'm a software engineer with zero formal philosophy training and I've fallen down a rabbit hole about how we supervise AI systems.

Roughly there's two seperate ways people talk about it. Process-based supervision: you check and reward each step the model takes, a bit like a maths teacher giving marks for the working even when the final answer comes out wrong. Outcome-based supervision: you only care about the final answer, like a teacher who just marks the number at the bottom right or wrong.

I want a proper compare-and-contrast of the two, not the glossy blog-post version. But here's whats actually bugging me. I've been calling them "checking the journey" and "checking the destination", and I've got a hunch that framing is doing more work than anyone admits. Because you can game either one — a model can spin out lovely-looking intermediate steps that are basically just theatre, and it can also luck into a correct answer from completely wrong reasoning. So I'm not convinced "more steps checked = safer".

So please: compare the two properly. Where does each one actually get its leverage, and does the answer change depending on whether the model's mistakes are recoverable or not (i.e. can you catch a bad step before it poisons everything downstream)? Is one strictly better, or is it horses for courses?

And then the part I really care about: is my journey/destination analogy a good one, or does it quietly smuggle in assumptions — like there being one obvious destination, or the steps being independent of each other? Does it line up with anything in moral philosophy, e.g. the deontology vs consequentialism split (consequentialism being the "only outcomes matter" one, right?), or is that a false friend that sounds clever in a meeting but falls apart?

British English spelling please. And don't just tell me the standard tradeoffs — tell me where the standard story is wrong or oversimplified.
