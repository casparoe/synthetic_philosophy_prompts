I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy, largely to make sense of a question that has nagged at me since my laboratory days: in what sense, if any, is the thing between our ears doing what the machines on my old benches were doing? I keep running into a slogan that is used as if it settled matters, and I would like you to take it apart for me.

The slogan is 'the brain is a computer'. I last heard it deployed at a seminar in our philosophy department, where a graduate student ended a long dispute about whether visual illusions show that perception is inferential by announcing that the brain is a computer, therefore of course it is drawing inferences; the room seemed to accept this as a closing move, and I confess I did not see why it should be. Earlier, in a popular book on neuroscience, I met the same phrase used for something much weaker, and once, in a lecture on artificial intelligence, for something considerably stronger.

What I want from you is a disambiguation, not a defence. Lay out the distinct precise claims that the sentence could be making, arranged as a menu from the nearly vacuous to the genuinely radical, and for each one tell me what it commits its holder to, whether it is plausible, and — this is what I care about most — whether that particular reading would actually license the use to which I saw it put, namely treating 'the brain is a computer' as a sufficient answer to the question of whether perception involves inference.

Please include, among the readings, the ones that turn on distinctions I suspect the slogan flattens. I have in mind at least these: (i) the modest claim that brain processes can be simulated or modelled computationally, which I take to be a claim about our methods rather than about the brain; (ii) the claim that the brain literally implements some computation in the technical sense, and the further claim that any physically realizable system does; (iii) the computational theory of mind, on which mental states just are computational states over representations; (iv) the much more specific architectural claim that the brain is a digital computer of the stored-program, single-processor kind — which strikes me as almost certainly false of an organ that is massively parallel, has no central processing unit, and does not obviously separate its memory from its processing; and (v) the functionalist or substrate-independence claim that the mind stands to the brain as software stands to hardware.

I would also like you to say what John von Neumann himself is best read as holding, since he is so often invoked as the patron of the slogan. My impression, from a hurried reading of his posthumously published The Computer and the Brain (based on lectures he gave at Yale and issued in 1958), is that he was far more careful than the slogan suggests: that he treated the nervous system's elementary signalling as digital in character, but thought much of the underlying machinery might be analog, that the brain achieves reliability out of decidedly unreliable components, and that he expressly warned against assuming the brain's 'language' resembles any formal logic we happen to possess. If that impression is right, I would like to know which of the readings above he would actually have endorsed and which he would have rejected — and, if my impression is wrong, I would rather be told so than have it flattered. Treat this part as a genuine test of the slogan, not as a way of recruiting von Neumann to one side.

Two closing requests. First, be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict you give, and single out the one point in your whole answer about which you are least sure, explaining what would settle it. Second, do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the analysis itself, and I have no way to check references at the moment.
