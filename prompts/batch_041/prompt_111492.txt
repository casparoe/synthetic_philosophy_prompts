I'm a research assistant on a comparative-politics project that codes country-episodes for a democracy-monitoring index, and the codebook is still being argued over, so my supervisor has been making the borderline calls herself while the rest of us write them up. Because I have to justify each coding in a footnote, I've been keeping a log of her verdicts and the single line she gives for each. Hungary after 2010: a two-thirds majority wrote a new constitution, expanded and repopulated the constitutional court, and put broadcast licensing under a body it appointed — backsliding, note: "the incumbent rewrote the rules so that it could no longer lose." Poland from 2015 on: a new governing party captured the constitutional tribunal, then the body that nominates judges, then the disciplinary regime for judges who objected — backsliding, note: "the space for organised opposition closed." Turkey after the failed coup attempt: emergency rule, mass dismissals, and a referendum converting the system into an executive presidency — not backsliding, note: "after a break like that you're not watching a slide any more." Tunisia, when the president suspended parliament, dismissed the prime minister, governed by decree, and then put a new constitution to a referendum — not backsliding, and the note says only "starting point." The United States between 2017 and 2021: a president who would not accept defeat, pressured state officials and his own vice-president to overturn the result, and ended with a mob in the legislature — backsliding, but flagged as a distinct subtype, note: "reversible, so a different animal." And one pilot case I won't identify, a mid-sized European country where the governing party took over the public broadcaster and redrew the electoral map without touching the constitution — backsliding, no note at all.

What I can't extract from her is the operative rule. Every case she has called backsliding had both a governing party quietly loosening the constraints on itself and a visible narrowing of contestation; every case she has refused to call backsliding is one where she can point to something else in the background — a rupture, a low starting point — so the log never separates the two, and her notes sometimes lean on one and sometimes on the other. I genuinely cannot tell whether she is tracking the method, the outcome, or the setting.

Next week's case is the one I actually need help with. In a country with a long record of competitive elections and a constitutional court that had repeatedly blocked the governing party's programme, the government put a package to a referendum and won comfortably. The package writes a generous set of social and environmental rights into the constitution — genuinely new entitlements, popular with the public, and defended by the government as a deepening of democracy. The same package, however, removes the constitutional court's jurisdiction over the new rights chapter, gives the parliamentary majority the final word on appointments to that court, cuts the court's budget, and lowers the number of signatures needed to call a referendum. Elections are untouched, the opposition campaigns freely, and the government is comfortably ahead in the polls. Her preliminary note on the file says only "interesting." I'd like you to tell me how she will code it, and to be explicit about the reasoning: set out the rules that fit her whole log, say what each of them predicts here, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would best tell the surviving rules apart. I'd also like to know whether anything in the log would look different had the governing parties sat on the opposite side of the political spectrum, and which case would expose that.
