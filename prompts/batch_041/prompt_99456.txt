I write a column on philosophy and ideas for a general-reader outlet, and I'm trying to work out a piece on the "moral equality of combatants" -- the doctrine that soldiers fighting for an unjust aggressor are no more culpable, and no less entitled to kill, than the soldiers defending against them, so that neither side's troops wrong each other simply by fighting. The reason I'm stuck is that a retired officer I had lunch with (he's written a couple of well-recieved op-eds on the ethics of war) defends the doctrine with a boxing match. Two fighters climb into the ring, both consent to be hit, both are permitted to hit, neither wrongs the other when he lands a punch, and the fact that one of them is fighting for a lousy reason -- the purse, or a promoter he can't stand -- doesn't make the punches he throws any less legitimate or his opponent any less wrong for hitting back. War, he says, is that, with the stakes raised. I've also heard a version of this with a duel instead of a boxing match, and there's a legal-philosophical cousin of it where what matters is just that each side has signed on to a rule-governed contest.

What I want from you is an audit of the analogy, not a verdict on the doctrine. Lay the correspondence out explicitly -- I'd actually love a table: what plays the role of the boxer, the consent, the referee, the rules, the purse, the promoter, the crowd, the ring itself -- and then tell me where the mapping breaks. Which disanalogies are doing real work and which are just noise? In particular I want to know whether the analogy quietly imports things war doesn't have: boxers are roughly matched by design, they can quit between rounds, the referee can stop it, the crowd isn't in the ring, the consent is individual and informed and revocable; soldiers are conscripted or trapped, civilians are in the middle of it, and the "rules" are contested and often unenforced. And then the thing I actually care about for the column: does the conclusion -- combatants on both sides are moral equals, so killing one of them isn't the same as murder -- survive once those disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument, so that the doctrine needs a different, non-analogical defense? If there's a better analogy people reach for to get the same conclusion, give me that too, and say what it buys and what it costs.

One request about how you answer. Keep track of which parts of this are actually settled among people who work on the ethics of war and which are genuinely contested -- I don't want some contested reading of the doctrine handed to me as if it were the consensus, and I'd rather you flag the places where you're unsure than smooth them over.
