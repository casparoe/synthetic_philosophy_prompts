I have agreed to lead the second session of a reading group on paradoxes for advanced undergraduates in philosophy; most have had one formal logic course, but their modal logic is rusty, and I have fifty minutes. The topic is Fitch's paradox of knowability — the argument that if every truth is knowable in principle, then every truth is in fact known — and I do not want you to explain the paradox to me. I want a teaching plan. In particular, I need the order in which the components have to be introduced so that the proof feels like a discovery rather than a sleight of hand: what must be in place before the knowability thesis can even be stated, what has to come before the proof's modal step, and where the factivity of knowledge and the distribution of knowledge over conjunction should enter. I also want to know which misconception each stage is likely to produce and exactly where in the sequence I should head it off — I already expect them to think the paradox is about self-reference, to hear "knowable" as "known by someone right now," and to conclude that the argument simply refutes anti-realism rather than forcing a restriction on it.

For the carrying example, I was going to use a specific unrecorded fact: the exact number of crumbs on the floor of a particular bakery at 3:17 a.m. on a night in 1972, a number no one ever counted and no record preserves. Tell me whether that example can carry the whole session, and if not, what the example must have and lack — it seems to me it must be a determinate contingent truth that no one in fact knows, must be the sort of thing that could in principle have been known, and must not depend on self-reference or future contingents. If it works, show me how to build the proof out of it step by step; if it does not, give me a better one and say why. Finally, give me one question I can ask at the end that would reveal whether they have understood why the proof turns on the interaction of knowability, factivity, and conjunction rather than merely being able to recite the contradiction, and tell me what I should openly refuse to explain at this level — for instance, the full modal semantics, the historical literature, or the various restrictions on knowability — so that the session stays honest about its limits.
