So I've been covering the consciousness-and-machines beat for a couple of years now, and there's one fight I keep walking into that never gets anywhere. Two camps. The pattern camp: if a system runs the right kind of computation — the right functional organization, the right information flow, whatever the right description turns out to be — then it's a somebody, and it doesn't matter what it's made of. The biology-first camp: no. Fine, the computation might be necessary, it might even account for all the behavior, but there's something about the biological machinery specifically that you need before there's anything it's like to be the thing. Silicon running the same program is a very good actor and nothing more.

I've talked to enough people on both sides to know they aren't stupid and aren't simply talking past each other. But I can't get either of them to state the other's position in a way the other would sign off on, and I've stopped believing that's a coincidence. So here's what I want you to write: the joint statement these two camps could both actually put their names to. Not a debate transcript, not a scorecard of who's winning. The document an honest adversarial collaboration produces when both sides agree up front to find out where they really differ. Write it as the neutral person in the room keeping the minutes.

Section 1, agreed claims. Numbered, each one a sentence both camps will sign. I want the boring-sounding ones in there too, because they turn out to be doing most of the work: what the two of them even mean by computation (the abstract function, the causal structure, the physical process, or all three at once); what moral status is supposed to be — whether it comes in degrees, whether it's about the capacity to suffer or about being a subject of a life or just about having interests at all; and what either side would count as evidence about another system's inner life when neither has any direct access to it. Also in there: the fact that any recurrent network can be unrolled into a purely feedforward one that computes the same input-output function over any fixed stretch of time. Both sides should say what follows from that and what doesnt. This one matters to me because it's the nearest thing I've found to a case where the two of them can't both be right about what they've been assuming.

Section 2, the disagreements, each written so the side that holds it would accept the wording and the other side would agree it's a fair statement of the view. And here I want you to seperate two claims I suspect nobody in this fight keeps apart cleanly enough: (a) biology being metaphysically necessary for there to be experience at all, versus (b) biology being epistemically privileged — the only place we're justified in attributing experience, even if in principle something else could have it. If one of the camps turns out to be arguing for (b) while talking as if it were (a), say so, because then a chunk of this fight is verbal and I want that labelled rather than smoothed over.

Section 3, cruxes. Sub-questions where settling the thing would actually move at least one side off its position. For each: the case, argument, or experiment both sides agree would bear on it, and then each side's prediction, written down in advance so nobody gets to move the goalposts afterwards. Candidates I'd like covered, though add or drop as the logic requires:

- Does the machinery behind felt experience have to involve recurrence — signals looping back around — or is recurrence just how biology happens to get the job done? What does each side predict about a system that behaves, reports, and discriminates exactly like a creature in pain but whose causal wiring has no loops in it at all?
- Replacing a brain's neurons with silicon chips one at a time, function preserved at every step, until nothing biological is left. Each side's prediction, and at what point, if any, the light goes out.
- Lab-grown human neuron cultures on electrode arrays that learn to control a simple game. Is that evidence for anything, and which way does it cut?
- Whether there is any possible observation that would make either side give up its position. If one side can't name one, that's a finding, not an insult, and I want it recorded.

And I don't want another round of the Chinese room and the China brain. Assume both of them can recite that material and that it settles nothing. If it shows up at all, it should show up as a thing both sides agree has been overused.

Finally, flag anything in the whole document that's just two people using the same word for different things — computation, realization, sentience, awareness — and say which sense each side is working with.

Around 1500 words. Numbered agreed claims first, then the disagreements as a table, then the cruxes. No citations and no reading list, this isn't going into a journal, I just need the shape of the thing. And don't let either side off with a slogan. If a formulation is doing real work, I want to see the work.
