I've been chewing on this argument since a guy on my Discord server — the one who's read everything Hofstadter ever wrote — dropped it on me, and I honestly cant tell if it's a real problem or just words doing card tricks. He calls the key move the frame tax. Here it is in his steps:

(a) A tangled hierarchy is any setup where the levels loop back through each other: Escher's two hands each drawing the other, a proof that talks about proofs, a mind modeling itself.
(b) You can't see such a loop from inside it. To see the hands as drawing each other, you have to stand somewhere the hands can't reach — at what he calls the inviolate level.
(c) That inviolate level is what does the work of breaking the loop. It sits outside the hierarchy, so the loop can't grab it.
(d) But whatever breaks a loop is itself a representation — it's presenting you with something — and representations are exactly the kinds of things that get tangled.
(e) So a hand at the inviolate level could, in principle, be drawn by a hand below it, and the loop is back, one floor up. That's the frame tax: every fix costs you one more level and hands the trouble upward.
(f) Nothing here says to stop at any particular floor. There's no principled last level.
(g) So no tangled hierarchy is ever fully explained. You either loop forever or you quit somewhere for no better reason than that you got tired.

A friend of mine says it's worse than that — if (d) is right, it goes through for any representation at all, looped or not, since anything can in principle be embedded in a loop. So it doesn't just sink self-reference, it sinks explanation, period, which has to be wrong. I'd rather keep (b) and (c) if I can, since the whole inviolate-level picture is what makes the Escher thing click for me, and I don't want to give up on the idea that loops genuinely need a level above them. So which step do I drop, and what does dropping it cost me?
