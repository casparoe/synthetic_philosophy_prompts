You're a philosopher who also writes decent literary fiction, so I want you to think carefully here about both halves of the job before you start writing — but do the thinking silently, I only want the story.

I need a short story, 900–1500 words, that puts luck egalitarianism under real pressure. Not a fable where the egalitarian is obviously right and the libertarian is a cartoon — I want the reader to end up half convinced by the position and half repelled by it.

Here's the world I want, and this part matters more than the plot: a mid-sized town runs a Luck Reconciliation Office. Once a year it hands every resident a statement: on one side, everything the auditors classify as brute luck — congenital conditions, the family and postcode you were born into, freak accidents, being in the wrong place when someone else drove drunk — and on the other side, everything they classify as option luck, i.e. anything traceable to a risk you chose to run. The Office pays out for the first column and charges back the second. Crucially, the Office uses one clean rule to sort them: a loss counts as brute luck only if it was unforeseen and unchosen at the moment it was incurred; if you took a gamble with a visible chance of disaster, the disaster is yours to carry, however bad it turns out. They also compensate handicaps but not expensive tastes — if your flourishing happens to require something costly that other people don't need, that's on you.

Give me two people inside this scheme, at least one of whom the Office is squeezing. One of them should have declined the cheap insurance the state offered on future earnings when they were young (an insurance window, once, at eighteen), and now needs something expensive; the Office says option luck, she says the decision to decline was itself shaped by a childhood the Office already scored as brute luck, so charging her is double-counting. The other has a need the Office files under taste rather than handicap. Let them talk past each other in a way that's recognisable, not a seminar — no one lectures about Rawls or Dworkin or "the abandonment of the imprudent," the ideas should be sitting in the furniture of the world, in the forms and the queues and what people say to each other at the counter.

Two things I don't want: a tidy resolution where somebody invents a better formula and everyone's made whole, and a twist that reveals the Office is secretly evil. I want the machinery to be doing exactly what it says it's doing, and for that to be the problem.

Start with the story itself — first sentence is the story's first sentence, no preamble, no restating this brief. If you want, you can add one single sentence after the story naming the tension you were dramatising, but that's the only commentary I'll accept.
