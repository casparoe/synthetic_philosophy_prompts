You are a political philosopher whose research sits at the intersection of state neutrality, religious accommodation, and the comparative politics of laïcité and multiculturalism. I want you to write a rigorous, well-structured essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words that takes a definite position on the controversy described below. Before you begin arguing, spend a few paragraphs thinking on the page: set out the competing considerations, state the strongest version of each side, and identify the crux on which your eventual verdict will turn. Then commit to a thesis and defend it. Argue from first principles and from the internal logic of the positions; do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations, and do not lean on the authority of named authors to settle anything.

The controversy. Consider a liberal democracy, Valmont, whose constitution guarantees equal citizenship and freedom of conscience, and whose public schools have long enforced a strict secularism of the French type: no conspicuous religious symbols worn by students or staff. After a decade of polarised debate, a commission proposes replacing that ban with what it calls a uniform civic dress rule. Under the new rule every student, whatever their convictions, wears a plain unmarked uniform: no crucifix, no headscarf, no kippah, no turban, but also no political badge and no secular slogan. The commission's argument is that this is strictly more neutral than the old ban, because it burdens every worldview identically. Call this the symmetry defence of the uniform.

Critics reply that formal symmetry is a sham. A rule that burdens all practices alike can still leave the majority's unmarked secular appearance as the unmarked default, so that the minority experiences the rule as an erasure of identity while the majority experiences it as nothing at all. On this view the uniform is laïcité with better manners, not laïcité transcended.

A third camp, which I will call the reversibility camp, proposes a test: a restriction is genuinely neutral only if the group it burdens most would accept that same restriction were the tables turned, that is, were their own practice the majority practice and the majority's practice the minority one. The reversibility camp claims this test vindicates the critics; its opponents claim the test either collapses into a disguised preference for the status quo or is indeterminate, since what a minority would accept under reversed conditions is a counterfactual no one can settle.

Your essay should address, at minimum: (1) whether neutrality is the right ideal to appeal to here, or whether the dispute is better framed in terms of equal respect, non-domination, or the social meaning of state action; (2) whether the relevant metric is the size of the burden imposed or the visibility of the burden to the majority; (3) whether the reversibility test is well defined and whether it can be gamed; and (4) the problem of internal minorities — the fact that laïcité is sometimes defended as shielding vulnerable members of a community from pressure exerted by that same community, while multiculturalist accommodation is criticised for empowering community authorities over dissenters. Note that a religious minority might itself support the uniform for reasons of its own, and consider what that does to each camp's position.

Conclude by recommending what Valmont should actually do: retain the original ban, adopt the uniform, move to an Anglo-American regime of targeted exemptions, or something else. Defend your recommendation against the strongest objection you have identified. Do not end on a non-committal note.
