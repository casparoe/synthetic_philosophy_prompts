I serve on the language-standards committee of a mid-sized daily newspaper, and I have drafted, for the committee's approval, a written policy governing how the paper may print, quote, mask, or decline to print derogatory group terms. Please write that policy itself as a sustained deadpan parody: an in-universe document, in the committee's sincere institutional voice, whose clauses repeatedly conflict with one another in ways that follow from the actual semantics of the terms being regulated. The committee must never notice the conflicts, and the document must contain no commentary, no winking, and no explanation of its own jokes. The humor should be legible to a careful reader and, ideally, fair: someone who knows the semantics and pragmatics of slurs should be able to see that the drafter understands the problems even though the drafter is plainly not solving them.

Fictional particulars, to be used exactly. The paper is a fictional daily of your invention with a plausible name and masthead. The term at issue is one slur and one targeted group, both invented for this piece; do not use any real-world slur, even masked, starred, or abbreviated, and do not refer to any real targeted group. The invented term and group carry the whole argument.

The policy responds to at least these five incidents, each of which should appear in a short background section and drive at least one clause:

1. A reporter's source, speaking on the record, used the term about the group in a sentence the reporter wishes to quote verbatim.
2. A columnist who is herself a member of the group used the term about herself, on the record, arguing that her use is a reclamation; the group's two largest advocacy organizations disagree about whether the paper should print it.
3. The paper's automated summarizer generated a headline containing the term after ingesting a reader comment. No human at the paper wrote it, and the committee is unsure whether the tool's output is an instance of the term at all, or whose act it would be if it were.
4. A historian wishes to reprint an 1880s editorial from the paper's own archive that uses the term; the lawyers want the text silently altered.
5. The paper's current house style is to print a truncated or dashed form of the term; readers and the group's advocates report both that the masked form still does the term's work and that a fresh insult has grown up around the mask itself.

Each of the following should be felt in the clauses without being named or resolved: whether the term's offensiveness belongs to what it means or to what a speaker does in uttering it; whether an insider's use can be non-derogatory, and whether that turns on the audience; whether a machine with no attitudes can perform the same act as a person; whether the masked form conveys what the unmasked form conveys; and whether the paper's own attempt to gloss the term for readers can avoid the very act the policy forbids.

Form: a memo header (to, from, re, date), then the committee's ruling in a single sentence, then numbered clauses under short headings. The policy should end without arriving at a workable rule. Hard ceiling: 700 words; do not exceed it.
