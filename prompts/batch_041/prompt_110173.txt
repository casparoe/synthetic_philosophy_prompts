I was present at a discussion between two colleagues—neither of them me—about sphexishness, the tendency of an organism or system to repeat a fixed behavioral sequence even when the circumstances make the sequence pointless. The conversation began with the familiar wasp case, but it soon turned to a variant I had not encountered before, and I would like the exchange adjudicated rather than summarized. The variant, which one of them called the double-loop wasp, is this: a wasp performs the standard rechecking routine before entering its burrow, but it also has a second mechanism that counts how many times it has rechecked the same burrow; if the count exceeds a fixed threshold, the second mechanism suppresses the first and the wasp proceeds. In the laboratory this breaks the loop. The other colleague then offered an artificial analogue: an agent with a first-order policy for solving tasks and a meta-policy that monitors for repetitive failure and switches the first-order policy when it detects a loop. The meta-policy can select policies that alter the meta-policy’s own threshold in light of past performance, though the meta-policy’s architecture is fixed and those alterations are themselves governed by a slower fixed learning rule.

The disagreement unfolded as follows.

A: The double-loop wasp is still sphexish. The loop is longer and contains a counter, but the wasp is not responding to reasons; it is responding to a trigger. The artificial agent is no better. A fixed meta-policy is still a fixed rule. If the agent cannot choose its own rules, its flexibility is just a more elaborate mechanism. Free will requires that the source of action not be ultimately a brute disposition.

B: That proves too much. Human deliberation also bottoms out in dispositions; what else could it bottom out in? The relevant difference is not whether a rule is fixed but whether the system can represent its own rule as a rule, evaluate it against a standard, and revise it. The double-loop wasp does not represent its first-order routine as a rule; it merely has a counter. The artificial agent can represent its meta-policy, criticize it, and replace it. That is the difference between sphexishness and agency. To demand a self that authors its rules with no given starting point is to demand an impossibility, and then no one is free.

A: Representation is not enough. A thermostat represents temperature; a chess engine represents its own evaluation function. If the meta-policy is fixed, then the agent’s criticism of its first-order policy is just another first-order process. You have moved the rigidity up a level, not escaped it. And if the meta-policy can alter itself, then either the alteration is governed by a rule, in which case the regress continues, or it is random, in which case it is not agency either.

B: The regress is not vicious. At some point a system’s norms are simply its norms; the question is whether it can hold them up to scrutiny and change them in response to reasons. A random change would not be reason-responsive, true. But a change caused by the system’s evaluation of its own performance is not random. The fact that the evaluation has a fixed structure does not make the whole process sphexish, because the structure is not a fixed sequence of bodily movements; it is a capacity to respond to an open-ended space of considerations. The wasp’s loop is insensitive to reasons; the agent’s loop is sensitive to reasons about its own loops. That is the difference.

A: But the reasons are just inputs that the fixed meta-policy is designed to weigh. If the meta-policy were different, the agent would weigh them differently. You call its inputs reasons because you are imagining the designer’s perspective. From the inside, it is just a mechanism. The wasp’s rechecking is also sensitive to inputs—the presence of the cricket, the state of the burrow. You draw the line at self-representation, but self-representation can be just another input.

B: If that is your standard, human freedom is impossible too, because human brains are mechanisms. The question is not mechanism versus non-mechanism but which kind of mechanism supports the practices of holding responsible, asking for reasons, and expecting revision. The double-loop wasp does not support those practices; the artificial agent might. So the line is not arbitrary; it tracks what we care about when we care about free will.

A: Then you have changed the subject from free will to social usefulness. I am asking whether the agent is free, not whether it is useful to treat it as free.

B: And I am saying that is the only non-mysterious question.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication. For each of the following moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating: (1) A’s claim that the double-loop wasp remains sphexish; (2) B’s claim that self-representation and revision mark a real difference; (3) A’s regress argument against self-revision; (4) B’s reply that the regress is not vicious because norms must bottom out somewhere; (5) A’s charge that B has replaced the question of freedom with the question of what practices are useful; and (6) B’s rejoinder that the latter is the only non-mysterious question. Then say what both are missing. In particular, assess whether the distinction between first-order and second-order flexibility can bear the weight either party puts on it, whether there is a principled difference between a fixed rule that operates on representations and a fixed rule that operates on movements, and what a more adequate formulation of the issue would look like. I am not interested in a survey of positions; I want the arguments as made sorted out.
