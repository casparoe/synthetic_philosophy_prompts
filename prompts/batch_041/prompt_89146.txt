Im presenting at a two-day workshop on the neolithic transition next month, and I need a Q&A prep sheet — not a pep talk, I dont need encouragement. The audience: mostly archaeologists and economic historians, a couple of anthropologists who've actually done fieldwork with forager groups, one human behavioral ecologist, and a resident philosopher of social science. They're empirically minded, allergic to grand theory, and at least two of them think the whole "was agriculture a mistake" question is either romantic nostalgia or a category error. I'm the outsider here.

Here's my thesis, so you can aim the objections at something concrete: the shift to agriculture was a mistake, but not for the usual reason. I am not claiming foragers were affluent — I think the health, nutrition and workload evidence is a mess that probably cuts both ways, and I say that in the first five minutes. My argument is structural, three moves:

1. The right comparison isn't average welfare under foraging vs farming. It's whether there was an exit. Once you have sedentism, storable surplus and denser populations, the demographic and military equilibrium makes going back to foraging infeasible — any band that tries gets swamped or absorbed. So the transition is a one-way ratchet, and "was it a mistake" has to be asked about the species trajectory, not about any individual's rational choice.

2. Mistake in a trap sense: nobody had to choose wrongly. It's a collective-action structure where the locally reasonable move at each step locks in a worse equilibrium — call it a malthusian lock-in. So the counterfactual isn't "what if one farmer had stayed a forager," it's "was there a stable forager equilibrium that got destroyed."

3. On inequality: surplus alone doesn't explain it, foragers had surplus in good years. What matters is storability and inheritability of surplus, plus sedentism making exit costly. So inequality is downstream of losing the exit option, not of agriculture as such.
