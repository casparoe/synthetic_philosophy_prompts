I lecture in a jurisprudence seminar — fourteen students, one ninety-minute session — and this term I sat on the panel that heard a student's appeal under an assessment rule I had voted against when we adopted it. I lost the vote, the rule stands, and I applied it; I still cannot decide whether that was a betrayal of the student or simply the point of having rules at all, and I want the students to feel that bind rather than hear me describe it. Design a session that runs them into it. In the first part they should have to apply a valid but repellent rule to a concrete case that carries small real consequences for people in the room; in the second they should have to draft a replacement rule aimed at a just outcome, with the exercise arranged so that each step they take toward that outcome costs them one of the features that make a rule workable as law — generality, publicity, prospectivity, clarity, freedom from contradiction, the possibility of compliance, constancy over time, and congruence between the rule and how officials actually apply it. What I want them to arrive at without being told is that the same eight constraints can be read either as conditions of legality or as good advice that a valid rule may flout, and that the second reading is what preserves the sentence "this is law, and it is unjust." Give me the setup and materials, the role assignments, a minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that ties each moment back to the disagreement between Hart and Fuller. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — where they will quietly bend the constraints, where the thing will feel rigged, where someone will refuse to apply the rule — and how to handle each. And say plainly which feature of the position the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in my own voice. Please write the whole answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — the way you would put it in a letter to a colleague.
