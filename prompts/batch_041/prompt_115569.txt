OK so context: I'm a retired soil chemist — 34 years measuring how contaminants crawl through groundwater, mostly tedious, occasionally not — and since I hung up the lab coat I've been reading philosophy of mind and now AI stuff. It's a bigger jump than I expected.

Here's what's bugging me. Everyone says we want AI systems that "avoid side effects" or are "low impact," and I keep running into the claim that you can't specify that without a full theory of what's actually valuable — otherwise how would the system know which changes are the bad ones? Fine. But I've also seen people insist there's a workable "don't mess things up" rule that doesn't presuppose a complete value theory, and I can't tell how much of that is real and how much is wishful thinking.

So: rank the following attempts from most to least promising, as ways of getting a usable definition of "avoid side effects" *without* already having a full specification of value. I've put them in my own words, so tell me if I've mangled any of them.

(a) The reversibility rule — impact is how hard your action is to undo; prefer moves you can back out of.

(b) The compare-to-inaction rule — impact is the difference between the world where you acted and the world where you did nothing, measured by how many of the options open in the do-nothing world are still open after you act.

(c) The other-agents rule — penalize actions that make it harder for other agents, present or future, to get whatever it is they happen to want, without assuming you know what that is.

(d) The corrigibility rule — a side effect is anything that makes you harder to correct, override, or switch off.

(e) The overlap rule — count something as a side effect only if it would count as a harm under pretty much every value system you can imagine; i.e. define impact as the intersection across values rather than the union.

For each one, a couple of sentences on where it lands and why. But what I really want is your verdict on the question underneath all of them: is any of these actually free of a value specification, or do they all just smuggle one in somewhere — in what counts as "doing nothing," or what counts as an "option," or whose goals get counted, or which value systems make it into the intersection? If I've accidentally built the answer into how I phrased one of them, say so.

Last thing. Some of this is probably genuinely contested among people who work on it and some of it is settled. Please keep those two apart instead of giving me one smooth answer that turns out to be one camp's position dressed up as the consensus. And I'm not asking for a reading list — I know the papers exist — I want your actual reasoning.
