For the past year I've been part of a fortnightly reading group on the foundations of probability — six of us, two statisticians, a physicist, and three philosophers — and we have settled into a rut I can now predict turn by turn. The frequentist says a probability is a limiting relative frequency in a suitable reference class, and that talk of chances for single events is at best shorthand. The subjectivist says a probability is a coherent degree of belief, that the numbers are the numbers, and that everything else is a manner of speaking about them. The propensity theorist says a probability is a real disposition of a chance setup, single-case and irreducible, with frequencies serving only as evidence for it. Every meeting produces the same three moves: the subjectivist wins on single cases and unrepeatable events; the frequentist wins on the reference class problem and on the thought that credences, however coherent, must answer to something; the propensity theorist wins by asking what either of the others means by 'should track' if there is no chance for credence to track. Nobody has moved in a year, and the arguments on all sides have been public for decades. I've come to suspect we aren't disagreeing in the way we think we are, and I'd like a diagnosis of the dispute rather than another round of arguments. I am not asking for a survey of the positions, a history of who said what, or a reading list — I want to know what kind of disagreement this is.

My working hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that this is not one dispute but three questions wearing one word: a metaphysical question about what in the world makes a probability claim true, an epistemic or decision-theoretic question about which credences and decisions are defensible, and a practical question about which number to plug in when you have to act. On that view the impasse comes from someone asking a question in one register and answering it in another. What makes me doubt it is that I have spent months trying to construct a case where the three views actually license different assertions or different decisions about one fully specified situation, and I keep failing: the disagreement always reappears as a disagreement about the truthmaker, never as a disagreement about what to say or do. I have also wondered whether the real work is being done by a hidden formal question — whether the representation theorem for exchangeable sequences already reconstructs everything the frequentist wants, so that the residue is only about the direction of explanation; or whether the reference class problem is a genuine obstacle rather than an artifact of demanding that a probability be assigned before you have chosen a class; or whether the propensity view is doing anything beyond naming the gap the other two leave. Test the diagnosis against cases I'd like you to consider and, where useful, invent: a sealed device whose output frequency has stabilized at three in ten over a year but whose innards are a deterministic chaotic circuit; a radioactive source whose half-life is so long that it will decay at most once in the history of the universe, so no long run exists; a coin that is fair by every physical measure but, by cosmic coincidence, lands heads on every toss ever made; and a forecasting system that is well calibrated in the long run while every individual forecast it issues is, on the best available evidence, indefensible.

Give me a prose diagnosis, as long as it needs to be and no longer, and then a single JSON object with these fields: `diagnosis_label` (one of "verbal", "substantive", "different_questions_under_one_name", "hidden_formal_or_empirical_question", "mixed", or your own label with a gloss); `one_sentence_diagnosis`; `per_side`, an array of objects with fields `position`, `core_commitment`, `what_it_is_really_claiming`, and `what_would_refute_it`; `test_cases`, an array of objects with fields `case`, `does_it_separate_the_views`, and `how_each_view_answers`; `status_of_my_hypothesis` (one of "supported", "partly supported", "refuted") with a short `why`; `what_would_count_as_progress`, an array; `what_would_not_count_as_progress`, an array; `confidence` (one of "low", "medium", "high"); `weakest_point_in_this_diagnosis`; and `pushback`. Under `pushback`, tell me plainly if my framing is confused — if the debate I have described is really three debates that never touched, if my demand for a case that separates the views is itself the wrong demand, or if the diagnosis you can honestly give is thinner than the question presupposes, say so rather than manufacturing a tidy answer on my terms. I would rather be told the question is ill-posed than be given a diagnosis that only works because I asked for one.
