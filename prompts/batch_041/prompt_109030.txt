I have been trying to determine whether Kripke's fixed-point theory genuinely resolves the Liar or merely relocates it, and I believe I have found an inconsistency in the theory itself. My reasoning is this. The construction starts with a language whose truth predicate is entirely undefined, then repeatedly places sentences into the extension of the truth predicate when they are safe and into the anti-extension when their negations are safe. A sentence that says of itself that it is not true, call it L, never becomes safe in either direction, so at the fixed point L belongs to neither the extension nor the anti-extension. But to belong to neither is simply to be not true. And L says precisely that it is not true. Therefore, at the fixed point, what L says is the case, and so L is true. Yet the fixed point was supposed to leave L without a truth value. The theory thus both affirms and denies the truth of the same sentence, and the fixed point is not a fixed point after all. I suspect I am sliding between the sense in which the theory says that L is not true and the sense in which L says of itself that it is not true, but I cannot identify the exact step where the slide occurs. Please locate the mistake, state what the theory is actually committed to regarding L, and then tell me whether the remaining difficulty is the familiar revenge problem or something else. If the proposed remedy is to enrich the language with a predicate that marks sentences as ungrounded, would that remedy simply generate a fresh Liar for the new predicate?
