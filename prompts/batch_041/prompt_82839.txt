I have been keeping a notebook of my own verdicts about when it is acceptable to say something flatly, and I want to know whether a single principle underlies them. The notebook began as a running argument with two friends — one holds that you may assert whatever you sincerely believe, the other that you may assert only what you know — and I have added to it whenever I caught myself reacting to a case. My own rough principle, which I already distrust, is that you may assert a proposition just in case you would be willing to be taken as vouching for it and to be held to it if it turns out false. Here is the list. (1) The bus: I say 'the 4:10 has already gone' because I know the timetable and it is a near-certainty, though I never saw it leave. Not acceptable, even though I am neither lying nor likely to be wrong. (2) Memory: I say 'the meeting is Tuesday' from memory, with no way to check it and no independent confirmation. Acceptable. A friend thinks this sits badly with (1), since in both cases I cannot rule out being wrong. (3) Sincere error: I say 'there is a pharmacy on the next block,' believing it on what I took to be good evidence, and there is not. Not acceptable — something went wrong with the assertion, though I did nothing wrong. (4) Sweater: I say 'that is a lovely sweater' when I think it is dowdy. Acceptable, and I would say the same of 'you look well' to someone who looks terrible. (5) Referee report: I write 'the proof of Theorem 3 fails' on an 80% hunch that there is a gap I could not chase down. Not acceptable; I should have written that I could not verify it. (6) Future: I say 'I will be at your talk on Thursday,' believing it, knowing the flu could fell me. Acceptable. (7) Surprise: I know about a surprise party on Saturday; asked what I am doing this weekend, I say 'nothing much.' Not acceptable — I want to call that a lie and not merely an evasion, even though nobody would hold it against me. (8) Gossip: I know from the editor that a colleague's paper was rejected; a mutual friend asks and I tell him. The assertion itself is in order, and the disloyalty is a separate matter. (9) Ferry, low stakes: I have read that the Sunday ferry runs but have not checked this season's schedule; over drinks, asked whether it runs, I say 'yes.' Acceptable. (10) Ferry, high stakes: the same evidence, but I need the ferry to get my sister to her wedding; asked by a stranger, I say 'yes.' Not acceptable. Nothing seems to have changed but what is at stake. (11) Testimony: on the stand I say 'I saw the blue car run the light' when I am 85% sure, having talked myself up to it overnight. Not acceptable; and yet I would say the same sentence in a pub without thinking twice, which is part of why I distrust my own list. (12) Interrogation: asked whether anyone was with me on a night when my brother was, I say 'I was alone.' Acceptable. (13) Clinic: a doctor tells a frightened patient 'the results are normal' when there is a benign anomaly she judges the patient cannot yet take in. Not acceptable.

Is there a single principle that gets all of these, or close? Test my vouching principle against the list and repair it if it can be repaired; if it cannot, give me the smallest set of principles that does the work, saying for each clause which case forces it and what it rules out. I also want to know which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate and which of them I should give up — I would rather lose a verdict than multiply clauses, but tell me honestly where that preference is costing me. Then tell me what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed: give me at least one case where it diverges from what most people would say, and one where it licenses an assertion that I would still expect the speaker to be criticised for. Keep the epistemic question separate from the rest: in (8) and (12) I take the fault, if any, to lie elsewhere, and I want to know what the principle is even saying in those cases.

If my list turns out to answer to a position that already has a name, mention it in a clause, but do not lean on the label; I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations, only for the principle and its verdicts. Please give the answer in this form: a short prose statement of the principle first; then a table with the columns Case | My verdict | Your principle's verdict | Agreement (saves, overrides, or silent) | Clause doing the work; then a JSON object with the fields principle (one sentence), clauses (an array of objects with clause, motivating_case, what_it_rules_out), verdicts_to_abandon (array of case labels), new_case_predictions (an array of objects with case, predicted_verdict, why), epistemic_vs_other (how the principle sorts the cases where the fault is not epistemic), weakest_step (the part of the reasoning most likely to be wrong), and what_would_change_your_mind (the case or argument that would move you to a different principle). Be explicit about where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and do not paper over a case the principle mishandles in order to keep my list intact.
