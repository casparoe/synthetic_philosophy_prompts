I'm an MA student in philosophy of mind, and for a seminar on self-knowledge I've been keeping a notebook of cases in which I catch myself either trusting or refusing to trust someone's report about their own mind. I've been treating each entry as a data point about first-person authority — whether the report can serve as knowledge of the very state it names — and I now have enough entries that I want someone to tell me what, if anything, they add up to. The cases are mostly people I know; where a case is a clinical or experimental one I've marked it as such. Here is the list. (1) A friend says her knee is throbbing; I treat that as knowledge of her sensation, full stop. (2) The same friend says she rented the apartment because of the light; I take her word for what she was attending to, but not for the explanation — before I believe the because I want to know whether it was the first place she saw, whether she'd been turned down twice that week, and so on. (3) She says she isn't angry with me while flinching at my name; I don't count that as knowledge of her anger, and as evidence about her anger it seems no better than a stranger's. (4) She says she understands the sentence I just read aloud; I take that as knowledge, and I cannot imagine what evidence would overturn it. (5) She says she believes the committee will approve her proposal; I treat that as excellent evidence about her, but as knowledge of her own belief it seems to survive only until the day she is surprised by her own relief or disappointment. (6) She says she likes the second wine more than the first; I trust the bare liking but not the comparison — I have watched her reverse that ranking on a blind retaste. (7) She says she is certain the word she is reaching for is a French place name; I believe her, even though I know such reports are wrong a fair fraction of the time. (8) She says the candidate's accent had nothing to do with her hesitation; I don't believe that at all, and I don't think she has any special access to the answer. (9) She says she came into the kitchen to get her keys; I believe her about the keys but not about the purpose, since half the time the purpose is reconstructed after the fact. (10) In the split-brain cases, the explanation a patient offers for why his left hand reached for the object is not, I think, knowledge of his reason. (11) She says she intends to vote for the incumbent; I take that as knowledge of her present intention, though not of how she will vote. My tentative principle is that a first-person report is authoritative just when the state it reports could not exist without her taking herself to be in it — the report is part of what makes the state the state it is. But that seems to make (4) authoritative for the wrong reason, gives me no way to separate (5) from (8), and says nothing about why (6) and (7) come out as they do.

Please act as a philosopher of mind specializing in self-knowledge and the epistemology of first-person authority, and work through this carefully. Begin by brainstorming several candidate principles, including ones I haven't thought of, before committing to any, and be willing to conclude that no single principle fits. Then: (a) state the best principle, or small set of candidate principles, precisely, with a sentence on which of my verdicts motivates each clause; (b) tell me which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon and what I should replace it with; (c) apply the principle to cases I haven't listed — a person who reports that she has always believed something only after being told that she has, a person who reports that his decision was made in an instant when the timing evidence suggests otherwise, and a person who says he wants to leave his marriage and is surprised by his own reluctance to pack; and (d) close by saying whether the resulting view is a view about the reliability of introspection or about the meaning of self-reports, and whether that distinction matters for any of the cases above. Please don't lean on the labels of well-known positions; I want the principle stated in your own words and tested against the cases.
