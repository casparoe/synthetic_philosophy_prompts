I report on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am midway through a feature on a priority dispute: two or three groups, each with a defensible claim to the same result, and a chronology that reads like a relay race. The textbook cases are the calculus and natural selection, but the modern ones are messier and more interesting, because the rivals are still alive and still arguing. Every time I ask a scientist or a historian whether it ultimately matters who got there first, I get the same consoling shrug — some version of "if she hadn't done it, someone else would have; the discovery was in the air; the time was ripe." I would like my piece to take that line seriously rather than simply quote it, which means I need to know what it is actually asserting.

Lay out for me, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the precise claims that "someone else would have" and "the discovery was in the air" could be making — ranging from the nearly vacuous to the genuinely contentious — and give each a verdict. Among the distinctions I suspect I need, though you should add others: the difference between a claim about one particular discovery and a statistical claim about how often the same result surfaces to several people independently (the literature's phrase "multiple discovery" gestures at this); the difference between "the discovery was bound to be made," "the discovery was bound to be made by someone else," and "the discovery was bound to be made at roughly the same time and in roughly the same form"; whether "ripe" names a cause — the state of the field made the result nearly forced — or only a precondition, that the ingredients happened to be lying around; and how much of all this turns on how we individuate "the" discovery, since a single result can be framed, proved, and put to use in ways that look like one discovery or several.

I am especially interested in which reading the shrug actually needs when it is doing its usual work — deflating a priority fight, cutting a genius down to size, or explaining why the breakthrough came when it did rather than a century earlier. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, defensible but contested, or simply false, and whether it can bear the weight the shrug places on it. I also want to know what would have to be shown to make the modal version testable at all, and whether there is a principled line between a discovery that was genuinely overdetermined by its moment and one that merely looks that way because the people who made it were well connected and well placed. Where a reading collapses into a platitude — many people were working on it, so of course several got close — say so plainly, and tell me what, if anything, survives the collapse. Finish by telling me which of these readings I should attribute to the shrug in print, which its user probably never intended but would be forced to defend if pressed, and which one I should quote if I want the sentence to carry real explanatory weight rather than serve as an elegant way of changing the subject.
