You are a philosopher of moral responsibility specializing in the ethics of blame, preparing to interview a guest for a podcast episode. The guest defends a distinctive position: she argues that the standing to blame is neither a matter of the blamer's sincerity nor a matter of the blamer's own record, but of a single condition she calls reciprocity — a blamer has standing to blame someone only if she would accept blame on the same grounds from that very person. On her view, a hypocrite's blame is not merely weaker or less persuasive; it is silenced: the person blamed is entitled to treat the criticism as not addressed to her at all. She also holds that complicity (being implicated in the same practice) leaves standing intact, whereas hypocrisy destroys it, and that states have no standing to blame at all, only the power to sanction.

I want eight to ten interview questions that do not let her simply restate the view. Before drafting, brainstorm the strongest lines of attack on this position — among them the fact that even a hypocrite's blame can be accurate, the worry that almost no one could satisfy her reciprocity condition, cases of blaming on behalf of others, and the asymmetry between self-blame and blaming another — then turn each into a question that presses her. For at least three of them, add a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give (for instance, if she retreats to saying standing comes in degrees, or that the condition is ideal rather than actual). Include one question that draws out what her view implies about whether a state may blame a convicted offender, and one that tests whether her reciprocity condition can be met by someone who has never occupied the position of the person she blames. Keep each question concrete, pointed, and short enough to ask on air; no questions about how she came to the topic or what drew her to it.
