I spent 38 years as a geophysicist — seismic work mostly, then a decade in a university department — and retired about six years ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy the way I used to read field reports: too much, too late at night. My old lab still runs a Wednesday seminar and they've asked me to do a session on machine learning, because apparently I'm now the guy who knows what a transformer is and also what a category mistake is, which is a strange combination to be. Anyway, I need help with the handout.

Here's the argument I keep having. Someone says a trained network has a direction in its activations that means "this sentence is about a city." Someone else says that's not a discovery about the network, it's a fact about the person holding the probe. Both of them think the other one is confused, and the argument always stalls, and I've started to think it stalls because they're answering different questions. I want a decision tree on a handout so that twenty people — half of them ML people who think philosophy is a waste of time, half philosophers who think matrix multiplication is — can each find themselves in it without already knowing the labels.

Build it from the questions first, not from a list of isms. Start from something nobody in the room will fight about: a trained network is a fixed function, a pile of numbers, and nothing in the training objective ever mentions meaning, truth, or content. Then branch wherever people actually come apart.

Some material to work with. These are toy results from a colleague, so treat them as the concrete cases in the tree, not as findings to look up:

(a) Two networks, different architectures, trained on different data, one of them noticeably worse at the job. Both end up with a linear direction in their activations that a probe can use to tell whether a sentence is about a city, and the two directions are nearly the same.

(b) From the same layer you can train a probe that recovers which of twenty source documents a paragraph came from, and also a probe that recovers a label you assigned at random to the training examples. Both work well.

(c) Knock out the direction the first probe uses and the network barely changes — same accuracy, same outputs. Change the input in a way that probe is sensitive to and the output changes too.

(d) Suppose we could write the network out as a lookup table returning exactly the same outputs for every input. Same behavior, no layers, no geometry.

(e) Somebody says the network represents the world the way a thermometer represents the temperature. Somebody else says a thermometer represents nothing at all, it just tracks.

I also want room for a rule we had in my old field, and I dont know if it has any analogue here: a model "represented" the subsurface only if it kept working when you used it somewhere you hadn't trained it.

What the tree needs to do:

- Eight to ten branch questions, each phrased so a person can answer it without knowing which leaf it leads to. No question of the form "so, do you think networks have representations?" Every node should be a case or a claim.
- Six to nine leaves. I'd like these among them, in my words, but call them whatever the space actually calls for: (1) yes, and the probes are finding something that is really there; (2) there's real structure, but content or aboutness is the wrong word for it; (3) the probes are our instruments, there's no fact of the matter, only what's useful to say; (4) yes, but only relative to a task we specify, so there are many equally real answers; (5) no, and the whole vocabulary is a mistake, a function doesn't represent anything; (6) yes, and what fixes the content is what the feature got selected to do by training; (7) it's a verbal dispute and nothing turns on it. If two of these turn out to be the same leaf, say so and merge them.
- For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't separate, and say why.
- Note the answer patterns that come out empty — combinations the tree logically allows but that nobody occupies — with a one-line diagnosis of each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
