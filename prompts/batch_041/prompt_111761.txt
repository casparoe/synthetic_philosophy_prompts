Something I keep running into in my corner of the internet is a certain analogy for peer disagreement, and I would like it audited rather than either endorsed or waved away. The pattern is this: two people who seem comparably informed, comparably careful, and comparably honest about some question arrive at different answers, and a third party — or one of the two — observes that this is just the situation of two instruments disagreeing. Two clocks read different times; two scales weigh the same parcel differently; two thermometers in the same room give different readings. Since neither reading can be privileged from the inside, the sensible response, on this picture, is to treat each disputant as a noisy measurement of a common quantity and to move toward the midpoint. The analogy is nearly always doing the same work in the argument: it is used to license conciliationism, and specifically the strong version of it on which both parties are required to update toward each other, and a refusal to do so is a species of arrogance — the epistemic equivalent of a clock insisting that it is right and every other clock is broken.

I have two reservations, and I would like them treated separately, because I suspect they fail in different ways. The first is that the analogy appears to presuppose that the two readings are independent conditional on the truth, since otherwise averaging buys nothing — and that is precisely what is at issue in the cases I care about, where the two parties share a body of evidence, a training, a set of heuristics, and often a culture. Two clocks disciplined by the same radio time signal are not independent instruments, and their agreement is no evidence at all; their disagreement is evidence of something, but not of the thing the averaging story wants. The second reservation concerns the metric. Averaging presupposes a common scale, a determinate location for the midpoint, and errors that are at least roughly unbiased, and I do not know what the midpoint is between a verdict of applies and a verdict of does not apply, nor how anyone could know from the inside that the two errors are symmetric rather than that one of the two parties is simply wrong.

What I would like from you is the following. Give me a correspondence table for the analogy — what plays the role of the instrument, the measurand, the calibration, the precision, the discrepancy between readings, and the averaging or error-correcting procedure the picture recommends. Then separate the disanalogies into those that bear on the conciliationist conclusion the analogy is being used to support and those that are harmless to it, and tell me whether that conclusion survives once the relevant ones are on the table. I am particularly interested in whether the analogy supports conciliationism as such, or only the stronger claim that the correct response is to land somewhere between the two positions; those come apart if the metric is missing. And I would like to know which side, if either, the picture actually favours. My own suspicion is that the steadfast position needs a standpoint outside both disputants, and that the instrument analogy quietly supplies one by imagining an observer who is not himself one of the instruments — precisely the standpoint the epistemic situation withholds. Finally, if the analogy is not fit for the purpose, say what would be: I would want something in which the parties can inspect each other's reasoning rather than merely register each other's outputs, and in which the truth of the matter is not stipulated to be independent of the procedures that are attempting to reach it.
