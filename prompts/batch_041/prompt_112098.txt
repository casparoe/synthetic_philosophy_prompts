I've just joined a small AI safety charity and we've got to bet the next year on one research direction — my colleague is definately convinced it should be AI safety via debate: two models argue, a weaker judge decides the winner, and the truth is supposed to win because a liar has to keep defending the lie. My worry is the obfuscated-arguments problem: the dishonest side can assert something whose disproof would take the honest side an astronomically long time to find, so the honest debater simply can't produce it inside the time allowed, and the judge has no way to tell who's bluffing. I've got a scratch list of patches people have thrown at me and I need to know which ones are actually load-bearing. Sort them into tiers by how much you'd trust each to defuse obfuscated arguments specifically — not to solve oversight in general — with a sentence or two on why each lands where it does, and tell me if any of them are the same idea in different clothes:

1. give the honest debater a much larger compute and time budget than the dishonest one, scaled to how lopsided the burden of proof is;
2. require every claim to arrive with a short certificate the judge can check by itself, so an obfuscated argument has to hand over something checkable;
3. only allow questions whose answers come with cheaply checkable witnesses, and refuse to debate everything else;
4. let the judge interrupt and interrogate mid-debate, so it can dig at the step it doesn't follow;
5. recurse — if the honest debater can't refute a step, spawn a sub-debate about whether that step is valid, and only judge the leaves;
6. an ensemble of judges with different training and different compute budgets, majority vote;
7. dock a debater's score later on if a claim it made turns out false, so obfuscation becomes a gamble with a delayed bill;
8. ditch the adversarial setup entirely: one model does its best, plus a critic that only hunts for internal inconsistency.

Which of these is closest to a real fix rather than a hope, and what angle am I missing?
