Sweden's 1992 school reform is the case I want to think about. Parents were given the right to send their children to an independent school, a friskola, and the child's home municipality was obliged to pay that school roughly what it would have spent on the child in its own schools; the money is usually called the skolpeng. Independent schools may not charge top-up fees and must be open to all comers, but when oversubscribed they may select on a short list of permitted grounds, chiefly siblings already enrolled, time spent in the queue, and proximity to the child's home. A new school has to be approved by the state before it can open, and once approved it is financed by the municipalities whose pupils it takes. Municipal schools, by contrast, cannot turn away a pupil assigned to them. Homeschooling, meanwhile, has been treated very restrictively, so that leaving the system altogether is a much harder thing to do than moving around within it.

I have been reading Albert Hirschman's Exit, Voice, and Loyalty, and I suspect this case does not sit comfortably in it. As I understand him: when an organization's quality declines, its members can leave for a rival, which is exit, or stay and agitate for improvement, which is voice, and loyalty is what holds people in place long enough for voice to have any force. His worry is that exit drains away precisely the members whose complaints would have mattered most, leaving the organization with a less demanding public and less reason to improve.

Do the mapping properly. Who is the member here, who is the organization, and what is the decline the reform was supposed to answer? Where does voice actually live in this system — with the head teacher, the municipal education office, the state inspectorate, the ballot box, the newspapers — and which of those count as voice in Hirschman's sense rather than as a complaint channel that costs the complainant nothing? What does loyalty have to be loyalty to for the mechanism to run: to one's own school, to the neighborhood, or to the idea of a common school that everyone attends? And what becomes of that loyalty when the choosing itself turns into the thing people are attached to?

Then the parts I think the framework cannot reach. Exit here was not a response to failure; it was designed in from the start and offered as a good in its own right. The incumbent is also the funder of its own competitor, and the entrant needs the state's permission to exist at all. Can a theory built around decline say anything about a system where the alternative was created before there was anything to escape? Can it distinguish an exit that carries information about quality from one that carries none, given that parents here also leave for proximity, siblings, religion, pedagogy, and the composition of the pupil body — reasons the receiving school may be unable to decode? And what about the family that leaves the system entirely, which is the one move that forecloses voice altogether?

I have no background in this field and may be bending his terms; straighten me out where I am. I am not after a reading list or a page reference. What I want is the analysis: what the theory explains about this reform, what it misdescribes, and what the reform shows about the limits of a framework whose whole drama is decline, exit, and the voice that exit silences. Where you are unsure of a Swedish fact, say so rather than assert it, and do not rest the argument on contested claims about how the reform turned out.
