I'm presenting a paper to my department in which I argue that free-floating rationales — the reasons for which a system behaves, which obtain even when no reasoner does — are a genuine and graded explanatory category, not a mere façon de parler. My proposal is that a free-floating rationale is real to the degree that the behavior is counterfactually sensitive to the reason: a plant's phototropism carries only a thin rationale, because the behavior would look much the same across a wide range of candidate "reasons," whereas a bird's broken-wing display carries a thick one, because it tracks the predator's position and the chicks' vulnerability in ways that would change the display if the reasons changed. I claim that the thick cases cannot be reduced without loss either to biological function or to information-theoretic correlation, and that this gives us a kind of reason-responsiveness that stops short of mentality.

The audience is my department: mostly philosophers of biology who are comfortable with teleological function-talk but reflexively suspicious of any talk of "reasons"; two philosophers of mind who work on intentionality and will hear "a rationale without a reasoner" as either a dead metaphor or a smuggled homunculus; and one animal-behavior researcher who will want the empirical cases pinned down.
