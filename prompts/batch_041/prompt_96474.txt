I am reconstructing for a graduate seminar a dilemma a classmate pressed on me — she calls it the tortoise's dilemma — and I cannot find the exit. The setup is this: whenever a reasoner moves from premises she accepts to a conclusion she newly accepts, either something licenses the move or nothing does. Here it is laid out.

(1) A move from accepted premises to an accepted conclusion counts as an inference only if the reasoner is entitled to make it.
(2) A reasoner is entitled to make the move only if the move is backed by a principle she accepts, a principle stating that moves of that form are permissible.
(3) If that backing principle is itself accepted as a premise, then applying it in order to reach the conclusion is one more move, which by (1) and (2) requires a further accepted principle, and so on without end.
(4) If nothing backs the move, then the move is unlicensed, and an unlicensed move is not a justified inference.
(5) So every inferential move either starts an infinite regress of accepted principles or is unlicensed.
(6) A finite reasoner cannot complete an infinite regress, and an unlicensed move is unjustified.
(7) Therefore no finite reasoner ever makes a justified inference.

She then offered an escape that I suspect is the real trap. Suppose we permit a principle that licenses its own application — a rule that, over and above telling us what follows, tells us that applying it is permitted. She says this closes the regress in a single step. I reply that it either does nothing, because the self-license is just one more premise and the regress resumes, or it quietly presupposes the very entitlement it was meant to supply. She says I have begged the question against the possibility of a basic rule. I would most like to keep (1), since I take an inference to be precisely a move one is entitled to make, and I am unwilling to accept (7). Tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling, what rejecting it costs, and whether any reading lets her self-licensing rule do genuine work. Give the answer as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, with no introduction or conclusion.
