In my department's welfare-economics reading group, the argument about interpersonal comparisons of utility has stalled in a way I can no longer make sense of. On one side: comparisons between one person's satisfaction and another's are either meaningless or covertly normative. Any two utility functions differing by a monotone transformation represent the same preferences, so the intra-personal ordering is all there is to represent, and lining two people up on a single scale is not measurement but a moral decision in disguise. On the other side: health economists and happiness researchers routinely place states on a common scale — a year lived with a given impairment against a year lived without it, say — add the numbers across people, and treat the sum as an answer to the question who is worse off. The first side calls this an artifact; the second calls it a discovery.

Nothing either side says reaches the other. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal, turning on whether "utility" names a representation of choice or a psychological quantity? Is it a hidden disagreement about what would count as evidence that two scales are commensurable, and if so, what formal result about measurement, or what experiment, would settle it? Or are the sides answering different questions under one heading, one asking what can be measured and compared, the other what can be said and meant?

My hypothesis, offered for correction rather than agreement, is the last: that the ordinalist is making a claim about the limits of representation and the comparativist a claim about the reach of predication, and that the appearance of contradiction comes from one word doing double duty. Test that against what each side actually argues, tell me if it is too neat, and say what would count as progress here — a distinction, a result, a change of question — or whether the impasse should simply be expected to last.
