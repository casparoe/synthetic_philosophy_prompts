I teach political philosophy at a state university and, as a civic side-project, I chair the planning commission of the mid-sized city where I live. I would like you to write the commission a decision procedure.

The setting: we hear about forty matters a year. Most are uncontested — variances, minor subdivisions, sign permits — and we dispatch them in minutes. The cases that divide us are rezonings, conditional-use permits, and demolition approvals, where we must adopt written findings that the city council can override only by a two-thirds vote and that are the record if a decision is appealed. We are seven appointed volunteers; none of us is trained in law, planning, or economics. Our only inputs are a ten-to-fifteen-page staff memo and a hearing that often runs past eleven; we deliberate for about half an hour per case, on the record, and the city attorney reviews our findings afterward.

Two years ago I drafted, and the council adopted, a preamble to our comprehensive plan that commits us to what the right-to-the-city tradition has always meant by that phrase, but which we have never managed to turn into anything we can actually apply. As adopted, it contains four claims. First, habitation: those who live somewhere have a claim to go on living there at a cost they can bear, and that claim is not extinguished by their being outbid. Second, participation: those who will bear the consequences of a land-use decision have a claim to shape it, not merely to comment on it, and who counts as affected is a question of fact about the decision, not of who happened to turn up at the hearing. Third, appropriation: a neighborhood is where a form of life is made, not merely a stock of dwellings, and its worth to its inhabitants is not exhausted by what they would pay for it. Fourth, openness: the claims of those not yet here — the priced-out, the excluded, the people who will need this city in twenty years — belong in the same ledger, and a city that cannot add housing fails them.

Write the procedure that turns this preamble into something a commissioner can run during a hearing. I want the questions in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which item in the staff memo would supply it; where the procedure stops with a no; where it must hand the question to a discretionary vote of the commission, and what exactly that vote is about; and where it must go to the city attorney rather than be resolved by us. Give us a fixed skeleton for the written findings, and say what to do when the memo lacks the datum a step requires — we cannot commission a displacement study mid-hearing. Keep what the preamble actually says while making it usable by someone without the theory; tell me which of its distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped. Do not launder an incommensurable trade-off through a scoring rubric or a weighted average: if two of the claims cannot be ranked on the facts of a case, the procedure should say so and say what the vote is really deciding. Flag every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases. (a) A sixty-unit market-rate building with twelve units at sixty percent of area median income, on a site now occupied by a twelve-unit rent-controlled building whose tenants have lived there for decades; they would get relocation payments and a right of first refusal to return at the new rents, which they cannot pay. (b) A downzoning petition from a historically Black neighborhood that has been losing its housing to teardown and speculation; granting it would cut citywide capacity by roughly two hundred units over a decade. (c) A community land trust's affordable project, beloved by everyone in the room, whose financing requires relocating thirty households and demolishing forty units of unsubsidized housing that will not be replaced. At least two of the three should come out in a way that will be politically intolerable to this commission, and I want you to say plainly which ones and why the framework forces the result.

Close by telling me what this procedure cannot decide, where it will misfire in practice, and which of the four claims is doing the least work — that is, which one the procedure could drop without changing a single outcome.
