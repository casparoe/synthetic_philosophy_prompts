I have been asked to chair a small working group advising a national public-health agency on how to spend a modest research budget — enough, roughly, to fund three postdocs for two years — on the conceptual and ethical questions raised by the agency's growing reliance on choice architecture: defaults for organ donation and for screening uptake, opt-out arrangements, social-norm messaging, simplified forms, and the like. The leadership is broadly sympathetic to what is usually called libertarian paternalism and is prepared to be constrained by arguments, but it will not fund a general seminar in political philosophy, and it has no appetite for another round of "is nudging manipulative?" pieces that restate familiar positions. What it wants from us is an agenda: a defensible answer to which unresolved questions in this area most deserve the next few years of someone's working life, and why.

I offer my own provisional list less as a menu than as something to argue with. Is there any principled way to specify the baseline against which a nudge counts as a distortion of choice rather than a feature of it — or does every candidate baseline smuggle in a conception of the good, so that the neutrality the framework advertises is unavailable? Does the permissibility of a nudge turn on the mechanism by which it operates, so that interventions working through a bias are suspect in a way that interventions supplying reasons are not, and can that line be drawn without collapsing? Is there a genuine aggregation problem, in that many effective nudges require a large fraction of the population to be influenced, so that even an intervention no individual has grounds to resent is one in which individuals are used as means to a collective end? Who is entitled to set the defaults, and can that authority be legitimated without anything resembling explicit deliberative authorization? Is libertarian paternalism stable, or do the considerations that license the gentle interventions also license the coercive ones, leaving the "libertarian" half of the label doing no independent work? And there is a phenomenon I have taken to calling nudge drift: the administrative machinery built to shift behaviour in one well-justified domain tends to be reused, once it exists, for harder and less well-justified ends, and it is unclear whether this is a contingent fact about institutions or something the framework itself invites.

I would like you to rank the most important of these open questions — including any I have not named — for a group with two years and a philosophical rather than behavioural-science skill set, and to defend the ranking rather than merely present it. Set out your criteria explicitly (how much downstream work depends on the answer, tractability, neglectedness, whether settling it would change what the agency actually does), say where those criteria pull against one another, and say for each item what would move it up or down the list within the next couple of years. Be careful to separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature; I do not want a contested position presented to the agency as the consensus. Structure the response as follows: "What is settled and what is not" (about 200 words), "Criteria" (about 150 words), "The agenda, ranked" (600–900 words, one item per open question, each with its case for priority and its chief liability), "What I would deprioritize, and why" (about 200 words), and "What would reorder this list" (about 150 words). Close with the two or three items you would bet on if you had to bet, and say plainly how confident you are.
