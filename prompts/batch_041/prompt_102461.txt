I serve on the review committee of a small foundation that funds research aimed at reducing suffering in the natural world through biotechnology: engineering animals so that they feel less pain, driving down the populations of parasites that cause lingering disease in their hosts, redesigning food webs so that creatures are not eaten alive. As a committee we are committed to a particular view, which I will state plainly so that you can work with it: what makes suffering bad is the suffering itself and not the loss of some further good, so a technology that removes a form of suffering is good in itself even when it kills, sterilizes, or permanently alters a species, and the misery of wild animals counts for exactly as much as the misery of animals we can see. Our difficulty is operational. We receive three or four proposals a month — a gene drive meant to crash a biting-fly population, a request to breed a line of farmed salmon with a blunted sensitivity to pain, a plan to extinguish a predator painlessly so that its prey stop being torn apart — and we have no consistent way to decide. We meet for ninety minutes once a month, none of us is a biologist, we cannot run trials ourselves, and any award above two hundred thousand dollars has to be signed off by a board that does not share our view. Two internal rules constrain everything we do: the charter forbids funding any intervention whose effects cannot be bounded to a single species, and the board has asked us never to fund anything irreversible.

Please write the decision procedure our committee can actually run. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to the board or to an individual because the question is one of values rather than of fact; a short set of tests we can apply inside the meeting; and an honest marking of which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and of the points where our own starting view, rather than the evidence, is doing the work. The procedure also has to face two problems I do not know how to handle: first, whether a being we engineer to suffer less thereby has less standing, so that reducing a creature's capacity for pain quietly reduces what it is owed; and second, whether removing suffering that already exists is the same kind of act as preventing suffering that has not yet begun.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. The first is a gene drive to eliminate a parasite that causes a painful, chronic disease in a wild mammal. The second is a request to engineer a farmed salmon line with greatly reduced pain sensitivity. The third, which I suspect the procedure will reject and which I will not enjoy watching it reject, is a proposal to eliminate a predator species painlessly and permanently in order to end the suffering of everything it eats. For each, give the procedure's verdict, show the steps that produced it, and say where the procedure misfires or falls silent and a person has to decide. Please write your answer in American English.
