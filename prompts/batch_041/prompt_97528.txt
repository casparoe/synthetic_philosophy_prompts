I teach a 3rd-year seminar in decision theory, about 18 students, one 75-minute session, and I want to run a betting game that makes ambiguity aversion actually bite rather than just being the two-urn story everyone's already half-read. Rough setup I have in mind: two opaque bags, one with a published 50/50 mix of red and black balls, one with a hidden mix, students bet small tokens on a colour. But I don't want it to stop at the stock Ellsberg result. I want to fold in the comparative-ignorance angle — the idea that the aversion is really driven by comparing your own state of knowledge to someone who knows more, and that it fades when nobody in the room is better informed than you. So I need the activity staged as a contrast: one round where someone is quietly clued in about the hidden bag, or where everyone's choices are made public so people can see who seems to know, against a round where the whole room is equally in the dark.

Give me the rules, materials, the token payoff scheme (no real money), the order of rounds, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the sure-thing principle, the risk-versus-ambiguity distinction, and the comparison effect. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where students game the announced choices, randomise out of indifference, or leak the hidden mix — and be honest about the one thing the game can't settle: whether preferring the known bag is a mistake or a sensible response to genuine uncertainty. Don't lecture the theory up front; let them walk into it.
