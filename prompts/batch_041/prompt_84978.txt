Please explain a conceptual issue rather than survey a literature. The issue is whether the basic axioms of arithmetic might be inconsistent, and I want the anatomy of the worry made explicit, with the technical content left intact and every term defined as you go.

Three things tend to get run together; separate them, then relate them.

(1) There exists a derivation of a contradiction from the axioms. This is a finite combinatorial fact about strings of symbols: either such a derivation exists or it does not, whether or not anyone knows it.
(2) The axioms have no model — no structure of which they are all true — so they fail to describe anything.
(3) We have, or could ever have, a reason to change our mathematical practice because of a contradiction, and could ever exhibit one.

Explain how (1) and (2) are equivalent, and how that equivalence is itself available to us only relative to a background theory whose own consistency is as much in question as that of the system under discussion. Explain why the result that a sufficiently strong system cannot prove its own consistency cuts both ways: it supplies neither a reason to expect inconsistency nor a proof of consistency, and by itself makes the worry neither reasonable nor unreasonable. Explain what justification the consistency of arithmetic could have if not a proof from within it, and what that justification would ultimately rest on. Say also what a single contradiction does to a classical system — I take it that it makes every sentence derivable — and why that consequence, rather than the contradiction itself, is what is supposed to be alarming.

Then evaluate this proposal, which I am inclined to take seriously and suspect is confused:

'The consistency of arithmetic is a live worry only if a contradiction could be derived by a proof short enough for human beings to write down and check. If the shortest derivation of a contradiction, supposing one exists, would be longer than any proof that could ever be produced, then the question is idle: the axioms would remain usable for every purpose we could ever have, and it would be superstition to be troubled by a fact that could never touch us.'

Say what the proposal gets right about the difference between a mathematical possibility and a practical one, and where it goes wrong. In particular: does it confuse the consistency of the axioms with our reasons for believing them? Does it treat the axioms as instruments rather than as claims about a subject matter, and is that a coherent thing to say about arithmetic? Does the length of a proof bear on what the proof establishes? And what would the proposal imply about the right response if a short contradiction were found tomorrow — abandon the axioms, weaken them, or move to a logic in which a contradiction does not infect everything — and what would guide that choice?

Finally, assess how much evidential weight our failure to find a contradiction so far deserves, and whether that weight depends on how long the shortest derivation would have to be.
