Take the role of a philosopher of social science: someone who works on social ontology and the metaphysics of social kinds, and who also knows the empirical literature on how racial, gender, and monetary categories actually function in practice. Think carefully before you answer, and treat the following as the whole of your brief.

I've been reading Less Wrong and the surrounding blog ecosystem for well over a decade, and I've watched the sentence "X is a social construct" settle arguments the way "the map is not the territory" does: everyone nods, no two people cash it out the same way, and the person who says it loudest wins the thread. I want it disassembled rather than debunked.

First, brainstorm before you evaluate. Write out every distinct precise claim that the formula could be asserting, from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical. Press on the dimensions that actually distinguish them: is the claim about our concept of X or about X itself? Is it about causation, constitution, convention, contingency, or merely historical origin? When the word "social" does the work, is it standing for "collectively believed," "institutionally enforced," "arbitrary," "optional," or "not really real"? Does the claim deny only a biological basis, or also a mind-independent one? Is it a claim about the category, the property, the members, or the practice of classifying?

Second, grade the readings. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, a live controversy, or simply false, with a sentence of justification. Then say which reading the people who repeat the slogan most likely have in mind, and flag where you think they are wrong about their own position.

Third, run the same sentence through three fillers — race, gender, money — and tell me for each which readings come out true, which come out false, and which are being leaned on in the standard arguments. The uses I keep running into: someone at a meetup citing money's constructedness to argue that monetary policy is essentially unconstrained; "gender is a social construct" deployed to settle whether trans women are women; and "race is a social construct" deployed to argue that race-conscious policy is incoherent. For each, say which reading of the slogan the conclusion needs, and whether anyone actually defends that reading.

Fourth, take the inference chain separately: constructed, therefore arbitrary; arbitrary, therefore changeable; changeable, therefore illegitimate or unworthy of respect. Which links hold as stated, which need extra premises, and which is the one usually skipped?

Fifth, the question I most want answered: is there a single thesis that is true of all three kinds, or does the formula equivocate, so that "race is a social construct" and "money is a social construct" differ as much as either differs from "sandwiches are a social construct"? If it equivocates, say exactly where the equivocation lives.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Where specialists divide, I would rather have "the field splits roughly along these lines, and here is what each side would say" than a confident verdict that papers over the disagreement. Skip the bibliography — I don't need citations and you shouldn't guess at them; identify a position by its content rather than attaching it to a work you may be misremembering. A complete and slightly sprawling menu beats a tidy and truncated one.
