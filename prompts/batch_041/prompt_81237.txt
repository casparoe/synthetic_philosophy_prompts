I am in my final year of high school, and for the last session of our philosophy club I have been asked to prepare a handout that will let the other members work out where they actually stand on a question we have been circling for two meetings: whether a reason for action has to be capable of moving the person it is a reason for. The way our teacher set it up, some people hold that a consideration counts as a reason for you only if it hooks onto something you already care about, while others hold that you can have a reason to do something even when nothing in your present motivational make-up would move you, and that failing to be moved is then a failure of rationality rather than proof that there was no reason to begin with. I would like you to write me a decision tree — a flowchart, with roughly eight to ten branch nodes — that takes a reader from an initial commitment to a named position, each leaf being one of the positions the tree can reach.

Two things matter to me. First, every node has to be phrased so that a reader can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to, which means the nodes should be concrete cases or plain yes/no claims, not questions like 'do you accept that there are external reasons?'. Please let the cases carry the weight, and make sure the tree includes at least the following: a person who is entirely indifferent to her own future wellbeing; a self-described amoralist who sincerely reports that he has no reason to be decent to anyone; someone whose apparent indifference to something we would expect to matter to her turns out on inspection to be depression or exhaustion; a person whose desire rests on a false belief about the facts; and a person raised in a community whose values he has since rejected, but who finds he still cannot bring himself to act otherwise. The leaves should include, at a minimum, a straightforward Humean view on which reasons are constituted by one's present desires; a view on which what you have reason to do depends on what you would be moved by after informed deliberation, which may revise your existing motivations; a Kantian-style externalism on which rational agents have reasons regardless of what moves them; a position on which the amoralist is not making a mistake but stands outside the practice of reasons altogether; and a quietist or deflationary leaf holding that the whole dispute is merely verbal. Second, where two of your leaves cannot be separated by any question you have written, say so explicitly and describe the further question that would be needed to prise them apart; and where the logical space permits a combination that no one seems to occupy, mark it as unoccupied and add a sentence on whether it is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Descriptive names for the positions are fine, but please do not build the handout around the names of particular philosophers or their books — I want the members sorted by what they say about the cases rather than by which label they happen to like, and I would rather not turn a club handout into a reading list.
