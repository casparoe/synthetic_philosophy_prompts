My mother turns seventy-six in March and is closing the bakery she has kept open, at the same corner, for forty-four years. The reason is her eyes: macular degeneration, diagnosed four years ago, now far enough along that she can no longer read the order slips or the recipe cards she has known by heart since she was thirty. She is not going willingly. We are holding the party in the back room — the long table, both ovens cold for the first time in my memory — and I am the only speaker. Forty-odd people will be there: my brother and me, her two surviving sisters, the women who worked the counter over the decades and their grown children, a solid block of the parish, and perhaps a dozen customers who have bought bread from her since the Carter administration. Some of them want a warm, funny, slightly tearful five minutes; the parish contingent will expect something that sounds a little like a blessing; and almost nobody in the room has read any philosophy, with the exception of a retired schoolteacher in the second row who would not forgive me an error of scholarship.

I want to build the speech, at least in its bones, on Cicero's dialogue on old age, in which he has Cato the Elder, at eighty-four, answer the four standard charges against growing old: that it withdraws a man from active life, that it weakens the body, that it strips away his pleasures, and that it leaves him within sight of death. I read it in college and again last month, and my trouble is that I cannot tell which of Cato's answers are of any use to me here. The answer about remaining useful through counsel, authority, and judgment is the one I most want to lean on, but my mother's work was not counsel; she was up at four and on her feet. The ending, where Cato is untroubled by death because he expects the soul to survive it, I cannot bring myself to use: she is a churchgoer and would not say she disbelieves it, but she would hear it as a change of subject, and she would be right. Meanwhile the parts I distrust are the parts that might actually reach her — that what one misses least in old age is the appetite of youth, that a life which has done its work is a harvest rather than a failure, that the old are owed a certain authority by those who owe them nothing else.

Write the speech. Keep it free of Latin, free of any quotation from the dialogue, and free of citations; I want the arguments working in plain speech rather than being delivered as scholarship. It should close on a toast, and it has to be honest enough that she does not feel handled — whatever Cato's consolations are worth, they are being offered to a woman whose body has just taken her work from her, and she knows exactly what has happened. After the speech, put a final line of the form \answer{...} naming, in a short phrase, the one of Cato's four charges that your speech decides not to answer head-on.
