I am a software engineer on a machine-learning team, with no formal training in philosophy, and I would like help with an argument I keep losing. Our internal evaluation practice is, by everyone's admission, unsatisfactory: we sweep hundreds of configurations and report the best one, we keep retuning our benchmark until it stops discriminating, and when a system fails we usually cannot say whether the method, the implementation, or the data is responsible. I proposed that we spend a few sessions on the philosophy of science, or failing that on the more basic question of what our experiments are supposed to establish, and a colleague closed the matter by observing that the philosophy of science is to scientists as ornithology is to birds. He attributes the line to Feynman; I have seen the same sentence credited to other people and to no one at all, and I have no idea who, if anyone, said it first, so please do not treat the provenance as settled. Another colleague's version of the objection is simply that philosophy has not kept up with science.

What I want is not a defence of philosophy but an inventory I can bring to the next team meeting. Lay out the distinct precise claims that the ornithology comparison could be making, arranged from the nearly empty to the very strong, and give a verdict on each: which are platitudes nobody would contest, which are substantive and defensible, which are dubious or false, and which are not about usefulness at all but about some other relation, such as who is entitled to speak about a practice, what authority description has over performance, or how intellectual labour ought to be divided.

It would help if the readings were separated along at least these axes: useful to an individual practitioner in the middle of a working week; useful to a scientific community over decades; useful for describing what scientists do; useful for criticising or reforming what they do; and useful for drawing the boundary between science and non-science. It also matters whether the target is the academic discipline of philosophy of science or the habit of methodological self-reflection among working scientists, since our proposal was the second and the line, as stated, appears aimed at the first.

Then take the comparison on its own terms. Ornithology is itself a science about birds; birds do not consult it; yet ornithology does change what happens to birds. Which of these features does the analogy need in order to work, and which does it get wrong when the objects of study are people capable of reading the literature about themselves? Finally, tell me which single reading my colleague must be relying on for the line to do the work he wants it to do, namely to end the discussion, and whether that reading survives the cases in which methodological reflection seems to have altered practice rather than merely described it: the control group, blinding, deciding in advance what would count as failure, the difference between testing a hypothesis and going looking for one. If some reading does survive, say precisely what it would and would not license us to skip.
