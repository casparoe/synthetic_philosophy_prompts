Ok so I'm in a normativity reading group and we have been stuck on the wrong kind of reasons for like a year now, and I want an honest diagnosis of what we're actually fighting about. The cases we keep recycling: a demon threatens to torture you unless you admire him; a bookie offers you a pile of money if you believe your lottery ticket is a winner; your doctor tells you the pill will work, and you know that the believing is part of what does the work. Camp A says these are reasons in the ordinary sense, facts that count in favor of an attitude, and the reason they don't make the attitude rational is that there's a second class of reason, the kind that bears on whether the attitude gets its object right, and only that class is doing the justifying. Camp B says the threat isn't a reason for the admiration at all, it's a reason to want to admire, or to take a pill that makes you admire, or to arrange to be the sort of person who admires, so there's one class of reason and camp A is just misdescribing what the reason is a reason for. Both camps agree that admiring someone because a demon made you is irrational, both agree the admiring can still be fitting in some thin sense, and I've been thinking alot about it and I cannot find a single case where the two camps predict different verdicts. Which makes me suspect we have a verbal dispute wearing a substantive-sounding name.

But then a friend in the group threw this at me and now I'm not so sure. In the placebo case, the fact that believing you'll recover makes recovery more likely is evidence that you'll recover, so the pragmatic reason is also an evidential one. Is that a counterexample to the whole distinction, or just a weird overlap that shows the distinction was never meant to be exclusive? And there's a nastier version where the doctor only tells you the pill will work because she knows your believing is what does the work, which seems to route the pragmatic consideration into the evidence itself. I don't know what to do with that, and neither does anyone else in the room.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, substantive about what makes an attitude rational, two camps answering different questions under one name, or a disagreement about how to individuate reasons in the first place (by the attitude they support versus by the question they answer)? My hypothesis, which I'd like you to either defend or kill, is that it's the individuation thing, and that the placebo case is the tell, because it forces you to say whether the reason is the belief's content being likely or the belief's being useful, and the two camps silently pick different answers.

Whatever your diagnosis, also tell me what would actually count as progress: a case, a distinction, a formal result, or the conclusion that nothing will move it because the question is ill-posed. And take a definite position. Don't hand me a reading list or a both-sides summary with a shrug at the end, I want you to land somewhere, tell me what you'd bet on, and give your reasons. American English please.
