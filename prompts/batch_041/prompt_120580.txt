I teach a seminar on political myth and memory, and the subject became unexpectedly personal this summer when I spent a week helping my father sort my grandmother's papers. Among them was a scrapbook of commemorative pamphlets from a small town that has built its civic identity around a defeat—not a famous one, just a local skirmish that the town has spent a century turning into a story about who it is. Since then I have been preoccupied with the grammar of lost causes: how a community takes a loss and makes it bearable, usable, and even authoritative. The familiar cases—the Confederate Lost Cause, Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk—are in the background, but I am less interested in any particular historiography than in the structure of the thing.

I want to read John Ford's 1962 film The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance through that structure. I have seen it many times, but I have never been sure what it is saying about the founding myth it dramatizes. The film gives us a town that needs law and statehood, a violent man who is defeated, a reluctant hero in Ransom Stoddard who becomes the legend, and a man in Tom Doniphon who actually fires the fatal shot and then disappears from the official story. Stoddard's career is built on a deed he did not do; the newspaper editor decides that the legend is more useful than the truth; and Hallie, who loved Doniphon, returns with Stoddard to the town where the lie began. What I want from you is a philosophical reading of the film as a study in lost causes and national myths—not a summary, not a list of themes, but an argument about what the film shows, complicates, and gets wrong about how a polity converts a defeat into a usable past.

To keep the reading from collapsing into generalities, I have been working with a rough distinction, and I would like you to use it, revise it, or argue that the film requires a third category. Call the first a redemptive defeat: the loss is narratively converted into a sacrifice that purchases a future, so that the dead or defeated are said to have made the present possible. Call the second a custodial defeat: the loss is kept open as a trust, a cause held in escrow, and the community's task is fidelity to what was lost rather than fulfillment of it. Which of these, if either, fits The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance? Is Doniphon a lost cause, or the sacrifice that a redemptive myth requires? Does the film treat the founding lie as a necessary condition of law and statehood, or as a corruption that hollows out the institutions it creates? Which scenes carry the argument—the funeral frame, the nested flashback in which Doniphon confesses to the shot, the burning of his unfinished house, Hallie's cactus rose, the editor's destruction of the reporter's notes, the final exchange on the train—and what does each show about the relation between myth, loss, and political inheritance? I would also like you to say where the film resists the reading, and where it is more ambivalent than either a straightforward endorsement of founding myths or a debunking of them. End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about lost causes that an argument alone might miss. Please write a continuous critical essay rather than a bulleted inventory, and do not bother with citations, bibliographies, or a reading list; I know the film and the surrounding history, and I am after your reading.
