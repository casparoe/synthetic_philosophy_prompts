I convene a fortnightly discussion group for first-year doctoral students in cognitive science — linguists, psychologists, two computer scientists, one philosopher — and next week I have ninety minutes for the topic I have been calling conceptual slippage. The idea, which comes out of the letter-string analogy program known as Copycat and the tradition of work on fluid concepts around it, is that solving an analogy is not a matter of finding the rule that fits the source case and applying it, but of re-describing the source first: a concept has to be nudged toward a neighbouring concept before any rule can apply at all, and different re-descriptions license different rules. The room arrives cold — nobody has read anything on the topic, though two or three have met letter-string puzzles in popular accounts — and I would like you to plan the session rather than explain the topic to me.

The material is four letter-string analogies, which I will put up one at a time in the form of a source pair and the first half of a target pair, with the group supplying the completion:

(i) abc → abd; ijk → ?
(ii) abc → abd; mno → ?
(iii) ab → ba; abcd → ?
(iv) abc → abd; cba → ?

I am inclined to carry the whole session on (i), since it is the one that every popular treatment of the subject reaches for, but I suspect that is precisely the reason not to use it, and I am drawn to (ii) instead, because mno gives the target's own structure more to do than ijk does. My own readings, for what they are worth: in (i) the two live answers are ijd and ijl, and I take ijl to be the better of them, though I am not confident I could defend that in front of this particular room; in (ii) the same contest reappears as mnod against mnop, and mnop is much the harder to resist; in (iii) the source pair is too small to fix any reading at all — bacd and dcba both fit it, and they come from quite different descriptions of what happened — which is the point I most want to make and the one I am least sure how to stage; and in (iv) the letter that changes in the source is both the last letter of the string and the largest letter of the alphabet, whereas in the target those two descriptions come apart, so the answer is cbb on one reading and dba on the other. Please do not solve these for me; I know the rival readings. What I do not know is how to get a room from cold to the point where the rival readings look like the interesting thing rather than a defect in the puzzle.

So what I want is the plan for the ninety minutes, with rough timings: the order in which the ideas have to arrive and what forces each one's place — in particular, what the group must already have accepted before the notion of a re-description can even be stated to them, and whether they need to see a case in which a fixed rule does the whole job before they can feel what is missing from that picture; which single case should carry the session and what it must have, and must not have, in order to do so, including what you make of my two candidates; the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will actually surface, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off — I am already braced for the source pair determines the answer and for its twin, that if the reading is not determined then anything goes, and I would like the rest of the list, including whatever the two computer scientists will say about a large language model learning all of this from data; the one question I should put to the group at the end that would show the idea had landed rather than merely entertained them — by the end I want them able to say why a rule that fits the source pair perfectly can still be the wrong rule; and what I should refuse to cover at this level and say so, in particular whether to say anything about the machinery of the program that gave the idea, and if so how much.
