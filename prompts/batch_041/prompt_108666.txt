I'm presenting at a weirdly mixed seminar in two weeks — its the astrobiology institute's monthly thing but the micro department comes too, so the room is geochemists who build flight instruments, exoplanet modelers, a couple of virologists (one on phages, one on giant viruses), a synthetic biologist trying to build a minimal cell, and exactly one philosopher of biology who thinks we just need a better theory of life and the definitional squabbling will resolve itself once we have one.

My thesis is that "alive" isn't a property out there in the world waiting to be carved up correctly — it's a stance we take. Roughly: X is alive for us just in case treating X as an agent with a stake in its own persistence, one that can be wrong about its own interests, buys us more predictive and practical grip than treating it as mere mechanism. So viruses come out alive-ish, prions not, a minimal cell depends on whether the thing actually starts pushing back, and the biosphere... probably not, or maybe yes on a scale nobody wants to talk about. I call it the agency-stance account, and I've got a paper-length version but the talk is 30 minutes.
