Six months ago I moved from Copenhagen to Doha for a two-year posting, and the adjustment has been harder than I expected. Rules I never noticed at home turn out to have counterparts here that are written down, enforced, and occasionally punished, and I have been complaining about it more than I would like. The people I complain to make a point I cannot dismiss: that a culture's rules are not a taste or a whim but something like an immune system. A society that has been threatened -- by war, by scarcity, by disease, by outsiders -- thickens its norms the way a body mounts a response, and the enforcement I find stifling is the mechanism doing the protecting. On that picture, criticizing a tight culture from the outside is like telling a body to stop fighting an infection, and the loose norms I grew up with are a luxury of a society that has not lately had to defend itself.

I would like an audit of the comparison, not a verdict on the politics. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the pathogen, the immune response, the antibody, the memory of past infection, the fever, the autoimmune disorder. Then tell me where the mapping breaks, and which breaks matter for the work the comparison is being asked to do -- I suspect some (norms are learned, contested, and revised; immune systems are not) matter and others (both are 'systems') do not, but I want your judgment rather than mine. Then set out what the analogy quietly imports from the biological side that the cultural side lacks: that the response is proportionate and self-limiting, that the threats are exogenous, that the cells have no interests of their own and no standing to object, that 'healthy' is a standard nobody has to argue for.

Finally, tell me whether the conclusion survives: does 'adaptive' get anywhere near 'legitimate', and does the analogy earn that step or assume it? One further test, in the opposite direction: the loose norms I grew up with are themselves enforced -- people who try to impose a rule are punished for it -- so looseness looks less like the absence of an immune response than like a different one. Does that break the comparison in a way that matters, or merely extend it? And if there is a better comparison available -- a constitution, a traffic code, an insurance policy -- say what it is and what it would change.
