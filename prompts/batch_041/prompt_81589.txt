I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so please correct my terminology where I misuse it, but don't answer the vocabulary question in place of the substance. Over the past year I've talked myself into a position about rational choice, and what I want from you is a map of its exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false, not a summary of what its critics are said to believe.

The position, in my own words:

1. Rationality is a property of decision procedures — programs, policies, algorithms — and only derivatively a property of acts. An act is rational when it is the output of a procedure that is rational across the class of situations you could be in.
2. So when your act is correlated with something that also fixes your payoff — a prediction of you, a copy of your program, a simulation of your source code — the question to ask is which procedure to run, not which act to perform. The answer can be to perform the act that is worse in the world you are actually in.
3. I take this to yield the one-box answer in the standard predictor problem, and I take it to handle the same underlying phenomenon in the twin-prisoners case, in the hitchhiker case where a promise has to be kept to be honoured, and in the case where you would profit from having a disposition that someone has already checked you lack. I have seen this cluster discussed online under labels I can't reliably reproduce, so please don't lean on the labels or tell me what they're called; I want the substance, including whether my formulation is even the same thing as what those discussions are about.

Two cases I built myself, which are what I actually want stress-tested:

A. A step-budgeted predictor. The predictor runs my decision procedure for at most N steps; if the procedure hasn't terminated by then, the predictor falls back on predicting what a naive procedure would do. My procedure is the same program the predictor is running. I claim rule 2 still tells me what to do: run the procedure that terminates inside the budget and emits the act I want predicted. But my procedure's control flow can depend on the contents of the boxes — for instance it might search deeper the more money it expects to find — and the contents depend on the prediction, which depends on my procedure. So whether I terminate within the budget, what I emit, and what is in the boxes all seem to refer to each other. I can't tell whether that is a genuine circularity in the position or just a hard case.

B. A diagonalizing environment. Take my source code, compute what my procedure outputs, and then set the payoffs so that that output is the losing one. For any deterministic procedure it looks like there is an environment of this kind that beats it, so "the best procedure over all situations" may be undefined unless I quantify over a distribution of situations — which seems to smuggle in exactly the priors I was trying to do without. I want to know whether this is a decisive objection, a known technical limitation with a standard repair, or a mistake on my part (for instance, whether the self-reference makes the payoff function ill-defined or fixed-pointless rather than showing anything about rationality).
