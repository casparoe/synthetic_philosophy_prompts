My best friend died two weeks ago, thirty-four, brain aneurysm, no warning, nothing anyone did wrong — he leaves a wife and a two-year-old. I'm giving the eulogy. Problem is I've spent the last decade as a genuine Leibnizian: I really do hold that God surveyed infinitely many possible worlds and picked the best one, that a world's perfection is a matter of maximum variety plus maximum order rather than each part being good, and that every substance expresses the whole universe, so you can't subtract one death without ending up with a different world entirely. Half that room is our old philosophy department — they'll clock it instantly if I water the view down into generic comfort, and they know all the objections (whether "better" across infinite worlds is even well-defined, whether the best world wouldn't have to be created by necessity, Voltaire). The other half is his family, who arent religious and will hear any version of "it's all for the best" as an insult to a dead man.

So write the eulogy, and make a definite call about how far the Leibnizian claim can actually be pushed at a memorial — whether the honest move is to stand behind the strong claim including this death, or whether the occasion forces a Leibnizian to retreat to something weaker — then write the speech that your call commits you to, with your reasons for the call stated plainly rather than a menu of options for me to choose from. If you go strong, don't hide behind "we can't see the whole"; give the family-facing case in words that don't require them to believe in God. If you go weak, say exactly which piece of the position the occasion makes you give up, and flag anywhere the speech leans on a contested reading of him.
