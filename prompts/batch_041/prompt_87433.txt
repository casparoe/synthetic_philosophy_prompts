I'm in a small reading group that's been working through Buddhist ethics and we've ground to a halt on karma. One bloke in the group keeps saying karma is just cosmic desert — you get exactly what you deserve, full stop, so pitying someone who suffers is a category error — and the rest of us keep saying no, its supposed to be a law of nature, like fire burning whoever touches it, with no judge and no ledger and nobody keeping score. My suspicion, which nobody will take seriously, is that the natural-law reading is actually the harsher of the two, and that the desert reading is the one that can end up licensing us to step in and help. I need something concrete to argue with, so I want you to build me a thought experiment to order rather than another abstract distinction.

The case has to be engineered so the two readings come apart on a single practical yes/no question, and I want the divergence to run in the direction I just described. So: one ordinary scenario, a family or a village or a clinic, present day or near enough, with no supernatural machinery beyond the bare postulate of karmic causation across lives — no demons, no divine bookkeeping, no visions, no dying-and-remembering. There's a person suffering something genuinely bad, and it's stipulated flatly that the suffering is the fruit of something they did in a previous life; keep that past deed middling, not a murder and not a parking ticket, don't spend words on it, and don't let the case turn on how awful or trivial it was. The question on the table is whether a third party may intervene to relieve the suffering, and the case has to be built so that the desert reading permits it while the natural-law reading forbids it — structurally, by what the two readings are committed to, not by stipulating the verdicts. Two cheap moves I want ruled out: don't make it turn on the sufferer not remembering their past lives, and don't make it turn on karma being beginningless. Everyone reaches for those first and they don't actually separate the readings, since memory is irrelevant to desert and the regress cuts both ways. So the work has to be done by something else, and I want you to tell me what that something is. If it turns out no case can do this, say so and tell me which constraint is doing the blocking — I'd rather have that answer than a fudge.

Then walk it through: state the case, give the verdict each reading delivers and the reasoning that gets it there, and name the feature that's doing the work. Then the stress tests — delete that feature, or flip it, and tell me whether the case collapses back into the boring version of the complaint that karma licenses indifference. Then put on the hat of someone defending each reading and give me their best line of attack, especially any argument that the reading you've made look harsh can be reformulated so it comes out permissive after all. If one of the two readings can only reach its verdict by quietly importing something from the other, say so — that'd be the interesting result and I'd rather you flag it than paper over it. No citations please, I'm not writing an essay and half the group wouldn't recognise a footnote anyway.
