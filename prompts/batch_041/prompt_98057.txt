I am presenting a paper at a departmental workshop in which I argue that the rule-following paradox is genuine but mislocated: what it shows is not that there are no facts about what someone means, but that the correctness of a rule-application cannot be grounded in any fact about the speaker's past behaviour, dispositions or inner episodes, nor in an abstract rule standing behind them. My positive proposal is that a speaker's present capacity to take one continuation rather than another as correct is itself normative, and is not made normative by anything further — it is the bedrock against which all other normative facts about meaning are measured. The audience will be a mixed philosophy department: two Wittgenstein/Kripke specialists who hold that correctness is fixed by communal agreement and will press me on the individualist case; a dispositionalist who believes an idealised-disposition account survives the paradox and will want to know exactly where mine differs; a semantic naturalist who wants a reductive story and will treat my bedrock as obscurantism dressed as modesty; and a linguist who suspects the whole debate is a confusion about the word 'rule'. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from these four, phrased in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each would do, each with a reply deliverable in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft, plus what I should concede up front and the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Ground the replies in a concrete case rather than the usual series-continuation example: take a novice who, shown a single instance of a predicate being applied, goes on to apply it in a way we would call mistaken, and say what my account lets me say about them.
