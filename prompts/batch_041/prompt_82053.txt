The St. Petersburg paradox is normally presented as a consequence of an infinite expected payoff. I want that diagnosis tested rather than restated: construct a decision problem in which the tension between what the expected payoff recommends and what a sensible player would pay survives even though the expected payoff is finite, and derive everything from first principles.

The scenario is fixed as follows. A house offers a single gamble to a player of fixed, modest wealth. The payoff is 2^n with probability 2^(-n), for n = 1, 2, 3, ..., except that the house's reserves are finite: no outcome may exceed 2^N, for a fixed positive integer N, so payoffs above the cap are truncated to it. The player may buy the gamble once, at a posted price p. The cap is the only departure from the standard game.

Meet the following specifications.

1. Derive, in closed form and as functions of N, the expected monetary payoff, the median payoff, and the probability that the payoff is at least 2^k. State which of the three depend on N and which do not, and explain why the expectation and the median part company.

2. Show that the expectation diverges as N grows without bound, and that this is not what makes the game puzzling. To do so, exhibit an increasing, concave utility function, defined on all positive wealth, under which the player's certainty equivalent for the gamble — the sure sum they would accept in place of it — converges to a finite limit as N grows without bound.

3. Exhibit a second increasing, concave utility function that agrees with the first except in one respect, which you should name, and under which the certainty equivalent grows without bound as N increases. Then state the general condition on the utility function that decides which behaviour occurs, and show that your two functions fall on opposite sides of it.

4. Give the verdict on whether to buy at price p, with the reasoning, for each of: expected monetary value maximization; expected utility maximization under the utility function from step 2; and a rule that compares the gamble to the sure thing by median outcome. Say which verdicts are sensitive to N and which are not.

5. Identify the single feature of the case that does the work — the feature whose alteration makes the interesting behaviour disappear — and demonstrate this by changing exactly that feature and reporting what happens.

6. Close with the adversarial note: the strongest objection a defender of the expected-value verdict could raise against the construction, how much of the case survives it, and what you take the case to establish and not to establish.

Constraints: assume the player's wealth is finite and modest throughout; let nothing in the problem be infinite; and derive the results yourself rather than reporting what anyone has written about this paradox. I want the reasoning, not a survey, and no citations.
