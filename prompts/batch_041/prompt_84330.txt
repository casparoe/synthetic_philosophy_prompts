I've been asked to write up my ethical outlook in a few paragraphs for a group I've just joined, and I'd rather find out now where it comes apart than in front of them. It has also stopped being abstract for me. Last month I had to make a hiring call in which I leaned hard on my read of a candidate's character, and in the same month a colleague I would have vouched for in any court did something I still cannot square with the person I thought I knew. My first reaction, and I noticed it at the time, was that the situation had got to him rather than that I had been wrong about him for six years.

Here is roughly what I'd write down. The frame that makes most sense of moral life is virtue ethics: the question to ask about an action is what a person of good character would do, and what matters morally is the agent's character rather than the act's consequences or its conformity to a rule. Character traits are real and robust — calling someone honest is a genuine prediction about how they will behave across a range of situations, not a summary of what they have happened to do so far. The situationist research is convincing: small and morally irrelevant features of a situation, such as being in a hurry, ambient noise, or an earlier stroke of luck, shift morally significant behavior a great deal, and the correlation between what a person does in one situation and what they do in another is far weaker than trait talk suggests. When I hire, I weight character above skill, on the grounds that skills can be taught and character cannot. People do change, though, and moral improvement over a life is real and not rare. My own gloss on my colleague is that it was not the real him, that the situation got to him. And something I have been turning over lately: the right unit of moral assessment may not be the person at all but the life the person has arranged — a person of good character is largely someone who has built themselves an environment in which the bad situations do not arise.

What I want is an audit, not an essay. Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad. For each one, say what would have to give and what that would cost me. Be explicit about which pairs only look like they conflict and why they do not. Then tell me the single revision that would clean up the most, and what I would be giving up to make it. Please write it as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables — the way you would write me a long letter. I am not after a reading list or a survey of who has said what; I want the audit.
