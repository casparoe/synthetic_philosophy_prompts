Exam question: Explain the disagreement between counterpart theory and transworld identity, and evaluate the Humphrey objection to counterpart theory. Your answer should (a) state the core disagreement between the two views, (b) explain the main motivation for counterpart theory, (c) state the Humphrey objection as clearly as you can, and (d) assess whether Lewis's reply to the objection succeeds. Be specific about the technical notions involved.

Student's answer:

The disagreement between counterpart theory and transworld identity is about how to interpret de re modal claims — claims like "Humphrey might have won the election" or "this table could have been made of ice." On the transworld identity view, associated with Kripke, the very same individual exists in more than one possible world. When we say that Humphrey might have won, we are saying that there is a possible world in which Humphrey — the identical person — wins. On counterpart theory, associated with David Lewis, no individual exists in more than one world. Instead, when we say that Humphrey might have won, we are saying that there is a possible world containing a counterpart of Humphrey, an individual distinct from Humphrey but resembling him in the relevant respects, who wins.

Lewis's main motivation for counterpart theory is the problem of accidental intrinsics. Suppose Humphrey exists in two worlds and wins in one but loses in the other. Since winning and losing are intrinsic properties, Humphrey would have to be both a winner and a loser. But nothing can have both a property and its complement. So the same individual cannot exist in two worlds. Counterpart theory avoids this by making the winner in the other world a different individual.

Kripke's Humphrey objection is that counterpart theory gives the wrong subject matter for modal claims. When I say that Humphrey might have won, I am talking about Humphrey, not about some other person who resembles him. On counterpart theory, the truth of "Humphrey might have won" does not require anything about Humphrey at all; it requires only that there be a winning counterpart. So Humphrey's possible victory turns out not to be about Humphrey, which seems absurd — it would be odd for Humphrey to care about whether a mere counterpart of his won.

Lewis's reply is that the objection misunderstands the role of the counterpart relation. The counterpart relation is what makes de re modal predication possible in the first place. When we utter "Humphrey might have won," the name "Humphrey" serves to select a counterpart relation, and the modal claim is then about the world and that relation. So the claim is not that some unrelated person won; the counterpart is relevantly connected to Humphrey. Lewis also notes that counterpart relations are context-dependent, so different respects of similarity can be salient in different conversational contexts.

A further point of disagreement is over how possible worlds are to be understood. Kripke insists that possible worlds are stipulated, not discovered: we do not look around for a world containing Humphrey; we stipulate the world and thereby stipulate that it contains Humphrey. Lewis agrees that we stipulate worlds, but he thinks that what we stipulate is the identity of the world, not which individuals it contains; the individuals are fixed by the counterpart relation.
