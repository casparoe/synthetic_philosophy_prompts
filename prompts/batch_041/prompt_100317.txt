I have recently been appointed to my university's dining-services advisory committee, and our first substantive decision is due at the end of the month, which is why I am writing. The committee must recommend whether the dining halls should replace their beef entrée with chicken — the change is being presented to us as both the more sustainable and the more humane option — or instead commit to a fully plant-based default. I joined expecting an easy vote, but the more I have read about how food is actually produced, the less confident I am that the committee is even asking the question correctly.

The difficulty is this. The case for chicken over beef is almost always made in terms of land, water, and greenhouse gas per calorie, and on that measure chicken wins comfortably. But our committee adopted, at my predecessor's urging, a purchasing principle that we should minimize the total number of animals our food causes to suffer and die, counting every animal the supply chain affects and not only the ones we eat. Judged by that principle the comparison inverts in ways I cannot untangle on my own: a calorie of chicken comes from raising and slaughtering a great many small birds, a calorie of beef from a single large animal, and both ultimately rest on cropland whose cultivation and harvest kill enormous numbers of field animals that appear in no one's accounting. The plant-based default, which the committee treats as the obviously kind option, is grown on that same cropland and displaces or kills animals of the same unconsidered kinds.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. I am not asking for a literature review or a reading list; I want a document the committee can act on and defend. It should recommend a concrete course of action for the coming academic year — what the default entrée should be, and whether we should decide now or first gather specified information — with its reasoning set out plainly. It should engage the strongest case against whatever it recommends rather than dismissing it, and it should state the empirical assumptions and uncertainties the recommendation depends on, so the committee can see exactly what it is betting on. If our principle cannot yet yield an answer, say so, and say what would have to be true for it to.
