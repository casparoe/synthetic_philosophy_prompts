I'm doing a piece on guilt vs shame and I wanted to get my own reactions straight before I talk to anyone who studies it. So here's whats actually true of me, not what I think should be true. Lying to my editor about a deadline: guilt, no shame, and the guilt seems right. Being caught at a dinner party not knowing who some famous novelist is: shame, no guilt, and I'd say the shame is misplaced. Missing my dads birthday because I was filing: guilt, the bad kind. My father having done time in prison: shame, no guilt, and I'd defend that shame as tracking something real even though I did nothing at all. Being bad at small talk: shame again, and that one's just noise. Plagiarizing a paragraph in college: both, and they pulled opposite ways -- the guilt made me want to fix it, the shame made me want to never mention it to anyone. A colleague got laid off and I didn't: I feel guilt, which I know is irrational, but I don't think its worthless either. Voting for a candidate who turned out to be a disaster: something, but I honestly can't tell if it's guilt or shame, and I notice I call it shame in public and guilt to myself.

My half-baked theory is that guilt is about an act you could of done otherwise, shame is about a self you can't change, and the tell is that guilt pushes you to repair while shame pushes you to hide. The plagiarism case breaks the pairing, though -- I wanted to repair and hide -- and the dad case has no act of mine in it anywhere. Is there a principle that gets these verdicts, or close, and what does it say about the case I keep chewing on, feeling shame for something nobody knows about? Which of my verdicts would you make me give up? And since this is for a piece, tell me which parts here -- the act/self line, the repair/hide line, the whole idea that one of these two is the morally better emotion, and whether a culture can even choose which one to run on -- are settled among people who actually work on this, and which are still genuinely contested. Don't sell me a contested view as the consensus.
