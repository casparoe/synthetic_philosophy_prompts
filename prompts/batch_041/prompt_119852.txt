For the past year I have been on the editorial board of my high school's paper, and I am the one who reads the op-ed submissions before our weekly vote; anything we accept goes to our faculty adviser for a final check, and she has twice asked me to explain why a column that predicts the future from a pattern in the past should not run as written. I have read Popper's The Poverty of Historicism and I am convinced by its central claim that there cannot be laws of historical development, but I cannot reproduce the argument quickly enough to use it in a twenty-minute editorial meeting, and I cannot explain to a contributor why the fact that every empire in history has fallen does not license a prediction about the next one.

Please turn Popper's case against historicism into a procedure I can actually run, in order, on a submitted column, short enough to keep beside me while I read. For each step I need the question to ask, what a satisfactory answer looks like, the point at which a bad answer stops the column from running as a prediction, and the point at which the real issue is values or editorial judgment and has to go to the adviser rather than being settled by the procedure. I want the steps to use the distinctions that carry the weight: a trend as opposed to a law, and why a trend can always be broken; an unconditional prophecy as opposed to a conditional prediction, and the demand that the conditions be named; the way publishing a prediction can itself alter the predicted event (the Oedipus effect); the argument that a future growth of knowledge cannot be predicted in advance, so a course of history that depends on it cannot be either; and the habit of explaining large outcomes as the intended results of powerful people's plans rather than as unintended consequences. Tell me which of these tests a sixteen-year-old with no training in history or economics can apply honestly, which ones require me to admit that I do not know, and where the procedure will misfire — on a column that is badly argued but not historicist, or on a prediction that is properly conditional and yet still reads as a slogan.

Then run the procedure on three columns: one arguing that a great power's decline is inevitable because all empires fall; one arguing that the spread of some technology cannot be stopped once it exists; and one whose conclusion I am drawn to myself, that industrial civilization must eventually collapse because its resource base is finite, so that I can see whether the procedure treats my own side as strictly as the others. Say plainly where each column fails, and if one of them survives, say that too.
