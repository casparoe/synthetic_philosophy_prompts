I'm writing a long-ish feature on Kepler for the mag and I want a second pair of eyes on the argument before I file, so I'm pasting the thesis below in roughly the shape it'll take. I don't need line edits or anything about my prose (I know the first graf is a mess). What I want is somebody to tell me where the thinking is wrong or thin.

Here's the gist. Everyone does the same thing with Kepler and astrology: they treat it as an embarrassing side-hustle. The court mathematician had to crank out horoscopes to pay the bills, and the real Kepler is the guy who found the ellipses. I think that's backwards, and the astrology and the astronomy are actually teh same project. Both are attempts to read a geometrical order into the heavens that the human soul is built to respond to.

Point one: in his defense of astrology — a short Latin thing from around 1601, I keep getting the title slightly wrong in every draft — he doesn't defend the fortune-telling kind. He guts it. He throws out the houses, the signs acting as causal agents, the fatalism, and keeps basically one thing: the aspects, meaning the angular relations between planets. And he keeps them for a reason, which is that they're quantitative and geometrical, so they can be hooked up to his physics of light. His idea, as I understand it, is that the planets act on the sublunary world the way the sun acts — through light and some kind of ray or virtue — and that the soul, or whatever formative faculty is in things, resonates with certain ratios. That's the same Pythagoreanism that later drives the harmony book. So the astrology isn't a wart on the astronomy; it's the theology of geometry that makes the astronomy feel necessary to him in the first place. And I seem to remember him somewhere calling astrology the foolish daughter of astronomy whose mother would starve without her, which fits my reading — he can't quite let go of it even while mocking it.

Point two, and this is the part I care about: the move where he insists the stars don't force us, they merely incline, and the wise soul resists. I used to read that as a face-saving dodge. Now I think it's the load-bearing wall — it's what lets him keep a genuine causal celestial influence while protecting free will and providence. But it's also the move that makes the whole thing unfalsifiable, because if the influence is always defeasible by the soul's freedom, then no observation could ever count against it. So his reformed astrology is, in effect, insulated by design.

Point three, the twist I want to end on: I think this is a clean case study in a research program being motored by commitments that are, by our lights, completely wrong, and still spitting out the right answer. He had to give up the circle — the most beautiful shape — to get the ellipse, and his whole aesthetics of harmony was pulling the other way. So the question I want to leave the reader with: was the Pythagoreanism a ladder he climbed and then threw away, or is it still doing real work in the harmony he thinks he's found in the planetary ratios? I'm worried that's more of a nice ending than a real question.

So, comments please. Am I overclaiming anywhere? I have a feeling I'm flattening a live disagreement among historians — that there's a fight about how sincere his astrology actually was, versus it being professional obligation plus genuine belief in the reformed version only. If I'm walking into that fight without noticing, tell me. Is the incline-not-compel-slash-unfalsifiable point too cheap, like something a first-year could say? And does point three survive scrutiny or is it just rhetoric? Then give me the single change that would most improve the piece, and flag anything that's flatly wrong. Don't worry about citations, I'll handle those — I just need to know if the load-bearing claims hold.
