Im reading Capital vol 1, ch. 15 (the machinery chapter) for a seminar on automation and I keep getting stuck on the opening. Heres the bit I mean:

John Stuart Mill says in his “Principles of Political Economy”: “It is questionable if all the mechanical inventions yet made have lightened the day’s toil of any human being.” That is, however, by no means the aim of the capitalistic application of machinery. Like every other increase in the productiveness of labour, machinery is intended to cheapen commodities, and, by shortening that portion of the working-day, in which the labourer works for himself, to lengthen the other portion that he gives, without an equivalent, to the capitalist. In short, it is a means for producing surplus-value.

Then a page or two later he goes after the definition itself:

Mathematicians and mechanicians, and in this they are followed by a few English economists, call a tool a simple machine, and a machine a complex tool. They see no essential difference between them, and even give the name of machine to the simple mechanical powers, the lever, the inclined plane, the screw, the wedge, &c. As a matter of fact, every machine is a combination of those simple powers, no matter how they may be disguised. From the economic standpoint this explanation is worth nothing, because the historical element is wanting. Another explanation of the difference between tool and machine is that in the case of a tool, man is the motive power, while the motive power of a machine is something different from man, as, for instance, an animal, water, wind, and so on. [...] On a closer examination of the working machine proper, we find in it, as a general rule, though often, no doubt, under very altered forms, the apparatus and tools used by the handicraftsman or manufacturing workman; with this difference, that instead of being human implements, they are the implements of a mechanism, or mechanical implements.

Two things confusse me. First the Mill reply: is Marx saying Mill is simply wrong, or is he granting that machines-as-such haven't lightened toil but saying that was never the aim, so its a stupid complaint? Because “the capitalistic application of machinery” sounds like it leaves room for machinery on its own to lighten toil — but then the very next sentences say machinery “is intended to cheapen commodities” and “is a means for producing surplus-value,” which sounds like that's machinery's nature and not just what capitalists do with it. Which is it?

Second, the definition. He bins the mathematicians' answer (tool = simple machine, machine = complex tool) because “the historical element is wanting,” and he also bins the obvious one (a man is the motive power of a tool, something else is the motive power of a machine). But the breakdown he gives instead — motor mechanism, transmitting mechanism, working machine — looks just as physical as the answers he rejected. The one line that reads like his actual criterion is the bit where the old tools turn up in the machine “not as human implements” but as “implements of a mechanism.” So is the real difference that the tool is no longer held by the worker? If so, thats already a social/historical fact about who commands the labour process, not a fact about the hardware — and then it seems odd to talk about “machinery” as one thing and “the capitalistic application of machinery” as another. On Marxs view is there any seam at all between the machine and its capitalist use, or is the machine already capital all the way down? And does that answer change how I should read the Mill line?

Would love a close reading of just these paragraphs, not a general survey of Marx on technology.
