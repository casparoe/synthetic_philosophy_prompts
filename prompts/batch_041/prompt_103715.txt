Consider how the law handles a written advance directive. A competent adult signs a document specifying that if she ever reaches an advanced stage of dementia, she is to receive no artificial nutrition or hydration and no antibiotics for a life-threatening infection; she wants to be allowed to die of whatever illness comes. Years later she has advanced dementia. She does not appear distressed: she eats when fed, smiles, recognizes her daughter, and, as far as anyone can determine, is neither suffering nor wishing to die. The directive is honored; she is not fed, and she dies. Almost no one regards this as a violation of her rights, and many regard it as an exercise of them. Yet the same legal system will not enforce a contract in which a competent adult sells herself into slavery, will not let a person consent in advance to being killed, and generally treats an adult's present, expressed wishes as decisive against her own earlier instructions about her body. So the practice privileges the earlier self over the later one in the specific case of refusing medical treatment, while refusing to do so nearly everywhere else.

What conception of the person, and of autonomy, does this practice presuppose? Does honoring the directive require the thesis that the demented woman is literally the same person as the woman who signed it — so that she is simply bound by her own past choice — or can it get by on something weaker, such as the idea that a person has authority over the shape of her life taken as a whole, or that we should respect the values she endorsed while competent even after she no longer holds them? Distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with. Then say where the practice is underdetermined: which rival accounts of personal identity, autonomy, and welfare would license exactly the same conduct at the bedside?

Next, test the implicit theory against a variation. Suppose a jurisdiction extends advance directives beyond medical treatment, so that a person may pre-commit, while competent, to being physically restrained during future psychotic episodes, to having all contact with a particular relative refused, or to handing permanent control of her finances to a designated third party even after she recovers. Would whatever justifies the medical directive also justify these? If not, what does the difference reveal about what the medical practice was really presupposing — a general authority of the earlier self over the later one, or something narrower about the boundaries of the body and the definition of treatment?

Finally, reach a verdict. State which account of the authority of the earlier self you think best reconstructs the existing practice, defend it against at least one serious rival, and say what would have to change in medicine and in the surrounding law if that account were rejected in favor of one that gave the present self's interests or present wishes priority.
