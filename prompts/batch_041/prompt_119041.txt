I'm a third-year PhD student in psychology, mostly doing computational modeling, and I've ended up with a view I'd like you to try to break, because I keep defending it at lab happy hour and I'm starting to worry I'm just repeating a slogan. The view: the replication crisis is at bottom an inference problem. We handed p-values a job they can't do -- deciding what to believe, not just whether a pattern is distinguishable from noise -- and the fix is to replace significance testing with Bayesian model comparison, where your theoretical commitments have to be written down as a prior, you report the posterior or a Bayes factor, and prior belief becomes an explicit input instead of something smuggled in through researcher degrees of freedom. I think a field that genuinely made that switch would see its replication rate improve substantially, and that whatever failures remained would be failures of measurement and construct validity rather than of inference.

What would count against this? I want the full map, ranked from what should actually move me to what's merely awkward: observations, arguments, formal results, and developments that have arguably already happened. I'm especially interested in whether Bayesian inference really blocks the garden of forking paths or whether the forking just relocates into model and prior space (a friend keeps muttering about Bayesian multiverse analysis at me and I don't have a good reply); what prior sensitivity does to the view -- a postdoc in my lab reran her meta-analysis with a different but perfectly defensible prior and her Bayes factor went from around 40 to under 1, and she just reported the first one, which feels like p-hacking with extra steps; and whether the crisis might really be about incentives, careers, and plain error rather than inference, in which case a field could go fully Bayesian and replicate no better. Also, does my view actually predict anything its rivals don't -- say, that Bayesian-heavy subfields should have better replication rates -- and what should I do if that comparison comes out flat? And be honest about whether I've insulated the view: the Bayesians still fail because they're not doing it right sounds like a dodge to me, but maybe it isn't one.

I'd be most moved by a case where a Bayesian workflow, done in good faith by people who understood it, produced a claim that didn't replicate, or by a formal arguement that the forking-paths problem is invariant to which paradigm you're in. Also tell me which of these a committed Bayesian would themselves concede as counting against them versus dismiss as not their problem, and whether the inference/measurement split I'm leaning on is a real distinction or just a way of quarantining my view from bad news.
