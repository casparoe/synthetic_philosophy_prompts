I'm a second-year PhD student in early modern intellectual history, and I want to turn a persistent irritation into a two-page prospectus for what would become a dissertation chapter — possibly a standalone article later, if the chapter holds up. The irritation is the presentism debate. In my department it gets posed as a single yes/no question, can historians escape the standards of their own time, and the answers on offer are either that we cannot but should keep trying anyway, or that we can if we are disciplined enough about it. Neither has ever seemed to me to be saying anything that could turn out false. My half-formed suspicion is that the question is ill-posed because the standards of one's own time names at least four separable things that come apart in practice: (1) which past events are worth asking about at all; (2) which vocabulary and categories we use to describe what past people thought and did — their terms or ours; (3) which factors we treat as doing explanatory work; and (4) whether we pass moral or political judgment on past actors by present lights. My guess is that (1) is unavoidably present-minded and entirely harmless, that (2) is where the real methodological dispute lives, that (3) is what people are gesturing at when they warn against reading the past as a story that culminates in us, and that (4) is not a historiographical question at all but a moral one in disguise. If that is right, a good deal of the apparent stalemate comes from arguments for a thesis about one of the four being answered by considerations that properly belong to another — and in particular I suspect (2) and (4) get run together under the single word anachronism, which is why the argument keeps re-starting. I tried this out at a workshop last month and got nowhere, which is part of what convinced me the question itself is badly posed rather than merely hard.

What I want from you is the prospectus, not a survey of the debate. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer. Explain why the existing positions leave it open — that is, what each of the familiar moves actually establishes (the move that perspective is inescapable; the move that one need only be faithful to the sources) and exactly where each falls silent. Then the approach: I want to import a distinction from outside history — the contrast anthropologists and linguists draw between an observer's categories and the categories of the people being described, alongside the linguist's separation of a description that merely fits the data from one that is faithful to the system the speakers themselves command — and ask whether history has anything playing the same role, or whether the analogy breaks down and why. Say why this route rather than the obvious one of simply doing a more careful case study. Then work one extended case, the particulars of which I will supply: the long-running argument among historians of the Scientific Revolution over whether science, experiment, and laws of nature are usable categories for people who did not have them. Tell me what that case shows about each of the four distinctions and which of them it leaves untouched.

Stage the work month by month for a single semester, say what would count as the project's succeeding and what would count as its failing, and give an honest assessment of whether a chapter-length treatment can do this or whether it is really a book in disguise. Throughout, mark clearly the difference between what is settled among people who actually work on this and what is genuinely contested; I don't want a contested reading presented as the consensus. Don't bother with a bibliography or citations — I'll fill those in myself — and don't lean on the names and dates of particular works; argue from the positions.
