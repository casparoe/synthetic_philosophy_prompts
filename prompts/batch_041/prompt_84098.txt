Eighteen months ago I agreed to help run a small philanthropic fund that gives away roughly two million dollars a year, and the work has turned a philosophical puzzle I used to enjoy from a distance into something I have to live with. We justify our grants by their consequences, but the consequences we can trace are trivial beside the ones we cannot: whether a grant to a policy shop shifts a regulation in a decade, whether a fellowship produces someone who later does something enormous, whether the entire portfolio would have been better spent on something we never thought to consider. I know the standard move here is to distinguish two grades of the problem, one where we are ignorant of the causal upshot and one where even full causal information would leave the evaluation unsettled, because of infinities or because the value of the far future is dominated by outcomes of vanishing probability and enormous magnitude. I suspect that distinction is doing less work than its users assume, and I have a half-formed reason: it sorts cases by the kind of ignorance involved, when what matters is where in the chain from act to verdict the unknown quantity enters, whether in the causal model, in the value function, or in the aggregation rule. If that is right, only the last of the three is a problem for consequentialism as such; the first two would trouble any decision theory that takes the distant future seriously. I am unsure whether a fourth entry point belongs on the list, namely the option set itself being partly unknown to us, and I would like the proposal to take a position on that rather than gesture at it.

Please turn this into a two-page research proposal for a nine-month, half-time fellowship, written for a review panel of philosophers and grantmakers who know the debate and will read quickly. It should state the question so that it has a determinate answer; explain what the existing positions leave open, and why the familiar two-grade distinction does not already settle it; set out the method as a concrete sequence of stages, saying what I would actually do in the first weeks and what a finished piece of work would look like at the end; say what would count as the project failing, including the real possibility that my three entry points collapse into a redescription of the distinction I am criticizing; and give an honest account of scope, naming what I can realistically complete in nine months at half time and what I should explicitly leave aside. Argue for the approach against the obvious alternative of simply surveying what people have already said, and make the case that this is worth funding. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or a literature review organized by author; refer to positions and arguments rather than to names and titles, and I will add the references myself. Keep the argument reconstructible by a reader who knows only the broad shape of the debate, so avoid resting anything on a technical result or a piece of jargon that needs prior acquaintance.
