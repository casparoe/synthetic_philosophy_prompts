I teach a graduate seminar on confirmation and inductive inference, and one of my students, a committed Bayesian, has constructed the following argument, which he offers as a proof that Bayesian confirmation theory cannot solve the grue problem without smuggling in a substantive and contested theory of prior plausibility. I would like your help in diagnosing it before I present it to the class. (1) Bayesian confirmation says that evidence E confirms hypothesis H just in case P(H | E) > P(H), and that rational learning proceeds by conditionalization. (2) Let the language contain the predicates green and blue, together with the grue and bleen definitions relative to a future time t: an object is grue if it is green and first examined before t, or blue and first examined at or after t; an object is bleen if it is blue and first examined before t, or green and first examined at or after t. (3) Consider any prior probability function P over the hypotheses expressible in this language such that, after the evidence E that all emeralds so far examined are green (and therefore also grue), P assigns higher posterior probability to the hypothesis that all emeralds are green than to the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue. (4) Define a new prior P* by systematically swapping green with grue and blue with bleen throughout the language, so that P*(H) = P(H*) where H* is the image of H under the swap. (5) P* is a perfectly well-defined probability function over the same hypothesis space, and it is supported by exactly the same evidence as P, since the observed emeralds are both green and grue. (6) Under P*, the posterior probability of the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue exceeds that of the hypothesis that all emeralds are green, just as under P the reverse holds. (7) Nothing in the evidence and nothing in the axioms of probability distinguishes P from P*. (8) Therefore Bayesianism contains no resources for preferring the green projection to the grue projection; any such preference must come from the prior, and the prior's choice is not itself constrained by the evidence. (9) If any prior is rationally permissible, then both projections are rational, and induction is arbitrary, which proves too much, since we are plainly not equally entitled to project grue. (10) So Bayesianism must either forbid P* by an additional axiom or concede that it does not solve the problem.

I would like to keep (1) and (9) if I can, but I am not sure where the cost should fall. Which premise or step should be rejected, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to surrender, please price the alternatives against one another, including the option of accepting (10) and abandoning the claim that Bayesianism by itself solves the grue problem. I want to be able to tell my students exactly what the argument establishes and what a Bayesian must give up to resist it.
