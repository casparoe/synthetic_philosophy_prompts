I have a half-formed picture of infinite magnitude that I want to stress-test, and I would like you to work out what follows from it. The picture: talk about the "size" of a collection is not one thing. At least three criteria are in play, and they agree for finite collections while coming apart for infinite ones. (a) Pairing: two collections are equal in size when their members can be matched one-to-one, the criterion sometimes called Hume's principle and the basis of the standard transfinite arithmetic. (b) Part–whole: a collection is larger than any of its proper subcollections, the principle associated with Euclid's name. (c) Extent or density: how much of a line, a list, or a space the collection fills, in the sense in which the interval from 0 to 1 is shorter than the interval from 0 to 2 even though the two can be paired off one-to-one. On this picture, Hilbert's hotel is a theorem about (a) narrated as though it were about (c) — the guests, the rooms, the plumbing and the water supply do work in the story that the mathematics never licenses — and the old observation that the perfect squares can be paired off with all the natural numbers while also being a proper part of them is not a paradox but simply the divergence of (a) and (b). Cantor's theorem and the diagonal argument are then claims about (a) alone, which leaves open how much of what we ordinarily mean by "more" or "larger" they settle.

Assume the picture is at least roughly right, or at any rate interesting enough to take seriously. Please describe the most important implications of it for one concrete decision I face. I have to run a session for graduate students in mathematics and physics who are split, roughly evenly, between those who think actual infinities are indispensable to their subjects and those who think talk of completed infinite totalities is a philosophical fiction that has been smuggled into mathematics. Rank the implications by how much they should change what I actually say in that room, tell me which single one you take to be the most consequential and why the others are downstream of it, and tell me what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would move you to a different ranking, or to a different verdict on the picture itself, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Don't worry about citing sources or pointing me to reading; I want the argument, and I will chase the references myself.
