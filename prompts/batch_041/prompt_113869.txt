I'm putting together a handout for a small reading group on truth and paradox — mostly grad students who've done one course in logic but aren't specialists — and what I want out of you is a decision tree, not a survey chapter. Start from a commitment nobody in the room disputes: that the little argument where you take the sentence that says of itself that it isn't true, ask whether it's true, and seem to derive both that it is and that it isn't, is a valid bit of reasoning, and that any view has to say where the trouble is. But heres the angle I actually care about. I dont want the usual list of named theories with a paragraph each; I want the tree built from what each position surrenders, because as far as I can tell every solution gives up exactly one of the Liar's ingredients — the unrestricted T-schema, bivalence (or excluded middle), the classical behaviour of negation or of the conditional, one of the structural rules the derivation leans on (contraction, or substituting identicals, or moving from a schema to an instance of it), the assumption that the sentence expresses a single stable proposition, or the assumption that truth is a substantive predicate rather than just a device for generalising. So the top node should be the question of which ingredient is on the chopping block, and the leaves should be the real positions located in that space: Tarski-style restriction to a hierarchy of languages, Kripke's fixed-point gappy approach, Field's paracomplete construction, Priest's dialetheism, the revision theory of Gupta and Belnap, the contextualist line (Burge, Simmons, Glanzberg — the one where the word true quietly shifts its extension between the two halves of the argument), a Horwich-style deflationism that insists the paradox is a problem for our logic and not for the concept of truth, and the substructural people who think the culprit is contraction or transitivity of the consequence relation. Each leaf needs the name of the view plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and please mark any combination of answers that comes out unoccupied, with a guess at whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Two nodes I specifically want in there. One is the revenge / strengthened-liar test: once a view has declared the Liar not true, can it still say that thing it just said, or does it need a second truth predicate to say it, in which case hasn't the paradox just reappeared one level up — I want that as a branch node because it's what actually kills a lot of the gappy views, and I want it phrased as a question someone could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to. The other is a generalisation test: Curry's paradox, and the knower paradox (the sentence that says of itself that nobody will ever know it), and the provability and validity versions, so the tree can separate views that are really about truth from views that are really about the underlying logic or about self-reference as such — and for that branch bring in Yablo's infinite chain of sentences, which has no self-reference in it at all, and say what a view that blames self-reference is supposed to do about it. Keep it to about seven or eight branch nodes, and dont let any node be a lazy one like do you accept classical logic — every node should be a concrete case or a question about what the view can still say after it's committed itself.

One more thing: if two leaves come out the same on every question, flag them as a pair the tree can't separate rather than pretending the tree sorts them. I have a suspician the two gappy options and the two contextualist ones will collapse into each other like that unless the conditional node does some real work, and I'd rather the handout admit it than fake a distinction. Audience is people who will argue with me, so keep the prose tight, and no reading list please, we already have way too much of that. I got into this because a friend insists the whole thing is just a trick about grammar, and I'd love a tree that shows exactly which leaf that friend is standing on and what it costs them.
