Something about the death penalty has been bugging me alot and nobody I bring it up with wants to take it seriously. Everyone argues about whether it stops crime, and whether we might kill somebody who didnt do it. Fine, whatever. But here's the thing I keep coming back to: we go to enormous trouble to make sure it doesn't hurt.

There was that whole mess a few years ago when states couldn't get the drugs for the injection anymore, and there was all this drama about whether the new recipe would cause pain, and people on both sides were horrified that a guy might gasp or take forty minutes to die. The same people who wanted him dead. And I thought, hold on. We're killing him. Either way, in half an hour, he's dead. Why does it matter so much whether it hurts on the way out? Nobody says "at least it was painless" about a car crash. If the wrong thing we're doing is the killing, then a painless killing is the same wrong thing as a painful one. The pain part only makes sense if what we're really upset about is the suffering and not the death, which is a weird thing to assume when ending the guy's life is the whole point of the exercise.

And its not just the drugs. The last meal. The chaplain. Letting him say something at the end. The rule that you can't execute someone who's too mentally ill to understand what's happening to him, which I've never understood at all, because if the point is just that he's dangerous, his mental state has nothing to do with it, so somebody somewhere must think the execution is supposed to mean something to him. And the family of the victim gets to stand up and say what they lost, and sometimes what they want to happen to him, which seems to assume the punishment belongs to them and not only to the state.

So here's what I want. Take all of it, the painlessness and the last meal and the chaplain and the final words and the mental illness rule and the victim's family getting a say, and tell me what it all assumes. What does a person have to believe about a man on death row, about what makes killing wrong in the first place, and about what the state is doing when it kills somebody, for all of those rituals to hang together? Is it one picture, or two or three that don't fit? And does the practice actually need that picture, or could somebody who thinks the death penalty is only about keeping dangerous people off the street run the exact same ritual without contradicting himself?

And the part I really want: suppose you took seriously the idea that the wrong of killing is just the death itself, that a dead man is equally dead whether it took thirty seconds or thirty minutes. What would have to change? Would the last meal go? The chaplain? The rule about him understanding what's happening? Would anything survive?

Just to be clear, I don't want the usual arguments for and against. I've heard those. I want to know what all this machinery is quietly assuming.
