I am a high-school student writing a paper for our school's philosophy society, and I would like your credence in a specific claim about practical rationality, with the reasoning decomposed rather than delivered as a summary.

The claim, stated as precisely as I can manage:

(T) Necessarily, if an agent is rational, then there is a real-valued function on outcomes such that the agent's preferences among uncertain prospects can be represented as ordering them by expected value, and the agent chooses so as to maximize that expected value.

As I understand the argument for (T): from a preference relation over prospects plus a short list of conditions — completeness, transitivity, continuity, and some independence or sure-thing condition — one obtains a representation theorem, and violations of the conditions are said to expose the agent to a money pump, meaning a finite sequence of trades that the agent accepts at every step and that leaves it worse off by its own lights. I want a number, not a survey of the axioms. Please give me your credence in (T), broken into the sub-questions it rests on, each with a rough probability and a one-line justification, and then the combination.

Treat at least these as distinct terms, and add any others you think belong:

1. Whether the conditions are genuine requirements of rationality or idealizations a rational agent may fail — especially completeness: is an agent who has no preference either way between two prospects, and regards them as incommensurable rather than equally good, thereby irrational?
2. Whether exposure to a money pump demonstrates irrationality or only that the agent's preferences differ from the pump-runner's, and what extra premises the pump arguments need — that the agent must accept any trade it does not disprefer, that it cannot see the sequence coming, that it cannot commit in advance, that its preferences hold fixed across the sequence.
3. Whether an agent whose preferences are merely incomplete, not cyclic, can be pumped if indecision is itself costly — say, if she will pay a small fee to have the choice settled for her — and what that would show about the completeness condition.
4. Whether the argument establishes a representation of preferences rather than a norm of choice: what licenses the step from 'your preferences can be represented as maximizing expected value' to 'you ought to maximize expected value'?
5. Whether the independence or sure-thing condition is a requirement of rationality or an artifact of the particular theorem, given preferences that are sensitive to how a prospect is described or to how its uncertainty is resolved.
6. Whether the theorem's domain — lotteries with known probabilities, finitely many outcomes, no infinite payoffs, no correlation between the act and the state of the world — is broad enough for (T) to bear on the choices people actually face, or narrow enough to be nearly vacuous.
7. Whether 'rationality' in (T) is one thing at all, or whether the argument trades on a thin notion (consistency among preferences) while the conclusion requires a thick one (a standard governing what to do).

Then give me the number for (T) and, separately, your number for the weaker claim that a rational agent's preferences must at least be representable, and tell me which single term my number is most sensitive to and what argument or result would move it most. If (T) as stated is not a single proposition, say so and estimate the version you would actually defend.
