I've got a stack of midterms to get through and I keep putting this one back down because I'm not sure how to weigh it; I don't wanna lowball or highball the kid. The question was Q4, the long one: "Take this case. A district education officer approves a school's application for a new classroom block; on the merits the application plainly met the criteria and any competent officer would have approved it. A week later the school's head teacher visits the officer at home and leaves a gift — some money and a sack of maize — which the officer accepts. In the same district the head teacher also pays the fees of two of her late brother's children, and nobody in the village thinks twice about either payment; both are described as 'what one does'. (a) Using the working definition from week 2 — corruption as the abuse of entrusted power for private gain — is the gift to the officer corruption? (b) Some anthropologists argue that in settings like this, obligations to kin and to people who have helped you are morally mandatory rather than optional, and that the sharp public/private divide the definition assumes is not universally shared. Does that argument change your answer to (a)? (c) Is the standard definition of corruption a parochial standard being applied to a context it doesn't fit, or a universal standard that some contexts simply fail to meet? Defend your view."

And here's the answer they wrote: "On the week 2 definition — abuse of entrusted power for private gain — the gift looks borderline at first. The officer did what the rules required, the decision wasn't distorted, and the money and maize are personal rather than institutional. But I think that reading is too quick. Entrusted power includes the discretion to decide at all, and accepting a benefit after a favourable decision creates a standing obligation, so the gift can be corrupt even when this particular decision was correct. The definition is tracking a relationship, not a single transaction. (b) I'd say the anthropological argument changes what we're describing but not whether it counts as corruption. The point, as I understand it, is descriptive: the participants don't experience the exchange as corruption, because for them the binding obligations run to kin and to people in a relationship of mutual support, and the public/private line we take for granted isn't drawn in the same place. But that's exactly the issue — if the villagers don't experience it as corruption, then it isn't corruption, since corruption is at least partly a matter of how the practice is understood by the people who take part in it. Imposing our understanding is the thing the critique warns against. So my answer to (a) is no. (c) I think the definition is parochial. It was built for a state bureaucracy with a clean separation between office and person, and that separation is a historical achievement of one kind of state rather than a feature of social life as such. Where the separation isn't drawn, the definition doesn't get a grip — it's not that those contexts fail the standard, it's that the standard was never theirs. This is also why the universalist/relativist framing is a bit of a red herring: nobody is really denying that obligations can be betrayed, the fight is over who gets to name the betrayal. One more thing: the clientelism literature seems relevant, and I think it settles the case. A patron-client tie is just corruption under another name, material benefit exchanged for loyalty, and the gift establishes exactly that kind of tie. So the practice should be called corruption after all, even if the villagers wouldn't use the word."
