You are grading a midterm exam in an upper-level undergraduate course on scientific reasoning. The question and one student's answer appear below. Give the answer a grade out of 10, along with the point-by-point comments you would return to the student, keyed to parts (a) through (d). The mark should be one the comments actually support, so that the student can see both what was done well and exactly what needs fixing.

Question. Consider the alloy cavorite and a fixed future date T (1 January 2030). Say that a cavorite sample is "dax" just in case either it is assayed before T and reads high on the standard gauge, or it is assayed at or after T and reads low; say that it is "nax" just in case either it is assayed before T and reads low, or it is assayed at or after T and reads high. Every cavorite sample assayed so far (all of them before T) has read high. (a) What is puzzling about the pair of hypotheses "every cavorite sample reads high" and "every cavorite sample is dax"? (b) How does the puzzle relate to, and how does it differ from, the classical problem of justifying induction? (c) A classmate says the puzzle dissolves once we notice that "reads high" names a genuine, intrinsic property of the sample whereas "dax" is a gerrymandered, time-indexed one, so a sound account of confirmation should admit only intrinsic properties. Assess this. (d) Set out Goodman's own way of sorting the two hypotheses, and say what kind of criterion it is and what kind it is not.

Student's answer. (a) Both hypotheses fit the evidence exactly: every sample assayed so far is at once high-reading and dax, and neither has any advantage in what it says about the samples already in the ledger. The difficulty therefore cannot lie in the data; it lies in the predicates. The same body of evidence appears to support "the next sample reads high" and "the next sample is dax" equally well, yet only the first looks like a prediction we are entitled to make. The two hypotheses do come apart after T, and one of them will then be refuted, but that is no help in deciding now.

(b) This is the new riddle of induction, and it should be kept apart from Hume's problem. Hume asks what justifies any inference from observed to unobserved at all; here we grant that we will project some regularity, and ask instead which regularities the evidence confirms. The new riddle is thus about the relation between evidence and hypothesis, not about whether projection is licensed in the first place.

(c) My classmate is substantially right. "Dax" is manufactured out of "reads high" and "reads low" together with a time clause, and the dependence runs in one direction only: "dax" can be defined from the ordinary gauge predicates, but not conversely, so "reads high" is the more basic of the two, and it is the basic, intrinsic predicates that a sound account of confirmation should restrict itself to. Under that restriction the dax hypothesis never gets off the ground.

(d) Goodman's own answer is entrenchment. "Reads high" has a long record of projection behind it in our practice, whereas "dax" has none, and it is that record, not logic and not the evidence, that separates the projectible predicates from the rest. The criterion is historical and practical, and Goodman concedes that it is conservative, favouring the vocabulary we already have. What it reveals is why the entrenched predicate is the one that carves nature at its joints: our history of projection is a record of which predicates have latched onto the real divisions among things, and that is what makes the high-reading hypothesis the one the evidence genuinely supports.

Please make clear which parts of the answer are solid and which are not, and how the 10 points are distributed.
