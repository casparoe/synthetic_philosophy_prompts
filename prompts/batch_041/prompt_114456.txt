I'm giving the commencement address at a small journalism school next month. The graduates are mostly going into local news, a few into PR, one or two into policy work, and their families will be there -- so half the room will definately know Orwell's essay on language and politics pretty well, and the other half will just be there to clap and take pictures. I want a speech that actually argues something, not just alot of follow your passion stuff. Here's the argument: the standard reading of Orwell is that vague or technical language is how people dodge responsibility, but I think that's half right and half backwards. The real target isn't jargon or long words as such; it's language that lets you avoid having a thought at all, or that hides who did what. So I want the speech to draw a distinction between two kinds of obscurity: obscurity that hides the agent (mistakes were made, things happened) and obscurity that specifies the agent (the permittee shall do X by date Y). The first is the thing Orwell was really against; the second is what good journalists and good regulators both need. The speech should make the case that plain language is not the same as clear responsibility, and that sometimes technical precision is the more honest choice. It can also note that Orwell's own prose is full of metaphors and even technical terms, so he can't have meant a literal ban on them. It should land for the journalism grads without losing their parents. No citations or quotes from Orwell, obviously; just the idea.
