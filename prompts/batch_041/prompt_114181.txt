My mum is 81 and has finally gotten round to redoing her will, and I've realised I have no idea what I actually think about any of it, so I want to use someone else as a mirror before the arguing starts. The someone is my uncle Ray — retired tax lawyer, opinionated about everything, and he is going to sound off about this at Christmas whatever we do, so I'd like to know in advance what he'll say and whether he'll even be consistent about it.

What's in play: mum's estate is about $9m. A house she bought in 1986 in a city where the market went insane, a big index fund account, a 25% stake in the little office building her late partner's family owns, and shares in a private company from way back. She wants to split the whole thing outright between me and my sister, no trusts, no conditions, "so you two can do whatever you like with it." Neither of us is rich but we're both fine — I'm a physio, my sister runs a small design studio. Two things I've learned in the last month that I didn't know before: if mum sold the index funds tomorrow she'd owe an enormous amount in capital gains, but if she just holds them till she dies the basis resets and we inherit all that gain tax free, which is apparently a much bigger giveaway than anything the estate tax would have taken; and the federal exemption is high enough that she'd pay nothing federally, but our state's threshold is way lower, so there'd definately be a state bite.

Ray's track record, as best I can piece it together from arguements at family dinners:

- A neighbour died leaving about $1.2m, split outright between three kids. Ray: no problem, that's just a family getting by, nobody's buying a yacht.
- His old firm's senior partner set up a trust paying income to his kids for life, then to his grandkids for life, principal never distributed, family trustee company, all of it designed to stay out of estate tax for two generations. Ray went on about this for an hour: it's a dynasty, it ought to be illegal, this is how you get an aristocracy.
- His cousin's farm, 600 acres, maybe $6m, left to the son who's worked it since he was fourteen, with cash to the other two kids. Ray: obviously right, "the boy did the work, that land is his."
- A friend who left everything to a foundation and nothing to two perfectly comfortable children. Ray: fine, his money, his call.
- A brother in law who left $400k to a nephew he'd barely met, because the nephew had visited him every week for the last three years of his life. Ray thought that was lovely.
- Also, for what it's worth, Ray has a trust of his own for his grandkids' education, capped at $200k each, ends at 25. When my sister pointed that out he said it's not the same thing at all, which I don't think is an argument.

My instinct was that Ray is against big inheritances, full stop. But the farm seems to contradict that unless he's got some exception for cases where the heir "earned" it, and now I can't tell whether the thing he actually hates is the size, or the trusts and the dead hand and the two generations of control, or people getting money they didn't work for, or just tax avoidance schemes specifically.

So: what will Ray say about mum's plan? Which rule is he actually running, and which other rules fit his five verdicts just as well? If the rules disagree about mum's plan, which one should I bet on, and why? And then tell me what case would actually settle it. My own idea was to suggest to mum that she put the money in a trust that only pays out if me and my sister hit certain milestones — finish the physio masters, keep the studio going five years, that kind of thing — and I want to know whether that would sort Ray out in a way the outright plan doesn't, or whether it's a dud test. If there's a better case for separating the rules, tell me what it is and what each rule predicts about it.
