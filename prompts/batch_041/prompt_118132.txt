I sit on the clinical ethics commitee at a large teaching hospital — I'm the philosopher-in-residence, which in practice means I take the minutes and get asked about Kant at parties — and we've now burned four meetings going round in circles over our death-verification policy. The problem isn't really the policy, it's that two of my colleagues have been talking past each other for a year. One is a neurologist who thinks a whole-brain criterion is simply what death is: once the brain, brainstem included, has irreversibly stopped, the organism as a whole has ceased to be an organism, and the ventilator, the hormones, the tube-feeds are just keeping tissues warm. The other is an intensivist who thinks that's a legal convenience wearing a lab coat. He points to patients declared brain-dead who then sat on ventilators for years — bodies still integrating themselves, as he puts it — and says the honest description is that they're alive, which is exactly why we need a separate argument about organ procurement rather than a redefinition of death to make the paperwork tidy. They agree on almost nothing face to face, but I have a strong hunch they'd agree on a great deal if someone forced them to write it down.

So could you draft the joint statement the two of them could actually both sign? I'd like (a) a numbered list of agreed claims — what death is a concept of, whether there's a biological fact of the matter or only a status we confer, what the dead donor rule does and doesn't say, and what neither of them is claiming; then (b) the residual disagreements, each phrased in wording that the person who holds it would accept as fair, so that my neurologist doesn't read it and say it's a straw man; then (c) the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling things one way would actually move one of them — and for each crux the case, evidence or argument they'd both concede bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. If it turns out that part of the row is really just about words, say which part and why.

One thing I'm fairly sure is a crux and want handled properly is the difference between a heart that can't be restarted and one that simply hasn't been restarted yet — the permanent-versus-irreversible business, which I gather is a policy choice in a lab coat — together with the fact that we wait some number of minutes after asystole before we say anything, and nobody can tell me whether that number came from physiology or from a committee. Around 900 words, no citations or reading list please (I can't get to a library this month and I want the argument, not the footnote trail), and write it in British English — it's going to a UK trust and 'authorize' with a z will start a whole new fight. Finish with one last line beginning 'Crux:' naming the single disagreement you think further evidence could actually settle.
