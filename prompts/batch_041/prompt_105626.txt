I have no academic training in philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about Moore's paradox for some time, and I want a piece of writing that is not an essay or a verdict. Two camps have been arguing past each other about sentences such as It is raining and I do not believe that it is raining. Call them the contradiction camp and the pragmatist camp. The contradiction camp holds that such a sentence is in some strict sense self-contradictory or logically inconsistent—perhaps because putting the first part forward commits the speaker to believing it, while the second part denies that belief. The pragmatist camp holds that the sentence can express a consistent proposition, possibly even a true one, and that its absurdity belongs to the act of asserting it rather than to its content. I want the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between these two camps would produce.

To keep the exercise from becoming a recital, I want you to work through a distinction I have been calling the two-ledger model. In this model, every assertion has two entries. The public ledger records what is put forward as true. The private ledger records what the speaker is willing to be held to as a believer. A Moorean sentence is one where the public ledger says p and the private ledger says not-p. The camps disagree about whether these ledgers are linked by a semantic rule, so that a Moorean sentence is a kind of bookkeeping contradiction, or whether they are governed by separate norms, so that the sentence can be true while the assertion is defective. Use the model where it helps, and say where it distorts.

I also want two test cases that I have not seen discussed. First, the calibration twist: a forecaster says It will rain but I do not believe it will rain, not because she is confused about her own mind, but because she knows her forecasting model is overconfident and her private credence is lower than the forecast. Second, the silent machine: a device prints the same sentence but has no beliefs and no capacity for belief. For each case, ask what each camp must say, and whether the case distinguishes them.

I am also curious about the two directions of the sentence: first the plain claim and then the disavowal of belief, or first the disavowal and then the plain claim. Do the camps treat these as the same paradox, or does one direction put pressure on the semantic view and the other on the pragmatic view? Include this in the cruxes if it belongs there.

Please give me the document in the following sections and order.

Section 1: Agreed claims. Numbered. About 500 words. These are claims both camps can sign. They should cover what phenomenon needs explaining, what a contradiction would have to amount to, the difference between the content of a sentence and the act of asserting it, and what each camp can accept about the two-ledger model.

Section 2: Disagreements, in each side's own words. A table, about 400 words. Columns for the issue, the contradiction camp's formulation, and the pragmatist camp's formulation. Each formulation must be one its own side would accept, not a caricature or a setup.

Section 3: Cruxes. About 500 words. The sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each, say which side it would move and why. Say explicitly whether any crux is merely verbal, and if so, which.

Section 4: Shared tests and predictions. About 500 words. For each crux, give a case, argument, or test that both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Use the calibration twist and the silent machine if they fit; if they do not, say why. You may also use embedding tests, such as under negation, conditionals, past tense, or third-person reports, if they help.

Section 5: What would move each side. About 250 words. For each camp, state the strongest consideration that would force it to abandon or revise its position, and what it would revise to.

Section 6: Where this document is most likely to fail. About 200 words. Identify which agreed claims are most likely to be empty or verbal, which disagreement is most likely to be a matter of wording, and what argument would force a different partition of agreement and disagreement. Also say what would change your mind about the partition you have drawn.

Do not give citations, reading lists, or appeals to who said what. Reconstruct the positions from the inside. If you need to mention a view, do it in plain language without names. End with signature lines for the two camps.
