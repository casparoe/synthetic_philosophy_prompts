Here is the question as it appeared on the exam.

Question 3. Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal states that no social decision function can satisfy all three of the following conditions at once, for any society of at least two individuals facing at least three social states: unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism. Minimal liberalism requires that at least two individuals each be decisive over at least one pair of social states. The weak Pareto principle requires that if every individual strictly prefers one state to another, then so does the society.

Two colleagues, Ana and Ben, are the only people in a position to raise a contentious item at next week's departmental meeting. There are exactly three relevant outcomes:

(a) Ana raises the item;
(b) nobody raises the item, and it is dropped;
(c) Ben raises the item.

Their preferences are as follows.

Ana: c over a over b. She would rather that Ben raised the item than raise it herself, but she would rather raise it herself than see it dropped.
Ben: b over c over a. He would rather that the item were dropped, but if it is to be raised, he would rather raise it himself than have Ana raise it.

(i) Identify the pair of outcomes over which each colleague is decisive under minimal liberalism, and show whether the case generates Sen's cycle.
(ii) Suppose we adopt the restriction that, in applying the weak Pareto principle, we do not count an individual's preference over a pair of outcomes that differ only in some other individual's personal sphere, where the personal spheres are the pairs you identified in (i). Does the cycle in this case survive that restriction? Explain.
(iii) In light of your answer to (ii), assess the claim that the liberal paradox is an artifact of meddlesome preferences.

The student wrote the following.

Sen's theorem says that no social decision function can satisfy unrestricted domain, the weak Pareto principle, and minimal liberalism at once, provided there are at least two individuals and at least three alternatives. In the case at hand the three alternatives are a, b, and c as described. Ana's personal sphere is the pair {a, b}, since these two outcomes differ only in what Ana herself does; Ben's is the pair {b, c}, since these two differ only in what Ben himself does. Minimal liberalism therefore makes Ana decisive over {a, b} and Ben decisive over {b, c}. Ana ranks a above b, so the social ranking must place a above b. Ben ranks b above c, so the social ranking must place b above c. And since both Ana and Ben rank c above a, the weak Pareto principle requires the social ranking to place c above a. These three requirements cannot be satisfied together, because they yield a above b, b above c, and c above a: the social preference is cyclic, and no social decision function can generate a choice for this profile. The example is thus a clean instance of Sen's result.

The further question is what generates the cycle. The familiar diagnosis is that it is generated by meddlesome preferences, that is, by preferences about what other people do in their own spheres. Ana's preference for c over b and Ben's preference for b over a are exactly of this kind: Ana has a view about whether Ben raises the item, and Ben has a view about whether Ana does. If we follow the restriction that Pareto comparisons are to be confined to preferences that are not meddlesome, the unanimity over the pair {a, c} disappears. The only reason Ana prefers Ben's raising the item to her own raising of it is that she would rather he carried that burden, and the only reason Ben prefers his own raising of it to Ana's is that he would rather she did not raise it at all. Strip those preferences out and the cycle is broken, leaving a consistent ranking in which Ana raises the item. The example therefore supports the view that the paradox is an artifact of meddlesome preferences, and that the incompatibility Sen proves is narrower than it first appears.

It does not follow that the weak Pareto principle should be surrendered. Since that principle merely requires that a unanimous preference be respected, and it is difficult to see how a social choice could set aside the preferences of everyone concerned, the natural response to the cycle is to restrict the liberal claim, for instance by confining it to choices in which no one else takes an interest. This is a small concession, since minimal liberalism asks very little of a constitution in the first place: two individuals, one pair each.
