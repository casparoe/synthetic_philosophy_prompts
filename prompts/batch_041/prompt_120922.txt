I'm in a small reading group that has been working through twentieth-century philosophy of social science, and next week we are on Popper's The Poverty of Historicism. One of the members kicked off our last session by summarizing the book from a chapter of a popular trade book on the philosophy of the social sciences he had been reading, and his summary left me more confused than the book itself did. As best I can reconstruct it, the chapter says this:

The target is historicism — the outlook we inherit from the German tradition of Herder, Ranke and Dilthey, according to which every epoch must be understood in its own terms, since there are no timeless standards by which to judge it. These look like the opposite of the belief that history obeys laws, but on Popper's reading they are two faces of the same mistake: both treat history as a process with a shape of its own that the social scientist has merely to read off. His argument against that mistake is that the future course of society depends on future knowledge, and we cannot know today what we will discover tomorrow, so the course of history is in principle unpredictable; and that since history is a single unrepeatable process, whatever regularities we think we detect in it are at best trends, and a trend is not a law. This is why he insists that history has no meaning, and why he holds that there can be no general laws in the social sciences at all — sociology may describe and interpret, but it cannot explain or predict, and it is the attempt to make it do so that he is attacking. His alternative is piecemeal social engineering: we should give up trying to grasp society as a whole and treat it instead as a machine to be tinkered with one part at a time. He also coined the term 'the Oedipus effect' for the way a prediction can bring about the very event it predicts, and he took this to show that social science can never be a science in the same sense as physics, since in physics the objects do not read the theory. It follows that Marxism, which rests entirely on a law of historical development, was refuted: Marx's predictions about the collapse of capitalism and the coming revolution failed, and the theory fails with them.

I have two reasons to distrust it. The picture I come away with — that Popper thought history is meaningless, that social science can at most describe, and that he had refuted Marxism — is not the Popper I half-remember from an undergraduate seminar, where I was told that he thought social science could and should have general laws of its own. And several of the claims in the passage strike me as doing work that the book's own argument cannot support.
