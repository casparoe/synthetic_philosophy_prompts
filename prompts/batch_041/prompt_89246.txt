My old advisor has a track record on questions about when a distribution counts as just. Her calls so far:

1. The Wilt Chamberlain setup: fans voluntarily pay to watch him, ticket by ticket, and he ends up with vastly more than everyone else. She says the resulting distribution is just -- nothing is owed back, no correction.

2. A commune whose members voluntarily pool their incomes and split them equally, with anyone free to walk away whenever. Just.

3. A woman inherits a large fortune her father built up through a long chain of ordinary, uncoerced, non-fraudulent trades. Just.

4. A hospital that charges a desperate patient an astronomical price for a drug when the patient has no realistic alternative. She says unjust -- the price is a kind of extraction.

Now the case I actually care about. A private collector buys a painting at a fair market price from a dealer. The collector knows full well -- it's not hidden from him -- that the painting was seized from a family during the Second World War and then passed through several hands before reaching the dealer. Nobody involved in the current sale is the original wrongdoer; the family it was taken from can no longer be traced; and the collector pays the going rate with his own money, no pressure on anyone. Is he entitled to the painting, on her view? Would she say he owes something to someone, and if so to whom?

What I really want: (a) the candidate rules that fit her four earlier verdicts equally well, (b) what each of those rules predicts about the painting case, (c) your best guess at which rule she's actually running, with your reasons, and (d) one further case that would force the rules apart -- ideally a case where they give different answers.
