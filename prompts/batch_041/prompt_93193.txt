My friend teaches decision theory and she has this line she's always repeating, that costless information can't hurt you. It's her actual verdicts on particular cases I care about though. Six I can remember, all of them free information she could have for nothing:

- a radar look at tomorrow before she decides whether to move the department picnic outside: yes, take it
- the forecast high for a city she's never been to and has no stake in: worth nothing, not even a little
- a test that tells her which of two flights will be delayed, in a case where she's booked on the early one either way: nothing
- a test whose answer she'd only get the day after the decision is due: nothing
- a test that might show she's been giving patients the wrong adivce for two years, which would mean retracting it in public and eating crow: yes, take it, and she says the crow doesn't count against it because you're valuing it before you look
- a test whose result goes in her own file, nobody else reads it, and no rule anywhere forces her to act on it: yes

Now my cousin has to decide whether to sign up for the free genetic screening at her new job, the wellness-program kind, where the results land in a portal her health insurer can see and she's been told that once a result is in there she has to answer honestly about it on any life insurance application later. The panel covers a couple of things where there'd be something to do if it came back bad, and a couple where there's nothing to do at all.

Will my friend say take it or skip it? And is she on the flat version of her line, or the narrower one where it only holds when it's nobody's business but hers and nothing forces her hand? What third rule fits all six verdicts just as well, what does each of them predict for my cousin, and if they come apart here, which one further case would tell me which rule she's realy running? I care more about the rule than about the test.
