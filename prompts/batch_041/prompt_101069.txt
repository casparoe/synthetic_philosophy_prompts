My friends and I put out a monthly newsletter for our old grad-school group — bad drawings, a crossword, and a fake advice column I write called "Dear Posterior". The running bit is that the columnist is a totally sincere Bayesian agony aunt who has absorbed Aumann's agreement theorem and now runs every reader's personal problem through it as if it were a warranty claim. She never winks at the reader, not once. Write this months installment: three letters and three replies, around 700 words all in, legible to someone who's never heard of the theorem and funny to someone who teaches it.

Letter one: a couple who finally sat down and announced their credences to each other about whether their dog will bite the mailman (0.7 and 0.35), and are now stuck, since each is quite sure the other is rational and neither wants to be the irrational one. Letter two: two partners on a trading desk who keep buying positions off each other and both keep coming out ahead, and whose consultant told them that with a common prior this is impossible, and they want to know if he's right or just describing a world where nothing ever happens. Letter three: a woman whose family has reached a private understanding about her late fathers estate but never told the one cousin who was abroad — she wants to know whether they've "agreed" in the sense that matters or whether something is still missing.

The replies have to be technically right, because half our group does decision theory and will notice. So: the theorem needs a shared prior and the posteriors to be genuinely common knowledge — everyone knows, and knows that everyone knows, and so on up — plus both parties actually conditioning on their information and updating by Bayes; the announcement is itself informative, so it's the fixpoint that matters, not the first round; the theorem tells you the two of them can't both keep those posteriors once it's out in the open, but it is silent about who's right, and if their priors were never the same then disagreeing forever is perfectly rational and nothing has gone wrong. The trading letter should get the no-trade idea right — with a common prior and a setup that's common knowledge, the two of them can't both see the same trade as profitable, though real desks trade for other reasons (hedging, liquidity, different endowments, risk tolerance), so the theorem isn't "no trades happen" — and the aunt can bring in the sharper version, where the impossibility of both of them agreeing a bet is favourable is equivalent to the existence of a common prior. She can also note that the iterated version converges: keep announcing, keep updating, and the disagreement can't last forever. Let the objections in sideways and answer them rather than dodging — one reader's frequentist colleague thinks common priors are a fiction, another thinks the whole thing only rules out an idealized nobody. No citations, no references, it's a newsletter. And please don't have anyone say "well, feelings aren't probabilities" — the joke has to come from the theorem being applied correctly and remorselessly, not from the aunt being oblivious in some generic way.
