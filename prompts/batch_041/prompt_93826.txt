I am a high-school student, and for the final portfolio of my philosophy seminar I want a sestina — six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi — that does the work of Rawls's veil of ignorance through its form rather than through statements about it. Use six end-words that are six social positions in one small imagined town, and keep them concrete: the landlord, the night-shift nurse, the child who cleans the school before dawn, the shopkeeper, the elected mayor, and the person the census missed. Let the sestina's rotation carry the argument: by the end each of the six should have occupied every line-position in the poem, so that no voice stays fixed and I, reading, have been made to imagine being each of them in turn — and do this without ever using the words veil, ignorance, justice, or fairness. Save the choosing for the envoi, where all six finally stand together, and let it land on the difference principle without naming it: the rules the town settles on are justified only if they are bearable from the place of the worst-off. I want the poem to leave me with both the pull of that thought and the suspicion that I stacked the six positions so the conclusion would come out my way.
