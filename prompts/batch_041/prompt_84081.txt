I sit on the institutional review board at a university-affiliated hospital. I spent thirty years as a toxicologist in a state environmental-health laboratory, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy on my own — slowly, and not always well.

Our policy permits us to enroll adults who cannot consent for themselves only in research that poses no more than minimal risk, and it defines the term by borrowing the federal language: minimal risk means that the probability and magnitude of harm or discomfort anticipated in the research are not greater in and of themselves than those ordinarily encountered in daily life or during the performance of routine physical or psychological examinations or tests. That is the whole of our guidance. There are no worked examples and no statement of whose daily life is the comparison.

The protocol before us is a biomarker study of early neurodegeneration. Each participant would give a few extra vials of blood, spend an hour in a scanner with a contrast agent, and undergo a lumbar puncture. The lumbar puncture is what worries me, but the investigators point out that many of these patients have scans and spinal taps as part of their ordinary clinical workup anyway, and that each element taken alone is something we approve for competent adults without a second thought. The board has split. One member says the protocol as a whole is plainly more than daily life and that we are fooling ourselves to pretend otherwise; another says that if every component falls within the range of what our patients already endure, then the sum cannot be the thing we are forbidden to approve.

I keep noticing that our disagreement has the shape of the sorites paradox, and also that it may not: minimal risk is not a property of a heap, it is a comparison between a research procedure and the risks of ordinary life, and no one has told us whose ordinary life.

In my old field we set thresholds constantly. We identified a no-observed-adverse-effect level, divided by uncertainty factors, and published a number that regulators and industry could both act on. Everyone understood that the number did not mark a seam in nature; its point was to be usable, revisable, and candid about its conventional character. I would like the opinion to say plainly whether the philosophical literature on vagueness adds anything to that practice or whether it diagnoses something else.

One member has proposed a three-zone rule: procedures clearly within minimal risk approved as before, procedures clearly beyond it sent for full review, and a middle band referred to a subcommittee that must record its reasons case by case. I want to know whether that genuinely helps or merely moves the same problem up one level.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our Thursday meeting. It should recommend a concrete disposition of this protocol, with a time frame; state the test the board should apply to cases like it in future, in words a reviewer can actually use at a desk; give the strongest argument on the side I am inclined against its due rather than a token hearing; and be explicit about which parts of the sorites apparatus bear on our situation and which do not, including an honest verdict on the three-zone proposal. I do not need citations or a reading list — I am reading on my own and want the arguments in your own words.

Finally, please say how confident you are in each substantive claim you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of.
