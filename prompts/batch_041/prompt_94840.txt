A philosopher of art I have been corresponding with assigns each work a single number for its aesthetic value, on a 0 to 10 scale, and is careful to insist that the number is her best estimate of the work's value rather than a record of how much she personally likes it. She has sent me seven verdicts, and I would like to predict the eighth before she sends it.

Vermeer's original painting, in good condition: 10.
A skilled forger's canvas, indistinguishable from the Vermeer both to the unaided eye and under every technical test we can devise, exhibited as the forger's own work: 5.
A meticulous hand-painted copy by a living painter, signed and exhibited as a copy: 3.
A high-resolution photograph of the Vermeer: 2.
A Vermeer whose surface is 60 percent repainted by a nineteenth-century restorer: 6.
An image produced by a machine-learning model that is pixel-for-pixel identical to the Vermeer: 3.
A pane of glass fused by lightning striking sand, which from every angle looks exactly like a Rodin bronze: 2.

The eighth case is a canvas that is in fact a forgery, but that every living expert and every viewer believes to be an original Vermeer, and that will never be exposed.

What number will she give it? Set out the principle she appears to be running; then give at least one rival principle that fits all seven verdicts just as well, and say what each predicts for the eighth case. If the principles diverge there, tell me which you would bet on, which further case would best drive the surviving principles apart, and what else you would want to know about her commitments in order to choose. I care less about the number than about the space of rules her verdicts leave open and where the new case falls within it.
