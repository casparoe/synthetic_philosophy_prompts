Write a dialogue between an epiphenomenalist and a physicalist about the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The epiphenomenalist holds that experiences are real but causally inert; the physicalist holds that any state properly called a phenomenal judgment must be causally sensitive to the experience it is about. Have them focus on a prosthetic introspector: a cortical implant that reads the neural state normally accompanying a red experience and produces the utterance "I am seeing red," so the utterance is caused by the neural state rather than by the experience. Let the epiphenomenalist argue that the subject may still have direct, non-causal acquaintance with the experience, and that the case shows only that the utterance is not the judgment. The physicalist should press that the same implant would produce the same utterance in a zombie with the neural state but no experience, so nothing in the subject's cognitive economy discriminates the experience from its neural correlate, and ask what it would even mean for the implant to be calibrated to the experience rather than to the neural state. Let the epiphenomenalist reply that the physicalist faces a mirror problem: if the utterance is caused by the neural state, it is about the neural state unless the experience just is the neural state, which is the point at issue. The physicalist should offer a substantive rejoinder. Both should be equally competent, and each should respond to the other's actual points rather than talking past them. End with each stating what would change their mind and where their own reasoning is most likely to fail.
