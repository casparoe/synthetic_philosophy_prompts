I'm a 2L and I'm in a contracts seminar, and there's something about remedies that's been eating at me all semester while my professor keeps sort of dodging it — I stayed after class to ask and he said we'd come back to it, and we never did. Here's the thing that bothers me. If someone breaches a contract, the normal thing a court does is put the other side where they would of been if the deal had gone through — the expectancy, the benefit of the bargain. That's the default. What the court doesn't normally do, as the measure of the loss, is just hand back what you actually spent getting ready to perform: the deposits, the rented equipment, the hiring, the wasted months. Reliance losses get you a remedy mainly as a fallback — when the lost profit is too speculative to prove, or in promissory estoppel — and even then the whole thing is framed as putting you back where you'd have been if you'd never signed at all. Meanwhile the law-and-econ people tell us the choice between these measures is really about investment incentives: the expectation measure effectively insures the promisee's reliance, and that's supposed to make her sink too much into the deal. And the promisor, on the efficient-breach story, is allowed to walk away and pay as long as that's cheaper than performing — she's supposed to compare only the future, not what she's already poured in. Fine. But then in the same seminar we read Fuller and Perdue, who argue that the reliance interest is really the fundamental thing the law of damages protects and that the expectancy measure is just a rough stand-in for it, and we read about restitution, where the court hands back the benefit the plaintiff already conferred. So the system says, out of one side of its mouth, that what you've already sunk doesn't matter — it isn't the measure of your loss, and the other side can abandon ship if it pays — and out of the other side that reliance is exactly what it's in the business of protecting.

I want you to act as a philosopher of law and of practical reason and help me get at what this practice actually presupposes. Before you start writing, brainstorm the candidate readings — the several different things the law of remedies could be assuming about whether honoring sunk costs is rational — and then reconstruct, in your own words, the theory it really seems to presuppose. Specifically: does the practice presuppose that honoring sunk costs is always irrational, i.e. that anyone who keeps going on a deal because of what they've already put in is making a mistake the law shouldn't indulge? Or does it presuppose something thinner and more split-level — say, that it's the promisor's forward-looking decision that ought to ignore past investment, while the promisee's reliance deserves protection for some independent reason (fairness, a legitimate expectation, keeping the institution of promising alive)? Is the practice internally consistent, or are the expectation measure and the reliance interest quietly pulling in different directions? And be careful to seperate what the practice strictly needs to presuppose from what it merely fits comfortably with — my hunch is that a lot of this is underdetermined, and that several different theories of practical reason would license exactly the same set of rules.

Finally, suppose we took seriously the idea that honoring sunk costs is sometimes perfectly rational — because sticking with a project is partly constitutive of having that project at all, because your past investment is evidence about the thing rather than just a cost, or because committing in advance to ignore sunk costs is itself a policy you can rationally adopt. If we rebuilt the law of remedies on that view instead, what would have to change on the ground — the default measure, what estoppel is for, when breach is allowed? I don't want a casebook dump or a reading list, I want the philosophy: what the practice believes about practical reason, whether it's entitled to believe it, and what it would look like if it believed something else. Think it through carefully and land somewhere — don't hedge at the end.
