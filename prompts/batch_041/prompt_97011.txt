I am assembling a small anthology of poems that double as thought experiments, and I would like you to contribute one. The poem should be about family resemblance, but I do not want it to be a poem about Wittgenstein in the abstract or a poem that simply names the doctrine; I want the philosophical work done by an invented language game. Imagine a community—call them the glaik-speakers—who use the word glaik in a way that is taught entirely by example. A child learns it by being shown a wet stone after rain, a horse standing asleep in the afternoon, a particular grey-violet colour at dusk, the sound of a door closing in an empty house, and the way a certain old man laughs when he is embarrassed. No one can give a definition. Asked what glaik means, the speakers point to more examples, correct one another, and say things like, not that, glaik. Some of them insist the wet stone is the real glaik and the rest are metaphors; others say the laughter is the centre and the stone is a distant cousin. The poem should be spoken by one of these speakers, perhaps the person who teaches the word to children, and it should be addressed to a visitor who keeps asking what glaik really is.

The form I want is a modified pantoum: four quatrains, sixteen lines, with the interlocking repeats of a pantoum (the second and fourth lines of each quatrain become the first and third lines of the next), and the final quatrain closing the loop by returning its second and fourth lines to the first and third positions of the opening quatrain. But there is a constraint: whenever a line returns, you must change exactly one word in it, and you must change at least one word in every return. No line may ever be repeated verbatim, and no single predicate or feature should survive from the first stanza to the last. The repetitions should enact family resemblance rather than assert it: each return should feel continuous with its earlier self while being pulled toward a different member of the family, so that the reader can track the overlap without ever being able to extract a common essence. The loop should make the absence of an essence feel like a movement—a way of going on—rather than a simple lack.

Please keep the poem in free verse within the pantoum’s repeating architecture; the lines should be short enough to be memorable but not so short that the syntax becomes a puzzle. The visitor’s question should appear once, near the middle, and should not be answered directly. I would like the ending to leave the visitor and the reader with the sense that they have learned how to use the word without having learned what it means—and that this is not a failure but the point. Accuracy matters: someone who knows the later Wittgenstein should recognize the family-resemblance structure in the poem’s returns and near-repetitions, while someone who has never read him should still find it a good poem about a community, a word, and the impossibility of a definition.
