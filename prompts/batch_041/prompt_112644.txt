I'm taking a course on social ontology and the philosophy of law, and for the past few weeks our seminar has been running a self-amending rule game as a kind of laboratory. The setup is this: we begin from a short rulebook, and the rulebook itself is what the players are allowed to change. The initial rules fall into two classes — entrenched rules, which can be amended only through a special procedure (in our case a two-thirds vote plus a one-round delay), and ordinary rules, which a simple majority can amend at any time. One rule, the amendment rule, specifies how a change is proposed, debated, voted on, and brought into force; another, the entrenchment rule, says which rules are entrenched and what it takes to move a rule from one class to the other. Every rule is numbered, and rules refer to one another by number, so the amendment rule can be turned on itself: we can propose to change the amendment rule by the very procedure the amendment rule lays down, and we can change the entrenchment rule the same way. The first time we tried to raise the threshold for amending the amendment rule from two-thirds to unanimity, the room split. Half insisted the change was valid because it passed under the old two-thirds rule; the other half insisted it could not be valid, on the grounds that a rule which authorizes its own replacement is presupposing the very authority it is trying to confer.

I'd like you to explain, as carefully as you can, what the underlying conceptual issue actually is, and to separate the parts of it that are genuinely troubling from the parts that are only apparently so. Specifically, I want a clear account of three things. First, the circularity-or-regress worry: the amendment's validity seems to depend on the rule being amended, whose authority in turn seems to depend on the amendment, so that the whole thing either grounds out in some unamendable rule or loops forever. Second, whether that worry is real — and if it isn't, what actually does the work of licensing a valid self-amendment, given that there is no rule outside the book available to authorize it. Third, why the two classes of rules and the delay and entrenchment machinery matter at all: what would go wrong if every rule were equally easy to change, and whether an unamendable master rule is even a coherent thing to have in a book whose entire point is that it can be rewritten.

Please use our game as the running example throughout, and whenever you introduce a distinction, say concretely what would differ in the game if the distinction were drawn the other way. I'm less interested in a survey of the positions people have taken on self-reference than in a genuinely worked-through answer to whether the players of such a game can coherently change the rule that lets them change rules — and if they can, exactly where the appearance of paradox comes from.
