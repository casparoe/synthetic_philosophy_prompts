I spent last term in a reading group on causation with students who had already taken a first course in philosophy of science, and the term ended with two of them locked in an argument that we never resolved. One is a committed counterfactualist: causation is difference-making, and difference-making is to be cashed out in counterfactuals about what would have happened had the cause been absent — the tradition descending from David Lewis. The other is a committed interventionist: causation is a matter of what would change under an idealized intervention, and the natural home for the theory is a causal model with a fixed set of variables, in the tradition of James Woodward and the structural-equations literature that grew out of Judea Pearl's work. They argued for weeks, and while neither was a caricature of his own side, they could not even agree on which cases were the hard ones. I would like you to write the document they should have produced: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration, a text that both of them could sign. Open with a numbered section of claims they genuinely share — about the formalism, about what a theory of causation is for, about which cases are already settled for both of them and why — then state their remaining disagreements one at a time, each phrased in the words its own side would use, and follow each with the crux on which it turns. For every crux, name the case, argument, or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side predicts about it. If part of the apparent dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why; if part of it is really about what a theory of causation is for rather than about the facts, say that too. Use a disagreements table with columns for the crux, the counterfactualist's prediction, and the interventionist's prediction.

To give the statement something to bite on, here is a case I invented for the group, and I want each side's verdict on it recorded in the table, along with a note on whether the case actually separates them; if it does not, say so plainly and supply a case that does. A data center rack draws power from two feeds that come off different substations, and either feed alone is sufficient to keep the rack running. On the night in question both feeds were live and the rack stayed up. There is also an on-call engineer who could have tripped the rack's breaker from her desk at any point and did not. Which of these — feed A, feed B, the engineer's not tripping the breaker — count as causes of the rack's staying up, on each side's view, and does the answer change if you treat the two feeds as a single variable rather than as two? That last question matters to me more than it may look. One member's central objection to interventionism was that its verdicts are relative to a choice of variables, so that lumping the feeds together as "the power supply" or splitting them apart is a decision the theory leaves to us, whereas counterfactual dependence is not relative to anything; the other replied that this relativity is not a defect but the whole insight, since causal claims are always claims at some chosen level of description. I would also like the statement to confront the further sticking points we kept circling: whether a merely accidental counterfactual is enough for causation or whether the relationship has to be invariant across a range of circumstances; how each side handles omissions, and whether either survives the observation that there are indefinitely many absences available to serve as "the" cause of anything; and whether causation is transitive — one member insisted that causation is obviously transitive while counterfactual dependence obviously is not, and that this alone settles the dispute. If transitivity is a real fault line, fold it in; if it is not, say why not.

Begin directly with the joint statement itself, with no preface, no restatement of this request, and no commentary on what you are doing.
