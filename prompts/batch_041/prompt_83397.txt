I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy working at the seam between classical Indian epistemology and contemporary analytic epistemology, and in two weeks I have to run a session of our departmental graduate workshop: fourteen participants, mostly philosophers of mind, language, or science who have never read a Sanskrit text, two hours including a break. What I want from the session is not a lecture on the pramāṇas but an activity that makes the group feel, from the inside, that the question how many knowledge-sources there are is a substantive and costly commitment rather than a piece of bookkeeping. They should have to file real cases under a fixed list of ways of knowing, find that a few cases will not sit still, and then find that both ways of handling the stragglers — adding a new source, or insisting the old ones suffice — carry a price they feel in the game before anyone explains it to them. Since none of them can look anything up, and since I would rather the activity not turn into a vocabulary quiz, everything the design presupposes must be something I can put on a one-page handout in plain English.

Here is the material I would like the activity built from, and I would rather you take it as given than reconstruct the tradition from memory. The list they are stuck with has four items: perceiving for oneself; inferring; knowing something by being told it; and knowing by comparison or analogy, the last illustrated by the case in which someone tells you that an animal you have never seen is like a cow, and later, on seeing one, you know what it is. The cases that should not sit still are at least these: knowing that there is no pot on the table, where the ground of the knowledge seems to be that you do not perceive one; knowing that a man who never eats during the day must eat at night, on the ground that he is stout and would otherwise be thin, where you seem to know by postulating something that dissolves an apparent inconsistency; a case where the only thing distinguishing the comparison case from the testimony case is when the learning happened; and a case where the speaker is untrustworthy but the content is true and unverifiable, which should force them to say whether being told is a source in its own right or borrows its credentials from the teller. The four-item list should be a hard constraint, not a suggestion: no fifth desk without paying for it in whatever currency the game uses — points, tokens, credibility, no real money — and a rival faction in the room should want to compress the list to two, perception and inference, insisting that being told is just inference from the speaker's reliability and that comparison is just perception plus memory. I also want the game to force a decision about when a filing is allowed to stand: under one policy a filing counts as good the moment it is made and is dislodged only if something later defeats it; under the other nothing counts until independently confirmed, which costs resources. The structure I am after is that both policies lose, differently, when the audit comes at the end — the permissive policy banks filings cheaply and then bleeds when the audit exposes the wrong ones, the strict policy is safe and starves. If it fits in the time, add a short third phase in which participants are secretly assigned one of three registers — arguing to find out what is true, arguing to win, and arguing to embarrass the other side without committing to anything oneself — and the room has to guess who was in which, since the tradition's own thought is that whether an exchange conduces to knowledge depends on the register it is conducted in, and I want them to watch the second and third registers eat the first.

What I need back is a runnable design rather than a description of one: the setting as given, a materials list (case cards, counters or score sheets, the handout), rules and turn structure, timings for each phase, what the facilitator does and does not say while the thing is running, and a scoring scheme that makes the trade-offs bite. The participants must not hear the names of the four sources in Sanskrit, or the names of the schools, or the technical vocabulary, until the debrief; the desks should be labelled in plain English, so that the reveal lands as a surprise — that they have just re-run a dispute that occupied several schools for centuries, and that the awkward cards are exactly the ones on which the lists were argued over. Then give me the debrief, mapping particular moments in the activity onto particular moves in the underlying dispute (why one school multiplies sources, why another reduces them, what the reductionist must say about each awkward card and what it costs her, and why the choice between the two policies about standing filings tracks a real disagreement about whether a cognition is valid by its own nature or only once something else vouches for it), and be candid about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in my own voice — my own guess is that the deepest unreachable thing is the case in which the authority of what you are told rests on no speaker at all, because the text in question has no author, and I do not see how a classroom stages that; tell me if you see others. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: I expect at least one faction to try to win by redefining the desks rather than using them, and I want to know in advance how to handle that without the debrief turning into a scolding.
