I got into it with my flatmate after a lecture on natural kinds and I've been chewing it over all week. He thinks the whole idea that science sorts things along nature's own seams is a confidence trick, and he has this arguement he reckons nails it. (1) A category is a natural kind only if what falls under it is settled by the world and not by us. (2) But any feature you could sort things by is a feature you first had to single out as worth sorting by. (3) So what falls under any category is settled by us and not by the world. (4) So there are no natural kinds: electron, gold and water are no less arbitrary than things-in-my-kitchen. When I said that proves too much he said that's rather the point, it does prove too much, and the joint-carving picture was always too good to be true.

I'd like to keep (1) if I can, because without it I genuinely don't know what the point of classifying is meant to be, and (4) seems to me to throw out the baby. But I don't want to just dig my heels in and say the conclusion is absurd, that isn't an answer. He has a fallback as well: if I make him drop (1), he says fine, natural kinds are just whatever categories pay their way, and then he asks me why a chemist's table beats one sorted by how the metal feels in your hand. If the only answer is that the chemist's one is more useful, he thinks he's won.

So: which step do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'm especially worried about the fallback, so tell me whether giving up (1) really lands me with his deflationary line, and if it does, whether that's actually as bad as it sounds.
