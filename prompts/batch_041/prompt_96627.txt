I am a novelist at work on a book set in a society whose moral code is not my own — a real historical society, not an invented one, and I want its moral texture rather than my own convictions dressed in period costume. This means I make the same decision dozens of times in a draft: a character does something my readers will find monstrous, or admirable, or simply unintelligible, and I have to decide whether the book presents it as wrong full stop, as right by the lights of the world that made her, or as something the two moralities cannot even put into a shared question. The decision is not academic for me. It settles what kind of book I am writing — indictment, portrait, or elegy — and reviewers will read a thesis out of it whether or not I intended one. I am not a philosopher, I cannot consult one scene by scene, and I need to make the call in the time it takes to draft the scene. The final text is mine, so I would rather have a procedure that is candid about its own seams than one that pretends the judgment has been automated away.

Please write me a decision procedure — a checklist I can run while drafting, not a lecture — grounded in what the philosophy of moral disagreement and the empirical psychology of moral variation actually support, working from what you know rather than from any reading list. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the places where the procedure stops with a verdict, and the places where it stops and hands the decision back to me because the question is about the kind of book I am writing rather than about the world. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of method, and where the procedure is likely to misfire on a scene.

Build it around at least these materials, which I think are the load-bearing ones and which I want you to make usable without jargon: the difference between a disagreement in moral verdict and a disagreement in the concepts that carry the verdict; the possibility that an apparent moral clash is really a clash of non-moral belief, dissolving once the parties share a picture of what the act does; the moral/conventional distinction and the question of whether it survives translation; the looping effect by which a category and the people it names reshape each other; and the fact that the empirical case for cross-cultural variation rests on samples that are themselves narrow, so the procedure must not treat widespread variation as a settled premise. I also want you to build in two tests of my own devising, and to improve them if they are bad. The first is a translation test: restate the disputed act in the vocabulary the other society uses for its own central moral concepts, and see what is left over that neither vocabulary can express. The second is a load-bearing check: ask how much of the surrounding normative system would have to move if this practice moved, and what that tells me about whether I am looking at a local rule or a different architecture. Add a third if you can think of one that earns its place.

Then run the procedure on three cases from my draft, end to end, showing me the answers at each step and the narrative stance each answer implies — neutral portraiture, implicit judgment, or staged incommensurability — and what technique would carry that stance on the page. The cases: a society that exposes unwanted infants; a funerary practice that strikes my readers as desecration; and a family that kills a daughter for a sexual transgression, where the daughter is my most sympathetic character. I expect the last one to give me an answer I will not enjoy, and I want you to give it anyway, including what it costs me dramatically.

Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do, the kinds of scene where it will produce a confident answer that is wrong, and how I will know when I have left the procedure behind and am simply deciding what I believe.
