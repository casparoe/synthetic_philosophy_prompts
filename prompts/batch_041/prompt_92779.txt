I'm writing a piece for a science-and-ideas slot about conditionals, and I've got a freind who prices football and racing markets for a living who reckons philosophers have made a mountain out of nothing. His argument, roughly: if a bookie quotes you a price on 'if the ground comes up soft, Fiddler's Elbow wins', there's no mystery in it. Ground comes up good, the bet's void, stake comes back. Ground's soft and the horse wins, you get paid. So the price he quoted you just was the chance of the horse winning given soft ground — which is to say the probability of the conditional just is the conditional probability. He says this isnt a philosophical thesis at all, it's the plumbing of the betting ring, and it's why he doesn't lose any sleep over Lewis's triviality proofs: if the equation were really impossible, the ring would have noticed centuries ago, so the proofs must be smuggling something in — a daft logic, conditionals buried inside other conditionals that nobody in their right mind would ever bet on. He wants me to write it up as philosophers baffled by bookmaking. I'd rather not embarrass myself, so before I file it: how far does his analogy actually hold?

Give me the correspondence first — a table, I like tables — what plays the role of the stake, the odds, the void condition, the price you take versus the price you'd take later, and whatever else is in there. Then the breaks. I'm especially after (a) whether the number the bookie quotes is the probability of anything, or a price that only means something conditional on the bet being live, and how much that costs my friend; (b) what in the conditional is supposed to correspond to the stake coming back in the worlds where the bet is void, since the conditional doesn't obviously have a refund in it; and (c) whether the bookies' margin, which he says you obviously strip out first, repairs any of this or makes it worse. Separate the disanalogies that matter for what he wants to conclude from the ones that are just colour, and tell me what his picture is quietly importing about conditionals that doesn't come free with the betting story.

Then the payoff. Grant him everything the analogy can carry. Does he get any reason at all to think the triviality results are flawed? Or does he only get a claim about our degrees of belief and what we're entitled to say, leaving the theorem to bite anyone who insists the conditional is a proposition with truth conditions? If it's the second, say plainly what he has to give up and whether that's worse for him than just accepting the theorem. Finish with a better analogy if you've got one, or tell me the called-off bet is the best we can do. Pitch it at someone who's happy with conditional probability but has never read a paper on conditionals. British English, and no bibliography — I can't use citations in the piece anyway.
