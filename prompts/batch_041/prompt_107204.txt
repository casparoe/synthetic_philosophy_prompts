I am preparing a decision-tree handout for a graduate seminar on decision theory. The seminar has just established the standard result, often called Good's theorem, that a costless information signal cannot lower the expected utility available to an agent who will choose the act that maximizes expected utility after seeing it. I need the handout to map the positions one can take once one asks when that result fails to capture the rational status of information for actual agents.

Write the decision tree. Each internal node must be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Each leaf must carry the name of a position, or the word unnamed if no one defends it, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of authors or labels.

The tree must include nodes that separate positions according to at least these distinctions: whether the agent is an expected-utility maximizer; whether the signal is free in the narrow sense of having no resource cost, or also free of deliberation, attention, and self-control costs; whether the signal can change the agent's utility function or preferences; whether the signal can change the agent's available option set, as when learning a test result triggers a duty to disclose or a legal restriction; whether the signal is correlated with the agent's act through a non-causal route; whether the agent can precommit to ignoring the signal, and whether the relevant unit of evaluation is the act, the policy, or the decision procedure; whether the signal is about an exogenous state or about the agent's own future choice; whether the agent's credence update is Bayesian; and whether the signal is self-fulfilling or self-defeating, so that learning it changes the probability of the state it concerns.

At least three nodes must turn on concrete cases of this kind: a medical test whose result triggers a duty to disclose and thereby removes an option; a hiring committee that can read a candidate's file for free, where reading it changes the committee's legal obligations; an agent who learns that she is the sort of person who would take a certain action, where that knowledge itself makes the action more likely; and a strategic interaction in which observing another party's signal changes that party's incentive to send it.

Include at least one leaf for a position that accepts Good's theorem but denies that it captures rational information avoidance, and at least one leaf for a position that denies the theorem's assumptions apply to any actual agent. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and briefly diagnose any unoccupied combinations: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

The handout should have ten to fourteen nodes and seven to ten leaves. Do not cite literature or provide a reading list; the instrument must stand on its own.
