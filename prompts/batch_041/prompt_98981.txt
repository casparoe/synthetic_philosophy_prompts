I'm a graduate student in metaphysics and I've been asked to run a session of our dispositions seminar, since the assigned reading for that week is thin and the instructor wants us to work through the puzzles ourselves. Rather than rehash the usual examples, I've invented a set of cases and I'd like you to work through them with me first, so that I can anticipate where the discussion will go and where I've accidentally built a case that collapses into another. Please assume throughout what I'll call the simple conditional analysis: an object is disposed to produce manifestation M in response to stimulus S just in case it would produce M if S were to occur. I also want to fix three labels, in the way they're usually used, so that we're all applying them the same way. A fink is an external arrangement that is triggered by the occurrence of the stimulus (or by the presence of the disposition itself) and that either removes the disposition or prevents its manifestation, so that the object does not in fact M when S occurs even though it is disposed to. A mask is an interfering condition that is present when S occurs and blocks M, while leaving the disposition and its causal basis intact. A mimic is an arrangement that brings about M when S occurs in an object that lacks the disposition altogether, so that the object M's when S occurs without being disposed to.

Here are the cases. (1) A glass beaker is fragile; whenever it is struck, a sensor detects the impact and an instant later a field stiffens the glass so that it flexes and does not shatter, and the stiffening never occurs in the absence of an impact. (2) A fragile glass beaker is struck while submerged in a fluid that damps the vibration so effectively that it does not shatter; its molecular structure is untouched, and the very same beaker shatters when struck in air. (3) A beaker made of a shatter-proof polymer is struck, and a hidden mechanism shatters it on every such occasion; left alone, it would never shatter. (4) A sugar cube is soluble; whenever it is placed in water, a robotic arm snatches it out before it can dissolve and returns it to the shelf, leaving its structure unchanged. (5) A woman has a genuinely generous character, disposed to give to those in need; whenever a needy person enters the room, a device induces a temporary paralysis of the relevant deliberation, so she gives nothing, and her character is as before once the person leaves. (6) An antibiotic is disposed to cure a certain infection; in every actual patient a second, otherwise harmless microbe is present that degrades the drug on contact, though the drug cures reliably in patients who lack that microbe. (7) A copper wire conducts when touched by a conductor, but the circuit is rigged so that touching the wire closes a relay that instantly inserts an insulating sleeve, so it does not conduct on those occasions. (8) A fragile glass is permanently encased in a tight foam shell, so that it never shatters however it is struck.

Please sort these eight cases into four tiers according to how much of a threat each poses to the simple conditional analysis, from most threatening to least, and within each tier order them by how confident you are of the placement. For each case, say which of the three labels fits it, or that none fits cleanly and why, and then say what the smallest amendment to the conditional analysis would be that accommodates it, and whether that amendment is doing principled work or is ad hoc patching. I'm especially interested in any case where the disposition's causal basis seems to be doing the real explanatory work and the counterfactual is merely riding along, and in whether it matters that the interference is triggered by the stimulus rather than being simply present. Where two cases threaten the analysis equally but for quite different reasons, say so explicitly, since I suspect that will be the most productive part of the seminar.
