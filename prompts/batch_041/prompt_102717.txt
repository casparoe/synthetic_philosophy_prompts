I'm a graduate student splitting my time between a philosophy department and a museum studies program, and I keep butting heads with a friend who works in conservation. Her position is that once a forgery is good enough to defeat every available test — same materials, same technique, marks indistinguishable from the artist's own, so that even the artist's closest associates and the artist's own dealer could not tell the two apart — there is simply nothing left for a connoisseur to value in the "original" beyond its price tag, and the entire premium is a market artefact dressed up as metaphysics. I suspect she is folding several distinct claims into one, and I'd like your help sorting the following six proposed explanations of why people prize originals over indistinguishable duplicates. Please rank them into tiers — S, A, B, C, D — by how much of the phenomenon each genuinely explains once the perfect-duplicate stipulation is held fixed, and give two or three sentences per entry on why it lands where it does and what it would have to claim in order to climb a tier.

The six, in no particular order: (a) the causal-achievement account, on which the original is valued because it is the product of the artist's own agency and a duplicate, however flawless, cannot carry that achievement; (b) the perceptual account, on which originals differ from copies in subtle perceptible properties detectable only by the trained eye; (c) the relic or trace account, on which the original is a physical trace of the artist's causal contact with the world and is valuable the way a saint's finger-bone or a moon rock is valuable, quite apart from any perceptible feature; (d) the revelatory account, on which originals give access to facts about the artist's mind, intention, and process that no copy can supply; (e) the practice or social account, on which the premium is sustained by the institutions of connoisseurship, authentication, and the art market, and would survive even if every other account here were false; and (f) the anti-deception account, on which what we actually object to in a forgery is the deception, so the premium tracks the wrongness of the lie rather than any property of the object.

A few things I want from the execution. Keep the perfect-duplicate stipulation firmly in view — several of these look designed to be killed by it, and I want you to say so out loud rather than quietly redefining "duplicate" into something visibly imperfect. Tell me whether the six are really six, or whether some collapse into one another; I have a nagging suspicion that (a) and (d) are one claim wearing two hats, and that (f) is a thesis about a different phenomenon altogether. And if my framing is confused — if "the premium on originals" is not a single thing to be explained but a bundle of distinct ones, or if the ranking can only be done by taking a contested stand on what "value" is doing here — please say so plainly instead of ranking anyway, and flag exactly where you're taking that stand. Finish with an honest read on your confidence in the tier boundaries: which adjacencies are close calls and which are solid.
