I'm moderating a session for our department's AI-ethics reading group, and the guest is an unnamed philosopher I've invented for the exercise who defends what she calls "strong assistive paternalism": the view that an AI assistant may, and sometimes must, override a user's stated request -- not to prevent harm to the user, but to protect her capacity to form and revise her own ends. She leans on two analogies I want you to put pressure on: the attorney's duty of zealous representation, which she claims licenses refusing to help a client do something self-defeating even when told to, and the Ulysses-contract / advance-directive model, where a user's calm earlier self can bind her in-the-moment self. Draft exactly five interview questions as a numbered list, each a sentence or two, with no intro or conclusion. I want you to interogate her where it actually hurts: the disanalogy between an attorney (whose client can fire him) and an assistant (whose user often can't even tell she's being overridden); what makes a preference "self-defeating" without quietly smuggling in the assistant's own values; and whether her view just collapses into ordinary soft paternalism the moment she grants that a competent user can wave the protection. For at least two of the five, build in a follow-up aimed at the reply she's most likely to give.
