I am a high-school student, and I am trying to decide how much to trust a summary of an idea that I have now met twice — once in a history elective and once in a popular book about the origins of nationalism. The summary runs roughly as follows:

Anderson and Hobsbawm showed that nations are imagined communities, which is to say that they are not real but invented — stories people tell themselves. Hobsbawm in particular demonstrated that national traditions are manufactured: the Scottish kilt and the clan tartans, the pageantry of the British monarchy, and the great national holidays were all fabricated within the last couple of centuries, chiefly by governments and elites who needed to keep ordinary people loyal. Since the nation is therefore a fiction and its supposedly ancient traditions are a lie, nationalism is essentially a fraud; and because nations were invented, they could just as easily be uninvented or replaced by something else.

I suspect the summary is being unfair, but I cannot yet say where the unfairness lies, and I do not want to reject it merely because it sounds cynical. Could you audit it? Please go through it claim by claim and tell me, for each, what a specialist would accept, what they would wince at as an oversimplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation — paying particular attention to any distinctions the summary collapses that the original arguments were at pains to keep apart. I would also like to know whether the concluding inference, from "invented" to "a fraud, and therefore disposable," follows from anything true in the summary, and what the strongest defender of each of the two thinkers would object to. Where the summary is wrong, please give me the accurate version of the claim rather than simply telling me that nations are real; I want the summary corrected, not replaced by its opposite.
