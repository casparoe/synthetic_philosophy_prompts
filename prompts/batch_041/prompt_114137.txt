I'm writing a short piece on Sidgwick's psychical-research phase and I'd like you to tear my notes apart before I draft. Take the role of someone who knows nineteenth-century British moral philosophy cold, and start by brainstorming rather than just agreeing with me — I'd rather find out now if the thesis is thin.

The recieved view treats his involvement with the Society for Psychical Research (founding member, and its first president, 1882) as an embarrassment or a Victorian eccentricity. My claim is that it's continuous with the Methods of Ethics, not a detour from it. Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason leaves rational egoism and rational benevolence both standing, with no proof that they converge; the only way out he can see is something like a theistic postulate — a moral order in which virtue and happiness eventually coincide — and the cheapest empirical component of that postulate is survival of death. So the psychical research is him trying to test, by ordinary canons of evidence, the one factual premise his ethics can't do without. What makes it interesting is that he mostly concluded the evidence didn't hold up: he wouldn't let the hope do the evidential work, so you get a rationalist clinging to a postulate he can neither establish nor drop. His wife Eleanor was doing much of the actual investigating, which raises its own question about whose project it really was.

Think hard about these, and answer as a numbered list:

1. Is the continuity thesis genuinely defensible, or a just-so story that flatters a hobby by making it look systematic? What would count against it?
2. Am I conflating his motives for joining the SPR with the philosophical work survival does in his system? If so, how do I separate them?
3. Is the "he tried to empirically test a Kantian postulate" framing anachronistic or misleading?
4. Where am I most likely wrong on the facts, and which claims are load-bearing enough that I have to check them?
5. What's the one paragraph this piece still needs?

Be blunt — if it's thinner than it sounds, say so.
