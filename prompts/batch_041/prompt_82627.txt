I recently read a short essay by an economic commentator on Malthus, and I would like to know how much of it I should believe. The essay runs roughly as follows. Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population (1798) showed that population, when unchecked, increases geometrically while the food supply can increase only arithmetically, so that the human race is condemned to recurring, and in the end catastrophic, famine. History has not borne this out: the world's population has multiplied several times over since Malthus wrote, yet the mass starvation he foretold has not arrived. Malthus, then, was simply wrong. The one defence still occasionally heard — that he was 'right but early', that the reckoning has merely been postponed — is the standard refuge of a refuted theory: it concedes the evidence and then explains it away by pushing the prediction into an unspecified future. Since no observation could now tell against it, the claim has stopped being science and has become prophecy.

My instinct is that this is too quick, but I cannot quite see where the difficulty lies. Please go through it and separate three things: what a historian of economic thought or a philosopher of science would accept as fair; what is a common simplification that is defensible but needs qualification; and what is straightforwardly mistaken. I want to know, in particular, what a defender of Malthus — or of the 'right but early' move — would say in reply, and whether the essay's closing conclusion, that Malthusianism is unfalsifiable and therefore not science, actually follows from whatever in the essay is correct. I also have a vague recollection that Malthus himself wrote about different kinds of check, and that later editions gave more weight to restraint; I do not know whether that bears on any of this. And I have a half-memory that the reply turns on something about tendency laws and auxiliary assumptions, though I do not trust it; if that is the relevant machinery, please spell it out, and tell me what would distinguish a legitimate postponement of a long-run prediction from an ad hoc rescue of one. Assess the argument on its merits; I am not looking for reading lists or citations.
