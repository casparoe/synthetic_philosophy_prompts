I have no training in philosophy or neuroscience, but I live with chronic back pain and have spent a few years reading popular books about pain while waiting for appointments. One of them, which I have since lent out and can't check, had a chapter on people who after a stroke seem to lose the ability to mind pain. The gist was this. These patients can still feel pain, the author said, they simply do not care about it; he called the condition pain asymbolia and treated it as the key to an old puzzle. Because such a person feels the very same sensation the rest of us call pain and is not bothered by it, pain cannot be bad in itself: the badness must be a separate emotional reaction the brain adds on top, a second alarm wired to the first, and the unpleasantness is therefore not part of the sensation at all but an extra event that can be disconnected from it. He drew two conclusions. First, the old idea that pain is by definition an unpleasant experience is simply wrong. Second, if we could find the switch that asymbolia throws, we could spare people in chronic pain their suffering without blunting their ability to feel their bodies — which, he added, is why drugs acting on the emotional side of pain help some patients whom ordinary painkillers leave cold. He mentioned people born unable to feel pain at all as further evidence that sensation and suffering are separate systems.

I am inclined to believe the lesson, partly because it flatters something I already want to think about my own pain — that the agony is my reaction rather than the sensation itself — and partly because it sounds like hard clinical evidence rather than philosophy. But I have been burned before by popular science, and I would rather know now than repeat this at a dinner table.

Please audit the chapter for me. Go through its claims one by one and tell me which are accurate, which are simplifications a careful clinician or philosopher of mind would wince at, and which are simply wrong — including anything it gets wrong about what the patients themselves are like. Then tell me what the phenomenon, properly described, actually shows. In particular: does it really establish that the badness of pain is an addition rather than something intrinsic to the sensation, and does the practical conclusion about chronic pain follow from whatever is true in the chapter? Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options for me to choose from; if the honest verdict is that the chapter gets the patients roughly right but the lesson wrong, or that the lesson survives only in a much weaker form than the author suggests, say so plainly and tell me why.
