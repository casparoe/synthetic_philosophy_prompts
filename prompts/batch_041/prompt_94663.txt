I belong to a small reading group that has spent the term on population ethics, and a friend has produced an argument that I have not been able to dismiss. What unsettles me is not its antinatalist flavour — several of us are prepared to grant that coming into existence is, other things equal, something to be avoided — but that it reaches a conclusion nobody in the group, antinatalist or otherwise, is willing to accept, while using nothing but the asymmetry that Benatar defends as its engine. He sets it out like this. (1) Coming into existence can be bad for the one who comes to exist, since the life received may contain suffering that outweighs whatever good it holds. (2) Not coming into existence is never bad for anyone, because there is no one thereby deprived; and this is not special pleading about the unborn, since we do not count it a misfortune to have missed the centuries before our conception. (3) More generally, the absence of pleasure counts for nothing where there is no one to enjoy it, whereas the absence of pain counts in favour of a state of affairs even where there is no one who is spared anything. (4) So for any possible person, never existing is either better than, or exactly as good as, existing. (5) Now take someone now alive whose life is, by her own lights, well worth living, and suppose she could be killed painlessly in her sleep tonight. (6) Since non-existence is at least as good for her as existence, her becoming non-existent cannot be worse for her than her continuing; and since the asymmetry tells us that the absence of pain is good even when no one is deprived of it, the killing secures a good — the absence of whatever suffering her remaining decades would have contained. (7) Her absence of future pleasures is no objection, since after the killing there is no one left to be deprived of them. (8) Nothing turns on her being unhappy, or on her not wanting to live, so the same reasoning covers everyone: it would be no loss, and arguably a gain, if every living person died painlessly tonight.

I would rather keep the asymmetry than accept the conclusion, and I suspect that is possible, but I want to know the price. Which step should I reject, and why did it look so plausible? What exactly does rejecting it commit me to? And if the rival diagnosis is that the asymmetry itself has to go, tell me what that costs. I am not after references or a survey of the literature; I want the reasoning itself. Please answer with exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: first the step you would reject; second, why it looked compelling; third, what rejecting it commits me to; fourth, the strongest rival diagnosis and what it costs; fifth, what the repaired position implies about someone in a persistent vegetative state who will never recover and so has no future goods left to be deprived of. Please write in British English.
