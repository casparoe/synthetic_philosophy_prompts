I'm putting together a seminar session that reads One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest alongside Foucault on discipline, and I want a reading that actually uses the machinery instead of name-dropping 'panopticon' and moving on. The ward is a good test case — the schedule, the group meetings, the charts and evaluations that turn the men into cases, the whole apparatus that makes bodies docile — right up until the lobotomy, where sovereign power comes crashing back in. So where does Foucault's account of power/knowledge actually fit, and where does the novel resist it? Kesey clearly wants a real McMurphy, an authentic self, and an outside to power — the Chief escaping into the world at the end reads as a genuine exit, not just another room in the prison — while Foucault insists there's no outside and that resistance is always already inside power. Who's right, or does the book dramatize something the theory can't quite say? And does it matter that the novel reaches us from inside Bromden's fog, a narrator pretending to be deaf, while the Forman film hands us a cooler, more detached camera — does that change what the power/knowledge story even is? Dont summarize the plot; I want the reading to be faithful in both directions.
