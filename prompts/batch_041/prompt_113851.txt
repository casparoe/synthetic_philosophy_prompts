It's from Turing's 1948 report on intelligent machinery, the one he wrote at the National Physical Laboratory, and it goes: "A man provided with paper, pencil, and rubber, and subject to strict discipline, is in effect a universal machine." I have that sentence in front of me, but I don't have the rest of the report handy and I'm definately going on memory for everything around it. What I remember of the context: in that report Turing is interested in machines that start out unorganised and get organised through something like education — training by reward and punishment, some kind of pleasure and pain arrangement — and he compares the whole business to the way a child's mind gets organised by experience. I also half-remember him treating discipline and initiative as two opposed things governing a machine's behaviour, with intelligence needing some of both: a machine that only follows rules is just a calculator, and a machine with no rules at all is useless. Then in the 1950 Mind paper there's the better known suggestion that instead of programming an adult mind you'd do better to build something like a child's mind and then educate it. I'm not asking you to quote any of that back at me; I know I'm fuzzy on the details.

My problem is that these two pictures of education seem to pull in opposite directions, and the quoted sentence is where I feel it most. In the sentence, discipline is what turns a person into a machine: take a human being, subject them to strict discipline, and what's left is a universal machine, something whose behaviour is fully mechanical, of the same kind as any computing machine. But in the child-machine picture, education is what gets you out of the merely mechanical — the thing starts out as less than a computer and education is what makes it intelligent. So is education in Turing's thinking the thing that mechanises or the thing that de-mechanises? And does the discipline/initiative pairing actually resolve this, or does it just rename the problem?
