I chair the grants committee of a small foundation that funds experiments in work-time reduction. Before us is a proposal for a three-year pilot: two hundred low-income workers in a mid-sized city would move to a twenty-five-hour week at full pay, with the freed hours left entirely to their own choosing. The proposal's stated rationale is to test whether material sufficiency plus expanded leisure can produce the good life that Keynes envisioned when he predicted that economic abundance would eventually force us to confront the question of how to use our freedom well. I am sympathetic, but I suspect the pilot may be testing the wrong thing. Keynes's vision was not merely that people would have more free time; it was that they would cease to treat wealth as an end in itself and would cultivate art, friendship, and the enjoyment of life. A pilot that simply pays people for fewer hours may end up measuring consumerism, status competition, and screen time rather than the good life. The design also conflates time affluence with what I would call time sovereignty—the ability not just to have free hours but to direct one's attention and energies toward ends one has reason to value. At the same time, I can see the force of the objection that any attempt to condition the pilot on a particular conception of the good life would be paternalistic and would betray the very freedom the experiment is meant to study. Draft the committee's decision memo. Recommend whether to fund the proposal as submitted, reject it, or fund it with conditions. Engage seriously with the strongest argument on the losing side. Give a definite recommendation and the reasons for it.
