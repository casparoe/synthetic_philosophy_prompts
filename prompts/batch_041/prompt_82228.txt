ok so I'm in a seminar on Hegel right now and we just did the Philosophy of Right material on war, and I can't stop thinking about how it lines up (or doesnt) with the laws of war I did in an IR class last term.

Here's the thing I keep chewing on. International humanitarian law treats soldiers on both sides the same. If you're in the army that started the war and you shoot an enemy soldier who's shooting at you, that's not a crime — you've got combatants privilege — and if you get captured you're a POW with the same rights as the other side's guys. But the people who actually decided to start the war can be prosecuted for aggression (crimes against peace at Nuremberg, and aggression is on the books at the ICC now, at least for states that signed up to it). So the ones pulling triggers get equal treatment no matter whose side is in the right, and the ones who gave the orders get treated unequally. It's the same on the duty side: in most places you can't get out of fighting a particular war on the grounds that you think that war is unjust — that's desertion — so the soldier's own judgment about whether the war is just is legally irrelevant in both directions.

Our prof said Hegel thinks war isn't just an evil to be minimized; it's where the state shows it's the real thing and individuals show they're not, and there's that image about war being like the wind that keeps the sea from going foul, i.e. a long peace rots a people. What I want to know is what the law of armed conflict actually presupposes. Does the combatant's privilege need something like that Hegelian picture — war as a relation between states, where the soldier's moral status comes from his membership and not from the cause? Or is that just one story among several, and a purely functional story (we grant immunity to yours so you grant it to ours; you can't expect a conscript to judge his own government's war; the law has to be simple enough to apply in a firefight) would generate exactly the same rules with no commitments at all about the ethics of war?

So: (a) what does the practice strictly presuppose versus what does it just happen to fit well with, and do the two halves of it — immunity for fighting and the duty to fight — presuppose the same thing? (b) does it presuppose Hegel's positive claim that war is ethically good for a people, or only the state-centered bit about which agents count? (c) if you swapped the presupposed view out for a cosmopolitan one where individual moral status doesn't depend on membership — say the revisionist line that unjust combatants are doing something criminal — what would the law of war have to look like, and would any of it still work?

Also please keep separate what's actually settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested — I don't want a contested reading of Hegel or of IHL served up like everyone agrees on it.
