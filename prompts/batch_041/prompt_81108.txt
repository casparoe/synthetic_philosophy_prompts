I'm a PhD student applying for a small internal summer research grant (2 pages max, the pot is like 4k, so scope matters) and I need a prospectus. Here's my half-formed idea.

The advice to "earn to give" comes in two flavors. One: take a high-paying job in something harmless (software, consulting, whatever) and donate the difference. Two: take a high-paying job in something that actually does harm -- finance, fossil fuels, tobacco, adtech, pick your poison -- and donate the salary. The second version is where I think there's real conceptual work that nobody's nailed down. The defense would be something like: the donation "offsets" the harm of the job, so the net is positive. That's structurally the same move as carbon offsetting, and I want to know whether it survives the same scrutiny.

So the question I want to nail down: under what conditions, if any, does a donation offset the harm caused by the job that funded it? And what would the offsetting relation have to look like to do that work? Does it need additionality -- would the harm have happened anyway if I hadn't taken the job? Does the good have to be the same kind of thing as the harm, or can money cancel a non-monetary harm? Does the donor have to avoid counting the same donation twice, once as an offset and once as their altruistic contribution?

The bit I'm most interested in, and the reason I think this is genuinely open rather than a rehash of the complicity literature: leakage. In carbon offsets, leakage is when your reduction just displaces the emissions somewhere else. The obvious analogue here is that if you vacate the harmful job, someone else takes it, so your counterfactual impact is roughly zero and the offset has nothing to bite on. But there's a weird flip side: if the job would be filled anyway, then arguably you aren't causing the harm at all, which makes offsetting easier rather than harder. I suspect those two thoughts are in tension and that resolving the tension is most of the project.

Second piece, and I think this is where the measurability-bias critique actually earns its keep: the harms in these jobs are diffuse and hard to quantify, while the good you buy with the donation is (or at least looks) crisp and countable, so people will systematically judge the offset by the side they can measure. I want to say something about whether that's straightforwardly a bias or whether it's actually rational to weight measurable goods more heavily when you have to decide under uncertainty.

Method is mostly conceptual. Distinguish the candidate offsetting criteria, build a handful of toy cases where they come apart (especially cases where the harm is near-certain and the good is probabilistic), see which criteria give intuitively right verdicts, then argue for a preferred set. I'd use the carbon-offset criteria as a scaffold and be explicit about where the analogy breaks. No empirical work, no surveys.

What would count as the project working: a clean statement of the conditions, plus at least one case where the standard defense of earning to give clearly fails or clearly succeds, plus a reason to think the measurability point cuts one way rather than the other. What would count as failure: if every plausible criterion gives the same verdict on all my cases, or if I can't say anything about measurability beyond "bias bad." Also flag for me if this is too big for a summer -- I'd rather cut the measurability half then the leakage half if it comes to that.
