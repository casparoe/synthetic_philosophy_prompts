I am a novelist, some way into a book whose spine is a war-crimes trial — principally the Tokyo tribunal, with Nuremberg standing behind it as precedent and as rebuke. I write slowly at the best of times, and I have been slowed to a stop by a single line of dialogue.

One of my characters is a defence lawyer. He is not a fool, and he is not a cynic; he is the most serious person in any room he enters, which is what makes him hard to write. When the court asks how he pleads, he will decline to plead. He will say that these proceedings are victors' justice and that the court has no authority over his client. I first met the phrase in a paperback history of the trials I read at fifteen, and I have met it since in two quite different mouths: people who mean that the trials were a sham, and people who mean that the trials were merely insufficient, since only the losers were ever in the dock. That the same four words serve both is the problem I am asking you to solve.

Give me a disambiguation, laid out as a menu rather than argued as a thesis. Take the phrase as it is actually used about Nuremberg and Tokyo and separate the distinct claims it could be making. Some of the seams I can already see, and would like you to press on: the provenance of the bench (the judges came from the prosecuting powers, and at Tokyo the court was constituted by the occupation authority rather than by agreement among states); the content of the law (whether waging aggressive war was criminal at the time it was waged, and whether a court may apply a law it has itself made); the difference between a retroactive offence, a retroactive mode of liability, and the mere retroactivity of the forum itself; the refusal to accept superior orders or the sovereign act as an answer; selective enforcement (the fire-bombings, the two atomic bombings, the Soviet record, the immunity granted at the top, the industrialists left untried); and the deeper jurisprudential claim that there is no criminal law without a prior sovereign to have enacted it. I am also struck that the victors were not of one mind — the Indian member of the Tokyo bench would have acquitted every defendant, and the Soviet member at Nuremberg dissented against the acquittals — and I want to know what that does to the simplest form of the complaint.

For each item on the menu, give me a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, contentious, or false. Tell me which of them would actually license the act my lawyer wants to perform — a refusal to plead on the ground that the court is not a court — and which would not, since a great many complaints about the trials are perfectly compatible with the tribunal having had every right to try the men before it. Then tell me what residue remains once selectivity is granted and set aside: if the Allies had also tried their own, which readings would survive and which would collapse into nothing more than the observation that the winners wrote the statute? Tell me which of the readings a serious jurist in 1946 would have recognised as an objection to jurisdiction rather than a complaint about the outcome, and which are the inventions of later historians. And tell me, finally, which single reading my lawyer most probably means when he says it, since that is the one that will tell me who he is.

I do not need citations and I do not need a reading list. I need the menu, in prose, with verdicts, and with an eye to which item on it my character is actually holding when he refuses to plead.
