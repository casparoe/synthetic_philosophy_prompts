Okay, I'm putting together a handout for the free will unit I'm TAing this term — third years, they've had one intro metaphysics course and that's it — and I've decided I'm not making them read anything else. What I want from you is a decision tree, a flowchart that fits on one side of A4, which takes someone who walks in already convinced that you're only responsible for what you could have avoided and sorts them into an actual position by the end. Aim for somewhere between eight and twelve leaves.

The whole point is that it sorts people by what they say about the cases, not by which label they've heard of. So every branching question has to be answerable by a student who has no idea where it leads. Asking whether the agent in this particular case is responsible is fine. Asking whether they accept the actual-sequence view is not. No node should presuppose how the previous node was answered, and none should be answerable only by someone who already knows the literature. Build it out of the questions first — the branches, and the combinations of answers they allow — and only then put the names at the leaves. If some combination of answers has nobody sitting in it, say so, and tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Some of the splits I care about, because these are where my students actually get stuck:

The difference between an intervener who would have stepped in but never did, and an arrangement that positively makes the action happen. Half the class treats those as the same case and I dont think they are; I want the tree to to force the choice.

The conditional reading of could-have-done-otherwise — the idea that it just means you would have done otherwise if you had chosen to. In the standard counterfactual-intervener case that conditional comes out false, so I want a node that catches the people who were quietly leaning on it and makes them either drop it or keep it and pay for it.

The minimal-alternative move, where you give up on could-have-done-otherwise but insist some thinner alternative survives — she could at least have chosen otherwise, or tried otherwise, or willed otherwise. I want a node or leaf that makes people see how much weight is being put on something that thin.

The dilemma objection: that a case which guarantees the action only does its job if the guarantee is deterministic, and determinism is the very thing in dispute, while if you set it up indeterministically the guarantee springs a leak. Someone who takes that line shouldn't end up at a leaf that says they accept the case's verdict.

The sourcehood thought, that what matters is where the action came from rather than whether an alternative was available. That deserves its own branch — don't fold it in with the people who simply drop the alternative-possibilities requirement.

And the split between needing only that the action flow from the agent's own reasons-responsive machinery, versus needing a kind of control that consists in being able to do otherwise. If that's the cleanest way to draw one of the branches, use it.

Positions I definitely want somewhere on the tree: the conditional-analysis compatibilist; the one who drops the alternative-possibilities requirement and grounds responsibility in the actual sequence; the hierarchical-desire line, where the agent endorses the desire she acts on; the libertarian who keeps the requirement and says the counterfactual-intervener cases beg the question; the agent-causal libertarian; the hard incompatibilist who thinks nobody is ever responsible and may not care about the cases at all; and the Strawsonian who thinks the whole could-have-done-otherwise question is the wrong question. If two of those land on the same leaf, or your tree genuinely can't tell them apart, flag it instead of manufacturing a distinction. For each leaf, one sentence on the most awkward thing its defender has to swallow.

Two constraints. No citations, no reading list, no names of papers — I can't check them and I don't want a bibliography, I want the tree. And if a node needs a specific case to work, invent one and describe it in your own words rather than gesturing at a famous example I might not have read. Some of the distinctions above I'm half-remembering from seminars, so if I've mangled one, fix it and tell me you did rather than building on my version.

Finally: tell me which branch you're least confident about and what would change your mind about it — what case, counterexample, or argument would make you redraw that split or move a leaf. I'd rather have a tree with your uncertainty marked on it than a confident one I can't argue with.
