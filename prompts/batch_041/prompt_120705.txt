I am trying to state a criterion for when an intentional-stance ascription is true of a system rather than a merely useful shorthand, and my verdicts on cases will not settle into a pattern. Here they are.

- A thermostat: "it believes the room is 18°C and wants it at 20°C." Not true. The design description of a set-point controller already yields every prediction the ascription yields.
- A strong chess engine: "it believes its queen is en prise and wants to avoid the exchange." True. The engine's own search runs over states that carry precisely this information, and the ascription is not a convenience laid over a mechanism that is really doing something else.
- An allele: "it wants to be copied into the next generation." Not true, even though the ascription predicts allele frequencies extremely well. Nothing in an allele distinguishes being copied from not being copied.
- A hurricane: "it wants to make landfall." Not true. The physical description gives everything.
- A firm: "the board intends to acquire Beta and believes the regulators will not object." True, despite there being no single head in which these states reside.
- A bond market: "the market expects a cut." True, and again with no head.
- A slime mold: "it remembers that the food was on the left." I want to say not true, and I have a reason: the mold's thickened tubes reliably encode where the food was and predict its next move, but left is my frame and not the mold's, so the content of the memory is something I supply. My trouble is that the same argument seems to make the market verdict false, since the market has no frame either, and I do not want that.
- A large language model: "it believes Paris is the capital of France." I go back and forth, which I take to be a sign that my criterion is doing no work on the case that matters most.

The test I have been using has two clauses: (i) the ascription must buy compression over the best non-intentional description of the system, and (ii) it must posit states that the system's own causal organization distinguishes, so that the system is sensitive to the distinctions drawn rather than merely correlated with them. Clause (i) seems to dispose of the thermostat and the hurricane; clause (ii) disposes of the allele and, so far as I can see, of nothing else — which leaves the mold, the market, and the language model all true on the test while my verdicts pull them apart.

Give me the principle, or the small set of rival principles, that gets as many of these verdicts as possible, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing which work and what each clause costs. If no single principle gets all of them, tell me which of my verdicts to abandon and why the abandonment should be mine rather than the principle's; if one does, tell me what it costs me elsewhere. Then say what the surviving principle predicts for two cases I have not put to it: an immune system "deciding" that a transplant is foreign, and a self-driving car "thinking" that the pedestrian will cross. Finally, tell me whether a real pattern in Dennett's sense and a true ascription in mine can come apart, or whether I have been pressing a question that the three stances were built to refuse.
