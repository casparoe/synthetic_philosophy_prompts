I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on practical reason, and there's a pattern I keep noticing that I don't have a name for.

Someone deliberates at length over two options, judges them genuinely equal, and then settles the matter arbitrarily — a coin, a whim, seniority, alphabetical order. Nothing new is learned about the options in the settling. Yet a week later the choice no longer looks arbitrary: the person who ended up with option A describes A as the better fit, produces reasons for it that she now treats as the reasons she had all along, and resists the suggestion that B would have done just as well — and she would have said the mirror-image thing had the coin landed the other way. I've watched it with two job offers, with a committee choosing between two indistinguishable candidates (members were arguing the winner's merits minutes after the toss), and with something as trivial as naming a dog by pointing at random and then finding the other names unthinkable. It doesn't look like ordinary post-hoc rationalisation, because the attachment is real and keeps steering action for months; but it doesn't look like the discovery of a reason either, since nothing about the options changed.

People keep pointing me to Buridan's ass, but that seems to be about paralysis and why it needn't happen. Sour grapes I know, and it runs the other way. I have a vague sense that there's a distinction between 'picking' and 'choosing' when the options are equal, but I can't tell whether that is about my pattern or about the separate question of whether arbitrary selection can be rational at all.

Please take on the role of an expert in the philosophy of action and rational choice, and think carefully before you commit to anything. Is there an established concept for this — and if there isn't, I would rather have a coined one than a shrug. A bare name won't help me: what I need is the carving — exactly what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
