I spent about thirty years doing experimental work on magnetic materials before I retired, and since then I've been reading philosophy — mind, metaphysics, whatever the reading group throws at me — and I keep meeting the same fight from a new angle. The one that's been nagging at me is Hofstadter's careenium (I think it's in I Am a Strange Loop, though I read his books out of order so don't hold me to it): a frictionless table covered in tiny magnetic marbles he calls simms, zipping around, occasionally sticking together into big ponderous clumps he calls simmballs, and if you poke the boundary of the whole apparatus the arrangement of the clumps ends up encoding a record of what happened outside. His punchline is that the simmballs shove the simms around just as much as the reverse — that the big sloppy higher-level objects are doing genuine causal work and not merely summarizing the little ones. My gut, as a physicist, says thats backwards: the simms do all the pushing, the simmballs are a compression scheme that's convenient for us and idle in the world, and if you gave me every sim's position and velocity I'd have everything there is. But I've noticed I can't say what the simmball-level story adds, and I also can't say what would show it adds nothing, so I'm going in circles.

So: take the position I'm attracted to — higher-level entities, symbols, patterns, whatever we call them, are not themselves causes; all the pushing happens down at the sim level; the higher-level description is useful to us and does no work in the world — and tell me what would count against it. Not 'Kim's exclusion argument' as a slogan; I want the actual items: which observations or experiments, which cases, which formal results if there are any, which intuitions about what causation is, would have to come out a certain way for me to be wrong, and which of those I should take seriously versus which are the sort of thing people wave around in seminars. In particular, tell me what happens to my view if you take the interventionist account of causation seriously — my suspicion is that it makes higher-level causes real and cheap by fiat, and if that's right I want to know whether the defect is in the theory or in my instinct. I'd also like the other side's own concessions, not just the critics' list: which counterarguments would a committed defender of simmball-level causation admit actually tell against them, and which would they brush off? Rank the lot from fatal to merely awkward. And if it turns out my position can't be falsified by anything at all, say so plainly and tell me whether thats a real problem for a view of this kind or just the price of admission in metaphysics — I genuinely don't know how to feel about that, having come from a field where you can seperate the two. One last thing, which I suspect is the crux: I'd be most convinced by a case where the simmball-level story predicts or controls something that the sim-level story, even in principle with unlimited computing, cannot — and if no such case exists, I'd like to hear that said as directly.
