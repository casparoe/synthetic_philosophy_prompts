I am a philosophy graduate student with some game theory and no laboratory, and I am putting together a proposal for the early-career session of a small interdisciplinary workshop on diachronic rationality. The organizers want a two-page document: a question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer, an argument that the question is genuinely open, a method, and an honest account of what would count as success or failure. I have the beginnings of an idea and need you to turn it into the proposal. Please work without citations or a reading list, since I will supply the references myself, and refer to positions and arguments in words rather than by name-dropping.

The idea, roughly. The dollar auction is the auction in which the highest bidder pays what they bid and takes the prize, and the second-highest bidder also pays what they bid and gets nothing. It is the standard exhibit for escalation of commitment and for sunk-cost reasoning, and it is usually read as showing that people throw good money after bad. But when I work through the structure, the escalation seems to come from the rules rather than from anyone's psychology. Suppose the prize is one dollar and I am the second-highest bidder at ninety-five cents. Quitting locks in a loss of ninety-five cents; raising my bid to a dollar and five cents buys me a real chance of finishing only a nickel down. On that reading, continuing is the forward-looking choice at that node, and the ruinous outcome is a property of the interaction rather than a fallacy in the head of either bidder. That is awkward for the way the case is normally used, and I do not know which of three things I actually want to claim: that the dollar auction is no evidence of sunk-cost reasoning at all; that it is evidence, but only in variants where continuing is not forward-looking rational; or that the usual discussions run these together because they never separate the decision to enter the auction from the later decisions to keep bidding. I would rather the proposal not presuppose that my hunch is right. Part of what I am paying you for is a framing under which I could discover that it is wrong.

Give me the proposal in these six sections, in this order, under these headings.

1. Working title and question, about 150 words. A title that does not oversell, and the question stated so that it has a determinate answer and so that I could be shown to have got it wrong.

2. Why it is still open, about 250 words. Why the standard treatments, described in words, leave the question undecided rather than merely unaddressed, and what exactly they would have to settle for my question to be closed.

3. Approach, about 300 words. What I would actually do, step by step, given one term and nobody to run as subjects. Be concrete about the shape of the work: for instance, whether the thing to do is build a small model of the auction with a stated prize and increment, characterize the region in which escalation is consistent with forward-looking maximization, and then ask what that region does to the psychological reading of the case; or whether the thing to do is conceptual analysis of how a decision problem is individuated across time. Say which you would do, and why that rather than the obvious alternatives of running a new experiment or writing a survey of the existing literature.

4. What a result would look like, about 200 words. The possible conclusions, which considerations would decide between them, and what would count as the project having made progress even if my hunch is refuted.

5. How it could fail, about 120 words. An honest account of the ways this could turn out to be trivial or merely verbal, in particular whether it risks collapsing into a dispute about how to individuate a decision problem, and what would keep it from doing so.

6. Scope and timetable, about 150 words. What can realistically be done in a term and fitted into two pages, what I should leave out, and which part of the argument the workshop audience will press hardest on.

About 1000 to 1200 words in total, in plain prose, written in the first person as me.
