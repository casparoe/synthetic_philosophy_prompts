The question (Part II of the final):

(a) In his 1953 essay "The Methodology of Positive Economics," Friedman is read as claiming that the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic the assumptions. Reconstruct the argument that gets him to that claim, and state what he takes the proper test of a theory to be. (b) An assumption can depart from the facts in more than one way: it can be an idealization, which abstracts away from factors judged irrelevant to the prediction at hand, or it can be a falsification, which asserts something untrue of the target system. Explain the difference between these two, and say whether Friedman's defense of unrealistic assumptions applies to both equally. (c) A macroeconomic model assumes (i) that all agents have perfect information and (ii) that markets clear instantaneously. Assign each of these assumptions to one of the two categories from part (b), and say for each whether Friedman's argument licenses its use.

The student's answer:

Friedman's target is the practice of testing a hypothesis by inspecting its assumptions. His argument, reconstructed, runs roughly as follows. A hypothesis is a device for picking out, from an indefinitely rich situation, the few features that matter for the phenomenon to be predicted. If a hypothesis predicts accurately while using less information than its rivals, then the detail it left out was, by definition, predictively irrelevant; that omission is precisely what makes the hypothesis simple and fruitful, and simplicity and fruitfulness are the two criteria Friedman gives for a good theory. His illustration is the expert billiard player, who does not solve the equations of mechanics in his head but who plays as if he did; on Friedman's view the hypothesis that he computes the angles is a good hypothesis, and it would be a mistake to reject it because the player has never seen a differential equation. The proper test of a theory is thus the accuracy of its predictions, not the realism of its assumptions.

This is what gives the F-twist its force. Because the theories we most admire are the ones whose assumptions are wildest — point masses in mechanics, perfectly maximizing firms in price theory — Friedman concludes that unrealism is not a defect but a mark of significance, and he advises the modeller to drive her assumptions toward the unrealistic rather than the descriptive. The more of reality an assumption discards, the more cleanly it isolates the mechanism that is doing the predictive work.

On part (b), I do not think the idealization/falsification distinction survives contact with Friedman's argument. To idealize is to leave a factor out, and to leave a factor out is to say, implicitly, that the factor is absent; so every idealization is already a falsification. Perfect information, for instance, idealizes by stripping away search costs and informational asymmetry, but in asserting that agents know everything relevant it also asserts something false about any real economy. Since the two categories collapse into one, Friedman's defense covers both, and both of the assumptions in part (c) are licensed: the model is to be judged by what it predicts — say, about the output response to a monetary shock — and if it predicts well, the perfect-information assumption has earned its place no matter how false it is of the agents actually being described.

The one qualification I would add is that Friedman does not treat assumptions as wholly irrelevant, since where two theories predict equally well, simplicity and fruitfulness break the tie, and these are properties of the assumptions.
