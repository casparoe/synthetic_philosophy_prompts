I have long treated the coherence argument for expected utility maximization as close to decisive: an agent whose preferences cannot be represented as maximizing expected utility is, to that extent, irrational, and the money-pump and representation-theorem arguments are what make the claim stick. I would like the case against this view laid out honestly, as a map of its exposure rather than a restatement of it. What should count against it? I am interested in the possibility that the pump proves less than the full axiom set — an agent whose preferences are incomplete rather than cyclic appears unpumpable, so it is unclear where the completeness requirement comes from — in the gap between the representational reading of the theorems and the normative one, and in cases where it is the axioms rather than the preferences that look defective (incommensurable or lexically ordered values; preferences that cycle only over options that can never be held at once, or that a suitably self-binding agent can never be exploited by). Please separate the objections a committed defender should concede from those she can dismiss and on what grounds, rank them from awkward to fatal, note anything the view predicts that its rivals do not, and say whether it has quietly been qualified into a position that risks nothing at all.
