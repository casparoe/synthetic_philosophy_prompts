I’m in a seminar on ancient political theory and have been working through Aristotle’s account of natural slavery in Politics, Book I. I’m comfortable with the broad teleological framework and with the familiar distinction between slavery by law or conquest and slavery by nature, but I keep getting stuck on the argument in Book I, Part II. In Benjamin Jowett’s translation, the passage reads: ‘For that which can foresee by the exercise of mind is by nature intended to be lord and master, and that which can with its body give effect to such foresight is a subject, and by nature a slave; hence master and slave have the same interest. Now nature has distinguished between the female and the slave. For she is not niggardly, like the smith who fashions the Delphian knife for many uses; she makes each thing for a single use, and every instrument is best made when intended for one and not for many uses. But among barbarians no distinction is made between women and slaves, because there is no natural ruler among them: they are a community of slaves, male and female. Wherefore the poets say, “It is meet that Hellenes should rule over barbarians;” as if they thought that the barbarian and the slave were by nature one.’

What I can’t get straight is how the sentences about the female and the barbarian are supposed to function. Is “nature has distinguished between the female and the slave” a claim about two distinct natural functions, or only a remark about the household’s division of labor? Why does the absence of a natural ruler among barbarians show that they are a community of natural slaves rather than that their slavery is merely conventional? And what work is done by the claim that master and slave have the same interest — does it follow from the definition of the slave as the bodily executor of another’s foresight, or is it an independent premise meant to soften the account? I’d like a philosophical reconstruction rather than a summary or a bibliography: please explain the inferential structure, the key terms, and the strongest and weakest readings of the passage. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
