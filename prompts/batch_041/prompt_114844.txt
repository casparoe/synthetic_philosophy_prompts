**Gain, not content**

Everyone in the cognitive penetration debate keeps asking whether thinking something can change what you see. I think thats the wrong question, or at least it's two questions wearing one coat.

Take the Müller-Lyer lines: you know they're the same length, and they still look different. The sceptics use this to say perception is encapsulated, cognition can't get in. Fine. But notice what the knowledge does do — it makes you distrust the percept. Something is being up-weighted or down-weighted, just not the content. Call that gain penetration.

Then take the ambiguous figure. You can't see the duck and the rabbit at once, no matter what you believe. But what you were told five minutes ago pretty reliably biases which one you see first, and once you've seen it, the alternative doesn't come back. So again: no new content, but the signal that wins isn't chosen by the eyes alone.

My claim: content penetration is rare or nonexistent, and the sceptics win that fight. But gain penetration is everywhere, and it's what the interesting cases were really about all along. The mistake is treating the sceptics' victory as a victory for encapsulation, when all they've shown is that the winning signal has to be in the visual format.

Prediction: if I'm right, manipulations that change what you expect should mostly move reaction times, thresholds, and what you report first, and only rarely change what you can report at all. That's testable, and I don't think anyone frames it that way.

Is this just me re-describing attention and calling it a new thing? Thats my worry.
