I am a high school student preparing a paper for my school's philosophy club, in which I reconstruct an argument I believe is in Hume's essays on money and trade. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Money has no use or enjoyment of its own. It is not eaten, worn, or lived in; whatever value it has is the value of the goods it can be exchanged for.
(2) Imagine that overnight every price, wage, and debt in a country doubled, and that every person's holdings of money doubled along with them. No one could then buy anything they could not buy before, and every person's real position would be exactly what it had been.
(3) A change that leaves every person's real position exactly what it was is not something a nation has any reason to care about, let alone something a government should try to steer.
(4) So the quantity of money in a country is, in itself, a matter of indifference. A nation is not richer for its gold, and a nation that loses most of its money is not thereby poorer.
(5) The same reasoning governs trade between nations. If one country buys more from another than it sells, the money it pays out raises prices in the seller and lowers them at home, which redirects demand until the flows reverse of their own accord.
(6) So trade imbalances cure themselves, and tariffs, export bounties, and every other policy aimed at the balance of trade are worse than useless, since they can only disturb a process that would have corrected itself anyway.

Taken together, (4) and (6) seem to commit me to a great deal I cannot accept: that a government can never have any reason to act on the supply of money, that a country whose banks have failed and whose money has evaporated is in no worse a position than one whose have not, that inflation cannot transfer anything between a creditor and a debtor whose contract is written in fixed sums, and that the order in which new money reaches people, the government's contractors first and everyone else later, cannot possibly matter. I am confident all of those are false. I also have a nagging recollection, which I cannot check, that Hume himself thought an increase of money, while it is still being absorbed, can quicken industry before prices have caught up; that looks to me like it should be impossible if (2) and (3) are right.

What I want is a diagnosis. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (2), which strike me as plain arithmetic, though a friend in the club insists (2) is where the trouble lies and I cannot see how arithmetic could be at fault. I am willing to sacrifice (3) or (5) if I must. If the chain is in fact valid and one of my premises is simply false, tell me plainly which false premise is cheapest to abandon and what I lose by abandoning it. If the error is in my reconstruction rather than in Hume, tell me where I have put words in his mouth and what he actually held. I would also like to know which of the possible repairs leaves the rest of the argument intact and which one quietly converts it into a different argument altogether, since I would rather hand in a paper that knows what it is defending than one that patches the hole with a claim I have not examined.

Please answer in continuous prose, without headings, numbered lists, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a long letter. Our club circulates these as essays, and anything in list form reads as notes rather than an argument. I do not need a reading list or citations; I need the reasoning.
