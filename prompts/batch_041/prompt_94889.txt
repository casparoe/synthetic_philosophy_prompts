For some months now I have been arguing on and off with a friend who works in the planning department of our town council, first over a plaque the council is putting up to mark the bicentenary of a mill that closed after a long strike, and more recently over the museum's plan to rewrite the labels in its industrial history gallery. Whenever I press him on whether some particular thing actually happened — whether the strike leaders were arrested before or after the fire, whether the owners really petitioned for troops — he says some version of "history is written by the victors," as if that settled it, and then carries on about how the whole gallery is a story told by whoever is paying for it. I do not think he is simply wrong that the surviving records are lopsided, and I have noticed two things that seem to cut against the way he uses the saying: the best-known account we have of the war between Athens and Sparta was written by an Athenian, and Athens lost; and the defeated side in the American Civil War managed to shape how that war was taught for a century afterwards. But I cannot tell what exactly he is claiming, or why any of it is supposed to mean that we cannot know what happened at the mill.

What I would like is for you to take that one sentence and separate out the distinct precise claims it could be making, arranged from the nearly trivial to the very radical. I want the readings kept apart rather than blended: what it amounts to as a claim about who produced and preserved the surviving documents; as a claim about what gets taught, named, and put on plinths; as a claim about the categories in which any account must be told, including an account written by the losers; and as the flat claim that no version of the past is truer than any other. For each reading, tell me whether it is plainly true, genuinely contentious, or false, and whether it is even the sort of claim that could be true or false in the way it pretends. Then, and this is the part I care about most, say for each reading whether it would actually license what my friend uses the saying for — treating any disputed fact about the mill as unknowable and the question of what happened as one no honest person would ask. Tell me which reading I should assume he has in mind and which one I should hold him to; whether the saying quietly refutes itself when it is offered as a general thesis about history rather than as a complaint about one particular archive; and whether "the victors" is even the right description of whoever usually does the writing, given how much of the record was kept by clerks, monks, and administrators who were neither. I would also like to know what happens to the saying in the cases where the losers did get to write the story, since I suspect it is a half-truth that survives mainly because nobody stops to check it. Lay the whole thing out as a menu I can take back to him, with a verdict on each item.
