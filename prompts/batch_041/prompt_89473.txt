I run a 90-minute session for a graduate reading group in history and history of science, fourteen participants, and I want them to catch themselves writing the past as a march toward the present rather than hear it named as a fallacy. Design a runnable activity on these constraints.

The core is a scoring game in which participants sit as an awards committee that must rank past figures, positions, or research programmes by how much they "contributed to where we are now," justifying each placement in one sentence. Tell me how to build a deck of roughly fifteen cards for this so that the material is genuinely readable both ways — some entries that look like winners in hindsight, some that were central by the lights of their own moment and now look like dead ends — and give me a procedure for generating such a deck in any domain, so I am not relying on participants already knowing the facts. Then specify a twist that changes the present from which they are ranking: they are re-seated as a committee at a different moment, or told the field's current consensus has shifted. The past on the cards must not change, and the re-ranking must move anyway. Include the timeline, the exact scoring and justification rules, the wording of the twist delivered without signalling that it is a trap, and what to do if round two produces no movement, since my worry is that with the wrong cards they will rank by "who turned out to be right" and reproduce the identical order.

Afterwards, a debrief that (a) uses the participants' own written justifications as the evidence, reading back the vocabulary of anticipation, paving the way, and leading up to, so that the teleological connectors are visibly doing the work; (b) maps the two rounds onto the habits of the Whig historian — studying the past for the sake of the present, praising those who anticipated us and treating those who did not as obstacles; and (c) separates the fact that all history is written from some present vantage point from the genuine vice of treating the present as the destination the past was travelling toward, and tells me how to get from "this narrative is present-centred" to "this narrative is false" without sliding into the view that no historical change is cumulative.

Close with what will predictably go wrong the first time, including the failures that are not about the cards — the participant who says the exercise is rigged, the one who refuses to rank, the group that games the scoring rule — and be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce, in particular the difference between a present-centred narrative that is well evidenced and one that merely flatters us, given that my cards carry no evidence. Tell me whether to patch that in the debrief or to leave it visible as the limit of the exercise.
