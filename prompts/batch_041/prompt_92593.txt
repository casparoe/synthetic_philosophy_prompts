I am a software engineer with no formal training in biology or philosophy, and I have been drawn into an argument about behavioral genetics that I cannot resolve on my own. A friend who reads more widely than I do laid out the following case. Every step looks correct to me, yet the conclusion is one I do not believe, and I cannot find the error. (1) The heritability of a trait is, by definition, the proportion of the variance in that trait across a population that is attributable to genetic variance rather than environmental variance. (2) So for a trait with a heritability of 0.8, genes account for 80% of the variance among people in that population and environment for 20%. (3) Adult height and adult intelligence each have heritabilities near 0.8 in the populations where they have been measured. (4) An intervention -- a school, a nutrition program, a tax credit -- can change only the environmental component; it does not rewrite anyone's genome. (5) So the most any intervention could accomplish is to remove the environmental share, roughly 20% of the variance, while the remaining 80% lies outside its reach by construction. (6) Because heritability is a property of the trait, that 80/20 division is a fixed feature of the trait, and the same ceiling therefore applies to every intervention we might attempt. (7) Hence large environmental programs can at best close about a fifth of the distance between people in such traits, and the widespread belief that they could do substantially more is a statistical error. I am content to keep (1) through (4): the definition, the arithmetic, the empirical findings, and the truism about what interventions are able to touch. So tell me which step is doing work it is not entitled to, why that step looks so innocuous beside the others, and what I have to give up in order to reject it -- whether the definition, the findings, or something else entirely.
