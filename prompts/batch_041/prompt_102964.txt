Bit of a strange one, sorry in advance. I was born with pretty severe hearing loss, got by on hearing aids and lipreading for 34 years, and last spring I finally got a cochlear implant. It works, more or less -- I can follow a conversation without staring at peoples mouths now, which still feels like a magic trick. But what I get isn't what everyone else seems to get. Music sounds like a cheap transistor radio. I still can't tell where a sound is coming from. My audiologist says my brain is still learning to use the signal, which, sure.

Anyway at dinner a couple weeks ago two of my friends got into it about this and I've been chewing on it ever since.

Sam: You hear. Full stop. Hearing isn't a thing your ear does, it's a job that gets done -- sound comes in, becomes a signal, the brain reads it as sound. Yours gets done a different way, that's all. Same reason I think a computer that did the same job would genuinely see and feel things. If you say it isn't real hearing because the machinery is different, you're saying only cochleas hear, and that's a weird thing to worship.

Robin: That's cheating. "Same job" is doing all the work there. At what grain? Your implant doesn't do anything remotely like what a cochlea does -- it's electrodes firing in a pattern nothing in nature produces, and your brain had to rebuild itself around it. So either "same job" is loose enough to just mean "gets sound info in somehow," in which case you've let in a smoke alarm, and for that matter a lookup table with every answer prewritten, which nobody thinks hears. Or you make it tight enough that what you do really is a seperate state from what I do, and then you can't use your case as proof that the mind doesn't care about its machinery. You don't get the loose reading when it's convenient and the tight one when I object.

Sam: But if you go tight then no two people hear the same thing, and the same person doesn't hear the same thing twice. Everyones wiring is a bit different. So hearing was never one thing to begin with, which is a strange result for someone who thinks only one kind of thing really hears.

Robin: That doesn't bother me. Take the madman. Stroke victim, same nerves firing in the same pattern as yours when you're in agony -- but he's cheerful about it, he doesn't pull his hand off the stove, he doesn't even dislike it. Is he in pain?

Sam: Of course he is. He just doesn't mind it.

Robin: Then look at what you just did. If he's in pain because of whats firing, you've stopped being a functionalist -- the physical state is doing the work, not the role. And if he isn't in pain, then your view tells you to ignore a man's own account of his own life, and you don't get to turn around and lean on the obvious intuition in the other case. So which is it?

I couldn't referee any of this. Can you go through it move by move -- who's right at each step, and where the other one still has a real point? And then at the end: is there a way to hold both intuitions, that my implant counts as hearing and that the madman is in pain, without contradicting yourself, or does one of them have to go? What are they both missing?
