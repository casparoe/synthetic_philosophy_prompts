I am designing a graduate seminar on indexical uncertainty, and before we work through the competing formalisms I want the specification rather than the theories. Write me the desiderata: what must any adequate account of self-locating credence be able to deliver?

By such an account I mean a rule, or a family of rules, for assigning and updating credences about where and when one is, in cases where the number of observers, or observer-moments, depends on which hypothesis is true. The two familiar candidates are the self-sampling assumption, on which one should reason as if one were a random draw from some reference class of observers, and the self-indication assumption, on which the bare fact of one's existence is itself evidence favoring hypotheses on which more observers exist. I want the criteria a rule has to meet whichever side of that divide it lands on.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a commitment of one camp. The list should cover the standard puzzles, at least Sleeping Beauty (a fair coin, one awakening on heads and two on tails, with amnesia between them) and the Doomsday argument (we should expect to find ourselves somewhere in the middle of the sequence of all humans ever born, and so should expect the end soon). It should cover the presumptuous philosopher, who claims that our existence alone makes a theory positing vastly more observers overwhelmingly more probable, so that a physical question can be settled without running the experiment, and more generally whether a rule may let armchair reflection on observer-counts settle empirical matters. It should cover duplication and branching, where a copy of an agent is made or an agent is split; cases where the reference class is itself in doubt, including nonhuman or artificial observers, and the choice between counting observers and counting observer-moments; cases with infinitely many observers, where the relevant ratios may not exist; and the cosmological cases where the rule is supposed to earn its keep, such as fine-tuning, the scale of the universe, and Boltzmann-brain-type scenarios.

I also want the formal constraints, and I want to know which are genuinely binding: consistency with the probability calculus and with ordinary conditionalization where no indexical uncertainty is present; agreement between the credence one now has and the credence one expects to have later; immunity to Dutch books, including books written by an agent who knows she will lose her memory; and usability, meaning that the rule tells an agent what to believe and do in the middle of a case rather than only in hindsight. Where a rule yields credences that cannot be squared with any defensible decision rule, with betting at even odds in Sleeping Beauty as the obvious pressure point, say what the conflict shows.

Then the part I most want. Identify any subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain why the conflict is forced rather than an artifact of one formalism; if the list, properly pruned, entails that no rule can both avoid the presumptuous philosopher and avoid the Doomsday conclusion, that is a result and I want it stated as one. Tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates, and add one case of your own devising that you think separates the leading rules, together with the requirement it tests.

Close with a brief scorecard, a few sentences each, for the self-sampling assumption, the self-indication assumption, and one position that denies that self-locating uncertainty is a distinctive kind of uncertainty at all. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is for orientation only.

Write in American English. Keep it self-contained: no citations, no reading list, no appeals to what some author is supposed to have said. I want the specification itself.
