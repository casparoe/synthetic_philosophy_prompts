I am a novelist, and I have spent the better part of a year constructing a narrator who, I am fairly confident, never states a single falsehood. Every sentence he utters is literally accurate; whatever deception the book works is achieved entirely through selection, emphasis, and the order in which he releases what he knows, so that a reader who trusts him will assemble a picture of events that is not merely incomplete but wrong. At a reading last month a member of the audience informed me, without much warmth, that I had lied to her for four hundred pages. I said I had done nothing of the kind, and a friend who teaches moral philosophy came to my rescue with an analogy he plainly regarded as decisive: to mislead someone by arranging true statements, he said, is to lying as letting someone die is to killing them. On that view my narrator is not a liar at all but a bystander who merely declined to intervene in the reader's own rush toward a false conclusion. I have been quoting this ever since, and I have begun to suspect it is carrying more weight than it can bear.

Please give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the killer and the killing act, of the one who merely lets die, of the victim and the victim's own bodily contribution to the outcome, of the causal chain, and of the agent's intention — and then tell me where the mapping breaks down. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely bear on the conclusion my friend wanted (that I am not a liar, or at any rate not culpably one) from those that are merely incidental. I am especially interested in whether the reader's active cooperation — she infers, she fills gaps, she wants the story to go a particular way — is a real counterpart to the physiology by which the dying patient dies, or whether that is precisely the point at which the analogy collapses; and in whether the fact that my narrator is a fiction, whom the reader knows to be invented, changes which side of the comparison he lands on.

Finish by saying whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and, if it does not, what analogy would do better. Keep the whole answer under seven hundred words.
