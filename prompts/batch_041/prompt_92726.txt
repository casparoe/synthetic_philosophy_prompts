Ok, I need help with something, and I'd rather get it from someone who actually thinks about this than from another press officer.

I've written about Maxwell's demon four or five times now, and every time I end up with the same tidy paragraph: the demon can't beat the second law because sooner or later it has to clear its memory, and clearing one bit costs at least kT ln2 -- Landauer's bound -- so the entropy the demon "found" gets paid back with interest. Demon dead, information is physical, second law intact, roll credits. At this point I basically believe it. I've been using it in talks as an example of a clean result that kills a clean thought experiment.

But I'm starting to suspect I'm repeating the story because it's the story. So: what would count against it?

Not in the vague sense of science is always provisional. I mean the actual things. What would a working demon have to do, and what's the strongest arguement that the erasure accounting is doing no work at all -- that the demon fails for some completely different reason, like the measurement itself costs something, or the demon's own body heats up, or you're required to count the demon as part of the system so of course the total entropy doesn't drop? That last one bugs me most, honestly. If you settle the question by definition -- the demon is inside the box, so the second law applies to the demon plus everything else -- have you explained anything, or just drawn the box around the answer? A physicist friend told me this is a real objection and not just me being dense, but I'd like to know how serious it is and who has to answer it.

I also want the honest state of play: which parts of this are genuinely contested and which are settled. Where would a committed defender of the standard story say the story is vulnerable? Not the critiques they wave off -- the ones they'd definately concede are real problems. And do the actual experiments that measure the kT ln2 cost test the bound or just illustrate it? It seems like you can measure a cost, find it at or above the bound, and never once test what happens when someone tries to go under it.

Other things I'd want ranked: the fact that on small scales you do see individual runs where entropy goes the wrong way (I've written that up as nothing to see here, it's the average that matters -- is that right, or does it mean the demon is only statistically impossible rather than actually impossible?); whether the reversible-computing result puts the blame on erasure for the wrong reasons, since computation itself can be made dissipationless; and whether anyone seriously thinks the bound is beatable for certain kinds of memory, say when the memory is already correlated with its surroundings. If that's a live loophole, what does it cost you to use it?

Sort it from would genuinely sink the standard view down to awkward but survivable. Tell me what should actually move me, and don't be shy about concluding that the whole thing is well insulated -- and then tell me whether that's a problem for a claim that's supposed to be a law of physics and not just a bookkeeping convention.

No reading list, please. I'm not going to cite any of this, I just want to understand it. And write it in American English.
