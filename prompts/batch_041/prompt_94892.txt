I don't have any academic background in this area, but I've been reading about inverse reinforcement learning -- the idea that you can watch how someone behaves and work backwards to the preferences or hidden score they're maximizing -- and I keep running into a conceptual worry I'd like to see two people argue about. Could you write a dialogue between two equally competent proponents of opposing views on whether this kind of preference-inference is really possible? One of them is an optimist: given enough demonstrations, a good model of the environment, and a sensible prior over possible preferences, we can recover what the person actually wants. The other is a skeptic: behavior only ever reveals what someone chose from the options they had, and the rest is what I've been calling a preference shadow -- a shape cast by the menu rather than a fact about the person. I'd like the dialogue to center on a case I've been turning over. A person always takes the same route to work, and a learning system watching them concludes they strongly prefer that route. But the other two routes are blocked by a gate that only opens for residents, and this person is not a resident. They never had the option. The optimist says a good model of the environment would include the gate, so the inference is fine. The skeptic says even with the gate in the model, indefinitely many preference orderings fit the same behavior: maybe they would take route B if the gate opened, maybe they would still take route A, and nothing in the data settles it.

I want them to press each other on the hard questions: does more data help, or does it only multiply the constrained choices? What happens if we observe them once when the gate is open -- is that enough, or is it a different context with its own constraints? Is there a fact of the matter about what they prefer when they have never had the chance to choose otherwise, or is preference only defined relative to a choice set? And if the latter, what does that mean for using these methods to infer human values? Please make the dialogue a real back-and-forth, with each character responding to the other's actual points rather than talking past each other. They can invent small examples and thought experiments, but I don't want them citing papers or reading lists -- just reasoning it out. End with them identifying at least one remaining disagreement and one point where they realize their dispute was partly about words.
