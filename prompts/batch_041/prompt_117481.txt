I'm in a reading group on higher-order theories of consciousness and a friend handed me this argument. I think it's a dilemma and I can't find the exit.

(1) A mental state is conscious just in case its subject has a suitable higher-order representation of it — a thought to the effect that they're in that state.
(2) Now ask whether that higher-order representation is itself conscious. If it is, then by (1) it needs a further higher-order representation of it, and that one needs one too, and so on. So if the higher-order representation has to be conscious, no state is ever conscious and the theory is false.
(3) So the theory has to say the higher-order representation can be unconscious. But an unconscious representation of a state is just a bit of information processing that registers that state, and thermostats and engine controllers register their own states. So every system with any self-monitoring at all has conscious states, and the theory proves far too much.
(4) Either way it's dead. The only escape is to give up (1) and go back to a first-order theory.

I'd like to keep (1) — it's the only story I've heard that explains why a state should be conscious at all rather than just causally active — and I don't think "go first-order" is as cheap as he makes it sound. One of us runs the dispositional version, where the higher-order state is just a standing disposition to judge that the first-order state is present; that version gets to skip the first horn by construction, but I can't tell if it just makes the second horn worse. So: is this a real dilemma, or is something sliding between the steps? If it's sliding, which step, and what does it cost me to fix it? I'm also curious wether the fix has to say what makes a higher-order state the right kind, and whether there's a way to say that which isn't circular or just "whatever representation happens to do the trick."
