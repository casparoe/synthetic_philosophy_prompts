I would like you to act as a political philosopher with a specialist's grasp of property theory, land-use regulation, and democratic theory, and to help me adjudicate a disagreement I witnessed but could not settle. Think carefully before you answer: for each move below, work out precisely what is being claimed and what would have to be true for the claim to succeed, though I do not need to see that preliminary sorting in the response itself.

Last month I attended a planning-commission hearing on a proposal to replace a single-family house on my block with a four-unit building. The block is a five-minute walk from a transit stop, the city's own housing plan designates the area for exactly this sort of small multifamily infill, and there is already a four-unit building on the corner that has stood there since the 1920s. The new units would be market rate, and the developer has offered three of them at below-market rents for the first ten years as a community-benefit package. Afterwards, two acquaintances of mine continued the argument in the parking lot: Ruth, who has owned a house on the block for thirty years, and Dani, who has rented an apartment on the same block for two. I stayed out of it and have no settled view. What follows is the exchange as well as I can reconstruct it.

Dani: The city says it is short thousands of units, and this lot sits on a bus line. A district zoned for nothing but single-family houses is a legal fence around a piece of the city's land. Every exclusion has its reasons, and the reasons are almost always about keeping the wrong people out; the only novelty is that the wrong people are now specified by price instead of by covenant. And the character of this block is not what you think it is — there has been a fourplex on the corner for a hundred years.

Ruth: Nobody here is trying to keep anyone out. What we are trying to keep is a say. The city did not ask us; a developer bought a lot, and now we are supposed to feel grateful for the chance to comment on it. People keep invoking the right to the city. I have always understood that phrase to mean that the people who actually live somewhere get to decide what it becomes, rather than the people who own the capital. If that right means anything, it has to include the right not to have your street rebuilt around you by someone who will be gone before the paint dries.

Dani: That is the inversion I cannot accept. If the right to the city belongs to the inhabitants, it belongs to all of them — including the ones who cannot get in, the ones who would live here if there were room. Your version makes it a right of the people who arrived first. It is a right to the city for those who already have the city.

Ruth: And yours is a right to the city exercised on behalf of people who are not here, by people who are also not here — the developer, the planners, the state. Someone has to have standing, and the only ones with a real stake in this block are the ones who will still be here in ten years. The renter down the hall will be gone in two. Why should his landlord's profit be counted as his interest?

Dani: Because the renter who would move into this building will also be gone in two years, and he needs somewhere to be leaving from. And notice what your principle amounts to: whoever arrives first sets the terms for everyone who arrives later. That is not a commons; that is a club with a waiting list. A commons is governed by rules that the users make together. A club is governed by rules whose purpose is to select the members. You keep using the language of the first while defending the second. I am not calling you a NIMBY; I am saying that the argument you are making is the argument that always wins, everywhere, and that is why nothing gets built.

Ruth: Then let the users make the rules together — including me. That is what a hearing is supposed to be. And do not tell me the block is a club when the people who want in are being selected by price rather than by us: at market rate these units go to whoever can pay, and the below-market ones are a concession the developer can withdraw the next time he needs a favour. What I refuse is being told that my objection is illegitimate on its face. You treat this block as a supply of units. It is also where I know my neighbours and where I have lived for thirty years. Price that at zero and of course your arithmetic comes out in favour of building.

Dani: I do not price it at zero. I say it cannot be a veto, because a veto held by whoever arrived first produces a city that admits only the already comfortable. If "I have been here thirty years" is a reason to stop this, then it is a reason to stop everything.

Ruth: And "the city needs housing" is a reason to approve everything. At least mine is a reason a person can be wrong about, and can be argued with, in a room where she has to live with the outcome.

I have gone back and forth on this and cannot decide, and by the end I suspected they were no longer arguing about the same question. Please go through the exchange move by move — in prose, not in a list — saying who has the better of it at each step and why, then say who is closer to right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing; in particular, tell me whether there is a distinction available that would dissolve part of the disagreement rather than merely relocate it. State explicitly how confident you are in the verdict as a whole and in each of the individual judgments, and single out the one point you are least sure of. Write this as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, no numbered lists, no bold text, no tables.
