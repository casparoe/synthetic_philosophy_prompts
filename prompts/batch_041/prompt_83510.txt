OK so I'm a novelist — 40,000 words into a book, and I've gotten myself stuck on a philosophical problem I did not sign up for. The scene: my character Marisol has spent eight months arranging to hand her sister's business over to a man who will gut it, and on the night it happens she does it entirely on her own. No pressure, no threats, she just does it. But her best friend had quietly set things up so that if Marisol had waivered, she would have been forced to go through with it anyway — the friend's arrangement never fires, because Marisol never waivers. So she did it on her own, and yet she couldn't have done otherwise. My first thought was that she's obviously responsible: she chose it, nobody made her. Then I read about the Frankfurt cases and thought, great, that settles it, some philosopher already did my homework. Except the more I poke at it the more I think the whole thing runs into a wall. Either the friend's arrangement makes a difference to what Marisol does or it doesn't. If it makes a difference, then she wasn't acting freely in the first place, and the case proves nothing about whether she could have done otherwise. If it makes no difference, then the friend and her whole scheme drop out of the story and what I'm left with is just the bare claim that a person can be responsible for something she couldn't have avoided — which seems like it needs an argument of its own, not a counterexample. A friend of mine said maybe the fix is that it isn't the whole action that has to be up to her, only some smaller piece of it, the deciding or the trying, even if the outcome was rigged from the start, and I honestly can't tell if that's a real escape or just moving the goalposts.

What I want from you: put on your philosopher-of-action hat and give me the most important implications of this debate for me — not a survey of the literature, but the handful of consequences that should actually change how I write this scene and what the book is allowed to say about whether Marisol is to blame. Take a side: does the Frankfurt-style case really show that blame doesn't require the ability to have done otherwise, or does the dilemma above sink it? If it sinks it, tell me what survives and what I have to give up. Think this through carefully before you answer — start by brainstorming the candidate implications, then ruthlessly narrow to the ones that matter most, ranked, with your reasons for putting them in that order. Be committed and specific rather than even-handed; if you're genuinely torn, say which way you lean and why, and flag the one place you're least confident. I don't need sources, I need a position.
