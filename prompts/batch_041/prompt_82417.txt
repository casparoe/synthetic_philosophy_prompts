I am preparing a graduate seminar on statistical inference and the replication crisis, and I want to subject a pedagogical analogy to a proper philosophical audit. A colleague in the statistics department routinely introduces the p-value as follows. A p-value is like a smoke alarm: it does not tell you the probability that the house is on fire; it tells you how surprising the current reading would be if there were no fire. A low p-value is an alarm sounding; a high p-value is silence. The colleague then extends the analogy to the replication crisis. An underpowered study is a cheap alarm that misses many real fires. A literature full of significance tests is a building full of alarms, some of which will sound even when there is no fire. And the fact that only the alarms that sound get published is like a fire department that records only the calls it receives. The moral drawn is that we should not trust published findings until we think in Bayesian terms about base rates and diagnostic accuracy, and that frequentist methods are therefore partly responsible for the replication crisis.

Please audit this analogy. First, set out the correspondence explicitly: what plays the role of the fire, the smoke, the alarm, the alarm threshold, the false-positive rate, the sensitivity, the prior probability of fire, the independence of alarms, the building, the fire department, and the publication record? A table is welcome. Second, identify where the mapping breaks. Be precise about the statistical details: the null hypothesis as a point hypothesis and the composite alternative; the uniform distribution of p-values under the null; the dependence of power on effect size; the difference between a p-value and a likelihood ratio; the distinction between a test's Type I error rate and the false discovery rate; the problem of multiple comparisons; researcher degrees of freedom; the fact that alpha is chosen by the researcher rather than fixed by a manufacturer; and the fact that hypotheses are not randomly sampled from a population with a known base rate. Third, separate the disanalogies that matter for the colleague's intended conclusion from those that are merely colorful. Fourth, assess whether the conclusion about the replication crisis survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether the analogy supports the stronger claim that frequentist methods are inherently defective or only the weaker claim that they are easily misused. Finally, if the analogy is worth keeping in teaching, propose a better version of it, or say what should replace it. I am interested in the conceptual structure, not in a literature review.
