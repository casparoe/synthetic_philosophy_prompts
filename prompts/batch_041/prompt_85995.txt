I would like a case of moral luck built to specification, together with a demonstration that the case does what it was designed to do.

The case is meant to serve as a test of whether three familiar accounts of responsibility can be held together or whether one of them has to be given up. Each account is stated here only in outline; work with it as you understand it. The control principle says that blameworthiness reaches only as far as what was up to the agent. The accountability account says that being to blame and owing something — an explanation, an apology, a repair — are two different things, so that an agent can owe without being blameworthy. The agent-regret account says that an agent who has done a bad thing may fittingly be distressed at having done it even where she is not to blame for it.

Specifications.

1. Two agents, A and B, do the same thing, for the same reasons, on the same information, with the same care and the same quality of will. Nothing about their deliberation, their character, or what was in their power differs. The one difference between them is a single fact about the world, F, which neither caused, foresaw, or had any reason to inquire into, and against which neither could have guarded at reasonable cost.

2. F is not a difference in damage. No one is injured or killed and nothing is destroyed in either branch, and the case must not turn on how bad the consequences were. F determines instead whether what A and B did counts as a wrong at all: in one branch the agent has done something that a strict control theorist must still call a wrong, and in the other branch the same act, under the fullest true description, contains nothing that any plausible account calls a wrong.

3. The wrong must be of a kind ordinary morality already recognises — a promise not kept, a thing used without the consent that was needed, a place entered without permission — and not something you have defined into existence for the sake of the case. Nor may the case turn on the agents being culpably ignorant: neither had any reason to look into F.

4. The setting is ordinary — a workplace, a school, a friendship, a household, a street — and the scenario itself is one paragraph. No drunk driving, no trucks, no trolleys, no operating theatres, no deathbeds, and none of the examples that already do this work in the literature.

5. The case must be one in which the three accounts give three different answers to the question of what, if anything, morally distinguishes A from B: the control principle answers that nothing does; the accountability account answers that what they owe differs; the agent-regret account answers that what it is fitting for them to feel differs. These answers must come out of the case, not be written into it.

Then, in order:

- Give each account's verdict on A and B and derive it from that account's own commitments. The pattern to aim at is equal blameworthiness, unequal debt, and unequal fittingness of regret. If you cannot reach that pattern without rigging the case, say where it breaks and what you had to change.
- Identify the one feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what becomes of all three verdicts when that feature is removed — in particular whether the case then collapses into the ordinary asymmetry of outcome luck, in which the unlucky agent is merely the one who caused more harm.
- Give the strongest reply available to someone who thinks the case shows that moral luck is real after all: the objection that the extra burden the unlucky agent carries, the debt and the regret, is itself a form of blame, so that the control principle has not been saved but only relocated.
- Finish with your own assessment: which of the three verdicts was hardest to produce, whether the case genuinely produces it or whether you had to lean on the specifications to get it out, and whether the case survives the complaint that, since one act was a wrong and the other was not, the two agents simply did not do the same thing.

Do not build the verdicts into the scenario. If the case yields the target pattern only because you have quietly made one agent careless, or because the wrong in question exists by stipulation, it fails.
