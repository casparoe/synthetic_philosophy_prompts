I write the ideas column for a general-interest science monthly, and for a themed issue on the way philosophers' toy problems keep escaping the seminar room, my editor has asked for something we almost never run: a straight-faced institutional document. What I want commissioned is a parody exposure draft — the numbered pronouncement a standards board issues when it proposes new rules for financial reporting — except that the thing being standardized is confirmation. Specifically, the recognition and measurement of evidential support, with Hempel's raven paradox as the test case the draft cannot stop tripping over. Every board member, staffer, and commenter should be invented; the document should carry a number, a date, a scope paragraph, a basis for conclusions, an effective date that has been postponed more than once, and a comment deadline. Nobody in it may acknowledge that anything is funny. The humor has to be load-bearing: the draft should read as absurd precisely because the accounting logic is the logic of the paradox, so a philosopher of science can audit it clause by clause and find the rules faithful, while a reader who has never heard of the paradox should still come away knowing what it is.

In the body, work the issues the way a standards board would have to. State the instantiation rule — a thing that is F and also G supports the claim that all Fs are Gs — and then the equivalence condition, which forces the board to book a black raven and a white shoe under the same line item, since each is an instance of a formulation of the same hypothesis. Take up materiality: the white shoe's contribution is genuine but vanishing, so the board must decide whether de minimis confirmations may be netted against the cost of making the observation, and whether a shoe-watching program may be reported as ornithological research. Add a transition provision for evidence already in hand before the hypothesis was formulated, rendered as a prior-period adjustment; a clause on irrelevant conjunction, under which evidence booked against a hypothesis is automatically booked against that hypothesis conjoined with any unrelated claim; and, if it fits without cracking the deadpan, a provision for predicates that apply to an object depending on when it is examined. Include an illustrative ledger with numbered entries — it should contain at least one item that is a joke to the reader and merely a line item to the board — and an excerpt from a comment letter in which an invented professor argues, correctly, that the board ought to recognize likelihood ratios rather than instances, followed by a written response from the board that politely misses the point. The board should be on its fourth exposure draft with no resolution in sight: the endless-resolutions industry, rather than the paradox, is what I am after.

Around 800 words, in numbered clauses, with a footnote or two if the register invites them. Invent the board, its coinages for the principles, and the commenters; don't cite real papers, don't attribute positions to real people, and don't let the piece end by declaring the puzzle solved.
