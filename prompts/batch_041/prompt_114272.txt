I serve on the actuarial review panel of my state's insurance department, and I have to circulate a written opinion before our hearing on the 14th. The filing before us is a homeowners rate increase from a mid-sized carrier that writes heavily along the wildland–urban interface. The company prices wildfire risk by blending three things: its own twenty years of loss experience, weighted 60 percent; a vendor's catastrophe model, which simulates a hundred thousand synthetic fire seasons from fuel, wind, and defensible-space data, weighted 40 percent; and, on top of that, a 12 percent model-risk loading that the filing justifies with two paragraphs of narrative — in substance, the company's judgment that the vendor's model understates the tail. Separately, the company wants to move a quarter of the rate onto a proprietary wildfire hazard score, sold by a third-party vendor, that grades each property from 1 to 10. We are not shown how the score is built; the company says its contract forbids disclosure. Our own rules require that rates be neither inadequate nor unfairly discriminatory and that they rest on actuarially sound methods, and neither of those phrases is defined anywhere in our materials.

Two of us are ready to vote against both the loading and the score on the ground that neither can be tested against anything: if the vendor's model is wrong, no one will ever learn it from the loading, and if the score is wrong, no one outside the vendor can tell. The company's reply is that everything in the filing is like this — its twenty-year loss record is one sample of one possible history, the cat model's synthetic seasons never happened, and the 60/40 weight is itself a judgment — so a line that excludes the 12 percent loading while admitting the 40 percent weight is arbitrary. The company also points out that the score tracks defensible space, which tracks parcel income in this county, so rejecting it means the poorer, more exposed parcels get subsidized by the wealthier, safer ones — and that if we make the rate inadequate, the carrier will simply stop writing in the county, which is worse for exactly the households the two of us want to protect. I think the company's reply is too quick, and I cannot say why.

That is because I spent most of a four-hour drive last weekend arguing about precisely this with a friend of mine, a physicist who now builds catastrophe models for a living, and I lost. Her claim was that every number in the filing is constructed: a probability is a summary of somebody's information, whether that somebody is an actuary with twenty years of claims or a modeler with a simulation, and the only real difference between the loading and the loss record is how easily we can check it. My claim was that there is a difference between a frequency and a guess — that the loss record is anchored to something that happened and the loading is not — and that if we cannot say which of the two is closer to the truth, then the phrase actuarially sound in our own rules is doing no work at all. She asked me which twenty years I would have picked if the company's history had been a run of quiet fire seasons, and I had no answer.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate. I need a bottom line the panel can vote on — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with conditions specific enough to be written into an order: what the company must file, by when, and what follows if it does not. Our window closes at the end of next month, so build the memo around a schedule rather than an aspiration. Give the argument I lost a fair hearing, since it is the argument the company will make at the hearing and the one none of us has answered, and say honestly what the strongest case is for the consumer advocate who wants the score struck entirely.

Tell me how confident you are in the recommendation and what would change it. And if I have the question wrong — if what counts as actuarially sound turns on the purpose our rules were written to serve rather than on what a probability is, and the philosophy of probability is a distraction from a question of regulatory purpose — then say so plainly instead of writing the memo as though my framing were the right one.
