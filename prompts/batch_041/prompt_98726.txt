For a long feature I'm writing on the state of the mind–body problem, I sat in on a small workshop where a philosopher of mind and a cognitive neuroscientist spent an afternoon circling one question: when a belief makes a difference to what someone does, is the belief itself doing any causal work, or is it riding on the neural events that realize it? Their exchange went several rounds, both of them landed real blows, and I came away genuinely unable to say who had the better of it. I'd like you to adjudicate it move by move.

They anchored the dispute in a concrete case. A patient in a trial reports that her chronic pain eases after a sham injection, and the two disagreed about what caused the relief. (1) The philosopher — call her the exclusionist — said that whatever the patient believes, there is some neural event that realizes that belief and is by itself sufficient for the endogenous response that produces the relief; because that neural event is sufficient, the belief is at best redundant, and because the belief does not cause the neural event that realizes it — it is fixed by it, not the reverse — the belief contributes nothing, so the relief is caused by the brain and the belief is a passenger. (2) The neuroscientist replied that this proves far too much: the same reasoning shows the neural event is redundant because the molecular events beneath it are sufficient, and those redundant because the atomic events are, so that either everything short of fundamental physics is a passenger or the argument is broken somewhere — and if it is broken for neurons and molecules, it is broken for beliefs; she added that we routinely cite causes at many levels without treating each level as a rival, so the belief is a real cause at the psychological level. (3) The philosopher answered that "levels" is doing all the work and none of it is spelled out: unless a higher-level property has causal powers of its own it is a redescription, and redescriptions do not cause — otherwise every true description of a cause would itself be a cause, and we would be committed to an explosion of causes. (4) The neuroscientist then offered a criterion: a factor is a cause just in case intervening on it would change the effect while other things are held fixed, and we can intervene on what the patient believes — vary the belief and the relief varies — so the belief qualifies by the very standard the special sciences already use. (5) The philosopher said this only equivocates, since on her opponent's own view intervening on the belief just is intervening on its neural realizer, so nothing has been shown to do independent work, and the criterion also admits any difference-maker, however incidental. (6) The neuroscientist tried once more: the neural story is too specific, packing in firing details that have nothing to do with the relief, whereas "believing the treatment works" is exactly the right grain. (7) The philosopher replied that a preference for the right grain is a preference about explanation, not about what causes what, and that it makes causation depend on what we happen to find salient — a steep price. (8) The neuroscientist came back: fine, then name the level at which the regress stops and why it stops there, and don't simply stop wherever your ontology happens to be convenient.

Please work through their exchange and tell me who is right at each step, not just who wins overall. For each of the moves above, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating; note any move that only looks like a reply because it shifts what is at issue. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and give me a bottom line on which of them has the stronger overall position — including, if you think it is the case, that they are talking past each other rather than genuinely disagreeing.
