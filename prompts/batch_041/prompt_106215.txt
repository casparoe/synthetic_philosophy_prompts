I am closing out a graduate seminar on Being and Nothingness, and for our final meeting I would like to end with a short poem rather than another round of exegesis. The chapter that gave us the most trouble was the one on bad faith, largely because the natural paraphrase — lying to yourself — keeps collapsing the moment you press on it: an ordinary lie presupposes a deceiver and a deceived who can be kept apart, whereas here the same consciousness is doing the deceiving and undergoing it, so the deception can never be completed once and for all but has to be re-established at every moment; Sartre's point is that this is not a defect of bad faith but its very structure. The other thing we kept losing hold of is the oscillation. The for-itself is never simply identical with what it is, and bad faith plays both sides of that — now treating a person as a fixed thing with a nature that settles what he is, now denying that any of it belongs to him at all — and Sartre is insistent that the person who prides himself on being sincere, who declares that he is exactly what he is and nothing more, has not escaped the circle but merely entered it from the opposite side. I want the poem to enact that oscillation rather than describe it.

I am asking for a pantoum of four quatrains. The form is the argument: in a pantoum the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing stanza folds the opening back into itself — the second line of the last stanza is the third line of the first, and the last line of the poem is the first line of the poem — so that nothing is ever said once and finished with. Use that recurrence the way bad faith uses it: the words of a line should stay exactly the same on their return, but the stanza they return into should force them into the opposite register, so that a line first heard as a claim of identity is heard the second time as a disclaimer of it, or the reverse. A reader who knows the text should be able to point to the recurring line that carries the facticity and the one that carries the transcendence, and to say why the closing stanza had to bring the poem back to its own first line rather than resolve anything.

Please keep the content accurate enough that someone who has taught the book would not wince, and please resist the obvious illustrations — the waiter in the café, the woman who leaves her hand in another's, hypocrisy, and self-deception in the everyday sense — since I can get those from any summary and they would not earn the poem its place in the seminar. What I want instead is the structure: the impossibility of the ordinary lie when deceiver and deceived coincide, bad faith as a belief that quietly withdraws its own warrant the instant it is asserted, and the sincerity trap, which is the part my students found hardest. It would help if one recurring line were a plain-sounding self-report that turns out, when it comes around again, to be doing something else entirely.
