I spent thirty-five years doing bench science, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy — badly, but enthusiatically. Right now I'm stuck on the old result about group accuracy: the more voters you have, each one even slightly more likely than not to get a yes/no question right, the closer the majority supposedly gets to infallible. My measurement instincts won't let it go, because in the lab I never once met two readings that were genuinely independant, and I keep feeling the theorem is quietly smuggling in assumptions no real group satisfies. I don't want another confident summary — I want a proper spec, and one I can actually use, because my real question is when I should trust a large anonymous crowd's verdict (a jury, a refereeing panel, a prediction market, the internet's opinion of the day) and what I'd need to know about it first.

So write me the desiderata an adequate theory of collective judgment has to meet. I mean the whole list: the cases it has to get right (voters who are correlated because they all read the same sources, voters whose competence isn't a fixed property but shifts with what the neighbours are saying, a handful of genuinely expert voters drowned in alot of mediocre ones, voters with a stake in the outcome), the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse (between the chance the majority is right and the size of the winning margin; between a voter being wrong and a voter being biased in a consistent direction; between a group being accurate and a group being legitimate), and the formal facts it has to live with — what actually happens to the theorem once you relax independence, or let competence vary across the group, or let the crowd split into two camps each slightly better than random but pulling opposite ways. For each requirement give me the case or result behind it, and say which ones you think are genuine demands of the subject and which are just the prejudices of one camp. A table would be fine.

Then the part I most want: tell me which of these requirements can't be satisfied together. My hunch is that the ones that make a group track the truth (independent, sincere, individually competent, roughly equal voters) pull against the ones that make it fair or legitimate (every vote weighed the same, majority rule come what may), and that you can't max both at once. Say whether that's right, which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate theories, and whether any contender on the table actually survives the whole list or they all die somewhere. And please skip the reading list — I can't look anything up and I don't want a bibliography, just the criteria and your reasoning.
