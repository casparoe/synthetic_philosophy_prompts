I teach a second-year epistemology unit and I'm tired of the closure lecture turning into a roll-call of names, so I want to hand out a decision tree instead that takes a student from their own verdicts on some cases to a named position on epistemic closure. The point is that they find out what they already think before I tell them who thought it.

Make every internal node a question a student can answer without knowing where it leads -- a verdict on a case, or a commitment about what knowing something amounts to -- and every leaf a named view. The leaves I need to come out somewhere: Nozick-style tracking (sensitivity), safety-based accounts, relevant alternatives, contextualism, the Moorean line that we know the mundane thing so the skeptic's premise must be false, dogmatism about perceptual justification, and whatever hinge / framework-certainty view you think has earned a leaf. But build the branching out of the logical space of closure principles rather than out of the name list: single-premise closure under known entailment, closure under known implication, multi-premise closure (the step where two separately known premises get conjoined -- the lottery cases live there), and the seperate question of whether warrant actually transmits across an entailment or whether it's just that knowing the premise can't sit alongside ignorance of the conclusion. The names should land wherever the answers put them.

Cases I want nodes to turn on: the zebra that might be a cleverly painted mule, the county full of barn facades, a stopped clock that happens to read the right time, a lottery ticket you own, the brain in a vat, one case where someone knows P and knows Q and the question is whether they know the conjunction, and one node on the "I know I have hands but I don't know I'm not a brain in a vat" style conjunction -- is that a contradiction, a plain falsehood, an unhappy truth, or evidence that one of the words is doing something different here?

Two extras. Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't seperate with those cases, and say why they collapse. And where the tree leaves a cell empty -- a combination of answers nobody actually defends -- say whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

About a page and a half, please. Start with the tree itself: no preamble, no musing about how hard taxonomies are, and don't restate what I asked for.
