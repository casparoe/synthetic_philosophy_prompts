Ok so I need help. I'm a novelist — four books, the last two historical — and somehow I'm now teaching a grad seminar on historical fiction at a writing program, and I have to write the final exam myself. 90 minutes, in class, no notes. The week I always end up fighting about is the one on writing real people, so that's where I want the question to live, and I want it to be an essay question with a model answer and a marking scheme I can hand my TA, who is a poet and has never thought about any of this.

Here's the knot. I tell my students: be true to the person, not the paper. What I mean is don't let the archive push you around — if the letters read brisk but the diary reads lonely, go with the loneliness even when it isn't documented, because you're writing a person, not a file. Fine. Then last term a student does this: she's writing about a real woman who ran a charitable foundation in the 1880s, described by everyone as tireless and beloved, and the student gives her a secret contempt for the poor that secretly drives the whole philanthropy. Invented from nothing, not a scrap of evidence, because it "made the character cohere." And I'm sitting there thinking that's a lie about a real woman, and also that by my own rule I'm not allowed to say so, since I'm the one who told them the paper doesnt get a veto. I want the exam to march them straight through that.

So: write me the question, plus a model answer of about 600 words, plus a marking scheme. The question should make them (a) say what my rule could possibly mean if it isn't just a license to invent whatever's convenient, (b) deal with the obvious objection that you can't do wrong to a dead woman because dead people can't be harmed, (c) say whether their answer changes if she were totally obscure instead of a name in the papers, or if she had grandkids alive today, and (d) name the best objection to whatever they land on.

For the marking scheme, 25 marks, and make it actually seperate the A's from the B's — because every B answer is going to be some version of "it's a balance between accuracy and artistic license," and I will lose my mind. So name the specific moves a top answer has to make, how many marks each is worth, and for each one the mistake that should cost them.
