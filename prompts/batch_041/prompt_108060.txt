I'm a second-year PhD student in economic history, and for the past year I've hosted a small dinner after my department's Wednesday seminar, mostly so that the visiting speaker and three or four of us can keep arguing after the chair calls time. Last week the argument outlasted the food by a good hour and a half, and I've spent the days since trying to reconstruct it as faithfully as I can. The two principals were Ana, a development economist, and Ruben, a historical geographer — both careful people who distrust slogans, which is why the argument was worth listening to. They fell into it over whether we can ever really know why some nations are rich. I'm revising for a field exam that covers exactly this material, and I've noticed that every time I reread my notes I end up agreeing with whoever spoke last, which I take to be a bad sign. What I want from you is not a general essay on growth and not a survey of the literature, but a point-by-point adjudication of the seven moves below, as they were actually made. For each, tell me whether it is sound, partly sound, or mistaken, and why. Then tell me which two or three assumptions the two of them quietly share and are doing the most work, and what each of them can see that the other cannot. I don't need names, dates, or references — I'm after the structure of the arguments, and I'd rather you reason it through than reach for a literature review. Neither of them knows I'm writing this, so please don't bother declaring a dinner-table winner until the very end, and only briefly.

1. Ana opened with the Korean peninsula. Same language, same geography, a single state and a single colonial experience until 1945, and today one half is an order of magnitude richer than the other. She said this is the closest thing we have to a controlled comparison at the scale of whole economies, and that whatever else is going on, a gap like that is not geography.
2. Ruben said it is one observation, and that the two halves were not identical after 1945: the North began with more heavy industry and more hydroelectric capacity, was then flattened by bombing, and then spent decades under embargo. So the comparison does not separate institutions from war, capital destruction, and access to markets. He added that the geographic arguments he takes seriously operate at a different scale — disease environments, latitude, transport costs, distance to coasts — and that a comparison inside one small peninsula cannot disconfirm a claim about continental patterns.
3. Ana said the too-many-differences objection proves too much. No two historical units ever differ in exactly one respect; if single-variable isolation is the standard, then historical causal claims are impossible, including Ruben's own. The real choice is not between a controlled experiment and nothing, but between comparisons that are more and less informative, and a shared border with a shared language and a shared pre-1945 state is about as informative as history ever offers.
4. Ruben said fine, but then the evidence is weak, and weak evidence cannot carry the policy weight this whole research program puts on it. He pointed to the quantitative flagship of the institutionalist case, which uses colonial-era settler mortality as an instrument for institutions today, and said the exclusion restriction is the problem: mortality has to affect modern income only through institutions, which is implausible when the same disease environment also shaped settlement density, agriculture, and human capital directly. He also mentioned that a colleague of ours re-coded part of the mortality series from the original sources and found the first stage noticeably weaker, which he took as a sign that the whole edifice rests on data assembled from scattered and barely comparable records.
5. Ana said geography has embarrassments of its own. Geography is fixed over centuries, but incomes move: the regions that were relatively rich around 1500 are often relatively poor now, and some tropical entrepôts are among the richest places on earth. If a fixed variable is supposed to explain a moving outcome, something else must be doing the work. She also said the culture story is worse — whatever institutions and geography fail to explain gets relabeled culture, and a residual category that absorbs every anomaly explains nothing.
6. Ruben said the reversal is not an embarrassment for geography at all. Geography matters through changing channels: when the technology of transport or the route of trade changes, the same coastline flips from advantage to liability. A fixed cause can have a variable effect, so a reshuffling of rich and poor is exactly what a geographic account predicts. And as for residuals, he said, the charge cuts both ways: institutions are very often measured by the very prosperity they are supposed to explain, so the causal arrow may simply be running backward through the index.
7. Ana closed by saying that at least institutions are something we can act on, whereas geography gives you nothing to do; even a modest causal claim about rules is more useful than a confident claim about rainfall. Ruben answered that being actionable is not the same as being true, and that we do plenty with geography — disease control, irrigation, transport infrastructure — so the practical contrast is a myth.

Please take them one at a time. I'd like each of the seven rated from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each rating, then your account of the shared assumptions and the blind spots, and only at the end a sentence or two on who, if anyone, has the better of the exchange overall.
