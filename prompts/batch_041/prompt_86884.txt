I have no philosophical training, but I have been thinking on my own about the disagreement between moral particularism, which says that what counts as a moral reason depends on the situation and cannot be captured in a fixed set of principles, and moral generalism, which says that there must be at least some true universal moral principles, however complex. I want to design a thought experiment that puts real pressure on the generalist without begging the question. The case should turn on what I have been calling a valence switch: a single, non-moral feature that in one situation is a moral reason in favor of an action and in another situation is a moral reason against that same type of action. The feature itself must be the same in both situations; only the surrounding circumstances may differ. The switch must not be explained by different consequences, by a change in the agent's duties, by a change in the meaning of the feature, or by moral relativism. It should be an ordinary feature of life, such as a relationship, a promise, a favor, a role, or a possession, and the two situations should be realistic and non-emergency: no trolleys, no lifeboats, no ticking bombs, no science fiction. Keep the consequences and the agent's other duties as close to identical as you can, and make the surrounding circumstance that flips the valence something that generalists would not normally count as a moral reason at all, so that they cannot simply say a competing reason outweighed the feature or that the feature was absent. The scenario should be one paragraph.

After you give the scenario, do the following. First, state exactly what the feature is, what the background condition is, and how the switch works. Second, explain what a moral particularist would say about the case and what a moral generalist would say. For the generalist, try to formulate the universal principle that would capture both verdicts, then assess whether that principle is genuinely informative or whether it merely restates the case by building the entire context into its antecedent. Third, say which feature of the case is doing the real work. Fourth, tell me what happens if that feature is removed or altered: does the case collapse into a standard generalist-friendly example, or does the valence switch survive? Fifth, explain how a defender of generalism would attack the case, and whether the attack succeeds. Finally, say what the case is for: what it would show about whether moral reasons are context-dependent in a way that resists codification, and what it would not show. I am not looking for citations or a survey of the literature; I want the scenario and the reasoning to stand on their own.
