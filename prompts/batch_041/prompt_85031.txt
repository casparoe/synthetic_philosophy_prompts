I write about science for a living, so most weeks I have to file a piece that says some model of the universe is closer to the truth than another model I know perfectly well is also wrong, and I've started to suspect I have no idea what I'm claiming. I want to run a 90-minute session on this at a science writers' conference: 16 people, mostly reporters plus two working scientists, no philosophers in the room, no wifi, just paper, index cards and a whiteboard. Design me an activity where they run into the problem themselves instead of hearing me explain it. What I want them to feel, in order: that we need some false theories to count as closer to the truth than others or there's no such thing as scientific progress; then that the obvious scoring rule (tally the true claims a theory makes, dock it for the false ones) either can't rank two wrong theories at all or ranks the plainly worse one higher; then that patching it by laying the theories out as sets of possible worlds and measuring distance from the actual world works fine, except the distance scale is doing all the work, so nothing was ever explained.

Mechanics I think I want, though you can push back: each team gets a hand of claim cards and builds a theory on a shared grid of four yes/no features, 16 worlds, with the actual world fixed and public; one or two teams are forced to state the same facts in a different vocabulary so the scoreboard simply can't compare them; then everyone gets one tweak, add a claim or drop one, and we watch the league table flip in ways nobody will defend. Give me the rules, the materials, a minute-by-minute run, the debrief that maps what just happened onto the real ideas (I'll name names there and not before), what will definately go wrong the first time and how to handle it, and an honest note on which part of the problem the activity can't reproduce and I have to supply by just telling them. No reading lists, no citations, nothing anyone has to look up.
