A chapter in an introduction to the philosophy of language that I have been reading characterizes the debate over vagueness as follows. Vague expressions such as "bald", "heap", and "tall" admit borderline cases, and the question is what is happening when we cannot say whether such an expression applies. The chapter presents two leading answers. Epistemicism holds that vague terms really do have perfectly sharp boundaries; the reason we cannot say where the line between bald and not-bald falls is that we are ignorant of it, though there is a fact of the matter. Supervaluationism holds that there is no fact of the matter: a borderline sentence is neither true nor false, since it comes out true under some ways of making the predicate precise and false under others, and truth is then defined as truth under all admissible precisifications. The chapter concludes that supervaluationists give up bivalence and the law of excluded middle and so must abandon classical logic, whereas epistemicism alone lets us keep classical logic intact, and that the whole dispute is therefore at bottom about how much of classical logic one is willing to revise.

I suspect the chapter is broadly reliable but that something has been smudged, and I would like to know what. Which of these claims would a working philosopher of vagueness accept, which are oversimplifications that specialists would wince at, and which are simply false? In particular: is it right to say that supervaluationists abandon the law of excluded middle, and is it right that only epistemicists can preserve classical logic? Are there further commitments of each view — about higher-order vagueness, about what a "precisification" is supposed to be, about whether the epistemicist's ignorance is merely contingent — that the summary omits and that change how the two positions should be compared? Finally, does the chapter's closing claim, that the debate is really about how much classical logic to revise, survive once the summary is corrected, or does it misdescribe what is actually at issue?
