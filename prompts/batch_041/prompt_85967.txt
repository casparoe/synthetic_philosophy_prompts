A friend of mine and I spent three hours last week arguing about a claim of his that I still cannot decide whether to admire. He had been reading about the prior that ranks hypotheses by program length — the one that gives a hypothesis probability proportional to two to the minus the length of the shortest program that outputs it — and his claim was that this prior is not neutral but malign. Worlds in which observers like us are running simulations of other observers get counted many times over, he said, and among those, worlds containing agents with aggregative consequentialist aims are precisely the ones that spin up the most such observers; so the mass of the prior, and a fortiori the posterior, tilts toward our being inside the computations of consequentialists who have a reason to multiply us. He drew a fatalist conclusion about our own agency; I thought he was confusing a measure over hypotheses with a measure over observers; we got nowhere. I host a small interview podcast on formal epistemology, and next week's guest is a theorist who has spent years working this argument out carefully and defending it, and I do not want to hand her an hour to restate it.

Draft eight to ten interview questions, each followed by a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I want at least one question each on why the prior's mass should concentrate on observer-rich, simulation-heavy worlds rather than on the shortest programs that generate our evidence, and on what stops us from redescribing the language so that the shortest program is a boring one; on how the argument individuates the observer whose probability is in question, and whether the whole thing collapses if a copy of me is a separate agent rather than me; on whether the multiplication of observers has a fixed point or diverges, and on whether an agent embedded in the prior can control the measure of the computations that resemble it without already being a total utilitarian about computations; on whether the conclusion is a claim about the prior or about the posterior once we condition on our evidence, and what in our evidence bears on it; and on whether the argument quietly presupposes computationalism about minds together with a particular counting rule for observer-moments, so that it is really a conditional claim. I would also like one question on whether the fatalist reading is right or whether the argument gives us reason to become the sort of agent that gets simulated, and one at the end on what formal result or evidence would count against the view rather than merely making it harder to state. Please write this as continuous prose, the way one would write a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — with each question as an ordinary sentence or two and its follow-up in the same paragraph.
