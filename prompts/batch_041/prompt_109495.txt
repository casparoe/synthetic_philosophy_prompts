Topic for next week is Dutch book arguments. I have an hour, a whiteboard, and permission to make them do arithmetic on it. I don't need you to explain the arguement to me, I can state the synchronic version in my sleep. What I need is a teaching plan: the order the ideas have to go in and why that order rather than another (what genuinely depends on what — do they need the betting-interpretation-of-credence move before they've seen a book actually built, or is that the thing that lands only afterwards?), the one example that carries the whole hour and what it has to have and not have, and the misconceptions I should expect with the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off.

On the example I have a hunch I'd like you to test rather than just rubber-stamp. The horse-race case everyone reaches for is too frictionless — you hand out odds, the bookie takes both sides, ta-da — and my lot will spend the entire hour arguing about the bookie instead of about the credences. I'd rather the carrying case come out of their own world: the season-long prediction game where you set a probability for your team finishing top four in August and then have to set it again after every match, since that lets me run the diachronic version off the same scaffolding and they'll already have opinions about whether their own later selves are being silly. Tell me whether that's the right call, and if not, what example would be, and what the example must do that mine doesn't.

Things I'm predicting they'll come out with, so you can place them in the sequence: that a Dutch book shows the credence is false, or that the person is logically inconsistent; that it shows they'll actually lose money in practice rather than that a certain kind of loss is available in principle; that the whole trick is the bookie's cleverness or his knowledge of your credences; and that it all collapses because the stakes are pennies or because you can simply refuse to bet.
