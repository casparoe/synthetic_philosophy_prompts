For thirty years I did experimental work in low-temperature physics, and I have spent the decade since my retirement trying to educate myself in philosophy. I keep returning to Einstein, because the way his religious and metaphysical views are usually summarized — "he believed in Spinoza's God" — flattens exactly what is interesting about them.

As I reconstruct the position, it has three parts that sit together less comfortably than the summaries suggest. First, his God is Spinoza's: not a person, not a legislator, not a hearer of prayers, but simply the necessary order of nature, so that to know that order and to know God come to the same thing, and nothing is done by God in the way that an agent does anything. Second, he was a thoroughgoing determinist, extending the point to the will itself: what we call choosing is, on his view, a piece of ignorance about the causes already moving us, so that freedom could only ever be the understanding of one's own necessity, never an escape from it. Third — and this is the part I find most striking — when indeterminacy entered physics, his objection reached for the vocabulary of the very God he had spent a lifetime denying, protesting that God would not play at dice. A God who might play is a God with intentions and preferences, which is not Spinoza's God at all; and a God who simply is the order of nature cannot "play," because there is nothing left over in Him to play with.

I would like a joke that lives in exactly this tension. It should turn on the content of the position as I have set it out — the impersonality of Spinoza's God, the determinism about the will, and the way the protest about dice smuggles a personal deity back in — rather than on the bare fact that a famous physicist said something piquant about religion. It should be intelligible to someone who has only what I have just written to go on, and it should not presuppose that the reader has read any particular essay, letter, or book. Someone who already knows the position should find it genuinely funny; someone who does not should still be able to follow it and see why it might be. Please avoid the stock material — the dice, the old man in the sky — unless you can find something in it that is actually new.

Give me the joke first, and then a section headed "Why it works," of roughly a paragraph or two, explaining which element of the position each beat of the joke depends on and why a reader who knows the position would laugh.
