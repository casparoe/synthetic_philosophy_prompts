For my IB Theory of Knowledge essay I have been assigned a case my teacher calls the starving donkey: a creature stands exactly between two identical bales of hay, has no preference whatsoever between them, and dies because it can find no reason to move one way rather than the other. What frustrates me is that everyone in my class seems to assume we already know what the lesson is, whether that the donkey is irrational or that the story is a trick, and no one can state what standard they are holding the donkey to. Before I read any more about this problem, I want the specification first: the list of things any adequate theory of rational choice under indifference has to deliver before we are entitled to call the donkey's death a failure of rationality rather than a limit of the story. My purpose is narrow, and you may hold the theory to it: it needs to be good enough to tell me whether there is anything for the donkey to have got wrong.

I have some demands in mind, and I would like them treated as candidates rather than as settled requirements, because part of what I am asking for is your judgement about which of them are genuine desiderata and which are merely the prejudices of one camp. First, the theory must say what an agent is to do when the options are genuinely equal in value, and whether that is the same phenomenon as a case in which the options are not ranked at all, being neither better nor worse nor equal, the way two possible careers or two possible lives may strike a person; it must not quietly collapse one case into the other. Second, it has to say what the agent is permitted to do: take either option, take one by an arbitrary device such as a coin, postpone, or refuse to choose at all, and whether refusing is itself a choice that then stands in need of justification. Third, it must survive an adversary who knows the agent's rule in advance and can arrange the options so that the agent's own procedure carries it into a worse outcome; a theory that allows the donkey to be drawn on indefinitely is in trouble however elegant it is about the single case. Fourth, it must be consistent over time: if the same symmetric situation returns tomorrow, the agent must not be forced into a rule that contradicts what it did today, yet it also must not be committed to always going left. Fifth, it must handle the case in which the choice is between two bales and a slightly worse third, and the case in which the options differ in a respect the agent cannot measure but can still rank. Sixth, it must respect the difference between an agent that has no preference because the options are equal and an agent that has no preference because it has not yet deliberated, since these appear to call for different treatment even though the theory may not be able to tell them apart from the inside. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. Where two of the requirements cannot both be satisfied by any single theory, say so plainly, tell me which one you would surrender, and tell me which single requirement on your list does the most to eliminate the candidate theories; that trade-off is what I actually need to defend in class. If it helps, close with a brief assessment of how the familiar positions fare against your list, namely the view that reason is simply silent here so that any choice is rational, the view that the agent must remain indifferent and may therefore do anything at all, and the view that a rational agent must manufacture a preference or a tie-breaking rule rather than wait for one. A short table is fine for that last part, but keep it brief; the list of requirements is the deliverable.
