I'm a graduate student sitting in on a seminar on medieval philosophy of mind, and last week two of my classmates, Nadia and Felix, got into a long argument about Avicenna's flying man — the thought experiment in which we are to imagine a person coming into existence all at once, fully formed, suspended in midair, with his senses blocked and his limbs held apart so that they never touch one another or anything else, and who is nevertheless said to affirm his own existence. I stayed out of it and have been turning it over ever since; I'd like a careful adjudication of who was right at each stage, not merely an overall verdict.

Nadia opened by calling the experiment a decisive refutation of materialism about the self: Avicenna removes every sensory channel and every bodily contact, yet the man still grasps that he exists, so the thing he grasps cannot be the body or any part of it — it must be an immaterial soul. She added that he affirms his own existence without affirming any bodily fact, not that he has a hand and not that he has a brain. Felix replied that this only shows self-awareness does not require the senses to be exercised at that moment. Avicenna never takes away the body, he said; he takes away the senses. Anyone paralyzed, or sitting in the dark and silence, still knows they exist, and no materialist is embarrassed by that — it just means self-awareness is not perception, so the setup isolates the man from stimuli rather than isolating a soul. Nadia came back with the point that the man is stipulated to be created in that instant, with no prior experience and no commerce with anything; if his self-awareness were a bodily process, some bodily structure would already have to be doing work, and nothing in the description supplies that. Moreover, she said, his "I am" is immediate rather than inferred from anything, which is precisely why it can survive the removal of all input. Felix responded that the immediacy is a feature of how he accesses himself, not of what he is: even granting that he cannot be wrong about being aware, that tells us nothing about what he is made of, since a physical system could monitor itself immediately — and he added that the argument looks question-begging, because it begins by describing a self that is already there and already aware and then concludes that this self is immaterial. Nadia granted that Avicenna is not claiming the flying man knows he is immaterial, only that he apprehends himself as something not given through the body, which she took to be enough to show the self is not presented as bodily; whether the underlying substance is matter is, on her view, a further question, and the burden has now shifted to the materialist to explain how a purely physical system could be aware of itself with no bodily input whatsoever. Felix closed by proposing a variant: suppose someone builds a device with no external inputs whose internal dynamics produce the report "I exist." By Nadia's reasoning, he said, the device would be aware of itself without bodily input, and although she would want to deny that it has a self at all, the flying man's scenario supplies no principle for distinguishing the two cases. So, he concluded, the argument at best exposes a gap in one materialist story; it does not refute materialism.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move, rating each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating. I would also like you to identify the crux on which the dispute really turns, say what each party is failing to see, and indicate who is more right overall. Finally, tell me what you think the right verdict is about Felix's device variant — whether it genuinely threatens Nadia's reading of the flying man, and whether the flying man can be made to do the work she wants without assuming what it sets out to prove.
