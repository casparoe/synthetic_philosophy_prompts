I could use a hand with a vote.

I'm on this small informal panel — eight of us spread across five or six labs, we call it "the second look" — where anybody in the network can circulate a plan that might have effects past their own bench and the rest of us vote go or no-go. Nothing binding, but people comply, mostly because ignoring a no-go is embarassing and word gets around. A friend of mine says what we're doing has a name, the unilateralist's curse: if enough people can each do a risky thing on their own, the odds that at least one of them eventually does it go to basically one. Sounds made up, but it also sounds exactly like us.

Anyway, I have to vote tomorrow and I genuinely can't tell what our own rule is, so I'd rather you work it out from the track record than from what I think we believe.

What we've done so far:

- Priya's lab wanted to post a preprint with the full protocol for a technique that's genuinely useful but that a bad actor could probably adapt. Three other groups were weeks away from posting near-identical stuff. We said no, hold it.
- Marcus wanted to send a strain to one lab overseas under a standard material transfer agreement. Single recipient, tracked, nothing public. We said yes.
- Dana wanted to be the first human subject for a compound she'd made herself — small chance of a bad reaction, she's a grownup, her institutional review board had cleared it. We said yes.
- A group wanted to seed a self-spreading organism at a fenced outdoor site. We said no. Lots of labs could each do that on their own, and it only takes one.
- Someone wanted to release a dataset publicly when there was a small chance it could be misused, and again it was the sort of thing any number of labs could just post. We said no.

The new one: one lab — literally one, nobody else on earth has this — holds the last remaining sample of a particular historical specimen. Not a pathogen, no bio risk, it's a natural-history thing. They want to grind it up for a destructive analysis that would give a modestly interesting result, and then the sample is gone for good. Since they're the only ones who have it, it isn't the sort of thing where a bunch of labs each doing it adds up to anything. But I asked around and most people in the field lean against it: the sample is irreplaceable, better methods are probably coming, and a lot of people feel it belongs to the field rather than to whoever happens to be holding it. Not unanimous, but that's the mood.

So how should I vote? And more to the point, what rule have we actually been running — is it the "no if lots of people could each do it and it only takes one" rule, or the "no unless the community would sign off" rule, or a third thing that fits all five? Do the candidate rules even come apart on this case? If they do, which one do I follow to stay consistent with our own precedents, and what sort of case would we have had to see in the past to tell them apart? Tell me which precedent is the closest analog, and say how confident you are and which piece of this you're least sure about.
