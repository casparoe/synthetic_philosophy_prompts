I am assembling material for a graduate seminar module on computational models of concepts, and what I need from you is a piece of conceptual analysis rather than a survey. The module is built around Copycat, the program Douglas Hofstadter and the Fluid Analogies Research Group built to solve letter-string analogies of the form abc : abd :: ijk : ?. Copycat does not search a fixed space of rules. Its long-term memory is a network of concepts, the slipnet, whose nodes are pre-programmed and fixed at the start of a run; what changes during a run is the activation of those nodes and the effective weights of the links among them, so that a concept currently in play can be displaced by a neighbouring one. That displacement is what the group calls conceptual slippage, and it is what allows the input to be continually re-described rather than merely parsed. Control is a parallel terraced scan: many small processes, the codelets, compete to build and dismantle perceptual structures, and a global temperature governs how randomly that competition is resolved.

I want you to explain one conceptual issue about this design, and I want you to reason it out rather than recite the literature. I am not asking for citations or for a reading list.

The issue is whether Copycat's fluidity is entirely lateral. Every concept the program can slip to is already wired into the slipnet before the run begins, so re-description looks like movement within a vocabulary the designer supplied in advance. What is not obvious is whether that is a defect or simply what re-description is. So I want you to draw and defend a distinction between lateral slippage — a concept in use is replaced by one already present in the network — and generative slippage, in which a run would need a concept that no node encodes and none can be reached. Is the second kind a coherent possibility for this architecture at all? And if it is, does the first kind fail to explain something for which the second kind is genuinely required?

To keep the discussion from floating free, I have a test case. I am considering replacing the letter-string microdomain with sequences of digits. Take the problem 2 4 6 : 2 4 8 :: 1 3 5 : ?. At least two answers are defensible: 1 3 6, on which the last element takes its immediate successor; and 1 3 7, on which the progression's common difference is preserved, equivalently the last element advances by two, equivalently it takes its successor within the same parity class. Use this case to say concretely what a slipnet would have to contain for each answer to be reachable, and whether the second answer demands a node — even, arithmetic progression, or something of that sort — that a letter-string slipnet would lack, or whether it can be assembled from structural concepts such as sameness and succession that any domain-general vocabulary would already carry. Then say what your conclusion implies for the claim that analogy-making simply is high-level perception.
