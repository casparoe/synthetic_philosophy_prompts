I've been trying to figure out what I actualy think about my own tiny contributions to big bad things, going case by case, and I can't get the verdicts to line up.

Flying once a year to see my parents: fine, I don't think twice. Eating beef at basically every meal for thirty years: I think that's genuinely wrong, and I'd think worse of someone who did it, though I can't point to the meal where it turned wrong. Not voting because my district is safe: a small failing, even though the outcome is identical either way. Shaving a bit off my taxes: wrong, even though my shaving changes nothing. Buying a cheap shirt I know was made in conditions I'd call indefensible if I watched them happen: fine, somehow, and I can't defend why that's different from the beef. Watering my lawn in a drought: fine while the reservoir is healthy, wrong once it's low, even though my lawn is never the thing that makes the difference. Swapping beef for beans on a Tuesday when it costs me nothing: I feel I should, and the fact that it changes nothing doesn't feel like a reply. Being one of a hundred people each tipping a bucket of paint into the river: not fine, and not just because it's illegal.

I keep oscillating between two stories. One is that what matters is whether I'm knowingly taking part in a harmful practice that only does its damage because people like me take part. The other is that only the difference I make matters, and since I never make one, all of it is fine — which can't be right, because I don't believe it about the paint buckets.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets these? What does it say about someone who flies long-haul once a month for work, and about someone deciding whether to have a third kid? And tell me honestly which of my verdicts it can't save — I'd rather drop one verdict than keep a principle that needs an epicycle for every case. Also, please keep apart where people who work on this actually disagree from where they agree; don't sell me a contested view as the consensus.
