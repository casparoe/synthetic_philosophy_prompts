I sit on the deployment review board at a five-campus hospital network — we're the last sign-off before any automated system touches patient-facing work — and next week we're deciding on the ops team's overnight resource agent, which reshuffles beds, staffing and equipment across all five sites while everyone's asleep. Their entire safety case is basically one word: low-impact. The agent is only allowed to act when it strictly improves the scheduling objective we hand it, and otherwise it has to leave the world as close as possible to the way it would have gone anyway. Their proposed definition of a side effect is any change that isn't part of the objective, measured against a 'null policy' — the old rule-based scheduler just running on its own, untouched. Anything the agent changes relative to that baseline counts as a side effect and gets priced in; anything that would have happened anyway doesn't count at all. They borrowed the framing from the AI-safety literature on impact measures — penalize divergence from a default trajectory instead of trying to spell out what's good.

I have to circulate the board's written opinion on Thursday, so draft it for me: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and the conditions need to be things we can actually put in the deployment contract — monitoring, a sunset clause, whatever you think is right.

The part I can't get past is our clinical lead's objection. She says the whole thing is circular. You can't say what counts as a side effect until you've already decided which changes matter, and deciding which changes matter is deciding what the hospital is for — a full value spec we don't have, won't have, and arguably shouldn't pretend to have. On her view 'low-impact' isn't a safety property at all, it's our ignorance wearing a lab coat: pick whatever baseline is convenient and the same system can be made to look careful or reckless. I half think shes right and half think there's something salvageable in the idea that you can be conservative about interference without knowing what's good. So don't split the difference — if the framing survives, say which version of it survives and why, and if it doesn't, say so plainly and tell me what we should require instead.

Also, seperate out where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what would have to be true — about the baseline, or about us — for you to come out the other way.
