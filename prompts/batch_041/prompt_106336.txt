Two ideas are often proposed for how a transparently designed agent should cope with acausal vulnerability — with the fact that other agents, present or future, can predict it and act on those predictions without ever interacting with it. The first is the acausal threat: the agent builds into its design a standing commitment to retaliate against any agent that would defect against it in an acausal bargain, on the assumption that others, reading the design, will treat the threat as already made and settle. The second is the surrogate goal: instead of defending its real values, the agent adopts and commits to a proxy it does not care about, so that any threat aimed at what it actually wants lands on the proxy and can be dismissed or even welcomed.

Compare them. What does each assume about how other agents read a design, about whether a commitment can be stable against the agent's own later reasoning, and about the decision theory that makes acausal reasoning pay in the first place? Is the surrogate goal genuinely an alternative to the acausal threat, or a variant of the same move — and if the latter, can a blackmailer construct a threat that survives it, or is there a form of the surrogate strategy that even a threat aimed at the proxy cannot touch? Finally, does either idea explain why acausal extortion strikes many people as incoherent, or do both merely presuppose the coherence they were meant to establish?
