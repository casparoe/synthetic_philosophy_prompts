Write a strict sestina in which the form itself carries the argument that determinism and predictability are two different things.

Take the following as given. Determinism is a claim about how things are: fix the complete state of the world at a moment, together with the laws, and exactly one history follows. Predictability is a claim about what an agent can know: given finite information and finite time to compute, how far ahead can anyone actually say what will happen. The two can come apart in either direction — a chancy world may be perfectly predictable in its statistics, and a rigidly law-governed one may be opaque in its details — but the case to build on is the second: a system whose laws leave nothing open yet whose future no one can get ahead of, because minute uncertainties in the present are stretched into large ones, and because the cheapest way to learn what happens next may simply be to wait. There is a further and less familiar point worth using. In the systems where this stretching is uniform and robust, a trajectory corrupted by error at every step — as any numerical simulation is, since it rounds off at each stage — is not merely close to a true trajectory for a while before wandering away; it is shadowed. There exists a genuine trajectory of the exact system that stays near the corrupted one for all time. So the simulation is not a fiction, and what it forfeits is not realism but reference: it tells us faithfully how such a system behaves while being unable to say which of its trajectories we are on.

Now build the poem:

- Use the strict sestina: six six-line stanzas and a three-line envoi, with the end-words permuted from stanza to stanza by the standard retrogradata (sixth, first, fifth, second, fourth, third), and the envoi distributing all six.
- Choose the six end-words so that the recurrence is the point. They should be plain words, and at least two of them verbs, so that each reappearance forces the sentence to rebuild itself around the word rather than merely re-seat it. The fixed order in which they return should read as determinism: the same six things, in an order settled in advance, coming back whether or not anyone wants them.
- Let the unpredictability live in the drift of sense. By the fourth or fifth stanza a reader should be unable to guess, from the word alone, what the line will do with it, even though the word was foreordained from the first stanza.
- Make the envoi perform the shadowing. Gather all six words, and let the closing lines hold two paths side by side — one that erred at every step and one that was exactly right — so that the reader sees the wrong one tracked rather than abandoned, and cannot say the difference between them matters.
- Keep the tone austere and plain: no butterflies, no weather forecasts, no named demon, and no use of the word chaos. A reader who knows the mathematics should find nothing false in it, and a reader who knows none of it should still be moved.

After the poem, add a short prose note of three or four sentences saying which end-word is doing the most work and whether the envoi is earned — that is, whether the meeting of the two paths there is produced by the poem or merely asserted.
