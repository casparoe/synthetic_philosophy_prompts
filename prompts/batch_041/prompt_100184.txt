In a reading group on the foundations of computation, we keep tripping over a slogan that everyone in the room treats as a conversation-stopper: 'everything computable is computable by a Turing machine.' It came up again last week when a labmate of mine who works on neuromorphic and analog hardware claimed that a recurrent network with real-valued weights, or an idealised analog integrator, can compute a function that no Turing machine can, and was promptly told that this is impossible 'because of the Church–Turing thesis' — as though the slogan had decided the question. I suspect the slogan is doing several incompatible jobs at once, that at least one of those jobs is trivially true by stipulation while another is a genuinely open empirical question of exactly the kind my labmate is qualified to argue about, and that the dismissal only goes through on a reading nobody in the room would defend if it were stated plainly.

What I want from you is not the history of the thesis and not a verdict on who is right, but an inventory of the precise claims the one sentence could be making, laid out as a menu I can bring back to the group. Distinguish at least the following as candidate readings, and add any I have missed: the definitional reading, on which 'computable' just means 'Turing-computable' and the slogan is true by fiat and says nothing about the world; the formal reading, on which the standard models — Turing machines, lambda calculus, recursive functions, register machines — provably coincide, so that what looks like a thesis is partly a theorem; the original reading, on which an informal notion of effective calculability by a finite procedure is claimed to be correctly analysed by the formal one; the physical reading, on which every process realisable in our physical world computes only Turing-computable functions; and the bold reading, on which human cognition or mentality itself is Turing-computable. For each, tell me whether it is trivial, a theorem, a substantive and defensible hypothesis, an empirical conjecture that could in principle be refuted, or something that has quietly stopped being a claim and become a verbal manoeuvre.

Then do the part I actually care about: say which reading the dismissal of my labmate needs in order to work, whether that reading is available to someone who would not also sign on to the others, and what would have to be true of physics for the analog and real-weight cases to count as counterexamples rather than category mistakes. Assume I am comfortable with Turing machines, universal machines, and the lambda calculus, and that I can follow a distinction between an informal notion and its formal explication; I want the readings separated cleanly enough that each could be affirmed or denied on its own.
