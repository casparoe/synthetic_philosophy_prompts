My reading group got into a fight after last week's session and I need help settling it. Setup so this makes sense: Jeffrey conditionalization is the rule where, instead of coming to fully believe some proposition and conditioning on it, you just shift your credences across a partition of cases — say you go from 50/50 that a patch is red vs orange to 70/30 — and let the rest of your credences fall in line. The argument was between Mira and Elias; I was just sitting there.

Mira says Jeffrey conditionalization is the only honest way to deal with uncertain evidence. Her line: when the light is bad and I'm only 70% sure the wall is red rather than orange, pretending I've learned some definite fact and conditioning on it throws away my uncertainty. I should just move my red/orange credences to 70/30 and let the rule do the rest.

Elias says the rule is broken for two reasons. First, it's order-dependent: if I get two pieces of uncertain evidence whose partitions overlap — say first I'm unsure whether the bird in a blurry photo is a crow or a raven, then unsure whether it's a raven or a grackle — then processing them in one order and then the other leaves me with different credences at the end. But the end state should depend only on the total evidence, not on the order I happened to think about it in. Second, the rule just takes the 70/30 as an input — it tells you what to do once you've somehow got those numbers, but not how the experience justifies them, which is the thing you actually wanted explained.

Mira's replies: the order thing only bites when the likelihoods aren't held fixed, or when you've mis-set the partition; in a well-posed case where the likelihoods stay put, it commutes fine. And the "where do the numbers come from" complaint is unfair — no update rule tells you where your prior came from either, and how perception delivers the 70/30 is a separate job from how you should update once you have it.

Elias comes back: "hold the likelihoods fixed" is doing all the work and theres no recipe for when that's allowed, so it's not a defense, it's a promissory note. And the prior is different — you're stuck with it, whereas the whole point of an update rule is to tell you how evidence moves you; if it can't, it's not really an update rule. He also says: if you're uncertain about your experience, the rule needs a partition over your experiences, but you can be uncertain about that partition too — so do you need a second-order version, and a third, forever?

Mira: there's a neat move — conditionalize instead on the proposition that your credences in the partition are now 70/30. That gives exactly the same result as Jeffrey conditionalization, so plain conditioning is enough, and it shows the "input" worry is misplaced, because the input is just a fact about you and you can be certain of it.

Elias: then you're conditioning on a claim about your own credences, which is circular — that's the very state you arrived at through the process you're supposed to be modeling. And people are alot of the time unsure of their own confidence, so treating "my credence is now 70/30" as certain is exactly the assumption that's up for grabs.

Can you referee this move by move? Who's right about which point, where is each of them overreaching, and whats the thing neither of them is saying? I don't have a dog in this fight, I just want to know how it shakes out.
