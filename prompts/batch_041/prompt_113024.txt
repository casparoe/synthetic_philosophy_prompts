I have just been appointed to the amnesty panel of a truth commission in a country emerging from a long internal conflict, and the first case assigned to me is the one everyone on the panel has been dreading. Our enabling statute requires us to decide "in a manner consistent with the panel's prior jurisprudence," so before I can vote I need to work out what our own record actually commits us to.

The panel has already disposed of six applications. It granted amnesty to a conscript who fired on a crowd on explicit orders and later testified fully; to a transport clerk who knowingly drove prisoners to a killing site, again under orders, and testified fully; and to a sergeant who beat detainees with his own hands at a superior's direction, and testified fully. It denied amnesty to a newspaper editor who wrote the columns that incited the killings; to a colonel who drafted the standing order that authorized the disappearances; and to a district official who signed the transfer lists. None of the three denied applicants testified; each invoked a right to silence.

The new applicant is the jurist who wrote the legal opinion that our own members now cite as the framework which rendered the disappearances "lawful." He never ordered or carried out a single act of violence. He has, however, given the panel an exhaustive, self-incriminating account, naming the ministers who commissioned his opinion and supplying documents that have already produced two convictions.

Recommend the decision that best coheres with our precedents. Identify the rule our record is actually running, name the other rule that fits the same record equally well, and say what each predicts here. Tell me which precedent is the closest analog, give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and name the application we are most likely to see next that would cleanly separate the two rules.
