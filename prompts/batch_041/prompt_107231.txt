I teach a seminar on social ontology and the philosophy of social science, and I want to build a session around Shirley Jackson's short story 'The Lottery' and David Lewis's account of convention. Please read the story through Lewis's theory — and, just as importantly, read Lewis's theory back through the story.

By way of orientation, since I want the reading to engage the account rather than a caricature of it: Lewis's central idea is that a convention is a regularity in the behaviour of a population facing a recurrent coordination problem. Everyone conforms; everyone expects everyone else to conform; everyone prefers to conform on condition that the others do; conformity is a coordination equilibrium, in that no one would have done better by acting differently on their own; and there is at least one alternative regularity that would have served just as well, so the convention is arbitrary in the sense that reason alone does not single it out. Such regularities are held in place by mutual expectation and common knowledge rather than by any intrinsic merit, and they can be learned, imitated, and handed down long after the situation that first called for them has changed.

I want a reading that takes both the story and the theory seriously. Where does the lottery satisfy Lewis's definition, and where does it slip through it? The villagers do conform, do expect one another to conform, and do treat the practice as beyond question — but no one appears to gain from it, and abandoning it would not frustrate anyone's coordination. Is the lottery therefore a genuine convention, if an unusually grim one, or is it better described as something else: a norm, a tradition, a case of pluralistic ignorance, or an instance of a practice outliving whatever purpose it may once have had?

Point me to the scenes and details that carry the philosophical weight — Old Man Warner's defence of the practice, the children's casualness, the ritual of the box, Tessie's late arrival and sudden dissent, and the report that other villages have given the lottery up. Does that last detail vindicate Lewis, since a convention can be dropped when a population settles on another regularity, or does it expose a gap, given that nothing here resembles a shift between equally serviceable equilibria? The session also covers the evolution of norms, so say whether the story supports an account on which such regularities arise and decay through precedent, imitation, and the cost of being the first to defect, or whether it dramatises a failure mode that Lewis's framework cannot name.

End by saying what the story adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not — what Jackson sees about the persistence of arbitrary practices that a definition of convention cannot capture. Be honest where the reading strains: if the lottery simply is not a convention, say so rather than forcing the fit. There is no need to quote from the story; I care about the reading, not the wording.
