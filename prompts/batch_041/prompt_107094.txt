I am working through Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit — specifically the opening of the self-consciousness chapter, in the Baillie translation (the version titled The Phenomenology of Mind, paragraphs Φ 166–168) — and there is a stretch of it I cannot get past. Here is the part that stops me:

"As self-consciousness, it is movement. But when it distinguishes only its self as such from itself, distinction is straightway taken to be superseded in the sense of involving otherness. The distinction is not, and self-consciousness is only motionless tautology, Ego is Ego, I am I. When for self-consciousness the distinction does not also have the shape of being, it is not self-consciousness. For self-consciousness, then, otherness is a fact, it does exist as a distinct moment; but the unity of itself with this difference is also a fact for self-consciousness, and is a second distinct moment."

A few lines later, in the same paragraph, he writes: "This unity must become essential to self-consciousness, i.e. self-consciousness is the state of Desire in general." And in the next paragraph: "the object of immediate desire is something living."

My difficulties are these. First, the phrase "motionless tautology" is obviously meant as a failure, but I cannot see whether the failure is a failure of self-consciousness as such or only of an inadequate description of it — is he saying that a self-consciousness whose distinction from itself has no "shape of being" is a real possibility that collapses, or an incoherent abstraction we have merely entertained? Second, the demand that the distinction "have the shape of being" looks like a substantive metaphysical requirement smuggled in as a condition of intelligibility, and I want to know whether the rest of the chapter actually earns it or merely asserts it. Third, and most importantly for me, I cannot tell how the talk of Desire gets us from a solitary self-consciousness to a second one. The move from "unity must become essential" to "Desire in general" reads to me as a leap: why should the required unity take the form of appetite for a living object rather than, say, the form of a persisting attitude or an activity of synthesis? And if desire consumes its object, how can it possibly supply the stable return-of-the-object that the preceding paragraph demanded? I suspect the answer has something to do with the object being "living," but I cannot reconstruct the argument.

I also have a half-formed hypothesis I would like you to test rather than simply confirm. It is this: there are two things one might call recognition, and the passage is already working with the distinction before it has names for it. Call them consuming recognition, in which the other is registered only by being negated and absorbed, so that nothing is left to recognize me back, and sustaining recognition, in which the other is registered precisely by being preserved as other, so that its independent standing is what makes its acknowledgment worth anything. My hypothesis is that Desire in these paragraphs is consuming recognition, that it fails for exactly that reason, and that the later struggle for recognition is the discovery that only sustaining recognition will do. Is that distinction genuinely at work in the passage, or am I reading a later result back into an earlier step? If it is at work, is it stated or only presupposed?
