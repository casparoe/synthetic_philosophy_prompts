I write a long-form column on philosophy and the human sciences for a general-reader magazine, and I am trying to turn a set of half-formed worries into a single defensible thesis. The subject is how far our interpretive charity can stretch — whether we could ever make sense of a mind whose basic orientation toward the world is not merely different from ours but organized around different concerns altogether. I have been reading about radical interpretation, and I want the column to commit to a claim rather than survey the options.

Please argue, as forcefully as you can, for the following position: that the principle of charity cannot do the work radical interpretation asks of it once we leave the comfortable case of a fellow human. The method, I want you to claim, presupposes not only that the speaker is largely right about the world but that interpreter and speaker already share a rough sense of what is worth caring about and what is worth noticing; and this second presupposition cannot be earned from the evidence an interpreter actually has. Radical interpretation, on this view, is a method for interpreting minds that already resemble ours, and its promise to let the evidence alone settle what the speaker means is an illusion produced by smuggling our own practical concerns into the procedure.

Keep it concrete. A reader who has never opened Davidson should still be able to follow the shape of the argument, so build it out of examples rather than allusions, and make the case that the difficulty is structural rather than a matter of our present ignorance — that no amount of further observation would close the gap.

I want a committed argument, not a balanced tour of the literature: take the position, give your strongest reasons for it, and then state the most serious objection you can see and say why it fails. No citations, no reading list — argue from the ideas themselves.
