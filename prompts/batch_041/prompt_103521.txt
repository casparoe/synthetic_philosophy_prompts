Me and two friends from an old reading group drove six hours to a wedding last weekend and spent most of it in one of those arguments that eats the whole trip, and I've been turning it over ever since. Ravi kicked it off. He's been rereading Dennett (or he says it's Dennett, anyway — the phrase is "free-floating rationales" and I've seen it float around LessWrong enough that I assume he's right) and his claim was this: natural selection builds things that are *for* something even though nothing ever thought about it. The spider doesn't do geometry, nobody designed the eye, and yet there is a reason the web has the angles it has and the lens has the curvature it has — a reason that's real, discoverable by reverse-engineering, and belongs to no reasoner. Reasons can float free of minds. Meg pushed back that this is just the old etiological theory of functions (a trait's function is what it was selected for) wearing a nicer coat, and that calling it a *rationale* smuggles in the idea that the process was smart or the outcome optimal, neither of which follows. Ravi said it's not the same thing, because the rationale is normative and forward-looking in a way selection history isn't: a web with the wrong angles is a *bad* web, you can grade it against the rationale, and that's what lets you say a malformed heart valve is defective — function-talk only tells you about the past, the rationale tells you what the thing is supposed to do.

Meg came back that this makes rationales dirt cheap and everywhere. Does the rock that happens to prop the door open have a rationale? If "in order to" only needs a benefit, then every lucky side effect gets one and the concept stops doing any work; the normativity he wants is just the selection history he was trying to get away from. Ravi said it doesn't need selection at all — things that learn or compute have rationales too, a genetic algorithm's population, a market price, chemotaxis in a bacterium — and you can draw the line: there's a rationale whenever there's a systematic, projectable mapping from a system's states to outcomes that lets you predict and intervene on it, whether or not anything represents it. Drift is a red herring, he said; a trait that arose by drift but now reliably helps still has the rationale, it just lacks the function. Meg's last word was that this is instrumentalism with a better press kit: he's describing when a model is *useful*, not a fact about the world, it's the intentional stance all over again, and if the stance only earns its keep pragmatically then calling it a "rationale" upgrades a modeling convenience into a metaphysically loaded claim about reasons. And the whole thing smells unfalsifiable to her — when predictions land the rationale was real, when they don't the rationale was "overridden" or the environment shifted.

I stayed out of it, which is why I'm asking you. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who wins? I'd especially like the verbal from the substantive pried apart — there's at least three different questions tangled here (does the phenomenon exist, is it distinct from selected-effects function, is the normativity real or projected) and maybe a fourth about falsifiability, and I suspect some of the heat is them answering different ones. Then say who's more right overall and what both of them are missing. No citations needed, just the argument.
