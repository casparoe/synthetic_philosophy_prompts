I'm a fourth-year PhD student in ancient history, and I've been conscripted to run a single ninety-minute session of our second-year undergraduate seminar on Athenian democracy: fourteen students, a flat room with movable chairs, no projector, a printer I can abuse, and no teaching budget to speak of. Most of them have read a bit of Thucydides and the Apology and can produce the standard list of complaints — the assembly was too emotional, the generals were second-guessed, the demagogues won — but they produce it the way one produces a fact about a foreign country. My supervisor wants them to leave with something more like unease, and I've come round to thinking that the usual hour of lecture on the Mytilenaean decree, the Sicilian expedition, and the trial of Socrates is exactly the wrong instrument: they will nod at the pathologies and then reproduce them the first time someone in the seminar proposes something popular. A friend in the law faculty keeps telling me the Athenians' only real problem was the absence of judicial review, and I suspect that's far too tidy. So I'd like to stage the pathologies rather than describe them, and I'd like you to design the activity.

Here is the brief. The session should be one continuous simulated emergency assembly of an invented city — not Athens, with an invented name, and the participants should never be told which historical episodes they are re-enacting, because the whole point is that they arrive at the structural problem themselves instead of recognising a story they already half-know. The city is a direct democracy of the kind where any citizen may speak and any citizen may move a decree; there is no permanent bureaucracy, no organised party, no loyal opposition, no civil service to carry out what the assembly decides, and no way for a proposer to know in advance how much of the consequences will land on him. I want three acts, each taking its shape from one of the episodes without announcing it. In the first, the assembly has just heard that a subject ally revolted and has been retaken by force, and must decide what to do with the surviving adult males; the rules should allow the decision to be reversed only by convening a second full assembly the following morning, so that the students feel both how cheap the first decision is and what it costs to undo it, and so that the reversal, when it comes, has to be argued for in front of the very people who voted the other way an hour earlier. In the second act, the assembly must decide whether to send a large expedition, with information deliberately unequally distributed — some students get briefing slips with the enemy's numbers, the distance, and the season, others get nothing but a rumour — and with command handed to whoever argues most attractively; the act must include an unmonitorable interval in which the appointed commanders act and the assembly can see nothing, and it should end with an outcome slip that makes the assembly responsible for a catastrophe it had no way to supervise. In the third act, after the disaster, a participant must move that the surviving commanders be tried together, in a single vote, on a single penalty, in open assembly, with another participant objecting on procedural grounds; I want a designated presiding officer who has to decide whether to put the motion at all, and I want the reconciliation agreement that ended the city's recent civil war — a standing bargain not to prosecute anyone for what they did under the previous regime — to be visibly in tension with the general demand for a scapegoat. I also want a hard deadline in that act, some ritual or military departure that means the verdict has to be reached before dusk, because urgency is itself a procedural weapon. If the shape of the third act admits it, add a short epilogue in which the assembly turns on a notorious teacher whose only offence is his association with the disgraced commanders, and in which the safeguards that were available a few minutes earlier are quietly not invoked because nobody wants them.

What I need back is the runnable design, not an essay about it. Give me a materials list, and where you use written slips, the actual text of them; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, including where the acts break and how much time the debrief gets; the standing rules of the assembly in enough detail to be printed and posted — who may speak and in what order, how a motion is put and amended, what the presiding officer can and cannot do, how a decision may be revisited, and what happens to a citizen whose motion is defeated; a stake mechanism that produces real pressure without real money (I am willing to use tokens, speaking rights, queuing, or a public tally of standing, but I would rather nobody loses anything material); and the facilitator's scripted interventions, including where to plant confederates, what they should say, and when to let a procedural objection fail. Then the debrief: it should map specific moments in the run onto the actual episodes — the assembly that reversed itself, the expedition voted by people who would not sail, the collective trial of the commanders, the prosecution after the amnesty — and it should distinguish the features of assembly government as such from the features peculiar to the last decade of the fifth century. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it without rescuing the students from the problem; I expect at least one act to collapse into ordinary seminar discussion, and I expect the students to be considerably nicer than the Athenians were. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. I already suspect that the fact that the participants know they are in a game with a facilitator who will stop it at ninety minutes, and that none of them will be killed by the decisions they take, is not a trivial difference, and I want to know which parts of the phenomenon I am therefore obliged to assert in the debrief rather than letting them run into.
