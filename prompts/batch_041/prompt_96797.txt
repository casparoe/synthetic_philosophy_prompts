Last night a friend and I argued for hours about Escher's Drawing Hands, the 1948 lithograph in which two hands rise out of a sheet of paper and each appears to be drawing the other, and about Hofstadter's treatment of it in Gödel, Escher, Bach as a strange loop whose hierarchy is tangled. My position was that the picture only reads as a loop because something in it refuses to be drawn — Hofstadter's inviolate level — and that this ground cannot itself be a level inside the depicted scene, on pain of an infinite regress; my friend thinks the regress is harmless and that the picture is just a picture. I want the argument staged rather than settled by fiat.

Write the deadpan formal ruling of a fictional municipal Office of Causal Attribution, convened to determine which hand authored which and to assign liability accordingly, in the manner of a planning-department determination letter: numbered findings of fact, numbered conclusions, a disposition, and a short dissenting footnote from one adjudicator. Keep the bureaucratic voice flat and procedural throughout; no winking, no jokes about art critics. The comedy should come entirely from the office trying to do its job honestly, so the piece must get the structure right: the two hands are mutually dependent at the same level, the depicted sheet is a depicted object and therefore arguably part of the loop, the physical sheet that bears the image is not depicted at all, and the office's search for a party that grounds the arrangement without itself requiring grounding keeps producing new loops — the artist, the workshop, the market, the viewer — until the only way to close the file is to assert jurisdiction over the inviolate level itself. Let that assertion be the ending, delivered without irony in the ruling's own voice, and let the dissent be the one place where someone notices what has just been claimed. Around 700 words.

Give me a committed piece: reach a definite disposition and defend it inside the fiction rather than presenting both verdicts and leaving the reader to choose. Where you take liberties with Hofstadter's formulation, or with what is actually visible in the lithograph, add a brief note at the end saying where, and which contested reading you leaned on.
