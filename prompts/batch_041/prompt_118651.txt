I run a small team building LLM-based "agent" products, and I keep having the same fight with my cofounder about whether what we've built is actually reasoning or just autocomplete with more steps — and, relatedly, whether we can put "reasons about your data" on the landing page without lying. Someone told me there's an old framework that might actually help instead of us just trading vibes, so I looked it up, and now I want to turn it into something I can run, maybe once a week, every time we ship a capability or write new copy.

As I understand it (I think it's Dennett; the four grades are named after Darwin, Skinner, Popper, and a psychologist called Gregory), it goes like this, and each rung is built on top of the one below rather than replacing it:

- Darwinian: born with a fixed repertoire, doesn't learn within its own lifetime, only "learns" across generations. The trial and error happens out in the world over evolutionary time, and a wrong guess costs you your life, or your lineage's.
- Skinnerian: can learn within its lifetime from reward and punishment, but only by actually doing the thing and getting the feedback. The error is real — it happens in the world, in real time, and it costs real reward.
- Popperian: can hold a model of the world inside itself and try candidate actions against that model before committing, so most of its errors are cheap, happening in imagination. It still acts, but only on the candidate that survived the simulation. The wrong guess dies instead of the creature.
- Gregorian: same as Popperian, except its head is stuffed with thinking tools it didn't invent — language, number, notation, other people's accumulated tricks, external memory. The tools do part of the thinking, and they're inherited from outside the creature, from culture and artifacts. Strip the tools and alot of the cleverness goes with them.

Here's what I want from you: a procedure I can run in maybe twenty minutes on any system we're about to ship or make claims about. It should:

1. make me fix the task and the performance measure first, since I know I can't classify "the agent" in the abstract, only an agent doing a particular job;
2. walk me through tests in order to find the highest rung the system actually occupies on that task, and each test has to be something I can actually go run, not a vibe check. I especially want a test that seperates rung 2 from rung 3 (does it have to act to learn, or can it screen candidates without acting?) and one that separates rung 3 from rung 4 (when I strip the tools — the prompt scaffolding, the retrieved docs, the calculator, the scratchpad — does the competence survive, or was the competence in the tool all along?);
3. tell me what I'm allowed to conclude once it's placed, and what I'm not, including the cases where the honest answer is "the competence lives in the harness, not the model" or "this is rung 2 and I've been calling it rung 3";
4. flag where a test is inconclusive, where I'm actually making a judgment call dressed up as a test, and what the whole procedure can't settle.

Then actually run it on three systems: (a) a plain model with a good prompt and nothing else; (b) an agent loop that proposes an action, calls a tool, reads the result and revises, optimizing a scoring function; (c) the same loop but with a big external scratchpad and a library of retrieved past solutions. Say where each lands, and don't soften the one that's least flattering to us.

One more thing I want built in, because I think it's the actual point: the reason to climb the ladder is that higher rungs make errors cheaper. So the procedure shouldn't just hand me a label — it should tell me whether the rung we're on is high enough given how expensive a mistake is on this task, or whether we're about to eat expensive errors because we built something that can only learn the expensive way.

And a warning to build in so I don't misuse it: these are supposed to be cumulative layers, not bins. A rung-4 system is also rung 3 and rung 2, and the job is to find the top rung, not to sort the thing into a box. If the procedure forgets that, I'll definitely misuse it.
