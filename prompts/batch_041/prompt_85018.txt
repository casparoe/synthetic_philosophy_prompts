You are a philosopher of mind who works on personal identity and autonomy, and you also know your way around the clinical ethics of end-of-life care. Help me dig out the philosophical commitments buried inside a practice I have always taken for granted, and please think carefully rather than reaching for a familiar position.

The practice is the advance directive — the living will, together with the durable power of attorney for health care that names a surrogate. As it actually runs in American hospitals, it has three features that will not sit still together for me.

First, once a patient has lost decision-making capacity, a directive she wrote years earlier, in full possession of her faculties, is treated as authoritative. If the now-demented patient sits up, cheerful and clearly without capacity, and asks for the very treatment she once refused, the earlier refusal generally governs. We say, in effect, that she is no longer the one who decides.

Second, so long as the patient still has capacity, the earlier document slides down to the status of a suggestion. If she now says the opposite of what she wrote, the present, competent word wins, and the old directive becomes mere evidence of what she wants.

Third, the practice will not enforce what people call a Ulysses clause — a directive that says, in substance, "even if I am later competent and I beg you to do otherwise, hold me to this." A competent patient cannot, it appears, bind her future competent self, even on her own terms and for her own good. Some psychiatric advance directives are written to do exactly this: a man with bipolar disorder instructs that he be medicated against his will during any future manic episode, knowing that he will refuse. My understanding is that the law is unsettled about whether such a clause is honored when he later objects while competent by the ordinary test.

A smaller wrinkle, and then my puzzle. Many forms and many ethicists recommend that the directive be reviewed and re-signed every few years, on the thought that a stale directive carries less weight. If authority came from identity, though, I would not expect time alone to erode it.

Here is what puzzles me. The first feature looks as though it presupposes that the earlier self has genuine authority over the later self, enough to override the later self's own voice, and that seems to presuppose a robust continuity of personal identity across the change. But the second feature suggests that the authority was never the earlier self's at all — that authority tracks present competence, and that we honor the directive in the first case only because the patient can no longer exercise authority, so we fall back on her earlier instruction as a default. And the third feature suggests something stranger still: that a competent self cannot alienate her own present authority, which makes present competence look like a kind of sovereignty that even the same person cannot contract away.

Please do three things. Begin by brainstorming, in a few sentences, the readings that might be in play — lay out the candidate views of personal identity and of autonomy without committing to any of them yet. Then reconstruct, as precisely as you can, the implicit theory that these features jointly require. Be explicit about what the practice strictly presupposes as opposed to what it merely fits well with, and say which rival theories it quietly rules out. Finally, tell me where the practice is underdetermined — where two or more incompatible views of the self would license exactly the same rules — and say what would have to change if we replaced the view you think it presupposes with its leading rival. In particular, I want your verdict on whether the third feature belongs to the same picture as the first two, or whether it exposes the practice as quietly committed to two theories at once.

Write in clear American English, for a reader who is at home with philosophy but has no patience for jargon for its own sake. Do not hand me a reading list; argue it out.
