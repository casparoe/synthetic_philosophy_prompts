A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about punishment, and the case we kept gesturing at never actually got built, so I want it built to order. The frame is the Dennett/Caruso disagreement: a Dennett-style compatibilist keeps desert as a real constraint on punishment, so that you may not deliberately make an offender worse off than his culpability licenses, with how much rational self-control he had at the time setting the ceiling; a Caruso-style hard-determinism abolitionist drops desert entirely and justifies confinement purely forward-lookingly, by the risk the person poses now.

What I want is a case in which the abolitionist comes out harsher: the offender is confined, or confined longer, under the no-desert view than under the desert view. That is the direction nobody expects, and I want it generated by the theories rather than stipulated. Constraints: (1) an ordinary criminal case in real institutions, using ordinary actuarial risk instruments, no science fiction and no perfect prediction; (2) the offender is not a monster, and the risk judgment is genuinely contestable, a probability with an error bar rather than a verdict; (3) the desert-based outcome is the lenient one for a reason internal to desert's role as a limiting condition, not because the desert theorist happens to be soft on crime; (4) the harsher outcome must fall out of the forward-looking justification itself, with no retributivist premise smuggled in to produce it; (5) the case must not be defused by observing that either theorist would also accept a proportionality floor or a human-rights side-constraint, so in the case as constructed the only live options are the two confinement regimes.

Then do the audit: give each theorist's verdict and the inference it runs on; isolate the single structural feature of the case that makes the inversion work, and say what happens to the case when you remove it; and say honestly whether the case can be built without turning the abolitionist into a straw man, naming the premise a real Caruso-style theorist would reject. If the constraints cannot all hold at once, say which one has to give and why rather than quietly dropping it. End with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing either "jointly satisfiable" or "relax: <the constraint that must give>".
