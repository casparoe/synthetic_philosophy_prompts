After a career in the earth sciences, I have spent the past four years as a visiting scholar in a history department, and next month I am presenting my first paper at their departmental colloquium. My thesis is that the long opposition between cyclical and linear philosophies of history is misleadingly posed. What actually separates the cyclical theorists from the linear ones, I argue, is not whether events recur but whether the historical process possesses a stable attractor. Read this way, Polybius and Ibn Khaldun are describing something closer to a limit cycle: a trajectory that, when perturbed, tends back toward a recurring sequence of regime types. The linear theorists, from Augustine through the Enlightenment progress narratives to modern growth economics, are describing a process with a drifting parameter whose short-run noise superficially resembles recurrence. I borrow the vocabulary of dynamical systems, which I know fluently and which almost no one in the audience will, and I claim that this vocabulary dissolves several of the standard objections to each camp rather than adjudicating between them. As a test case, I suggest that Ibn Khaldun's cycle of dynastic rise and decay is better modeled as a predator-prey dynamic between group solidarity and luxury than as a moral cycle. That last claim is the part of the paper I am least confident about.

The audience will be roughly two dozen people. Most are historians of particular periods — early modern Europe, late antiquity, and one Ottomanist — who are instinctively suspicious of grand theory and of anachronistic borrowings from the natural sciences. A minority are philosophers of history who know the cyclical and linear literature well and will press on what would count as evidence for an attractor rather than a mere periodicity in the record. One economic historian is sympathetic to cliometric methods and will be the most receptive person in the room. No one there works on complexity or dynamical systems. Assume I know the canonical figures and do not need their views summarized back to me; assume also that you cannot look anything up, so do not offer me citations or reading lists.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the eight or so objections I will actually get, ordered by how much damage they would do to my case, each phrased in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where the reply is weakest. Include the objection I am privately hoping nobody raises.
