I have been reading about the long dispute over the honeybee waggle dance, and I want to use it to think through a philosophical idea I have been trying to understand on my own — the idea, which I have seen called teleosemantics, that a state represents something because of the biological function it has, or because of what it is supposed to indicate, rather than because of any resemblance or inner sentence. I do not have a background in philosophy, so please explain the moves as you make them, but I would like a careful analysis rather than a summary.

Here is the episode as I have pieced it together. Karl von Frisch spent decades studying the dances honeybees perform on the comb after returning from foraging. He argued that the dance carries information about the direction and distance of a food source: the angle of the waggle run relative to vertical corresponds to the angle of the food source relative to the sun, and the duration of the waggle run corresponds to distance. Recruits who follow the dance then fly out and find the food. He shared a Nobel Prize in 1973 for this and related work. But for decades other researchers, most prominently Adrian Wenner, argued that the dance does not communicate the location of food at all. On their view, recruits find food by following the odor of the flowers the forager visited, or by searching near the hive, and the dance is at most an arousal signal that gets them out of the hive. Later experiments with mechanical bees and with radar tracking of recruited bees' flight paths have been taken to show that the dance does convey spatial information that recruits use, but the skeptical arguments were not silly, and some details remain contested — for instance, that colonies can still forage when dances are disrupted, and that recruits often do not fly straight to the advertised location on the first try.

What I want from you is an analysis of this episode through the lens of teleosemantics — the family of views, associated with philosophers like Ruth Millikan and Fred Dretske, according to which a state represents something because of the biological function it has, or because of what it is supposed to indicate. I am especially interested in a question I have not seen addressed directly: does the Wenner-style skeptical challenge threaten teleosemantics, or does teleosemantics give us a way to say what the dance represents even if recruits do not always use it? On a Millikan-style view, the dance would represent the location of food if its proper function — the thing it was selected for — is to produce recruitment to that location. But if the colony can forage perfectly well without the dance, or if the dance's only indispensable function is to raise the general level of foraging activity, then what exactly is its content? Is it a representation of a location, a representation of food out there somewhere, or not a representation at all? And how would a Dretske-style view, which ties content to the causal-indicator relation, handle the Wenner findings — does the fact that the dance is not a perfectly reliable indicator of food mean it is not a representation of the food, or does teleosemantics allow for misrepresentation here?

I would also like you to run the analysis in the other direction. Does the bee case show anything about teleosemantics that the usual examples — a frog snapping at flies, a beaver building a dam — do not? In particular, I am curious whether the bee case puts pressure on the idea that representation requires a consumer or an interpreter. In the bee case the consumer is another bee, and whether she uses the information is exactly what is at issue. Does that make the bee case a better or a worse case for teleosemantics than the standard ones? And is there a way, within a teleosemantic framework, to distinguish the dance as a mere signal, like a pheromone, from the dance as a representation with content — or does teleosemantics collapse that distinction?

Finally, I would like you to be honest about where teleosemantics runs out. The bee case is often used as a nice illustration of the theory, but I suspect it is actually a hard case, because the evolutionary function of the dance is contested, the consumer is not a mind in any obvious sense, and the informational content is carried by a behavior rather than a state. If the theory cannot say anything determinate here, that is worth knowing. If it can, I would like to see the argument step by step.
