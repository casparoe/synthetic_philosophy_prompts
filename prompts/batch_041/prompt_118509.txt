I'm teaching the last unit of a philosophy of science course on inference to the best explanation, and I want to end with a film rather than another paper, so the students have something to shout about on the way out. A collegue suggested The Usual Suspects and I'm intrigued but suspicious. The whole movie is a guy in an interrogation room telling a story that explains every scrap of evidence the police have, and at the end we find out the story was stitched together out of the names and clippings on the wall behind the agent's head. What I want from you is a reading of the film as a test case for IBE. Here's my own view, and I'd rather you push on it than agree with it: IBE says infer the hypothesis that would, if true, best explain the evidence, and Kint's story is by construction the best explanation of the evidence, because he built it out of the evidence -- perfect accommodation, no predictive content, and both the audience and Kujan fall for it precisely because the fit is so good. So the film looks like a counterexample generator for IBE: the rule has no built-in defense against an explainer who curates the hypothesis space, whereas a Bayesian who puts a prior on "this hypothesis was manufactured to fit these data" gets it right. My collegue thinks this is cheap -- it's just lying, and it teaches nothing about IBE that any con-man plot doesn't already teach about testimony -- and honestly I half believe him.

So: where does the film genuinely illuminate IBE, and where does it resist the reading? I know the usual machinery (loveliness vs likeliness, the bad-lot objection, accommodation vs prediction, whether IBE is just Bayesian inference in disguise) and I don't want a tour of it; I want to know which of those, if any, the film is actually about. In particular, is Kujan's mistake really a failure of IBE, or just a failure to consider one more hypothesis -- that the man across the table is making it all up out of Kujan's own wall -- and if it's the latter, does the film show us anything about the inference rule, or only about a careless detective? And is there something the film captures that the philosophical discussion of IBE tends to leave out: that the evidence was assembled by the investigator himself, that the story had to be told out loud to an audience, that the whole thing is a social exchange rather than a lone reasoner staring at data? I know Rashomon is the obvious comparison, so bring it in only if it earns its place.

Tell me where you actually come down, and be explicit about what would change your mind: which scene, counterexample, or argument would flip your verdict, and which step in your reasoning you trust least.
