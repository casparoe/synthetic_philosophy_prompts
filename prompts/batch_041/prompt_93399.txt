I am an associate editor at a general journal of behavioural and evolutionary biology, and I handle roughly eight manuscripts a month. Two years ago the editorial board, after a long and bad-tempered argument about the Gould–Lewontin critique of adaptationism, added a line to the submission guidelines: authors who claim that a trait is an adaptation must say so explicitly and must address the alternatives. The line has been applied about as inconsistently as you would expect. Some of my reviewers read a claim about a trait's current function as a claim about its evolutionary origin and wave it through; others demand population-genetic or phylogenetic evidence that no behavioural paper in our pages could possibly supply; and authors have begun to notice that the outcome depends on which of us handles the manuscript. I want to replace the guideline with something I can actually run.

Please write me a decision procedure for assessing an adaptation claim in a manuscript. I need to be able to complete it at my desk in about forty-five minutes. I am a field biologist by training: I can read comparative data, but I am not competent to judge whether a particular phylogenetic model is appropriate, and the procedure should not depend on my becoming so. The board's discussion kept circling the founding example itself — several members insisted that the spaces in the basilica are pendentives, the curved triangular surfaces that carry the dome, not spandrels in the architectural sense at all. I mention this only because I do not want a procedure that settles the question by definition in either direction.

The procedure should specify:

- the questions I ask, in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each;
- the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and what the authors would have to supply to get past it;
- the point at which it stops and hands the decision to a person, with that person named — me, the authors, the editor-in-chief — rather than a hand-off concealed inside a step;
- the smallest set of verdicts I need to be able to reach, with a defence of each distinction: why collapsing any two of them would lose something a reader of the journal needs. I want labels I can paste into a decision letter, not prose.

Four failure modes must be handled, because they are what actually goes wrong in the manuscripts I see:

1. current utility offered as evidence of origin — the trait does something useful now, therefore it was selected for doing it;
2. the design argument — the trait is intricate and well fitted to its job, therefore it was built for that job;
3. unfalsifiability — whatever the data turn out to be, some adaptive story can be told that accommodates them;
4. the single lineage — a trait found in one species, with no close relatives to compare against, treated as though its presence in that species were itself the finding.

The procedure should also be explicit about which of the standard non-adaptive alternatives — a by-product of a constraint on development or body plan, a correlated by-product of selection on some other character, a structure co-opted for a new role after originating for another one, and plain drift — it can genuinely discriminate, and which it can only flag as open. Where it cannot discriminate, say so, and say what the manuscript should then be permitted to conclude instead.

Then run it on three manuscripts:

(a) a paper arguing that the human chin is a mechanical adaptation to resist chewing loads, on the grounds of bone thickness and trabecular orientation in the mental region, where the rival accounts are that the chin is a by-product of the reduction of the face and dental arch, and where the developmental evidence is itself contested;

(b) a paper reporting that a particular courtship vocalisation is an adaptation for mate attraction in a songbird genus, based on a comparison of call structure across eleven species, with no correction for shared ancestry;

(c) a paper arguing that the unusually masculinised external genitalia of female spotted hyenas are an adaptation for a social function, against the rival account that they are a by-product of selection on other traits mediated by the same hormones.

Two further requirements. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like — I would rather find out now where the procedure will annoy me than in a decision letter. And close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do, and of the points at which I will be tempted to overrule it.

I do not need a tour of the literature; I need the procedure itself, in a form I could hand to a new associate editor on their first day.
