Two colleagues on my interpretability team have been arguing for weeks over a claim we have to settle for a safety write-up, and I have stopped being able to tell who has the better of it. The situation: we reverse-engineered a circuit in a small transformer trained on a synthetic task (the model learns to apply a short sequence of bracket-matching and stack operations, and we had a clean account of the components doing the work). A year later we trained a larger model on the same task and found components whose ablation and activation-patching behaviour matched the first finding across our whole test battery. Call the original M1 and the new one M2.

A's case, over three rounds. (1) M1 and M2 are the same mechanism: our team's working definition says two mechanisms are the same when their counterfactual profiles agree under a specified family of interventions, and M2 agrees with M1 under every intervention we can specify, so the question is closed. (2) When B objected that we only ran finitely many tests, A said the identity of a mechanism just is its causal role — if M2 does the same thing under every intervention that matters, "different mechanism" is a distinction without a difference, and functionalism about mechanisms settles it. (3) When B then demanded algorithmic identity, same intermediate variables and same order of operations, A said that standard is unusable: two models trained from different seeds implement the same task with different algorithms, and under B's criterion nothing but the very same weights could ever count as the same mechanism, which makes the phrase mechanism transfer incoherent.

B's case. (1) Matching on the tests we happen to have is exactly the failure mode anomaly detection exists to catch; the safety question concerns what the model does under interventions we have not tried, so agreement on the battery is evidence at best, not identity. (2) The real work is being done by the choice of abstraction level, and A is smuggling it in: a same-mechanism claim is always relative to a chosen level of description, so the dispute should be about which level is right for the safety decision, not about whether the mechanisms are "really" the same. A replied that this makes anomaly detection either trivial or impossible depending on the level you pick; B answered that this is at least an honest, arguable question rather than a hidden one.

Adjudicate the exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification. Say which criterion, if either, should govern a safety-relevant claim of the kind we have to make, and what both of them are missing.
