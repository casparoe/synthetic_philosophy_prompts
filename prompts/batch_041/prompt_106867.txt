I'm a law student who has drifted into a philosophy of mind reading group, and I keep running into something in my doctrinal courses that the philosophers can't seem to explain to my satisfaction. Consider three doctrines that every first-year course treats as utterly unremarkable. First, the rule that ignorance of the law excuses no one: a defendant who genuinely had no idea a particular statute existed is still liable as though the statute's content were already sitting in his head. Second, the civil standard under which a person is held to know whatever a reasonable investigation would have turned up — the buyer who never read the inspection report is treated as though he had read it and drawn every conclusion it supports. Third, the criminal doctrine of willful blindness, under which someone who deliberately arranges not to learn a fact is treated as though he knew it, on the theory that he suspected a high probability of it and chose to keep himself in the dark. Yet the very same system insists elsewhere that you cannot be liable for what you had no way of knowing, and that mens rea is a matter of the defendant's actual mental state — what he really believed, not what he ought to have believed.

What I want to understand is what theory of knowledge these practices presuppose. The epistemologists in my reading group tell me that the standard formal treatment of knowledge makes agents omniscient about logic: they know every logical truth, and they know every consequence of everything they know, and this is normally treated as a bug to be repaired rather than a feature to be preserved. The law seems to want something in that vicinity but stops well short of it — it imputes the knowledge an ideal inquirer would have obtained, but only where inquiry was feasible and only within a reasonable scope, and it converts a self-manufactured gap in one's information into something that functions like knowledge. Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of the practice. Is it a closure principle of some restricted kind, a capacity or dispositional notion of knowledge rather than an occurrent-state one, or a frankly normative notion on which to have known something just is to have been under an obligation to know it? Which rival accounts of knowledge does the practice genuinely rule out, and which does it leave open, so that the doctrines are philosophically underdetermined and several theories would license them equally well? Is the practice internally consistent — in particular, can the same account that licenses imputed knowledge also explain why willful blindness counts as a form of knowledge rather than as a separate wrong of recklessness or of failing to inquire? And tell me, finally, what would actually have to change in these doctrines if we took the problem of logical omniscience seriously and adopted an account on which an agent knows only what is explicitly represented to him.

Please answer in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic, no tables — the way you would write it out in a long letter to someone who is genuinely puzzled.
