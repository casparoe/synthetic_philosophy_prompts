For a graduate seminar I am preparing on temporal experience, I have been collecting the folk slogans my students use to domesticate the color phi phenomenon, and one keeps recurring: 'experience is edited after the fact'. I have now heard it deployed for two opposite purposes. One student uses it to deflate the phenomenon: of course the first dot seems to change color before the second arrives, because the brain simply revises the experiential record once the second flash is registered; there is no more mystery here than in any other post-hoc reconstruction. Another student uses the same sentence to radicalize the phenomenon: if the color change is genuinely experienced as occurring before the second flash, then experience itself must be assembled retrospectively, so that what we call the present moment of consciousness is partly constituted by events that have not yet happened at the time we seem to be living through it. I am less interested in adjudicating between those students than in seeing exactly how much work the slogan can bear.

What I want from you is a disambiguation, in the form of a menu rather than a single verdict. Set out the distinct precise claims that 'experience is edited after the fact' could be making, ranging from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical. For each reading, specify what it commits one to about the temporal order of stimulus, neural processing, conscious experience, and subsequent report or memory; say whether it is a restatement of the color phi data, a substantive empirical hypothesis, a conceptual claim, or a confusion; and indicate which reading, if any, would actually support each of the two uses described above. Please give particular attention to the difference between revision before experience, revision within experience, and revision only in memory or report, and say which of these correspond to the Orwellian and Stalinesque options and which fall outside both. I would also like you to flag any reading on which the color phi case ceases to be evidence for retrospective experience at all, and any on which the slogan turns out to be true but philosophically inert. No citations or reading lists are needed; I want the logical geography of the claim.
