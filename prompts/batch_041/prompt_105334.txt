I was at a reading group last week and someone brought up the absent-minded driver, and now I can't stop chewing on an argument one of the guys there made. Here's the setup as he told it. You're driving home down a straight road and there are two turnoffs before you get there. If you take the first turnoff you end up somewhere useless (payoff 0). If you drive past the first turnoff and take the second one, you make it home (payoff 4). If you drive past both, you crash (payoff 1). The twist is you're absent-minded: when you arrive at a turnoff you genuinely can't tell whether it's the first one or the second, and you have no recollection of having already passed a turnoff. So your information at the two turnoffs is identical.

His argument went roughly like this. (1) A rational agent at a decision point picks the option with the highest expected payoff given the information available at that point. (2) The driver's information at the first turnoff and at the second turnoff is the same. (3) So the option with the highest expected payoff is the same at both. (4) If you're at the first turnoff, continuing has a higher expected payoff than turning off, since turning off there gives 0 while continuing keeps alive the payoff-4 outcome. (5) So at a turnoff, the driver should continue. (6) So he continues at the first, and then, arriving at the second, applies (5) again and continues — crash, payoff 1. (7) But the plan "continue at the first turnoff, turn off at the second" would have paid 4. (8) So at every single point the driver did exactly what (1) says to do, and still walked away with 1 when 4 was sitting right there. (9) Therefore the principle in (1) doesn't actually maximize your payoff, and any decision theory built on "do the highest-expected-value thing given your information" is self-undermining.

I feel like this is rigged somehow but I can't see where the trick is. I'd definately like to keep (1) if I possibly can, because without it I honestly don't know what "rational" is even supposed to mean. So: which step do I reject, why did it look so obvious when he said it, and what does rejecting it cost me — what do I have to say instead, and what else does that commit me to?
