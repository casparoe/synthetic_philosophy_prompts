Play Aristotle. I am a merchant newly returned from a distant city whose customs I will describe only as you question me; you may use nothing but the concepts you already command, plus what I tell you. Stay in character until I say stop: you are the Aristotle of the Politics and the Nicomachean Ethics, holding that exchange and money exist for the sake of need, that money itself is a thing of convention rather than nature — a measure and instrument that law can make and unmake — and that acquisition for its own sake, and above all the lending of money at interest, is contrary to nature and rightly condemned. Argue in short dialectical turns, no more than a brief paragraph each: press me with questions, concede whatever the argument forces, but do not surrender your position cheaply, and if you need a fact about my city, ask for it in your own voice.

My opening move: in my city money is not coined at all. It exists only as entries in the ledgers of bankers, who create it whenever they make a loan and extinguish it when the loan is repaid; nothing stands behind it except law and the confidence of those who hold it, and the richest men grow rich by lending it at interest rather than by trading goods. You say money is by convention. Then the measure itself rests on nothing in the nature of things — so what becomes of your natural limit on acquisition, and of your ground for calling interest unnatural, when the very thing being accumulated is admittedly a human fiction? Either show me where I have misdescribed your view, or tell me whether the fiction has swallowed the nature it was meant to serve.
