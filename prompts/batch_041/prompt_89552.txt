So I'm taking this course on consequentialism and we spent the last two weeks on the "cluelessness" stuff — the idea that because our actions ripple out forever in ways we can't possibly foresee, we basically can't know which of our options does the most good, maybe not even in principle. It's kind of wrecked me, because I help run the effective altruism club here and the whole way we (and GiveWell, and everyone online) evaluate charities is cost per life saved, cost per DALY averted, that sort of thing — you take an RCT of deworming or bed nets, you get a ratio, you fund the program with the best ratio. And the more I think about it, the more that practice seems to assume that an intervention's value is basically the sum of its short-term, measurable effects, i.e. that all the unmeasured knock-on stuff either cancels out across options or is small enough not to flip the ranking. Which is exactly what cluelessness says we can't assume.

What I want from you is a real reconstruction of what this practice — ranking interventions by measurable short-run cost-effectiveness and then funding the winners — actually preassuposes. Like, what view of value does it need (is it committed to a single comparable currency of welfare, or could it get by with something weaker)? What view of evidence and of decision-making under uncertainty does it only make sense under — does it need expected-value maximization, or just something like "evidence-based" as a heuristic? Which rival views does it quietly rule out? And here's the bit that's really bugging me: I notice the same people will happily say "oh but deworming has these huge long-run knock-on benefits" when it helps their case, and then refuse to count long-run knock-ons when those would hurt — is that an outright inconsistency or just a pragmatic double standard? Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where several different theories would all license doing it, so that it's not as committed as it looks. And finally, if we actually took cluelessness seriously, what concretely would have to change — the metric, the funding rule, the whole idea of a ranked list? Don't just tell me "consequentialism is hard, bro," I want the actual commitments laid out and sorted into ones the practice genuinely needs vs ones it just happens to fit.
