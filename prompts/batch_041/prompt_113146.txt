Please read Christopher Nolan's Memento through the lens of money pumps and diachronic rationality. The film gives us Leonard Shelby, who cannot form new memories and so cannot carry a preference, a plan, or a reason across more than a few minutes; he externalizes his diachronic commitments into Polaroids, tattoos, and a filing system, and the plot turns on Teddy and Natalie each feeding him a fact calibrated to make his present self act for their ends rather than his. The money-pump argument, as I want to use it, is the claim that an agent whose preferences are cyclic or intransitive can be exploited by a sequence of pairwise trades, each of which she prefers to what she has, that leaves her worse off than she began -- and the diachronic version of the worry is that the same fate befalls an agent whose preferences shift over time, or who cannot bind her future self, so that a patient counterparty can harvest value from her one moment at a time. What I want is a reading that takes both the film and the argument seriously.

Some specific things I'd like you to address. First, does Memento dramatize the money pump as a real phenomenon, or does it undermine the argument by showing that the "diachronic agent" the argument presupposes -- a single self whose preferences could be transitive or intransitive across time -- is a fiction, leaving only a succession of momentary selves? Second, the ending, where Leonard deliberately falsifies his own notes to manufacture a reason to kill Teddy: is this an agent rationally manipulating his own future preferences, and if so is it a kind of self-pump that the standard argument, which always imagines an external exploiter, fails to anticipate? Third, Leonard's "system" -- the tattoos, the conditioning, the insistence that the facts, not the memories, are what matter -- is best read as a commitment device that restores diachronic rationality, or as a machine for laundering whichever preference he happens to hold into something that looks like a considered plan? Fourth, Teddy's line that Leonard has already killed the man he is chasing, and that Leonard will not remember it, seems designed to make us ask whether an agent who cannot be made worse off in any way he can register can be pumped at all, or whether the money pump requires a continuity of experience that Leonard lacks.

I'd like the reading to be honest about where the film resists the philosophical frame -- in particular, I suspect the film's real interest is in the narrative construction of agency rather than in preference consistency, and I'd rather you say so than force the analogy. Where the argument illuminates a scene -- the sequence in which Teddy and Natalie alternately supply Leonard with his next "fact," or the moment Leonard chooses to burn the photograph and write himself a lie -- say what it illuminates. And end by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate about diachronic rationality that the money-pump literature, with its tidy sequences of trades, does not: I'm after the thought that an agent can be complicit in his own pumping, and that the value of a unified diachronic self may be something a person can rationally decline.
