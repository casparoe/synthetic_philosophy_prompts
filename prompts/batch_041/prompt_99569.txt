I keep coming back to the old fight over whether there can be laws of history and I cant make the two sides meet. On one side there's Popper's attack on what he called historicism — the idea that there are large-scale laws or trends of historical development that would let you predict the future course of society, which he thought was both false and politically dangerous. On the other side are the people who took over the covering-law model from Hempel: to explain a historical event is just to show that it follows from some general regularities plus the particular initial conditions, and if historians don't state the regularities it's only because they're obvious or get suppressed. Then there's a third camp, the ones who said history explains by reconstructing what it was like to be in the situation — telling a story that makes an episode intelligible — and that this isn't a poor substitute for subsumption but a different thing entirely, and better. They've been going round this since the 1940s/50s. Same examples every time (why this war, why that revolution, why the bridge fell), everyone agrees history isn't physics, and yet nobody has moved an inch.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a ruling on who's right. My guesses, roughly in order of how much I believe them:

1. It's partly verbal. One side means by 'law' a strict, unrestricted, exceptionless universal generalization; the other is happy to count true but hedged, domain-restricted generalizations as laws. Fix the sense of 'law' and half the dispute about whether history has laws dissolves.

2. It's a real disagreement about what explanation is for. Subsuming an event under a generalization and making an action intelligible might be answers to different questions, so the two sides aren't even competing with each other.

3. Underneath there's a hidden empirical question nobody has actually settled: whether the social/historical world is stable enough for anything law-like to hold across it. A friend of mine in economics keeps saying it's really a question about whether the process is stationary / ergodic — if the underlying data-generating process drifts and changes regime, then no generalization survives the drift, and that's a fact about the world, not about the philosophy of explanation. If that's right, "can there be laws of history" partly turns on empirical facts about stability, and alot of the philosophy is beside the point.

Tell me which of these is doing the real work, using what each side actually says rather than a survey, and be concrete about which specific moves in the debate my diagnoses get right and which they get wrong. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that nothing would and why. And say how confident you are in the diagnosis overall, and which single step of it you're least sure about.
