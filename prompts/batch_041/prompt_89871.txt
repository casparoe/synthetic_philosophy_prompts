A colleague has been pressing an argument on me that I find both compelling and absurd, and I can't find where it breaks. He puts it this way. (1) For a striking number of major scientific results, historians can point to two or more people who reached the same result independently within a few years of each other — the calculus, natural selection, and many others. (2) A result that would have been reached by someone else at roughly the same time is a result that did not depend on any particular person. (3) An ascription of credit is a claim that some particular person's effort actually contributed to the outcome. (4) If a result did not depend on any particular person, then no particular person's effort contributed to it. (5) Therefore no one ever genuinely deserves credit for a scientific discovery. (6) So the entire apparatus of priority, prizes, and citation records is a collective delusion, kept up for incentives rather than desert.

I want to keep (1) — the pattern looks real to me, at least in some fields and at some periods, and I don't think it's an artifact. I want to reject (5), because I think credit for a discovery can be genuinely deserved. Tell me which step to give up and what giving it up costs me. Be explicit about whether the trouble lies in the inference or in a premise, and if it lies in an inference, say exactly what is doing the illegitimate work. If you think the honest move is instead to accept (5) and redescribe credit as a purely forward-looking instrument, argue that too, and tell me what it would cost to hold that view while still treating priority disputes as something other than mass hysteria.
