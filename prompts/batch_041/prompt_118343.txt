I'm writing a small internal grant application (six months, me plus maybe a grad student) and I want to turn a half-formed thought about consequentialist cluelessness into a real proposal, so I need you to help me frame it properly.

The thought: I think the cluelessness debate keeps running two different complaints together. One is that we can't compute the far-future consequences of what we do — that's a fact about us and our limits. The other is that for lots of pairs of acts there may be no settled fact about the long-run value difference at all, because the difference depends on what later people choose to do with whatever we leave them, and that hasn't been decided yet. The standard replies — swap actual consequences for expected value over some enormous space of futures, or look for a dominance argument — strike me as only really engaging the first complaint. If the second one is right, it isn't an epistemic problem at all, and telling me to maximize expected value is answering a question I wasn't asking.

So the question I want the proposal built around: for a given pair of ordinary acts, is the claim that their far-future value difference is unsettled — rather than merely unknown — coherent and defensible, and if it is, what's left of the expected-value response? I want it stated precisely enough that it could come out either way, not rigged.

What I need from you: the precise question, why the usual replies don't settle it, what I'd actually do month by month (I was thinking of working through three structurally different cases — a donation, a vote, and funding a line of research — and seeing whether the indeterminacy claim survives in all, some, or none of them), what a good result looks like, what would count as the project failing, and whether six months is realistic or whether this is really a book.
