My friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about Daniel Ellsberg and the Pentagon Papers, and we got stuck on a question neither of us could settle: whether Kant's universalizability tests actually return a determinate verdict on what Ellsberg did. I hold that they do — that the leak passes and that the government's deception about the war fails — and my friend holds that the tests are a machine for laundering whatever verdict you already wanted. What I want is the case worked through in both directions rather than adjudicated.

Take the facts as follows. Ellsberg, who had worked on the study as a Pentagon and RAND analyst, gave the classified forty-seven-volume history of United States decision-making in Vietnam to The New York Times, which began publishing it on 13 June 1971; when the government obtained an injunction against further publication, he passed copies to other papers; he had earlier tried and failed to interest sympathetic senators in releasing the material; he was charged under the Espionage Act together with theft and conspiracy, and the charges were dismissed in 1973 after the government's own misconduct came to light.

Now, in order:

First, specify the maxim. Give it to me twice, at two levels of description — an occasion-specific one, and a practice-level one — and say which specification you think is faithful to what Ellsberg actually did, and what makes a maxim description principled rather than gerrymandered. My friend's objection lives or dies on this point.

Second, run the Formula of Universal Law and then the Formula of the Law of Nature on each specification. Keep the distinction between a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in the will explicit, and say whether the verdict changes with the level of description, and if so, what that shows.

Third, apply the Formula of Humanity. Who is being used merely as a means here — the public kept in the dark, Ellsberg's colleagues whose material he copied, the researchers who worked alongside him, the newspapers, the government? Do the same for the state's own maxim of concealing the war's real prospects from Congress and the public, and tell me which of the two applications is the cleaner case.

Fourth, bring in the transcendental formula of public right from the perpetual-peace appendix on the harmony of politics with morals. The leak was built for publicity, and the deception cannot survive it. Do the publicity test and universalizability come apart on this case, and if they do, is publicity a rival to the first formula or only a heuristic for it?

Fifth, the reverse direction. Where is the universalizability procedure silent here, and does the episode confirm the theory or expose a defect in it? Then take Kant's late reply to Benjamin Constant, where he refuses any right to lie even to a would-be murderer at the door: does his rigorism about truthfulness commit him to condemning Ellsberg's secrecy, and does that reveal the late position as inconsistent with the Groundwork procedure, or show instead that the universal-law test was never meant to function as a decision procedure for particular acts?

Finally, briefly: if the maxim included a clause like "and I will stand trial," would the verdict change, and what does Snowden's case — where the analogous clause is flight rather than submission — show about whether that clause belongs in the maxim at all?
