I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy background, and somehow I've ended up being the one who writes the guidance note for a section of our internal complaints policy. We're about forty people, so "policy" means a Google Doc that three of us have edit access to.

The section is the one about getting waved off — you raise something and it's dismissed not because it's wrong but because of who's saying it. I'm told the wording came out of a book by a philosopher called Miranda Fricker, and that the term for it is testimonial injustice, though I've only read the summary. Priya, our ops lead, is the one who actually hears these and she's been ruling on them for about eighteen months. Her write-ups are terse and usually she doesn't say why, which is exactly my problem: I have to write the guidance note and I don't know what rule she's really running. Her rulings so far:

1. A blind engineer in our accessibility group said a new component library would be unusable with her screen reader. The product lead said she couldn't really judge the visual side of it and moved the ticket to the backlog. It shipped, it broke for screen reader users, took three weeks to sort out. Upheld. Her note mentions that three other people in the accessibility group had hit the same wall that quarter.

2. One of our test engineers has raised about five alarms over two years about the same flaky service, and every one turned out to be nothing. His fifth was triaged as low priority and it was nothing again. He complained. Not upheld. Her note says the call was about his record on that service.

3. A QA engineer raised a data-loss risk in three consecutive sprint reviews; the first two times she was told it wasn't really a risk. Then a bloke on the platform team said nearly the same thing and it was a P1 by lunchtime, and we were about an hour away from losing a day's writes. Upheld. Her note just says: same concern, different response.

4. Someone complained their pull request comments got ignored. The comments turned out to be wrong. Not upheld.

5. A night-shift support person kept saying that a particular error message was making customers do the wrong thing. Everyone believed her about the message, but nobody treated the customers-doing-the-wrong-thing part as a bug, because we didn't have a category for it. She filed under this section and Priya didn't uphold — said it was a real problem but not this one, and pushed it to product.

So that's two upheld and three not, and I can fit at least two different rules to all five of them, which is why I'm stuck.

Now next week there's a new one and I have to pre-write the guidance around it. We've just switched on an automated triage bot for incident reports — it scores each report and routes it, and it was trained on our own past triage decisions. A fairly senior backend dev filed a report about a permissions bug that could have let one tenant read another tenant's data. He didn't fill in the repro-steps field; he recorded a two-minute screen capture and linked that instead. The bot scored the report as low quality because the field was empty, so it sat in a low-priority queue for eleven days. Nothing came of it in the end: someone else found the same bug independently, and he only realised his report had been sat on when he went looking. Nobody thinks the bot has it in for anyone, and the bloke who wrote it is not the sort of person who usually gets ignored. It's definately the first complaint about the bot as far as we know, though we've no idea how many other reports it's quietly deprioritised.

Will she uphold it? And more to the point, what's the rule she seems to be running, and what are the other rules that fit all five of her earlier rulings just as well? For each of those, say what it predicts for the bot case, then give me your actual best guess about which one she's running and why. Then tell me which single further case would best tell the rules apart — I'll take it to her. And if it genuinely comes down to a coin flip, tell me what we'd be committing ourselves to in the guidance note either way.

One request: don't just give me the labels for the rules, I need to see how each one gets from her five rulings to a verdict on the sixth, because that reasoning is the bit I have to write up.
