Two years ago the six of us who run a small sourdough bakery restructured as a worker cooperative, and the first thing we did was set up what we call the cushion fund. If any one of us runs into a personal emergency — a medical bill, a car dying, a parent who suddenly needs full-time care — that person can stop working for up to six weeks at a stretch and keep drawing full pay from the fund, while the rest of us cover their shifts. Priya, who founded the place, proposed it, and we voted it through unanimously. I voted for it and, honestly, I would vote for it again; the reasoning at the time was that any of us could end up needing it and that the security was worth the weekly deduction, which comes to about £15 each.

Since then Dana has drawn on the fund three times, about five months of paid leave in total. The fund is empty, I have been working six-day weeks to keep the ovens going, I have postponed a root canal twice, and I am roughly £1,800 worse off than I would have been with no cushion at all. When I raised it, Priya did the arithmetic out loud: Dana has received about £9,000 she would not otherwise have had, the five of us who kept working have each put in about £1,800 more than we have taken out, so on paper it nets to nothing — and then she made what she clearly took to be the real point, which is that £9,000 to Dana, who was two weeks from losing her flat, is worth far more than £1,800 to each of us, who barely noticed it. Dana's version is gentler: she says it is insurance, and you do not get to complain about the premiums just because you never crashed the car. I follow both arguments, and I agreed to the rule, and I do not want to be the person who kills the cushion fund. But something in the way the two of them put it still strikes me as wrong — it is as though my £1,800 and Dana's £9,000 are being treated as money in the same pocket, and as though my being worse off is simply cancelled out by her being better off. I have been reading moral philosophy on my own and suspect there is a name for what is bothering me, and that it is a genuine objection rather than just me being stingy. We meet on Sunday; can you tell me what is actually going on in these two arguments, including whether my unease survives the fact that I voted for the rule, and what I can say to them? Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
