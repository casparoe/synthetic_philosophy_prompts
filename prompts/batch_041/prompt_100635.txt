I'm putting together materials for a grad seminar on the self, and I'm bored of the usual "read the narrative-gravity paper, read the anti-narrativist reply, discuss." So here's what I actually want, and it's a bigger ask than it sounds.

Take two camps that have been talking past each other on this for decades. Camp A: the self is the center of gravity of a narrative — not a thing sitting in the head, but an abstractum or a useful fiction that our storytelling practices project, and it's real in the load-bearing sense, the way a center of mass is real even though you can't point to it, because it constrains what the person actually does and says. Camp B: narrating yourself is just an optional psychological activity; plenty of perfectly functional people barely do it, they live episode to episode rather than as a continuous protagonist, and Camp A has smuggled a contingent habit of a certain kind of person into a claim about what a self even is.

Write the joint statement these two could both put their names to after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want, in order:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides accept — about what each side is actually claiming, about the phenomenology both can agree on, about the ambiguity of "narrative" (a story told, a disposition to tell stories, a structural feature of experience — which sense is each side using?), and about what would have to be true for something to count as a self at all.
2. A disagreements section where each side's position is stated in wording that side would accept — no strawmen, no sneaking in the other side's vocabulary.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one side. I expect at least one of these is whether the dispute is about the metaphysics of selves or about the psychology of self-experience, but I don't want you to just assert that — earn it.
4. For each crux, a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Somewhere in there, tell me straight whether part of this is a verbal fight. If one camp is answering "what is a self?" and the other is answering "how do people experience themselves?", say so, and then be precise about which apparent disagreements evaporate once you seperate the questions and which survive intact. That's the part I care most about.

Two cases I want both sides to predict about, at minimum. First: someone in mid-life adopts a wholesale new life story that flatly contradicts the old one, and their behavior changes to match it. Does that vindicate Camp A (the narrative was doing real causal work) or does it just show Camp B is right that the story is a swappable tool? Second: the existence of people who report no diachronic self-narrative at all. Does that refute the narrative thesis, or does it only show the thesis is about a subset, or a practice rather than a person?

Don't give me a bibliography, don't cite anyone, and don't lean on who said what — argue it out from the positions themselves. Keep the prose tight, not bullet-point mush, and write it in American English.
