I am a retired physiologist — most of my working life was spent on energy balance and feeding behaviour in rodents — and I have spent the seven years since my retirement reading philosophy with far more appetite than training. I have no settled view on the question below, which is part of why I am bringing it to you instead of settling it myself. I would like you to act as a philosopher of mind who is also technically literate in reinforcement learning and in the affective-science literature on valence, and to adjudicate the exchange I am about to report as carefully as you can, keeping distinct at every point two questions that my friends kept running together: what valence is, and how we could ever tell whether something has it.

Last month, at a reading group I attend, two of the younger members — I will call them Alex and Bea — fell into a dispute that the rest of us could not resolve. The question on the table was whether an agent trained by reinforcement learning can be said to suffer.

Alex said the reward signal simply is the agent's valence. Valence, he said, is nothing more than the way a system encodes how good or bad a state is, so that behaviour can be shaped accordingly, and a scalar reward does exactly that. If we train an agent whose reward is maximised by states that, in a creature, we would have no hesitation calling agony, then we have built something that suffers; we should say so, and worry about it.

Bea said that what Alex had described was a control signal, not a feeling. A reward is a number the training algorithm uses; the agent itself need not represent it at all, and where nothing represents a state as bad for the agent, there is nothing it is like to be in that state. She offered a test: if you replaced the trained policy with a lookup table producing the same behaviour in every circumstance, and nothing whatever were lost — no experience, no complaint, no change in what the system could do — then the reward was never anything to the agent, only to us.

Alex answered that the test begs the question. A creature whose reward channel were swapped for a table would notice, and would notice precisely because its reward channel is felt; you cannot use the felt character of the substitution to establish felt character. He then narrowed his claim: perhaps the reward is not pleasure but something thinner — wanting rather than liking, in the sense of the dissociation literature, where animals will work for things they do not, by the usual measures, enjoy. But wanting, he said, is enough. An agent whose reward is chronically unobtainable is not merely inefficient; it is thwarted, and to be thwarted is to be badly off in a way that matters to the agent itself.

Bea replied that this trade was a bad one. If the reward is wanting and not liking, she can grant the agent something like a drive and still deny it anything like a hurt; a thwarted wanting in a system with no experience is a stalled process, not a misfortune. She added that the reward function sits outside the agent: the agent never represents its reward, never represents the fact that it has ends at all, and so cannot be in conflict with itself about them. The gap between what one wants and what one does — the gap that makes weakness of will and addiction intelligible — has no analogue here.

Alex's last move was that the analogy to human beings is shakier than she thinks. Nobody chose their dopaminergic wiring, and an agent's policy can fail to maximise its reward through exploration, approximation error, or myopia, so there is a gap between the reward and the behaviour here too. If the gap in the addict is a conflict, why is the gap in the agent only an error?

At that point the group broke up, and I confess I had been nodding at both of them.

I would like you to do three things. First, before you judge anything, spend a little time mapping the terrain: set out the different things "valence" and "suffering" might be taken to mean in this discussion, and say which of the disagreements look verbal and which look substantive. Then go through the exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating — and where a move is weak, say precisely what its strongest version would have been. Finally, tell me what both Alex and Bea are missing, and give an overall verdict: which of the two is closer to right about the central question, and on which points, if any, are they both wrong? Think carefully, and be explicit wherever your answer turns on a choice of framework rather than on a fact.
