Examination question: Aristotle discusses money in two quite different places — the account of household management and acquisition in Politics Book I, and the account of reciprocal justice in Nicomachean Ethics Book V. (a) Set out the distinction Aristotle draws in the Politics between household management and the art of acquisition, and explain his grounds for separating a natural from an unnatural kind of acquisition and for his verdict on usury. (b) Set out the argument of Ethics V.5: why exchange requires proportionate reciprocity, what difficulty Aristotle raises about the commensurability of the goods exchanged, and what role he assigns to money and to need. (c) Do the two discussions fit together? Say whether the account of money in the Ethics is consistent with the criticism of moneymaking in the Politics, and defend your answer.

Student's answer: "In Politics I.8–10 Aristotle distinguishes household management from the art of acquisition. Household management uses things for their proper purpose, while acquisition is the art of getting them. He then separates two kinds of acquisition: the natural kind, which belongs to household management and is limited by the needs it serves, and the unnatural kind — chrematistics, or retail trade — which has no limit because its end is money itself rather than use. His criterion is that natural acquisition takes what nature provides (farming, herding, hunting), whereas exchange takes from other people; that is why he treats commerce as unnatural. On the same grounds he condemns usury most strongly: money was invented to facilitate exchange, not to breed, so lending at interest makes a profit from money itself and is the most contrary to nature of all the ways of getting wealth. In Ethics V.5 Aristotle asks how exchange can be just. Since exchange requires proportionate reciprocity — the builder must get back from the shoemaker a quantity of shoes proportionate to the value of the house — the goods exchanged must be commensurable. Aristotle notes that things so different cannot be commensurable in themselves, and he introduces money as the solution: money is a measure that makes goods comparable, and it exists not by nature but by convention, since it is we who fix it and can change it. So money expresses a commensurability the goods already have in themselves; it is a convenient token for a natural equality of value. He adds that need holds the community together, since no one would exchange if they did not need what the other has. Putting the two together: the Ethics makes money a conventional measure of value, while the Politics treats money as the end of an unnatural and unlimited pursuit. The two are consistent, because the same conventionality that lets money measure also explains why usury is wrong: if money is only a token by agreement, then treating it as something that naturally generates more of itself contradicts what money is for."
