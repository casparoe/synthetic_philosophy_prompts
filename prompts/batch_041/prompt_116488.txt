Here's the question that was on the exam:

Conditionals seem to come in at least two flavours — indicative ones like "If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, somebody else did," and counterfactual ones like "If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, somebody else would have." (a) Explain the standard reason for thinking these two sentences can't be given the same semantics, using that pair. (b) Adams tied the indicative conditional to conditional probability; say what the connection is and what it does and doesn't commit him to. (c) A classmate says the difference between the two flavours is "just pragmatic — same truth conditions, different conversational packaging." Assess that claim.

And here's what the student wrote:

"(a) The standard reason is the Oswald–Kennedy pair. Both sentences have the same antecedent and consequent clauses, and the only real difference between them is mood. Yet intuitively the indicative one is true — if Oswald didn't do it, someone else must have — while the counterfactual is false, or at least not something we'd assert, because in the nearest worlds where Oswald doesn't shoot, Kennedy survives. Since two sentences can't have different truth values while sharing truth conditions, they can't both be material conditionals built from the same antecedent and consequent, so the material-conditional analysis of 'if' has to fail for at least one of the two flavours.

(b) Adams' thesis is that the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent: P(if A, B) = P(B|A). So Adams is claiming that an indicative conditional is true just in case that conditional probability is high. This is why indicatives obey probabilistic rather than truth-functional laws — for instance, if you think the antecedent is probably false, you should think the conditional is probably false too, since there's little chance of getting both A and B together.

(c) I think the classmate is wrong. The Oswald–Kennedy pair shows the two conditionals differ in truth value, so the difference can't be merely a matter of packaging; it has to be semantic. Grice and Jackson do try to save the material conditional by treating the extra content as conversational implicature, but that only ever works for the indicative flavour, and in any case the counterfactual 'if Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have' has no truth value on the best treatments — Lewis showed counterfactuals can't be material conditionals, and if they can't be truth-functional then they can't have truth conditions at all."
