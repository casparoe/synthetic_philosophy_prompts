After thirty-five years as a marine biologist, most of them spent on the husbandry and physiology of farmed finfish and decapod crustaceans, I have retired and spent the last few years reading moral philosophy. I now find myself stuck on a question that my former colleagues regard as sentimental and my new ones as already settled. I would like an essay that settles it for me.

The controversy is this. Practices that are entirely unremarkable in commercial aquaculture — holding animals at high stocking densities, killing fish by slow chilling or asphyxiation rather than by stunning, boiling crustaceans alive or dismembering them before cooking, ablating the eyestalk of broodstock shrimp to induce spawning — are defended on the ground that the animals concerned either do not feel pain at all or feel something so rudimentary that no welfare duty survives contact with the ordinary costs of reform. The opposing view holds that the evidence is already sufficient for a duty of care, and that continued inaction is a moral failure rather than a scientific caution. Some jurisdictions have begun to write decapods into their animal welfare law; others treat the question as unresolved and therefore as no reason to act.

I want more than a survey of both sides. My own provisional position is a rule I have been calling graded precaution. It would scale the protection owed to a taxon with three things at once: my credence that the taxon is sentient, the number of individuals a given practice affects, and the intensity and duration of what the practice does to them. It would also place the burden of proof on whoever proposes to retain a harmful practice rather than on whoever proposes to stop it. I am uneasily aware that this may be a rationalization dressed up as a principle, or that it may covertly assume the very thing it is supposed to establish about sentience.

Please write an essay that adjudicates the controversy and returns a verdict. Consider at least the following: what the behavioral, physiological, and pharmacological evidence for pain in fish and decapods can and cannot establish; whether sentience is better treated as a threshold or as a magnitude; and whether uncertainty about other minds is a reason for restraint or merely a reason for hesitation. Say plainly where graded precaution succeeds and where it fails, and if it fails, say what principle ought to replace it. Reach a definite conclusion and defend it; do not close on a neutral inventory of the options. I have no use for a reading list — argue from the substance.
