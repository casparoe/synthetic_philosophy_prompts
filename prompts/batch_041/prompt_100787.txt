I teach a seminar that pairs late-Republican Rome with modern worries about democratic backsliding, and every year the room splits into two camps that just shout past each other. Call them the structuralists and the contingents. Structuralists: once you've got professional armies loyal to their commanders rather than to the state, a tribunate that any ambitious man can turn into a personal weapon, and a Senate that treats every serious reformer as a would-be tyrant, the Republic is already dead — Sulla and Caesar are symptoms, not causes. Contingents: no, it's all decisions. If a handful of people had acted differently at a handful of moments (the land commissions, the Italian citizenship question, the proscriptions, the crossing of the Rubicon), a working republic survives; the structural story is hindsight dressed up as necessity.

They've been at this for a term and it's gone nowhere, because "inevitable" is doing about four different jobs at once. I want the document they should have written instead: a joint adversarial-collaboration statement, numbered agreed-claims first, then a disagreements table.

Before you start, impose one piece of machinery on both camps, because I think it's the thing that's actually missing. Distinguish at least three grades of inevitability: (a) nomological or covering-law inevitability; (b) threshold or entrenchment inevitability — past some point the cost of reversing course exceeds what any realistically placed actor could bear, so no rational agent inside the system would even attempt it; and (c) strict "no available alternative," meaning no feasible coalition, at any point, could have held the thing together. Then give me an operational test both sides can sign for locating the threshold in (b). I've been calling it the restoration test: identify the last moment at which a competent, well-placed actor could have restored a functioning republican constitution at a cost that actor would plausibly pay, and treat that moment as the point of no return. Both camps should be able to sign the test even though they'll disagree wildly about where the moment falls.

Then give me: the agreed claims (about what the late Republic's institutions were actually doing — the veto, the emergency decree, the extended commands, the shift from a census-based levy to volunteers; about what counts as "the Republic falling," a constitutional event versus a slow social process; and about what the modern-democracy analogy can and can't carry). The dissagreements, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording of its own view. The cruxes, and for each crux a case or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I want at least one crux that's a comparative question — do other republics with the same structural features but different individual luck collapse on the same schedule? — and one about the analogy itself: if the structuralists are right about Rome, does that license any prediction about modern democracies, or does the disanalogy (no peer competitor, mass electorates, whatever you think matters) kill the inference? Keep the modern side schematic — no naming particular countries and thier current events, I want the structure, not the news.

Under two pages, no citations. And if part of their fight turns out to be about the word "inevitable" rather than about Rome, say which part and why.
