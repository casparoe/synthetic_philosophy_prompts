I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether the simulation argument should make us less fanatical about the far future, and I have come away genuinely unsure which of us was defending the more defensible line. His case, compressed: if we take seriously the possibility that we are living in a simulation — that some appreciable fraction of civilizations at our stage run ancestor simulations, so that simulated observers like us should assign non-trivial credence to being simulated — then a large class of longtermist reasoning loses its grip. The astronomical future whose value is supposed to swamp every competing consideration might itself be simulated, might be paused or switched off at the simulators' convenience, might be one run among many, and might be valued by whoever runs it in ways we cannot anticipate. And since the hypothesis is supposed to apply to observers like us symmetrically, it cannot be used to privilege the real future over the simulated one. The upshot, he says, is that the expected value of far-future-directed action should be heavily discounted, and with it the fanaticism that insists even a one-in-a-million chance of steering astronomical value settles what we ought to do. I think the argument proves too much — that whatever it does to far-future stakes it does equally to everything else, and that a discount applied uniformly is no argument against fanaticism at all — but I could not make the objection stick to his satisfaction, and I am no longer certain it is even the right objection.

What I want from you is not a survey of positions but a number and its derivation. Please give me your credence in the following claim: once the simulation argument is taken seriously, the expected value of far-future-directed interventions is reduced by enough to defeat fanaticism — that is, there is no longer any action whose astronomically large far-future stakes justify ignoring ordinary near-term considerations. Decompose it. I want at least: your credence that we are in a simulation, and a word on whether the exact value even matters or only whether it is non-negligible; the probability, conditional on our being simulated, that the far future we could influence still carries the kind of value longtermists assign to it, which I take to require that the simulation persists, that simulated welfare counts, and that the simulators do not intervene in ways that swamp our influence; the probability that the argument is symmetric in the way I suspect — that it discounts all actions roughly equally and therefore leaves the comparative case for far-future action intact; and the probability that fanaticism survives even a severe discount, because the far-future numbers are large enough that a heavy haircut still leaves them dominant. Add any term you think I have left out, give each a number and one line of justification, and show how they combine.

Then tell me, on the strength of that decomposition, whether my friend or I have the better of the argument, and which single sub-question the final number is most sensitive to — the one where a modest change in the input would flip your answer. I would also like to know what evidence or argument would move you most, and in which direction. I would rather have your committed verdict, with reasons, than a list of the ways the question could be framed. For what it is worth, I came into the argument at about 0.2 on the claim, and I suspect I am now talking myself lower without any new reason, which is exactly the kind of drift I would like you to correct for.
