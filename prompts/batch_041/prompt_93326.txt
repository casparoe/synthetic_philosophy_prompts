I live in a small town in the hills that's been shrinking ever since the mill shut down, and last month my neighbour Rosa — retired schoolteacher, reads a lot, half crank — talked the farmers' market into running its own paper scrip. You buy the credits with ordinary money, spend them at the stalls, and the stallholders can either spend them back into the market or cash them out. Rosa asked me to keep the ledger, and I said I'd think about it, and now I'm catching it from all sides. My uncle keeps his savings in gold coins under the floorboards and says the whole thing is counterfeit — real money has to be *backed* by something, and paper from a schoolteacher isn't. My sister did two years of economics and says it's just barter wearing a costume, that anything you can buy with credits you could have traded for directly, so the credits are decoration. And then there's Rosa's actual pitch, which is the part that's bugging me. She says the town has spent thirty years trying to attract money — grants, a casino proposal, a call centre, tax breaks for a warehouse — and that we've got it backwards. Money, she says, is just a ticket you hand over to get at the real stuff: the food, the labour, the tools, the roof repairs. A town that can grow food and fix roofs and teach kids is rich, whatever its bank balance looks like, and we'd stop feeling poor the day we stopped counting tickets. She's got a photocopy of some old pamphlet she waves at people about money being a ticket or a counter, which does not exactly settle it for me.

What I want isn't a slogan, I want to know where this picture is actually right and where it cracks. Is the scrip money in the same sense the gold under the floor is money, or is one of them the real thing and the other a promise? Does it matter that one is a metal? Is my sister right that we've just reinvented barter, or is she missing something about what the credits are *for*? And here's what I can't square: if Rosa is right that a place can be wealthy without money, then why does it obviously matter how much money the town has — I can't pay my mortgage or buy diesel in market credits, and when the scheme ran short last spring the whole thing nearly folded. So either she's wrong somewhere or I'm misunderstanding her. Take the ledger job or not, and what exactly do I say to my uncle and my sister at dinner, because I'd like to say something true rather than something clever. If the picture is basically sound I want to know what it's committed to; if it isn't, I want to know precisely which step fails, since I'd be the one holding the books when it does.
