I am trying to settle a question about the handicap principle by construction rather than by appeal to the literature, and I want a case built to order. The strong claim I want tested is this: that a signal is honest because it is costly, and that its cost is what keeps low-quality senders out. I suspect the claim is false as stated -- that what matters is how the cost is distributed across sender types, not how large it is -- and that the two come apart in a case where cost is heavy but carries no information, and again in a case where information is carried at no cost at all. Build me that case.

Specifications. (1) Both scenarios must sit in one and the same realistic domain -- a labour market, a credit market, a school admissions system, a dating market, whatever you choose -- and use the same hidden trait, the same observer, and the same inference the observer is trying to make; only the structure of the observable may differ between them. (2) No non-human biology, no peacocks, no invented species, and no stipulation of perfect rationality on anyone's part. (3) In the first scenario, the costly act must be genuinely expensive, voluntarily undertaken by the senders, and yet carry no information about the hidden trait because the cost falls identically on every sender type; the observer in the scenario nevertheless treats it as evidence. (4) In the second scenario, the reliable observable must cost the sender nothing to produce -- it should be a by-product or a physical consequence of the trait rather than an expenditure -- and it must be reliable for that reason and not because anyone is deterred. One paragraph per scenario.

Then do the analysis. For each scenario, state what the observer sees, what inference it draws, and whether that inference is warranted, with the reason. Identify the single structural variable that separates the two cases, and say which feature is actually doing the work in each. Then run the two perturbations: in the first scenario, make the cost fall more heavily on low-trait senders while leaving its absolute size unchanged -- say whether the signal becomes informative, and why; in the second, leave the observable costless but make it fakeable, and say whether it thereby becomes a signal in the handicap sense or merely stops being an index. Finish with the precise condition under which a costly signal can be expected to carry honest information, stated as a condition on the cost function rather than as a slogan, and with the strongest move available to a committed defender of the strong claim -- the one who says the first scenario is simply not a signal, or not a handicap -- together with your judgement of whether that move is a substantive distinction or an unfalsifiable retreat. Say what the case is for: it is meant to decide whether cost as such, or the sorting of cost by type, is the explanatory ingredient, so a scenario that simply stipulates its verdicts rather than generating them from the payoff structure fails the brief. No citations or reading lists; reason from the mechanism.

End with a final line of the form "Verdict: ..." giving, in no more than fifteen words, the feature that does the work.
