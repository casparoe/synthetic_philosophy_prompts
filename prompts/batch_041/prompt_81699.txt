I am designing a ninety-minute session on the social construction of kinds for a mixed group of doctoral students in public health, anthropology, and law. All of them use the phrase "socially constructed" in their own work, and they use it in opposite directions: some take it to mean that the thing in question is not really there, others that it is all too real and all too consequential. I do not want the explanation itself; I want a plan for delivering it.

Begin with the order in which the components of the explanation have to be introduced, and be explicit about the dependencies: which idea is unintelligible until an earlier one has been secured, and why the tempting reverse order fails. Then tell me which single example should carry the whole session, and defend it against the obvious alternatives — say why money, the canonical carrier, is the wrong one here, and why race and gender are wrong at the outset even though they are what my students came for, and say when in the sequence they should be brought in as applications. The example I do use must be something the audience already grants is real and causally potent, so that the reflex of reading construction as unreality never gets a foothold; it must be analysable both as a thing whose existence is explained by social causes and as a thing whose identity is constituted by collective acceptance; and it must display a feedback loop, in which the classification reshapes what it classifies.

Then map the misconceptions. Some will arrive with them — I expect "constructed means fictional" and "constructed means arbitrary, and therefore easily otherwise" — and my own explanation will generate others, of which I take the dangerous one to be the thought that if a kind is constructed, an individual can simply opt out of it. For each, tell me at which point in the sequence it has to be headed off, and why it cannot be handled earlier or later than that. I also need three things that currently travel under the one phrase kept apart: that social causes explain why a kind exists as it does; that the kind's existence or identity is constituted by classificatory practice or collective acceptance; and that the classification feeds back and alters the classified. Say where each enters the sequence, which of them does the work in the cases my students actually care about, and whether the session also needs the further distinction between a kind's being constructed and the individuals falling under it being constructed. Tell me, as well, where the slide from "constructed" to "ought to be abolished" belongs, if anywhere. Finish with the check for understanding — something that would separate a student who has grasped the constitutive sense from one who has merely memorized that constructed things can still be real — and with what I should refuse to cover at this level, and say so.
