Ok so I'm a novelist, two years into a book set in the 1890s, and a researcher freind of mine has argued me into a corner I can't climb out of. Her argument, as best I can set it down:

(1) What anyone can know about the past depends on what got written down and what got kept.
(2) What gets written down and kept is decided by whoever has the power to write and to keep.
(3) So every archive is a portrait of the powerful and a silence about everybody else.
(4) So a historian working only from archives can reconstruct the view from above, and the lives of the powerless are strictly unrecoverable.
(5) And since every surviving source is somebody's archive, all history is the history of power.

I'd like to keep (1) and (2) — they seem like just, true. But (5) feels like it's eating its own tail, and not in an interesting way: her own evidence for (3) is that she's read alot of archives and found the gaps in them. And I keep tripping over a counterexample I can't place. In the parish and police records for the village my lot came from, there are page after page about paupers, poachers, lunatics, servants — names, ages, wages, scars — and almost nothing at all about the family that owned the place, who apparently did everything through a solicitor in London. The people with the power seem to have left the thinnest trail and the people with none left the thickest. That can't be right; or if it is right, it must be for a reason more interesting than "power = records".

So: which step do I give up, why did it look fine in the first place, and what do I lose by giving it up? Please in British English, and don't fob me off with "it's complicated".
