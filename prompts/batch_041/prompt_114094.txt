I am a high school senior writing an independent research paper in philosophy. I have roughly nine months, one supervisor, and no way to run experiments, surveys, or anything else that would generate new data, so whatever I produce will be conceptual and argumentative. My topic is interpersonal utility comparisons: whether, and on what basis, it can be true that one person is better or worse off than another.

What drew me in is that these comparisons are made constantly — a doctor triaging two patients, a donor choosing between causes, a legislature weighing two policies — while it remains unclear what could make any particular one correct. A friend has proposed a test that he thinks settles the matter: to compare two people, imagine a single person who has lived both lives completely, with both sets of memories, feelings, and attachments, and ask which of the two lives she would rather have lived. On his view this not only fixes the comparison but shows that comparability is something we construct rather than something we discover. I am not convinced, and I cannot tell whether the questions his proposal raises are the ones worth my year.

So: given that I can only work from the armchair, which open questions about interpersonal comparison should I actually prioritize, and why? I care more about the reasoning than the list. For each question, explain what it is upstream of — which other questions in ethics, political philosophy, or decision-making genuinely depend on its answer — how tractable it is for someone with no data, and whether it is neglected or already crowded. Tell me as well which questions look central but would be a poor use of nine months for a beginner, and what evidence or argument over the next year would move an item up or down. I am especially interested in whether the important work lies in whether comparisons can be placed on a common scale at all, in which comparisons a given ethical view actually requires (for instance, whether a view needs only to rank people, or also to compare the sizes of gains and losses), or in what to say about pairs of people who seem to be neither better nor worse off than each other.

Please separate what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who know it well, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus; where you are unsure which side is stronger, say so rather than smoothing it over.
