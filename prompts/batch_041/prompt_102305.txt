I have recently been appointed to my college's Faculty Academic Integrity Board, and the first case to come before us has turned out to be uncomfortably close to home. I have been asked to draft the written opinion the Board will circulate and vote on at next Thursday's meeting, and I would like your help producing it.

The case concerns a senior who submitted a laboratory report containing three consecutive paragraphs reproduced without attribution from a published article. She does not contest the facts. Her defense is that the charge was initiated by a classmate who sits on our student Honor Council and who, two years ago, was herself found to have copied answers on a problem set and was let off with a warning after her record was expunged under our confidentiality policy. The student argues that this classmate had no standing to accuse her, and that a process begun by a hypocrite cannot fairly end in her sanction. Our handbook is silent on the accuser's own record; it provides only that any member of the community may bring a charge.

Two further complications bear on the opinion and not merely on my own conscience. First, three years ago this same Board declined to sanction a professor who had used a graduate student's writing without attribution, on the reasoning that his conduct fell below the threshold for discipline. Whatever we decide about the student, we are a body with a record. Second, and this is why I am writing to you rather than simply handing the matter to a colleague, I have lately rediscovered a seminar paper of my own, written as a graduate student, in which I borrowed several sentences without marking them as quotations. No one noticed; I was not disciplined. I am not sure what, if anything, that should mean for my role in this case, but I do not want to write an opinion that quietly exempts me.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend a concrete course of action, whether to proceed, to decline, or to proceed subject to stated conditions, and it should give a time frame for it. It should treat the standing objection seriously rather than dismissing it as an excuse, and it should distinguish clearly between the question whether the student did what she is accused of and the question whether the person who accused her, or the body judging her, is entitled to do so. It should say whether the Board ought to address its own past decision and whether I ought to recuse myself. And it should state, in a form my colleagues can act on, the strongest argument for the course we are not taking. Assume that no one on the Board will consult any outside source before the vote; the reasoning has to stand on its own.
