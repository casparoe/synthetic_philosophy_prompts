For thirty years I was a field ecologist — population dynamics, mostly ungulates — and since retiring I have been trying, with more enthusiasm than method, to teach myself moral and political philosophy; the upshot is that I now hold opinions about rent control, which my family finds tiresome. What puzzles me is not which side is right but that neither side seems to work from a stated standard. When a city proposes a rent freeze, one camp treats the market price as the baseline and demands that any departure justify itself, then observes that a ceiling does not abolish scarcity but only forces the shortage into other channels — queues for vacancies, side payments to landlords, deferred maintenance, discrimination among applicants, and after a decade or so, fewer buildings; the other camp replies that the clearing rent is nobody's desert, that a tenant paying two thirds of her income for a flat she cannot practically leave is not a paradigm of free exchange, and that the relevant market was never competitive to begin with. The same shape recurs with the minimum wage, where one side treats the floor as the interference and the other treats the employer's wage-setting power, in a labour market with one dominant buyer, as the prior distortion; and both sides go curiously quiet about wartime rationing and price freezes, which appear to have been both necessary and workable, and about the difference between a ceiling imposed for six months of emergency and one maintained for thirty years. Everyone I read seems to reason backwards from the policy they favour to the criterion that blesses it.

I want the criterion first. Please set out what any adequate theory of justified price control — a floor or a ceiling, in labour or in housing — has to deliver, on the assumption that I mean to use it to decide a real vote and to defend the outcome to the people who lose by it, since a standard that cannot be defended to the losers is of no use to me. Give it to me as exactly five numbered items and nothing else: no preamble, no closing paragraph, no scorecard of named authors or studies. Each item should be a sentence or two naming one requirement, the case or consideration that forces it (a freeze in a supply-constrained city, a wage floor where a single employer dominates the local labour market, an emergency ceiling, a durable one, a queue that forms precisely because the price cannot move), and your judgement of whether the requirement is non-negotiable or merely the prejudice of one camp. Where two of the five pull against each other, say so inside the item where the pull is felt rather than saving it for a summary; and in whichever item does the most to rule out the theories people actually hold, say that it does.
