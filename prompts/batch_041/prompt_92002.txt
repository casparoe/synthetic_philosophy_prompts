I'm a backend engineer turned tech lead at a forty-ish person startup. I have no philosophy training at all, and I keep running into the same shape of problem: a move that's clearly best for me individually, that nobody will ever notice, and that makes things slightly worse for everyone if enough of us make it. Crank the concurrency on the shared CI box because my pipeline is slow today. Run the fat ad-hoc query against the prod replica instead of waiting for the nightly sync. Skip the design doc because we're already two days behind. Hardcode the config value instead of fixing the shared helper that everyone else also has to work around.

A friend pointed me at Hofstadter's superrationality stuff — the Platonia setup with all the perfect reasoners, and the lottery where the prize gets divided by the number of entries so every extra entry you send in is a small cost to everybody and a small gain to you. That feels like exactly the shape of my problem. But I don't want a philosophy essay. I want a procedure my team can actually run.

So: write me a decision procedure for this class of decisions, grounded in whatever superrationality actually says, that a senior engineer with no theory background can run in about fifteen minutes before a design review. Questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a hard no, and where it hands the decision back to a human because the remaining question is values and not facts. It has to be short enough that I can paste it into a wiki page, and it can't require any probability math beyond eyeballing rough numbers. The staff engineer and my manager both have to sign off on it, so it needs to survive a skeptical reading.

Two things I specifically want it to nail, because I'd get them wrong on my own. First, the difference between this and plain "what if everyone did it" golden-rule reasoning — I'm pretty sure those give different verdicts in some cases and I want to know which parts of the procedure are just the golden rule wearing a lab coat. Second, what exactly makes the other person's decision correlated with mine in the first place: whether my teammate deciding the same way is something I'm causing, or something that just happens because we're both the same kind of reasoner under the same pressure, and whether that distinction should change my answer.

Then run it on three cases and show your work: (1) me bumping my share of the shared CI box, (2) my team skipping design docs during a crunch, (3) me deciding whether to enter a company-wide thing where everyone who contributes a little gets a lot more back — I want at least one of these to come out in a way I won't like, and I want you to say which one and why.

Be upfront about where the procedure will misfire and which steps are just judgment dressed up as procedure. Don't send me to papers or books, I won't read them.

Open with the procedure itself — the steps in order, short enough to fit on a sticky note — and then unpack each step with the reasoning behind it. No intro paragraph, no restating my question.
