My partner and I are buying a flat, and we have narrowed our search to two in the same new development. As far as either of us can establish, they are indistinguishable in every respect that could matter to a buyer: the same layout, the same fittings, the same aspect and light, the same measured noise, the same distance from the station, the same view of the same courtyard. I know how contrived that sounds; take the indiscernibility as given. We have looked hard and found nothing, and the only thing anyone can point to is that one is on the left of the other as you face the block and carries a different number on the door. My partner says that since there is nothing to choose between them there is no fact of the matter about which we ought to buy, so we should draw lots and stop deliberating. I have a stubborn sense that this is wrong — that the left flat and the right flat are genuinely two flats, and that drawing lots would paper over a real choice rather than settle a fake one — but when I try to say what the difference consists in, all I can produce is "it is that one", the bare fact of its being this particular flat rather than the other, and I can hear how empty that sounds. A colleague insists that each flat has a thisness over and above its features, which is what makes it the flat it is; my partner says that when two options are equal you do not choose, you pick, the way you pick a card from a shuffled deck, and that there is nothing here to agonise over. I cannot tell whether these are two ways of saying the same thing or whether one of them is a confusion.

Two further thoughts keep me stuck. First, it seems to me that if the two flats were somehow to exchange places overnight — the left becoming the right and the right the left, in every detail — we would never know and nothing we cared about would be different, which makes me doubt that left and right are differences in the flats at all. Second, I half-wonder whether there is really only one flat here, counted twice, and whether "both of them" is merely a manner of speaking. I have read enough rationalist writing over the years to suspect that a tangle like this dissolves once one draws the right distinction, and I would sooner have the distinction than a verdict — though I will take the verdict if you have one.

So: is there a real difference between the two flats? If there is, say what it is and why drawing lots is or is not the right response to it. If there is not, say what my sense that there is one is tracking, and whether it should change what we do. Tell me how confident you are in your answer, and single out the point you are least sure of.
