I'm a third-year PhD student in economics, working on mechanism design and contract theory, and this term I've been sitting in on a political philosophy reading group mainly to steal problems. One idea from that group has stuck with me, and I'd like to see it rebuilt in the vocabulary I actually think in. The idea is moral trade. Two agents who disagree about which values matter most — say one is convinced that animal suffering is the cardinal moral issue, the other that global poverty is, and neither thinks the other is simply confused or wicked — may still be able to strike a trade that each regards as an improvement on the status quo: the animal person gives money to an anti-poverty charity, the poverty person goes vegetarian, and each gives up something she cares about comparatively little in exchange for something she cares about more. The claim, as I understand it, is that this is the moral analogue of gains from trade, so that the space of permissible moral disagreement is not zero-sum and an unexploited moral trade is a kind of inefficiency. I don't want a bibliography or an attribution — I want the machinery.

So: restate the debate around moral trade as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of mechanism design and contract theory. Types and preferences over allocations, the disagreement point, Pareto improvements, participation and incentive-compatibility constraints, transferable versus non-transferable utility, the revelation principle, the second welfare theorem, commitment and the credibility of threats. Be explicit about where you have to add structure that the philosophers never specify: what is a type when an agent's type is partly a moral view she takes herself to be committed to, and what is an allocation when the good being traded is the agent's own conduct rather than a bundle of goods she can hand across a table?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about: which parts of the philosophical debate survive the translation with their content intact, which survive only as a distortion, and which have no counterpart at all. I'm especially interested in three candidates for hard residue. First, deontological constraint: if an agent treats some act as simply off the table rather than as very costly, does she have a preference ordering at all, and does modelling the constraint as infinite disutility or as lexicographic priority beg the question rather than capture it? Second, moral blackmail: someone credibly threatens to do something you regard as monstrous unless you pay him, and the standard apparatus will cheerfully report that paying can be efficient; I want to know whether the framework can say anything about why that conclusion nonetheless strikes us as wrong, or whether the wrongness is precisely what falls out of the model. Third, integrity and the alleged inalienability of moral conduct: is there anything in the apparatus that can represent the thought that a moral action is not the sort of thing that can be traded at all, as opposed to a thing with a very high price? Finish by telling me whether the residue looks like a defect in my vocabulary — something mechanism design is blind to — or evidence that the philosophers' disagreement is doing less work than it appears to.
