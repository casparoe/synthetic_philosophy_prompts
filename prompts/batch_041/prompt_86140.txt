I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about backward induction, and I have collected a set of verdicts on particular games that I cannot get to hang together. I would like you to tell me what principle, or what small family of principles, they add up to; what those principles imply for cases I have not considered; and which of my verdicts I ought to give up.

One stipulation first, so that the arithmetic is not in dispute. In an N-node centipede, the player to move at node k may take, in which case she receives k + 2 and the other player receives k, or she may pass; if all N nodes are passed, each receives N + 1. At every node, then, taking pays the mover one unit more than passing and being taken from at the next node, and taking at the first node is the unique subgame-perfect outcome.

1. The centipede with eight nodes. My verdict: both players should pass all the way, and taking at node 1 is a mistake. I hold the same verdict, and hold it more firmly, for the hundred-node version, where taking at node 1 wins 3 and passing to the end wins 101 apiece.

2. The centipede with three nodes. My verdict: taking at node 1 is entirely defensible. The cooperative prize is 4 apiece, the sure thing is 3, and I would not think badly of a player who took it.

3. A finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma, fifty rounds, with payoffs of 4 for defecting against cooperation, 3 for mutual cooperation, 2 for mutual defection and 1 for cooperating against defection, held fixed throughout and with the final round known to both players. My verdict: defecting on the last round is obviously correct, the unraveling argument is sound, and a rational opponent will defect from the first round, so I defect from the first round too.

4. The chain-store game: an incumbent faces twenty markets in a fixed order; in each, a single entrant chooses whether to enter; if entry occurs the incumbent chooses to fight or to accommodate; entrants observe the whole history of earlier markets; fighting is costly for the incumbent, accommodating is cheap for it and profitable for the entrant. My verdict: the incumbent should fight in the early markets and accommodate in the last few. I think backward induction gets the final market right and the sequence wrong.

5. The traveler's dilemma: two players simultaneously name a whole number between 2 and 100; the lower number is paid to both, with a reward of 2 to the player who named it and a penalty of 2 to the other. My verdict: I name 100, and I think naming 2 is a mistake even against someone I know to be a careful student of these games, though I grant that the iterated-dominance argument for 2 is airtight.

6. A transparency variant of case 1. Before playing the eight-node centipede, each player writes a program that will play on her behalf, and the two programs are then shown to both players before either is run; each program is then played against the other. My verdict: both write programs that pass at every node if and only if the other program does, and the outcome is 9 apiece. I take this to show something about what players can achieve when their strategies are observable, not something about the centipede's game tree.

7. The prisoner's dilemma repeated indefinitely with a fixed continuation probability. My verdict: cooperation is obviously right, but I do not think this is the same phenomenon as case 1, and I would be suspicious of any principle that treats the two alike.

What I most want resolved is the pair in 1 and 3. In the centipede I ignore a one-unit last-node gain and cooperate; in the repeated prisoner's dilemma I let a one-unit last-round gain unravel fifty rounds of cooperation. In both games the cooperative prize is large, the last-round gain is small, and the opponent is stipulated to be rational and self-interested. I cannot see what distinguishes them, and every distinction I can think of seems to cut the wrong way somewhere else. Case 4, for instance, seems to me to require something like reputation, yet case 1 has no reputation mechanism and I still want to cooperate there. Case 6 embarrasses me a little for the same reason: if transparency is what makes cooperation available, it is not clear why I cooperate in 1 without it.

I have heard the standard replies — that common knowledge of rationality is a strong assumption, that a little uncertainty about the other player's type can sustain cooperation, that an equilibrium concept is a claim about what happens at a node rather than a claim about which nodes are reached — and none of them tells me which of my verdicts to keep. So please do not simply survey them. State the principle, or the small family of principles, that best fits these verdicts; say for each clause which case forces it; say what the principle predicts for at least two cases I have not listed; and say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and why that verdict rather than its rival should go. If you think one of my verdicts is simply confused — if, say, I am importing a norm of cooperation into a game where nothing supports it — say that too, and say what should replace it.
