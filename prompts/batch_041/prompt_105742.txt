The Tuskegee syphilis study — the Public Health Service's forty-year observation of hundreds of Black men in Alabama, begun in 1932 and halted in 1972 after a journalist exposed it — is usually discussed as a case in research ethics. I want it used instead as a test case for the epistemology of conspiracy theories. Long before the exposure, the suspicion that the government was secretly experimenting on Black patients was widespread and was routinely dismissed; afterwards, the dismissal looked like the error. Bring two positions from that literature to bear. First, the generalist one: conspiracy theories as a class deserve a presumption of disbelief, both because they characteristically explain away contrary evidence by positing further concealment and because large, durable secrecy is hard to maintain. Second, the particularist reply: a conspiracy claim is a causal hypothesis like any other and should be assessed on the evidence for it. Say what each position implies about the pre-1972 suspicion, and about whether that suspicion even has the structure the debate assumes — it looks less like a specific explanatory hypothesis than a diffuse judgment about an institutional track record. Then reverse the lens: what does the episode show about the generalism/particularism debate itself? Does Tuskegee refute generalism, or does it show that the debate ran together two different questions — whether a particular belief is justified and whether the class is reliable — and does it move the burden of proof? Close by saying whether the case is a confirmation of the theory, an anomaly, or something it never anticipated, and whether calling a claim a conspiracy theory should be read as an epistemic verdict at all.
