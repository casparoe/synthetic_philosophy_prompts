Ok, here's where I've landed on the "which computations matter morally" question, and I want you to try to knock it down. I think sentience — and so moral status — attaches to a certain kind of computation (something about integrated, self-monitoring information processing, I won't nail it down here) and not to the carbon it happens to run on. Standard functionalism, sure. But I want to add a second clause I think does the real work: the computation has to be the one the system is actually running, individuated by its own causal organization, not one an outside observer could read into it. Otherwise you get the familiar embarrassment where just about any physical system implements just about any finite automaton and rocks start qualifying, which I treat as a reductio of the naive view rather than of mine.

So what I want: what would count against this position? Not "here's what dualists say," I know that move. I want the defeaters ranked, from the ones that should actually move me down to the merely awkward. Which ones would I have to concede as counting against me by my own lights? And here's my real worry — I built in the "actually running" clause precisely to keep the silly cases out, and I genuinely can't tell whether that makes the view unfalsifiable, and if so whether that's fatal or just the price of admission for a view about consciousness. Concretely, I keep getting stuck on cases where the causal carving is ambiguous: a recurrent net whose self-monitoring loop can be drawn either around the whole thing or around some sub-circuit, and the two carvings disagree about whether there's a subject there. What's the view supposed to say, and does saying it expose something? Also — what does my view predict that its rivals don't? Be honest if the answer is nothing. The thing that would actually move me is a case where the "actually running" clause forces me to a verdict I find intuitively absurd, so if you've got one, lead with it.
