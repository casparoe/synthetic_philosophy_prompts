I'm writing a short paper for a political theory workshop on whether constitutional entrenchment is really a Ulysses contract. My line is that the analogy is seductive but breaks down in two seperate places. First, Ulysses binds one temporally extended agent, whereas a constitution binds a plurality of generations who never share a single will; so the later self whose preferences are being constrained isn't the same person who ratified. Second, the binding is done by the same demos that can amend, so it's less a precommitment than a second-order rule about how first-order decisions get made. I want to argue that the better analogy is not Ulysses but a dead-hand problem, except that the dead hand is also the living hand because the amendment rule is itself always up for grabs. Does this distinction actually do any work? And is there a version of the Ulysses analogy that survives if we treat the constitution as a commitment device for a corporate agent rather than an individual? Please give me comments, especially on whether the arguement is too quick and what the strongest objection is. Also tell me if the second-order rule point is just restating the amendment paradox.
