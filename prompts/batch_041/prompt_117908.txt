I sit on the benefits appeals panel of a mid-sized public university that runs its own employee and student health plan, and I need to circulate a written opinion before next Tuesday's meeting. Two appeals are on the agenda and we have decided to hear them together because our medical director says they turn on the same question. The first is from a staff member with a long-standing diagnosis involving persistent inattention, whose physician is asking us to keep paying for a medication at a dose above our published ceiling; the physician's argument is that the higher dose brings her up to where her colleagues are, not past them. The second is from a doctoral student with no diagnosis at all, who wants the same medication, at the same dose, covered for the months he is preparing for qualifying exams, on the grounds that without it his performance is not the performance his work requires. The medical director proposes that we resolve both by asking whether the requested dose returns the person to the range of functioning typical for their age and sex, and denying any request that would carry them above it. I think that test is being asked to do something it cannot do, and I want the opinion to say so without falling back on the line between treating disease and improving the healthy, which is the line everyone on the panel already distrusts and which I do not think survives contact with either appeal. What I would rather we adopt, and what I want the opinion to argue for, is a two-step test. First, compare the person not to the population but to their own trajectory: does the requested intervention restore them to a level they have previously occupied, or hold them there against a decline, or does it move them past anything they have had before? Second, where the answer is that it moves them past their own previous level, ask whether the level they are being measured against is set by their biology at all or by a competitive arrangement we ourselves maintain — an exam curve, a promotion ladder, a job that rewards hours beyond what a body tolerates — and treat the request as a claim about that arrangement rather than as a medical claim about the person. I am aware that this may simply relocate the problem rather than solve it, and part of what I want from the opinion is an honest account of where it relocates it to and whether the new location is any more tractable than the old one. Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a course of action the panel can actually vote on, with the two-step test set out precisely enough that we can apply it to these two appeals and reach a determinate answer on each; a time frame for phasing it in for people already mid-treatment; the strongest case against my proposal stated as its supporters would state it rather than as a straw man; and my response to that case. Do not cite any literature or attach references, since I will add those if we publish. Write it as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold or italic, no tables — the way you would write a long letter to colleagues who will read it once before the meeting. Lead with the recommendation itself, then the reasons, then the objection and my answer, and close with what we should do on Tuesday.
