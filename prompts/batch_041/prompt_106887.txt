I am not a philosopher and have never taken a course in political theory, but I have ended up in a long, repetitive argument with a group of neighbors about a rule in our neighborhood association. The rule requires every household to leave a two-foot strip of unmowed grass along the alley behind our houses. The association wants to remove the rule and pave the strip. The older residents oppose this, and the line they keep returning to is that you should not tear down a fence until you know why it was put there. They say the strip was probably put there for a reason, that we cannot see all the reasons, and that the burden is on the people who want to change things. One friend, who calls himself a Burkean, says this is the whole of conservative wisdom in one sentence. I have heard the same line used about inherited institutions, constitutional conventions, and even about deleting old code from a software project. I want to know what the line actually claims.

Please lay out, as a menu rather than a single interpretation, the distinct precise theses that the fence saying could be expressing. For each, say what the thesis is, whether it is trivial, substantive, or false, what it would require in the case of our grass strip, and whether it would actually support the older residents' conclusion that the rule should not be removed. I am especially interested in the difference between readings that are about our knowledge and readings that are about the fence itself: for example, the claim that we do not currently know the function; the claim that the reformer must prove the rule is useless; the claim that the rule's persistence is evidence that it serves a function; the claim that it does serve a function even if no one remembers what it is; the claim that it was put there to solve a problem we can no longer reconstruct; and the claim that the reformer's ignorance of a reason is not itself a reason to think there is a good one. Please also consider the possibility that the fence was put up for a bad reason, or for no reason at all, or for a reason that no longer applies, and say which of the readings survive those possibilities.

Finally, I would like you to include a few readings that are not usually distinguished but that seem to me to be doing different work: one on which the fence is a load-bearing part of a larger package of practices, so that removing it alone is not a clean experiment; one on which the fence is a precedent, so that removing it changes what future removals are permissible; one on which the fence is a coordination point, so that its value depends on other people also keeping their strips; and one on which the fence is a vestige, retained by inertia but no longer connected to any living problem. Tell me which of all these readings the older residents most likely mean, which of them would actually justify blocking the change, and which are the ones that sound profound but collapse into the trivial observation that we should not act blindly. I want a list I can bring to the next association meeting.
