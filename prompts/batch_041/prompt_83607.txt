I am a student in a course on consciousness and the self, and the following argument is one I reconstructed from our unit on split-brain patients. It is not a straw man; every step looked defensible when I wrote it down, and the conclusion is one I cannot accept. I cannot locate the step that does the damage, and I would rather keep the claim that the split-brain data give us our best evidence for genuine disunity of consciousness than keep the general criterion that seems to generate it. But I want to know what that costs me.

The empirical background the argument leans on: after surgical separation of the hemispheres, when information is lateralized so that only one hemisphere receives it, that hemisphere can be shown to perceive, learn, and control action in ways the speaking subject denies; the two hands can work at cross-purposes; and the speaking hemisphere will offer confabulated explanations of acts it did not initiate. Many people take this to show that such a patient has two streams of consciousness, and therefore two subjects.

(1) A subject of experience exists, at a time, just in case there exists at that time a sufficiently integrated, report-capable information-processing system.
(2) In the split-brain patient each hemisphere is such a system: each integrates perception, memory, and the control of action well enough to guide behavior and, in the left hemisphere's case, to report on what it perceives.
(3) So the split-brain patient contains two subjects of experience.
(4) Nothing in (1) mentions hemispheres. The criterion is entirely general.
(5) Nor is the ordinary brain a single such system: it contains subsystems that are relatively integrated and that pass information onward in encapsulated, report-like ways.
(6) So by (1)'s own lights the ordinary case is also a multiplicity, and the number of subjects present is fixed only by where we draw the line of "sufficient integration" — a line we appear to be drawing arbitrarily.
(7) A criterion that assigns an arbitrary number of subjects wherever it is applied is not a criterion of subjecthood at all.
(8) So (1) is false.
(9) But (1) is the only principled reason in the vicinity for saying the split-brain patient has two streams of consciousness rather than one discontinuous or patchy stream.
(10) So we must either multiply subjects without limit or deny them precisely where the evidence for disunity is strongest.

Which step should go, and why did it look so plausible before I looked closely? If I keep (1), what exactly must I accept about how many subjects there are — that the matter is vague, or graded, or settled by our descriptive conventions? If I drop it, what replaces it as an account of what makes a collection of processes count as one subject, and how does that replacement handle the split-brain case without assuming the answer? Please price the repairs against one another, including the option that the number of minds is not a factual question at all.
