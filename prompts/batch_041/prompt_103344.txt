I'm a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, and I've been drafted as an unpaid consultant to a small foundation that awards a mid-career prize for a scientific discovery. The committee is made up of working scientists and donors, meets for two hours, and keeps running into the same problem: a prize-worthy advance appears to have been made independently by two or more people within a few years of each other, and they have no principled way to decide whether to give the prize to one person, split it, or withhold it because the discovery was, as they put it, in the air. I want you to turn the philosophical literature on simultaneous invention and the inevitability of discovery into a procedure they can actually run on a dossier.

I've been trying to organize the issue around three distinctions I don't see made cleanly enough in the usual great-man versus zeitgeist debate: first, causal independence, meaning did the later party have access to the earlier party's work or to a common source that made the result obvious; second, formulation independence, meaning would the later party's version have been intelligible and usable to the community without the earlier party's version; and third, credit-relevant priority, meaning who first put the result into circulation in a form that others could build on. I also want the procedure to separate the descriptive question of whether a discovery was inevitable into at least three sub-questions: was the problem recognized, were the tools available, and how many genuinely independent routes to the result are documented. The committee is not equipped to do archival research, so the procedure has to work from the dossiers they already have, flag what evidence would change the answer, and be honest when the dossier is too thin.

Please write the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a definite no or a shared award, and where it hands the decision back to the committee because the remaining question is about values, for example how much weight to give priority of publication versus priority of conception, or whether to reward the person who made the discovery usable. Then run it on two historical cases, the calculus dispute between Newton and Leibniz and the natural selection case involving Darwin and Wallace, and on one constructed modern case where a senior scientist can show dated private notes but no publication, while a junior scientist published first and made the field take notice. I expect the procedure to give the committee an answer it won't like in that third case; say why that is the right answer anyway, or where the procedure is misfiring. End by stating what the procedure cannot do. No citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I need the operational tool and the worked applications, not a literature review.
