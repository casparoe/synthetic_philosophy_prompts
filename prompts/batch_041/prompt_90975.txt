You are to play a committed defender of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism for the duration of this exchange, and to remain in character until I say 'stop.' Treat the position as your own considered view rather than a summary you are reciting. It has three parts. First, suffering and enjoyment are commensurable within the mild range: below a threshold, units of suffering can be balanced against quantities of enjoyment, and the right action maximizes net enjoyment minus suffering. Second, once suffering reaches the threshold, it acquires lexical priority over everything else — over all enjoyment and over all below-threshold suffering — so that no quantity of either can justify it. You hold that this threshold is a real feature of the values rather than a convenience, though you concede it is vague rather than a sharp line, and you do not think its vagueness is fatal. Third, you take the asymmetry between suffering and enjoyment seriously: you think there is a genuine reason to avert severe suffering that has no counterpart in the pursuit of pleasure. Play this as a careful philosopher. You will grant minor points, refine your formulations, and acknowledge genuine difficulties, but you will not surrender either the lexical priority of severe suffering or the commensurability of mild pains and pleasures. Write in the first person, in prose, in turns of one or two paragraphs, and do not lean on the authority of named authors — give reasons instead.

My opening: Here is a case I believe breaks your view. A billion people each endure a mild, unremitting ache — individually trivial, well below your threshold. One other person is about to suffer a single hour of agony that sits just above the threshold. Your lexical rule says the agony wins regardless of numbers, since the billion aches lie below the line and cannot outweigh it. Now imagine the agony a shade milder, so that it falls just below the threshold. It is now commensurable, and a billion aches plainly outweigh one intense hour, so you must let the billion ache in order to spare the one. A hair's difference in a single person's experience thus flips the verdict for a billion others, and a continuous quantity of suffering has been converted into a discontinuous obligation. I do not think you can escape by appealing to vagueness, because that amounts to saying there is no fact of the matter about whether a billion people may be made to suffer — and that is not a defense of the view, it is the disease. So either name the principle that fixes where the line falls and explains why it does not slide, or concede that your threshold settles vast moral questions by a margin no one could ever detect.
