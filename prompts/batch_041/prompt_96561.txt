I am a novelist, and I am applying for a one-month residency at a university humanities center that pairs creative writers with philosophers. I need a two-page research proposal, and I would like you to draft it in American English. My half-formed idea is this. The debate between Dennett and Searle over original versus derived intentionality has always struck me as a debate about authorship, and I think the case I know best — the case of fictional characters — falls through the gap between them. Dennett's coin-detecting vending machine, the two-bitser, is meant to show that we can legitimately attribute beliefs to a system with no inner life, because the attribution earns its keep in prediction. Searle's reply is that whatever aboutness the machine has is derived from us, the designers and interpreters, and that only brains have original intentionality. But a novelist spends years manufacturing agents whose beliefs, desires, and memories are neither original in Searle's sense nor merely observer-relative in the way a thermostat's sensitivity is. A character's intentionality is authored. It is derived from a mind, but not in the way a sentence is derived from a speaker, because once the book is finished the character can be right or wrong about things the author never decided. Readers argue about what a character would do, and some of those arguments seem to have correct answers that the author did not consciously put there. So the question I want the proposal to sharpen is this: does the case of fictional characters require a third category of intentionality — call it authored or delegated intentionality — that is neither original nor merely derived, and if so, what does that category do to the Dennett-Searle dispute? The proposal should state that question precisely, explain why the existing positions leave it open, and say what method I would actually use in a one-month residency to make progress on it. I imagine the method as conceptual rather than empirical: I would construct a graded series of cases from my own drafting practice and from novels where the text underdetermines a character's mental life — a belief explicitly stated, a belief clearly implied, a belief neither stated nor implied but determinately true of the character, and a belief that is genuinely indeterminate — and then test each case against Dennett's intentional stance and Searle's original/derived distinction to see which cases each can handle without strain. The residue, if there is one, is what would motivate the third category. The proposal should also say what would count as the project failing: for instance, if every case is smoothly handled by one of the two existing views, or if authored intentionality turns out to be just a subspecies of derived intentionality rather than a genuinely new kind, or if the whole idea collapses into a distinction between different sources of derivation rather than different kinds of intentionality. Finally, the proposal should be realistic about scope for a one-month residency — I am not going to settle the metaphysics of intentionality in four weeks — and it should say what a useful deliverable would look like: a draft essay of roughly five thousand words, plus a short public talk for the residency's seminar series. Do not bother with citations or a literature review; I will add those myself. I want the proposal to be honest about the fact that I am a novelist borrowing a philosophical vocabulary, and to explain why that borrowing might be an asset rather than a liability.
