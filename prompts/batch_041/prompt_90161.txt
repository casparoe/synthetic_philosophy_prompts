At a departmental seminar on moral revolutions, I watched two participants, A and B, argue over whether norms such as slavery and dueling end through arguments, interests, or honor. I am reporting their exchange as a bystander and have no stake in which one wins. The disagreement unfolded roughly as follows.

(1) A: The abolition of slavery is best explained by shifting economic interests. The moral arguments against slavery had been available for centuries; what changed was that slave labor became less profitable and free labor more attractive to metropolitan capital. Where the moral case ran ahead of the interests, it had little effect. The Williams-style thesis—that economic interest, not moral argument, drove abolition—is basically right.

(2) B: That confuses a permissive condition with a cause. Economic change may have made abolition feasible, but feasibility does not explain why it was undertaken. Honor did the proximate motivational work: abolition became a marker of national honor, and slaveholding became a source of shame. The same pattern appears in dueling: the practice did not end because the arguments against it suddenly improved, but because it became dishonorable to duel. An Appiah-style honor-centered account captures what the interest story leaves out.

(3) A: Honor is just another interest—reputational interest. Once you see that, the Williams-style thesis absorbs your objection: interests explain everything, including honor. And honor codes themselves tend to shift when elites' material position shifts. The apparent autonomy of honor is an illusion.

(4) B: If interest is stretched to include every motive, the thesis becomes unfalsifiable. Honor is not merely an individual's reputational advantage; it is a shared normative status with its own grammar. People routinely sacrifice material advantage for honor. Arguments matter because they can reconstitute honor: showing that a practice is inconsistent with an identity one already prizes can make the practice shameful. That is a causal path from argument to conduct, not a rationalization after the fact.

(5) A: Then why do some arguments catch on and others do not? The ones that catch on are those that align with interests. Moreover, many moral revolutions are top-down and coercive: states abolish practices for geopolitical or fiscal reasons, and the moral vocabulary is supplied afterward.

(6) B: That is an empirical dispute, not a conceptual one. But even top-down abolition required a normative vocabulary to be legitimate; honor supplied it. And the interest story often leans heavily on the British case. Dueling and foot binding had very different economic profiles, yet they show similar honor dynamics. A single case cannot carry the thesis.

(7) A: Here is a hybrid I would accept: moral revolutions are tipping cascades in reputational networks. Arguments are the activation energy; interests are the payoff matrix; honor is the threshold. On this model, the Williams-style thesis and the honor-centered account are not rivals but different parameters of the same process.

(8) B: That hybrid is attractive but risks being too elastic. If every outcome can be redescribed as a tipping cascade, the model explains nothing. You still need to say which mechanism does the work in particular cases, and whether honor is a cause or merely the medium through which other causes operate.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and give a brief justification. Where a move depends on an ambiguity, identify the ambiguity. In particular, address: (i) the difference between explaining why a norm ended and justifying its ending; (ii) the difference between material interests and interests broadly construed; (iii) the difference between honor as an individual motive and honor as a shared social fact; (iv) whether the Williams-style thesis and the honor-centered account are competing explanations, complementary at different levels, or one reducible to the other; and (v) whether the tipping-cascade hybrid is a genuine synthesis or a verbal redescription. Finally, state which participant has the stronger overall position and under what conditions the other would be right.
