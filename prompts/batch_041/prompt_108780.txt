Ok so I'm building a teaching unit around the hedonistic-imperative idea — the biotech-abolitionist line that we should engineer suffering out of existence for every sentient creature, not just humans. I want to push students past the slogan and onto the mechanics, because the slogan is easy and the mechanics are where it gets uncomfortable. Here are five concrete mechanisms that get floated in that literature, restated in my own words so people cant just parrot a paper:

(a) heritable analgesia — edit nociception out of a whole lineage, so no member of it can feel pain, wild animals included;
(b) bliss-gradient rewiring — replace the aversive channel with a signal that only ever runs from mild unease up to euphoria, so nothing is ever experienced as bad, only as less good;
(c) ambient mood dosing — a stable, non-addictive, non-sedating mood brightener delivered through water, air or food, so that distress never gets a foothold;
(d) predator overhaul — make carnivores either non-sentient or non-predatory and feed them synthesized protein, on the theory that predation is the single biggest reservoir of suffering;
(e) retro-repair — leave the genome alone and just get very good at cheaply healing existing trauma and chronic pain in humans and other animals.

Rank these five from your strongest bet to your weakest, where the axis is: how much of the problem the mechanism actually dissolves, rather than relocating it, concealing it, or buying it at the price of some new catastrophe — loss of agency, a hedonic monoculture, an evolutionary arms race, whatever you think the real risk is.

Give it back as a numbered list, exactly five entries in rank order, each entry a sentence or two saying why it lands where it does — no preamble and no wrap-up at the end.
