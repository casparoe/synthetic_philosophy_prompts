Two mates of mine, Dan and Priya, had a proper row at the pub on Friday over a hiring call at Dan's old lab, and I got dragged in as the neutral one and came away more confused than before. Here's the story. The lab was hiring a postdoc, and the first two people on the panel both came out of their interviews strongly in favour of one candidate. Both of them were also in the same subfield, had both read the same two reference letters, and had both watched her give the same seminar the week before. Everyone else on the panel interviewed her after that, heard the first two raving, and basically fell in line. She was hired. Eighteen months on it's gone badly and she's leaving.

Dan's line: textbook information cascade, and the panel behaved irrationally. Nobody after the first two was weighing evidence about her, they were weighing each other. If everyone does that the group can lock onto a dud on almost no evidence, and the fact that it can shows the whole setup is broken.

Priya's line: each individual step was fine. If you think the first two are decent judges, it's perfectly rational to give weight to their verdict, and nobody ignored their own read, they just updated it. You can't call a decision irrational because it turned out badly; ex ante it may have been the best any of them could manage. The failure is in the aggregation, not in the individuals.

Dan: but their verdicts werent independent evidence. Same letters, same seminar — so when the rest of the panel treated "two people independently rate her highly" as two bits of evidence, they were double-counting one bit. That's not structural, that's just a mistake.

Priya: fine, but a good Bayesian discounts shared evidence, so that's still an error in individual reasoning, not proof that cascades are irrational as such. And anyway, at some point you have to decide whether to trust your own fifteen-minute read of a stranger over two colleagues who actually know the area, and deferring there isn't obviously daft.

Dan: then it's not really about the numbers at all, it's that "two people agree" starts to feel like a fact about her rather than a fact about them, and nobody can tell the difference from the inside.

Priya: and that's just social epistemology being hard, not a fallacy.

They've asked me to settle it and I can't. Please go through it move by move — who's right about what, where the real disagreement is versus where they're talking past each other, and what they're both missing. I want an actual verdict at the end, not a survey. In British English please.
