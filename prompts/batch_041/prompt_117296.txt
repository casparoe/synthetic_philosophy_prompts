Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon — in both its original novelette form and its expanded novel form — tells the story of Charlie Gordon, a man with an intellectual disability who becomes the first human subject in an experiment to increase intelligence, and who eventually regresses to something like his original condition. Read the work through the lens of Daniel Dennett's fourfold taxonomy of minds: Darwinian creatures, whose behavior is fixed by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures, which can learn by reinforcement and trial and error, letting the environment shape them; Popperian creatures, which can simulate possibilities internally and let their hypotheses die in their stead; and Gregorian creatures, which are Popperian creatures equipped with mind-tools acquired from culture, language being the master tool. I want a reading that takes both the novel and the taxonomy seriously, and that is honest about where the novel resists the philosophical frame.

Press on several questions. Does Charlie's arc track the four stages, or does the surgery simply accelerate a transition that in ordinary development depends on language and cultural scaffolding? When Charlie begins to read and write, and when his progress reports grow more complex, is he acquiring the tools that constitute a Gregorian creature, or is he merely becoming a more capable Skinnerian? What does the regression show about the status of the Gregorian mind — is it a stable level of cognitive architecture, or a fragile set of external supports that can be stripped away? The novel is told entirely through Charlie's own progress reports, which are themselves a cultural artifact; does the form of the novel dramatize Dennett's point that the mind is not just in the head, or does it undercut that point by making the written record the only thing that survives the regression? And what should we make of Algernon, the mouse who undergoes the same surgery and whose decline precedes Charlie's? Does the mouse show that the hierarchy is not species-specific, or does it expose a tension in the taxonomy — that a creature can be Popperian or Gregorian in some respects while remaining a mouse in others?

I am teaching this to a class that has read Dennett's account, and I want to use the novel without flattening either it or the philosophy. So please give me a reading that says where the taxonomy illuminates a scene or a choice, where the novel complicates or resists the taxonomy, and what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not. Write in American English.
