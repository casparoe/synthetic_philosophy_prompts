Ok so I've just spent about three hours in the pub having an arguement with my mate Tom about whether the statue is the clay, and I came out of it defending the two-things view: the lump and the statue are two numerically distinct objects that happen to occupy exactly the same place and time, and neither of them is identical to the other. I'm definately not sure I believe it, but I defended it for three hours, so now I want it properly attacked.

Don't just give me the greatest hits though. I've heard the greatest hits, and most of them come down to someone having a different intuition about the same case, which doesn't move me. What I want is a map of what my view actually risks: what counts against it, ranked from fatal down to merely awkward, and a clear-eyed note on which of those a defender like me would just concede and shrug at. If it turns out the only objections are ones I can relabel as 'costs' rather than 'refutations', I want to hear that too.

Two things I'd find genuinely decisive. First, if the view collapses into a rival — specifically if everything I say about the two objects turns out to be a fact about one object plus our ways of describing it, so that 'there are two of them' was a grammatical illusion all along. Second, if I can't produce a single case where treating them as two gives me a different answer to a real question — what happens, what to do, what's true of what — than treating them as one does. If the view predicts nothing its rival doesn't, say so.

The test I've been leaning on, by the way, is that a difference between them only counts if it's a difference in the objects rather than in our descriptions of them. 'The statue is a masterpiece, the lump of clay isn't' — fine, that's about them. 'The lump would survive being squashed and the statue wouldn't' — I honestly can't tell which of those it is, and if it's the second then I think I'm sunk. Tell me whether that's the right test or whether I've rigged it so my view can't lose.
