Two of my housemates, Ravi and Sam, spent an afternoon arguing over a puzzle, and they have asked me to adjudicate, since I was in the room and neither of them trusts the other to summarise fairly. I could not settle it. The details matter, so I will set the puzzle out exactly and then reconstruct their exchange as closely as I can.

The puzzle. Dana holds a lease on one of three flats: A, nearest her work but the smallest; B, intermediate; C, cheapest and furthest out. A broker can arrange swaps between any two of them for a flat fee of £40 per swap, and he is forbidden to deceive: before any trade he must state, truthfully, that he intends to offer the full round of swaps, that the round is repeatable without limit, and that he will offer it again each time she completes it. He also offers, as a separate option, a single bundled contract — all three swaps executed at once for £120, returning Dana to C. Dana's rankings are A over B, B over C, and C over A, and each of the three is worth roughly £100 to her, so that a single swap at £40 is a clear gain by her own lights while the full round at £120 is a clear loss. Asked why her rankings run in a circle, she says she is judging each pair on the balance of rent, size, and commuting time, and has never managed to hold all three considerations steady at once.

Ravi (1): This settles the matter. If Dana's rankings were consistent, no sequence of swaps she herself endorses could return her to her starting point at a loss. The existence of such a sequence is not a curiosity; it is a proof that her preferences are defective, and no amount of charm about rent and commuting time changes that.

Sam (2): The pump proves much less. It shows that a Dana who appraises each offer in isolation and takes whatever looks better at that instant will bleed money. A Dana who sees the round coming declines the first swap — and declining is not irrational. She is refusing a trade that is locally an improvement because it is a step in a pattern she rejects. The unit of rational assessment is the path, not the step. And exploitability is not incoherence: a shopper who always buys the first thing he is shown is perfectly consistent, and can still be fleeced by a seller who knows it.

Ravi (3): But notice what declining costs her. Each swap, taken alone, is an improvement by her own lights, and she knows it. A rule telling her to refuse improvements whenever they fall inside a pattern she dislikes is a rule for acting against her own preferences. So the foresighted Dana must either act against her present preference or be pumped; you have moved the defect from her preferences to her will, not removed it. And the appeal to the path is either the conclusion restated — the path is bad because she loses money, and she loses money because the rankings cycle — or it is circular, since there is no moment at which the path itself is what she chooses. She faces one swap at a time, and each later swap is contingent on her own earlier choice.

Sam (4): She can occupy that standpoint in advance. She can adopt a policy — hold B, whatever is offered — and keep to it, forgoing the swaps it forbids. That is a coherent plan, chosen for the sake of the pattern, and it need not be blind: at every step the same reason is available to her, that she is not going to be the sort of person who walks in circles, so she refuses the swap for a reason rather than merely resisting it.

Ravi (5): Then suppose the broker's fees go to a charity Dana supports, or that he charges nothing at all. Going round the loop now costs her nothing she cares about and leaves her exactly where she began — and on your account she is still irrational. If you say she is not, that with the losses gone the loop is harmless, then your path-rationality has no content beyond 'do not lose money', and the transitivity requirement evaporates along with the fee. If you say she is still irrational, you owe an account of what is wrong with a loop that leaves her no worse off.

Sam (6): That is a feature, not a defect. Rationality is relative to the agent's ends, so a standard that changes its verdict when the ends change is behaving correctly; a Dana whose fees fund a cause she loves is not being defeated by the loop, and there is nothing to criticise. What you owe is an account of the cycle's badness that survives the removal of the loss — and I do not think there is one, which is why the pump is an argument about prudence rather than about coherence.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. Rate each of the six moves on a scale running from clearly mistaken through questionable to clearly right, with a brief justification for each rating, and separate claims within a single move if they deserve different ratings. Then say who has the stronger position overall, and identify what each of them is missing. In particular: is the step/path distinction a genuine distinction or a restatement of the conclusion; does the policy in Sam's fourth move genuinely rescue Dana, and if so from what; what should a correct treatment of the charity variant in the fifth move conclude; and is Dana's own explanation of her rankings — that she cannot hold all three considerations steady at once — relevant to whether the cycle is a defect or an artefact? There is no need to cite any literature; neither of us has read any, and I want to know whether the reasoning stands up on its own.
