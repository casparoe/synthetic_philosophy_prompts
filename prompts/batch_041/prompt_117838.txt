For some years I have been trying to pin down a pattern I first noticed in a small single-issue campaign group I belonged to, and I would like to know whether it already has a name. The group was entirely sincere: nobody lied, nobody was paid, nobody was coerced. Yet over about two years, questions of fact stopped behaving like questions of fact. Whether a tactic worked, whether a claim about our opponents was accurate, even whether the evidence we had gathered supported the conclusion we published — raising any of these was heard as a question about loyalty, and was settled by the standing of the person who raised it rather than by the evidence. What interests me is that the members who stayed did not become hypocrites: they believed what they said, and the doubt did not have to be suppressed because it dissolved. When the group folded, several of them recovered within weeks judgments they had not held for years, as though the belief had never been theirs. I have since seen something like it in a laboratory that had closed ranks around a contested result. I have a vague sense that a French writer of the 1940s made something like this the centre of an argument about why political parties are inherently hostile to thought in their members, but I am not asking for the reference — I want to know whether the mechanism itself has been isolated. Is there an established concept for the state of a person who sincerely holds beliefs about what is true because the collective needs them held, and whose beliefs lapse when the collective dissolves? If there is, say which of the nearby notions it should be identified with — groupthink, adaptive preference, epistemic closure, motivated reasoning, false consciousness — and precisely where each of those fails to fit. If there is none, coin a term, define it, and mark its edges: what resembles it without being an instance, and what would count as an instance even where no formal organisation exists.
