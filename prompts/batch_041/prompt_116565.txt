My family's little foundation is having its annual dinner next month — my grandmother started it back in the nineties, it hands out maybe $200k a year split between a food bank, a legal aid clinic, and a scholarship for first-gen students at the local state school — and I've been asked to give the closing remarks, like five minutes, after dessert. The tricky part is the room. About a third of the people there are donors, several of them finance types, and they've been getting deeper into the effective-altruism thing; two years ago one of them stood up and basically said we should stop funding the food bank because the numbers don't show much long-run impact, and it got tense. The other two thirds are the volunteers and a couple of the original board members, including my great-aunt, who thinks the whole spreadsheet mentality is a way of not looking at the person in front of you, and who has never once asked what a program's "counterfactual" was.

Here's what I actually want the speech to be about: the thing my cousin (philosophy PhD, insufferable at Thanksgiving) calls cluelessness. The idea, as I understand it, is that when you try to trace out what a donation really causes, the causal ripples keep going — the kid who gets the scholarship marries someone, moves three states over, votes differently, has kids, one of those kids invents something or starts a war — and at some point we honestly have no idea whether the total downstream effect is positive or negative, or whether it gets swamped by some other thing that wouldn't otherwise have happened. So we're pretty sure the food bank is good this month and totally in the dark about the next two hundred years. My cousin also keeps insisting on a distinction between not knowing the odds and not even knowing what the possible outcomes are, and I think it's definately the second one that's doing the damage here. Both camps in that room are right about something and wrong about something, and I want the speech to hold that instead of dissolving it.

So: please write the closing remarks. Constraints — no quoting philosophers, no name-dropping books or studies (the finance people will fact-check me, the volunteers will tune out), and no jargon like "expected value" unless it's unpacked in plain words first. The speech has to take the cluelessness seriously as a structural problem: it can't resolve it by saying "so we just do what's in front of us," and it can't resolve it by saying "so trust the numbers." It also shouldn't end in hand-wringing — I need to end with something people can actually do on Monday, and both the great-aunt and the quant have to be able to hear it as respect. Maybe 650 words. Oh, and my uncle Dave is giving a short toast right before me where he's going to say that since nobody can know anything, we should all just fund whatever makes us feel good, so if you can have me gesture at that without humiliating him, that'd be great.
