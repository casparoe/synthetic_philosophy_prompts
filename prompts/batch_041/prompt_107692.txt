I'm working on a paper about the raven paradox and would like an outside assessment of some candidate responses to it. The paradox, as you know, is that the hypothesis that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens, so that a white shoe — a non-black non-raven — appears to confirm that all ravens are black, which is absurd.

Below are seven responses. Some are recognizably in the spirit of positions already in the literature; others are framings I have constructed myself and am not sure what to make of. Please sort them into tiers: a top tier of responses that genuinely dissolve the paradox, a middle tier of responses that relocate it or exchange it for a different puzzle, and a bottom tier of responses that fail on their own terms. For each response give one sentence explaining its placement. Judge each by how much of the paradox it actually removes, not by how famous or well-defended it is, and if two or more of my items collapse into a single response, say so and merge them rather than forcing seven separate placements.

R1. Vanishing confirmation. The white shoe does confirm the raven hypothesis, exactly as the logical equivalence requires; it confirms it by an absurdly small amount, because the class of non-black things vastly exceeds the class of ravens. The paradox is not an error in the logic but an illusion that a genuine confirmation must be appreciable.

R2. Sampling procedure. Confirmation is always relative to a procedure for gathering evidence, and an observation confirms a generalization only if it came from a procedure that could have produced a counterexample to it. The white shoe was not drawn from any procedure that surveys non-black things and checks them for ravenhood, so it carries no confirmatory weight; logical equivalence does not transfer evidential relevance.

R3. Non-projectible complement. Confirmation passes between logically equivalent formulations only when the predicates involved are projectible. Raven and black are projectible; non-raven and non-black are not. The equivalence licenses the inference on paper but not in practice, so the shoe cannot confirm anything.

R4. Partition-relative confirmation. Whether an observation confirms anything depends on the space of hypotheses being contrasted. Fix a partition containing the raven hypothesis and its natural rivals about the colour of ravens, and the shoe raises no hypothesis's probability at the expense of any rival in that partition. The paradox assumes a partition — black versus non-black objects, simpliciter — that no inquirer actually uses.

R5. No test, no confirmation. A positive instance confirms only if it resulted from a genuine attempt to falsify. Examining ravens for blackness is such an attempt; examining white shoes for ravenhood is not, since that procedure was bound to succeed. The shoe therefore confirms nothing.

R6. Background-relative irrelevance. Relative to a background that says nothing about the colour distribution of things, the shoe does confirm the raven hypothesis a little. Relative to our actual background, in which it is already known that non-black things are overwhelmingly non-ravens, it adds nothing. Confirmation is relative to background knowledge, and ours already contains what the shoe would tell us.

R7. Explanatory confirmation. The raven hypothesis earns its support by explaining why the ravens we examine are black. A white shoe presents nothing for that hypothesis to explain, so it cannot support it. Confirmation is a matter of explanatory contribution, not of instantiation.

Then tell me, in no more than 150 words, which single response you would stake most on, and what its most serious remaining weakness is.

Two things about how I want you to answer. I want a definite verdict rather than a survey: commit to the tiers, and put a rough confidence figure on your ranking as a whole. And if the exercise itself is ill-posed — if how much a response dissolves the paradox turns out not to be a single scale, or if you think several of my items are the same response dressed differently, or if you think no ranking of responses is worth much because the paradox is best handled by a technical result rather than a philosophical stance — then say so plainly instead of producing a tidy list. A correct objection is more useful to me here than a compliant answer.
