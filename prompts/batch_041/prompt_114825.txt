Two of my colleagues, Ines and Tobias, spent most of a lunch hour arguing over a report from a national public-health body, and I would like a point-by-point ruling on who was right at each stage. The background is this. The body convened a panel of 999 experts to settle whether a particular intervention works. The panelists were chosen for their expertise, and the body's own summary of their records puts each of them at roughly a 51% chance of getting a question of this kind right on their own. The panel's headline vote went 55% to 45% against the proposition that the intervention works.

1. Ines invoked the Condorcet jury theorem. If each panelist is even slightly better than a coin flip, she said, then a large majority of them is essentially certain to be right, so the panel's verdict should be treated as decisive and we should simply defer to it.

2. Tobias answered that the theorem holds only when the votes are independent conditional on the truth. These 999 people read the same briefing, the same two studies, and deliberated in the same room, so their errors are correlated; 999 correlated votes are not 999 independent pieces of evidence, and in the limiting case they amount to one voter copied 999 times.

3. Ines replied that independence is required only conditional on the truth, not unconditionally, and that people agreeing because they are both right violates nothing. Each panelist worked from a different set of case files and came from a different specialty, so their errors can be idiosyncratic even after a shared briefing. She added that correlation does not reduce the theorem to nothing: there are generalizations in which the accuracy of the majority still improves as the group grows, only more slowly, so long as the voters are on average better than chance.

4. Tobias made three points. First, she was assuming the very thing in dispute. If the panelists all anchored on the same flawed meta-analysis, then relative to the information they share they are one voter replicated, and neither distinctness of persons nor variety of specialties establishes that their errors are independent. Second, the theorem is knife-edge and symmetric: at exactly chance, majority accuracy remains at chance for every group size; below chance, larger groups converge on the wrong answer; and if competences are distributed symmetrically around chance, majority accuracy sits at one half however large the panel. A 999-member panel is therefore an amplifier of whatever the average competence is, and its size makes it more dangerous, not safer, if the shared briefing is systematically misleading. Third, even granting her figure of 51%, 999 barely-better-than-chance voters do not deliver near-certainty; the theorem's guarantee is a statement about a limit, not about 999 people.

5. Ines said that this was to ask the theorem for something it never offered. It is a conditional, and she was using it as one: it tells us what group size buys, given competence. It never promised certainty at any finite size, and it does imply that a large, modestly competent majority beats any single member of it, including either of us. And the choice is not between the panel and certainty but between the panel and her own prior, which is nowhere near 51%.

6. Tobias closed on a different objection. The panel's answer, he said, is not well defined. The panel was asked three questions, and roughly 75% said the mechanism operates in humans, roughly 70% said the trial was adequately powered, and 55% said the intervention does not work, where the panel's charge defined the intervention as working only if the mechanism operates in humans and the trial was adequately powered. Every panelist was internally consistent. So which majority should we defer to: the majority on the conclusion, which denies that the intervention works, or the majority on the premises, which affirms both of them and therefore entails that it does? Either way, we would be choosing an aggregation rule rather than following the majority.

I stayed out of it, and I would rather not simply pick a winner myself. Please work through the exchange move by move, rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justifying each rating, then say what, if anything, both of them are missing and who has the better of the argument overall.
