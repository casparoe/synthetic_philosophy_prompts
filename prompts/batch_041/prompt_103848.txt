Introspection is standardly treated as a form of inner observation, and the question of its reliability as the question of whether those observations are accurate. A colleague has been pressing a different picture, and I would like you to adjudicate the dispute it opens. On this picture — call it the constitutive account — an introspective report is not the observation of a pre-existing state but an act that constitutes the state it reports. To sincerely avow that one is in pain, or that one believes the meeting is on Tuesday, is not to describe something already present but to commit oneself, much as a verdict does not report a pre-existing legal fact but makes one. If that is right, the demand that introspection be reliable is misplaced: commitments are kept or broken, not accurate or inaccurate, and the familiar worry about introspection would be a category mistake rather than an empirical discovery.

Write an essay adjudicating whether the constitutive account dissolves the problem of introspective reliability, merely relocates it, or leaves it essentially intact. Reconstruct the account in its strongest form, then press the strongest objections against it: that avowals can misfire and be corrected by the speaker or by others; that the account cannot make sense of the everyday cases in which we discover our own attitudes were not what we took them to be; that it struggles with two people who sincerely avow incompatible motives for the same act, since it threatens to make both correct; and that it covertly presupposes the very first-person access it purports to explain away. Consider what work a notion of reliability can still do if the account is correct — whether there is a defensible sense in which a commitment can be warranted or unwarranted — and how the account bears on the empirical disputes that made reliability a live question, such as the recurring finding that people confidently and systematically misidentify the causes of their own judgments and choices. Reach a definite verdict, defend it against the strongest objection you can raise to it, and say what, if anything, the constitutive account gets right that its critics overlook.
