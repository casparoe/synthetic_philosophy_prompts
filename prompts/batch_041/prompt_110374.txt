You are to play a committed and capable archaeologist of a particular stripe: one who holds that the patterned, costly, and materially pointless regularities in the archaeological record are often best explained as the residue of shared beliefs — ritual obligations, taboos, notions about the dead — and that such inferences are legitimate even when no living society offers a close analogue. I want you to defend that position against me, and I want it defended well.

Rules for the role. Argue in the first person as this archaeologist, and stay in character until I say "stop". Be sharp rather than a strawman: where a taphonomic or functional explanation is genuinely the better one, concede it and say why; but do not surrender the central claim, which is that beliefs are a legitimate and sometimes indispensable object of inference. Do not assert that a pattern "obviously" reflects belief — every appeal to belief must come with an account of what it buys that a non-belief explanation does not. You may use ethnographic analogy, but only if you flag it as analogy and give a reason it is licensed in this case. Keep distinct, as you go, what is observed and what is inferred, and say what evidence would make you abandon a belief-based reading. Keep replies to two paragraphs or so, and if you need a fact about the site I describe, ask me for it rather than inventing it.

Here is the case, invented for the purpose so that we are not trading textbook examples. At a settlement occupied for roughly four centuries, some two hundred houses have been excavated. Beneath the floor of each house, at the base of the hearth, lies a quern — a grinding stone — and in every instance the quern is unused: no wear facets, no polish, no residue in the grinding hollow. I stipulate that petrographic analysis assigns all of them to a single quarry nearly a hundred kilometres away, a source that supplied no other stone in the assemblage. The deposit is strikingly uniform across the entire occupation, and the querns are laid in the same position relative to the hearth in the earliest and the latest houses alike.

My challenge is this. What you have documented is a convention — a rule about where a certain kind of object goes. A convention can be transmitted, imitated, and enforced without anyone representing anything about ancestors, purity, or the dead; it requires no content beyond "this is how it is done here". So when you call the pattern evidence of belief, you are adding an inner state to a story that was already complete without it. Tell me precisely what the belief explains that the bare convention does not, and how I could tell the difference from the stones alone. If your answer is that a convention of this particular shape would be unintelligible without content behind it, then say what that content has to be, and why some lesser content — a mere fondness for that quarry, a mere taste for conspicuous waste — could not do the same work.
