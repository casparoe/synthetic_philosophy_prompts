I'm a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and for the past few months I've been trying to give a sharp statement of the view — familiar from Dennett — that the self is a center of narrative gravity: not a thing the brain contains, but an abstraction posited by the story the brain tells, on the model of a physical body's center of gravity. After a long argument with friends I've ended up separating three positions and I want to pressure-test them against one tightly controlled case. The first is the fictionalist reading: any sufficiently unified narrative has a center of narrative gravity, and that is all there is to having a self — the unity of the story, not any act of self-reference, does the work. The second is a realist-of-patterns reading: the self is real exactly when the narrative earns its keep as a predictor of the subject's behavior, better than a chance-level alternative, and nothing further is required. The third, which is my own tentative view, adds a reflexivity condition: a narrative constitutes a self only if it contains a representation of the one whose narrative it is — coherence and predictive success are not enough, because without self-representation you have a behavioral profile, not a self.

Please construct a thought experiment to these specifications. Hold the first two conditions — internal coherence and predictive adequacy — fixed and fully satisfied, and vary only the third, self-reference. The scenario should be realistic (ordinary life, a clinic, a diary, a case study — no teleportation, brain-splitting, uploading, or other science-fiction devices) and the scenario itself should run no longer than a paragraph. The case must come out so that the first two views agree that a self is present while my reflexivity view dissents — and that split has to fall out of the theories themselves, not be stipulated by me. After the scenario, walk through what each of the three positions says and why; tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work; say what happens if I restore self-reference and whether the case then collapses back into an unremarkable default; and then, in the voice of a defender of the reflexivity condition, give me the strongest way to attack the case — especially any way of arguing that self-reference has been smuggled in rather than genuinely removed.

I'd like a committed answer: don't just lay out the options and leave the verdict to me. Tell me whether the case actually succeeds on its own terms, and if you think it fails, say precisely which specification it violates and why.
