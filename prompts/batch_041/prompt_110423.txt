ok so a couple of friends and i put out a tiny philosophy zine, and for the next issue i want to do a mock agony-aunt column. the gimmick: the columnist -- call her Aunt Fitting, she writes for an invented magazine called The Buck Stops Here -- is a dead-sincere believer in the fitting-attitude picture, i.e. that calling something good just means it's fitting (correct, appropriate, whatever) to value it, and that a reason to value a thing is a reason full stop, no matter where it comes from. object, threat, bribe, tax break, demon -- if you've got a reason to have the attitude, the attitude is in order. that's the whole of her ethics of the heart, and she's very calm about it.

so every letter she gets is from someone whose reason for the attitude isn't a feature of the thing at all. i'm thinking four:

- the classic: admire this cactus or something horrible happens to you
- someone offered a pile of money to believe a proposition they've got no evidence for
- someone who collects the prize for intending to do a thing they know they'll never actually do
- someone whose love for their partner is literally subsidised, there's a payout

four letters, four replies, 550-700 words all in, deadpan start to finish, no winking, and she never once clocks the problem. i want the letters themselves to do the work, so a reader who's never heard any of this still feels the squeeze: by the end you should see that if threats and bribes count, then 'fitting' has stopped doing any work -- anything you can be threatened into admiring comes out good -- and you should also feel the real question under it, which is whether there's any principled line between the reason the demon gives and the reason the cactus gives, or whether the whole fitting-attitude / buck-passing picture has to go.

she's not a strawman though. every reply should be a move a real defender might actually make -- seperating the reason to have the attitude from the reason the attitude is about is the obvious one -- just applied in a way that quietly comes apart by letter four. and no citations, no name-dropping philosophers, it's an advice column, not a seminar.
