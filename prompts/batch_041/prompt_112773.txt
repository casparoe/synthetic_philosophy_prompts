Two people in my reading group spent most of last week's session arguing about the hitchhiker case — the one where your stranded in the desert, a driver offers to take you to town if you promise to pay him once you're there, and you both know you'll never see each other again. I wasn't part of it and I dont have a side; I'd just like the thing sorted out.

Maya: The case cheats with the word 'promise.' If you already know you won't pay, saying you will isn't a promise, it's a lie. If instead you're the sort who does pay, then paying isn't irrational, it's just what you do. Either way the puzzle never gets off the ground, and 'rational irrationality' is a contradiction. You can't launder a bad act by pointing at the character that produced it; if the act is a loss, it's a loss.

Dev: The character is the right thing to evaluate, and it comes out rational — being a promise-keeper is a policy, and the policy pays for itself in rides and jobs and friends. You don't evaluate a chess strategy move by move.

Maya: But that only works if the promise is sincere, and it's sincere only if you already have the trait. So either it's useless advice (you've already got it) or it's an instruction to acquire a trait on the spot, which is self-defeating, because the moment you cultivate it for the payoff it stops being the thing that makes the promise sincere. It's the same shape as the case where a rich eccentric offers you a fortune if by midnight tonight you sincerely intend to drink a vial of something vile tomorrow, with the money landing in your account before you'd have to drink it. You can't intend that on demand, even though it's plainly in your interest to intend it. If you can't manufacture an intention at will you can't manufacture a character at will, and Parfit's fix is just a description of the lucky people who already have it.

Dev: Dispositions aren't intentions, and they aren't one-shot. People do make themselves into things — enlist, take vows, go to therapy — and the change is causal and slow, not something you re-decide each time. And you don't need to be a saint: if you'd feel like dirt for stiffing him, or you care about not being the kind of person who stiffs people, then paying is rational relative to your actual ends, and the driver can see that, and everyone's fine.

Maya: Then you've given up what the example was supposed to show. If paying is rational only because you happen to care about your self-image, the driver is relying on a contingent taste of yours, not on anything the theory of rationality delivers, and the man who genuinely doesn't care can't be argued into paying, only predicted. So 'if only you weren't so rational' is the wrong lesson; the right one is that rationality alone can't get you out of this one, only enforcement or luck can.

Can you go through this point by point — the promise-or-lie move, policy versus act, whether the toxin-style case really transfers, whether caring about your self-image rescues the act — and say who's right about what, and what both of them are missing? I'm less interested in a scorecard than in which of the moves land.
