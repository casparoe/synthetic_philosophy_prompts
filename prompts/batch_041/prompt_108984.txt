You are a philosopher who works on population ethics and the aggregation of small harms, writing for an audience of decision theorists who have already read the familiar torture-versus-dust-specks debate and are tired of it. Think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming three candidate scenarios; discard any that violates a constraint below before settling on one. I want a fresh case built to order, not a recitation of the old one.

Design a scenario in which a totalist aggregator (maximize summed welfare, under expected value) and a non-aggregator who holds that certain harms are lexically prior or threshold-protected reach opposite verdicts. Constraints: (a) the numbers must be human-scale — no 3^^^3-style tricks and no astronomical multitudes doing the work; the largest affected group has to be one a reader can picture; (b) every harm must be one the affected person would, on reflection, genuinely mind, so the aggregator cannot be dismissed as counting imperceptible specks; (c) the harms must be certain, with no lotteries or probabilities as the lever; (d) the setting must be realistic — a hospital, an insurer, a city agency — not a philosopher's fantasy; and (e) the verdicts must be generated by the two theories rather than stipulated by the setup.

This is for a seminar on whether the anti-aggregation intuition can be defended without smuggling in an arbitrary threshold, so I care most about whether the two views really come apart once the astronomical numbers are removed — that is what fixes what counts as cheating.

After the scenario, do all of the following in order: state what each theory says and why; name the single feature of the case that makes them come apart; say what happens if you delete that feature, and whether the case then collapses into the familiar specks case or into an easy one; and give the strongest objection a defender of each side would raise to show the case is rigged against them.
