For a first-year graduate seminar in rational choice and political philosophy that I am co-teaching this term, I need a decision tree — not an essay — that walks a student from a starting commitment to a named position on the paradox of voting. The starting commitment is the standard one, which I hand them before they begin: in a large national election the probability that my single ballot is decisive is astronomically small (tell them to assume something on the order of one in ten million, and it makes no difference if the true figure is smaller still), while casting the ballot carries a small but genuine cost in time and effort, and the outcome, if my preferred side wins, is worth a great deal to very many people. My co-instructor insists the whole puzzle is a pseudo-problem, so I would like the instrument to make it visible where that verdict comes from rather than assuming it away. Every branch node should be phrased as a question a student can answer about their own reasoning without already knowing which position it leads to — never 'are you an expressive voter?', but rather something like 'when you picture deciding to vote, does the value you get out of it depend on your ballot actually changing the outcome?'. Build the tree from the logical space first and attach the names second: begin with the questions, work out which combinations of answers they permit, and only then hang the actual positions on the leaves.

I want the tree to be able to reach at least these leaves, though you should tell me if any collapse into one another: instrumental altruism (the calculus is not obviously negative once the enormous benefit to others is multiplied by even a minuscule pivotality probability); evidential or team reasoning (one's vote treated as evidence about how the correlated bloc of people like oneself will act); pure expressive value (the act is intrinsically rewarding to the voter); civic duty or universalizability (vote as one would will everyone similarly placed to vote); rule-consequentialism or norm maintenance; coordination or reciprocity (I vote because others like me do, and the norm is worth sustaining); the debunker (voting is instrumentally irrational, so the behaviour needs an error-based or social-pressure explanation); and the dissolutionist (no paradox, because narrow self-interest was never the only rational ground for action). Two distinctions I specifically want the tree to exploit, because they are where the interesting action lies: first, the difference between treating one's vote as a cause of the outcome and treating it as evidence about how people like oneself will act — use the case of a voter who knows that thousands of people with identical preferences are deciding at the same moment and who has noticed that such people tend to reach the same conclusion; and second, the difference between a vote whose value is intrinsic to the act and a vote whose value depends on a norm being shared with others, since only the second can survive the observation that one's own ballot is not decisive. Include a node testing whether the student treats the negative verdict as a reductio of the voter, of the assumption of self-interest, or of the pivotality framework itself, and add a leaf for the position that pivotality is simply the wrong measure — that what matters is 'mattering' through margin, mandate, or downstream norms rather than decisiveness.

Where the tree cannot separate two leaves, say so explicitly instead of inventing a question that fakes the distinction (I suspect expressive-intrinsic and Kantian duty will be hard to prise apart, and rule-consequentialism and evidential-bloc may run together); likewise note any combination of answers that no one seems to occupy, with a one-line diagnosis of whether the gap reflects incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect. Aim for seven branch questions and roughly nine leaves, phrase each node so it is answerable without the labels, give each leaf a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment, and flag which leaves would change their verdict if we swapped the tiny pivotality probability for a large one — say a local race decided by a handful of votes — since I want to use that swap as a follow-up exercise.
