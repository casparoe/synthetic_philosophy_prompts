For the last few years I've hosted a monthly discussion group that has settled into a comfortable niche — eight or nine people, mostly working software engineers with a couple of lapsed academics, all of us steeped enough in the Sequences and the old Overcoming Bias archives to be dangerous and none of us ever having sat through a graduate seminar in metaphysics. Next month's session is mine to run, and I've chosen dispositions: the claim that properties like fragility, solubility, magnetism, and irritability are genuine properties whose identity is fixed by what their bearers would do under certain conditions, together with the pair of counterexamples that are supposed to wreck the simplest version of that claim. A fink is a case in which the triggering circumstance itself destroys the disposition before it can ever manifest; a mask is a case in which the disposition survives and the trigger occurs, but some separate interfering condition keeps the manifestation from happening. My prediction, which I'd like you to plan around, is that the group will fuse these two within the first ten minutes — both will get filed as "something is stopping the disposition," and the distinction will look to them like exactly the sort of philosopher's hair-splitting that pays no rent.

What I want from you is not an explanation of dispositions but a plan for teaching them. Set out the order in which the pieces have to come, and say why each has to precede the next — what must already be in place before the counterexamples can even be felt as counterexamples rather than as tricks, and where the conditional analysis belongs in the sequence — the proposal that "x is fragile" is just shorthand for "if x were struck, it would break" — so that it is seen to earn its keep before it is broken. The case I had intended to carry the whole session is one I invented and am not sure how to use: a person is disposed to tell the truth when asked a direct question, except that being asked a direct question is itself what makes them defensive, and their defensiveness is what makes them lie. My instinct is that this is a fink; a friend who has read more of this literature than I have insists it is a mask, since the disposition to tell the truth is plainly still there and is merely being prevented; and I have a nagging suspicion that we are both wrong and that it is a third kind of case entirely, or that the example secretly changes the subject. Tell me which it is, and then tell me whether it can bear the weight of the session or should be swapped out — and if it should be, what its replacement would have to have and to lack.

Then map the misconceptions I should expect and pin each one to the point in the arc where I should head it off. I'm fairly sure of the first (the fink/mask conflation) and of the second (the thought that these counterexamples refute dispositionalism outright, rather than only the naive conditional analysis), and of a third that runs the other way: that once you have named the categorical base — the microstructure, the wiring — the disposition is a mere label and the whole fink/mask business is uninteresting bookkeeping. I'd also bet someone reads "mask" in the everyday sense of something concealed, and that someone else concludes that because a fink can destroy a disposition, dispositions must be inert and not real causal players. Which of these will actually surface, which am I imagining, and where exactly in the sequence does each have to be defused? Finish with a single question or fresh vignette I can put to the group at the end that would show me whether they can now classify a case correctly and defend it, and with an explicit list of what I should refuse to get into at this level — I'd rather name the things I'm skipping than have them think the topic is exhausted when I stop.
