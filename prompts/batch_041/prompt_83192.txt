I am a junior in high school and the student representative on my district's wellness curriculum committee. The district has adopted a Stoic-inspired advisory module for grades 9 through 12 whose core is the dichotomy of control, taught as a sorting exercise: students list what is worrying them, draw two columns, put each worry under what they can control or what they cannot, act on the first column, accept the second. The committee chair has asked me to draft the decision procedure that advisory teachers will actually run, and I want it to be more defensible than the two-column version, because I have been reading the Stoics themselves alongside the modern popularizations and I do not think they are saying the same thing.

Please write that procedure: the questions an advisor asks, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops and hands the student to a counselor because the issue is one of values or of safety rather than of control; and where it should not be run at all. Fit it to our constraints: twenty-five minute advisory periods, twice a month, teachers with no philosophical training, and a principal who must sign off on anything a parent might quote back at us.

Three things I want the procedure to get right, because I think the popular version gets them wrong. First, in the Stoic sources what is up to us is not our outcomes but our judgments and our assent, so a student's grade, their health, and other people's opinions all fall outside it, and the sorting cannot be done by asking what a student can influence. Second, the things outside our control are not therefore things we are told not to pursue: health, money, and the good opinion of others are preferred indifferents, pursued with the reservation that nothing may prevent it, so the procedure has to leave room for effort aimed at outcomes. Third, the Stoics treat emotions as judgments about value, which makes the advice owed to a student who is being harassed quite different from the advice owed to a student who is merely nervous.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show me the output: a student panicking about college admissions decisions; a student who is being harassed by classmates and whose advisor will be tempted to tell them to work on their reaction; and a student with a chronic illness. I expect the second case to come out in a way I will not like, and I would rather see it than have it softened.

Two requests about how to answer. Where you are unsure what the historical Stoics actually held, say so instead of asserting it; a marked uncertainty is more useful to me than a confident paraphrase. And if my premise is confused — if a Stoic ethics that drops the cosmology and the claim that virtue alone is good is not really Stoicism but cognitive-behavioral advice with a classical veneer, and if turning this into a twenty-five-minute sorting exercise for teenagers cannot be done without distorting the doctrine past the point of usefulness — then say so plainly instead of writing me the checklist I asked for.
