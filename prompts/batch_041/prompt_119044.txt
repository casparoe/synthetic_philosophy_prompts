I've been reading about the Arkansas balanced-treatment trial from the early 80s (the one where the state wanted creation science taught alongside evolution, and the judge struck it down and gave that famous list of what makes something science -- natural law, testable, falsifiable, tentative, and so on). Here's what's nagging me. The creationists in that fight kept citing Popper, because he'd said somewhere that natural selection isn't really a testable scientific theory but a metaphysical research programme, and that it verges on a tautology -- survivors survive. The plaintiffs' side had philosophers testifying (Ruse, I think) that falsifiability is the mark of science, and the ruling leaned on that. But Popper himself later said he'd changed his mind and that selection is testable after all.

So: analyze that trial through Popper's actual views on Darwinism, and run it in both directions. What did Popper really hold, and how much of it were the creationists entitled to use? Was the trial's falsifiability criterion Popper's criterion, or a courtroom simplification that Popper would have rejected? And then the reverse: does the episode tell us something about Popper's position -- that calling Darwinism a metaphysical research programme was a mistake he had to walk back, or that the whole demarcation exercise is what's unstable? Don't hedge into a survey. Give me a verdict on who misused whom, and say plainly where Popper's framework just doesn't speak to what the court needed.
