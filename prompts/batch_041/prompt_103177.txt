Say Mazzini — founder of Young Italy, died 1872 — got to read Hobsbawm on the invention of tradition and Anderson on imagined communities. Not the whole books, just the core claim: that nations arent natural ancient units, that the continuity with the past is largely manufactured, that the community is imagined by print capitalism and state schooling, and that the stuff that feels most ancient (kilts, folk songs, national myths) is often only a century or two old. Whats his reaction? I dont want just "he'd hate it" — I want to know where he'd actually have to dig in. Which of his own commitments survive the Hobsbawm/Anderson picture — the nation as the natural unit of humanity, its providential mission, the duty to liberate it — and which get pulled out from under him? And flip it: is there anything in his view that Hobsbawm or Anderson cant really touch, that they'd have to concede? Be concrete about the friction, not just "times changed."
