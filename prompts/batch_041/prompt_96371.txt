I am a PhD student in philosophy working on personal identity and the ethics of advance care planning, and I have been trying to formulate a principle that captures my own verdicts about when an earlier self's directive should bind a later self. I am not interested in a survey of the literature; I want a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that does justice to the pattern below, says what it implies for cases I have not listed, and tells me which of my verdicts I should give up.

My verdicts:

1. A competent adult with a progressive terminal illness signs a DNR order and, after losing decision-making capacity, develops pneumonia. I think the DNR should be honored, even if the later self seems to want comfort and would not resist treatment.

2. The same adult, while still competent but in severe pain, asks for the DNR to be revoked and for antibiotics. I think the later competent request should override the earlier directive, because the earlier self was not then in the position of the later self.

3. A person with early dementia signs a directive refusing antibiotics for pneumonia once she no longer recognizes her family. She later no longer recognizes them, appears content, and says she wants to live. I think the directive should be honored, though I find this verdict harder to defend than 1.

4. A person with early dementia signs a directive refusing all life-prolonging treatment once she needs help with all activities of daily living. She later needs such help, but she is cheerful, socially engaged, and says she is glad to be alive. I think the directive should not be honored, and treatment should be provided.

5. A person with bipolar disorder signs a psychiatric advance directive refusing electroconvulsive therapy during future manic episodes. He later becomes manic, refuses ECT, and is miserable and dangerous. I think the directive should be honored, even though he now refuses.

6. A person with anorexia nervosa signs an advance directive refusing artificial nutrition when her weight falls below a threshold. She later falls below it, refuses nutrition, and says she wants to die. I think the directive should not be honored, because anorexia is distorting her current evaluative standpoint.

7. A person with locked-in syndrome had previously said that if he ever became locked-in he would want to die. He is now locked-in, communicates reliably, and says he wants to live. I think his current wish should override the earlier statement.

8. A person in a persistent vegetative state had said he would never want to be kept alive in such a state. He is now in that state with no evidence of awareness. I think the earlier directive should be honored.

9. A person with a progressive neurological illness signs a directive refusing ventilation when he can no longer speak. He later can no longer speak but communicates by eye gaze, is cognitively intact, and wants ventilation. I think the current wish should override the directive.

10. A person signs a directive refusing blood products for religious reasons. He later loses capacity after an accident and needs blood. I think the directive should be honored, even though the later self cannot consent or refuse.

My tentative principle is this: the authority of an earlier directive depends not on the numerical identity of the later self, nor simply on the later self's current welfare, but on whether the later self can be said to have authorial standing with respect to the directive. A later self has authorial standing when its current evaluative standpoint is either continuous with the earlier self's or, if discontinuous, still capable of recognizing the earlier self's reasons as reasons it could have had. When authorial standing is absent, the directive binds as a precedent; when it is present, the later self's current wishes have priority. I call the first kind of authority authorial and the second kind experiential, though I am not sure the labels help.

I can see that this principle may not deliver all ten verdicts. In particular, cases 3 and 4 look alike in structure—both involve a later self who is content and wants to live—but I want opposite results. Cases 5 and 6 also look alike in structure—both involve a later self whose current refusal is bound up with the condition the directive was meant to address—but I want opposite results. I suspect the difference in 3 and 4 is something about whether the later self is still the one whose life is at stake in a way that matters, or whether the directive was aimed at a state that has already arrived. I suspect the difference in 5 and 6 is something about whether the later self's refusal is itself an expression of the illness or a response to it. But I cannot make these suspicions precise without generating counterexamples elsewhere.

Please give me a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. State it precisely. For each clause, tell me which case motivates it. Then tell me what it implies for at least two cases I have not listed—for example, a directive written by a young adult about a future dementia diagnosis, or a directive that a later self cannot remember having written—and whether those implications are ones I should accept. Finally, tell me honestly which of my ten verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should revise the verdict or the principle. I would rather have a principle that overturns one of my verdicts than a gerrymandered principle that saves all ten.
