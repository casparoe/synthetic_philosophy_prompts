I'm a third-year PhD student in comparative literature, at work on a chapter about sixteenth-century French verse, and the book that started the trouble is Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot — a single short poem rendered dozens of ways, no two versions agreeing on what to keep. Doing the work myself, I keep running into a structure I need to name and can't. Take a text whose formal constraint is not ornament but machinery: the constraint is what produces the sense rather than a frame placed around it. Then add a second, independent fact — in the target language the constraint is imperceptible. A rhyme can be heard and a line count can be counted, but a constraint on the sound or the spelling of the source cannot be perceived by my reader at all: a pun that lives only in French, an alliterative pattern built on a sound English does not have, an omission no reader of my English would guess was deliberate. The alphabetic psalms are the cleanest case I know, since a constraint that each line begin with the next letter of the alphabet is doing real thematic work, and in English it simply vanishes unless the translator steps outside the text to announce it. So I am choosing among three bad options. Reproduce the constraint mechanically and I get a look-alike that satisfies it inertly, produces nothing of what the form produced, and is invisible to the reader anyway. Let it go and keep the sense, and I have lost the thing that generated the sense. Substitute a different constraint that the reader can actually perceive and that does some work of its own — compensation, I suppose — and the reader perceives something the source's reader never perceived.

What I cannot tell is whether this has a name. I know the standard vocabulary — formal versus dynamic equivalence, compensation, untranslatability — but each of those seems to describe a trade-off between two goods that can be weighed against one another, and here the constraint and the meaning are not two goods: the constraint is what does the meaning, and reproducing it in the target does not reproduce the doing. The perceptual half of the problem, the constraint that survives only as an invisible discipline, I cannot find described at all; when I raise it with colleagues I am told it is just untranslatability and the conversation stops.

So: is there a term for the class of cases in which a formal constraint is both semantically load-bearing and undetectable in the target? If there is, I want to know what it covers and, more usefully, what it excludes. If there is not, I would like you to propose one, defined sharply enough that I can sort cases by it. A rhyme whose only office is musical should fall outside; a rhyme that carries an argument should fall inside; and I want to know where the hard cases land — a constraint the reader can only be told about in a note, a constraint the target language can honour only by accident, a constraint whose whole point is that it cannot be perceived. I am not after a survey or a list of things to read. I want to know whether the thing has a name, and if it does not, what to call it and where its edges are.
