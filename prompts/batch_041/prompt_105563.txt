You are a philosopher of biology and cognitive scientist who knows the cultural-evolution literature in detail and has no patience for surveys. I want a diagnosis, not a summary.

Background: For decades there has been a running dispute about whether cultural change is properly described as a Darwinian process, and it has not moved. One camp treats cultural change as genuinely Darwinian: cultural variants are transmitted between people with enough fidelity to support heritable variation, some variants are transmitted more than others, and the resulting dynamics can be modeled with the tools of population genetics, with cumulative cultural adaptation falling out. The other camp insists culture is not replicated but reconstructed: what passes between people is a partial cue that each learner rebuilds using their own priors, so transmission is systematically biased toward certain forms, and the explanatory action lies in those cognitive biases and the resulting attractors rather than in populational selection. The two camps cite the same ethnography and the same experiments and, as far as I can tell, agree on the facts of how transmission actually works.

My hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than assume: the impasse is not empirical. I suspect it is a disagreement about what the word Darwinian is doing — whether it names a substantive mechanism (heritable variation plus differential reproduction) that culture either has or lacks, or a family-resemblance label, or a modeling framework whose value is independent of whether the underlying process literally replicates anything. But I can imagine other diagnoses: that the camps have different explanatory targets (population-level distributions versus individual cognitive mechanisms) and are answering different questions under one heading; that they differ about what makes a formal model explanatory rather than merely redescription; or that there is a hidden factual question about the fidelity and discreteness of cultural variants that neither side has settled.

Do this. First, brainstorm a list of candidate diagnoses of the impasse and say briefly what evidence from each side's actual commitments would discriminate among them. Then commit to the diagnosis you find best supported and defend it concretely — tell me what each side is actually committed to, and why the other side cannot simply grant it. Say explicitly whether the disagreement is verbal, substantive, or a case of different questions sharing a name. Finally, given your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result, a decisive experiment, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected, and explain why. Think carefully before committing, and keep the whole answer grounded in the positions themselves rather than in who said what when.
