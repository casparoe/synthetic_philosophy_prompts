In two weeks I'm presenting a paper to a room of about twenty people at an AI safety org. The audience is mostly ML engineers who build or evaluate so-called impact measures / low-impact agents, plus two decision theorists, plus one person who's done formal work on corrigibility and will be the most dangerous questioner. My thesis (roughly): low-impact AI, i.e. trying to penalize side effects without fully specifying what the agent should value, isn't just hard but incoherent in a specific way. Every impact measure presupposes a privileged default trajectory — a what-would-have-happened-otherwise baseline — and that baseline is a normative choice wearing descriptive clothes. Concretely I distinguish three flavors of default that the literature keeps running together: the null-action default (what happens if the agent does nothing), the intended-action default (what happens if it does exactly what was asked and nothing more), and the human-counterfactual default (what the human would have done in its place). They give different verdicts on the same case, and nobody has said which one the measure is supposed to use. So any given measure is either empty (it penalizes nothing anyone could object to) or it secretly encodes a full value ordering, which defeats the value-agnostic selling point. I'll use a couple of small cases to make it bite — e.g. an agent told to fetch a file that also wrecks a wasp nest on the way, and an agent whose action changes how many people are born downstream.
