I'm working on a short essay for a blog that maybe four people read, and I'd like you to go at it hard. It's about effective altruism — specifically whether the three big lines of criticism (institutions, earning to give, measurability) are really three criticisms or one. Heres the draft:

**The Ledger Only Sees What It Can Count**

I used to think the standard criticisms of effective altruism were seperate complaints. Lately I think they're one complaint wearing three hats. The measurability criticism says EA privileges what can be quantified. The earning-to-give criticism says the movement spent a chunk of the 2010s telling ambitious graduates to take finance and consulting jobs and donate the salary. The institutional criticism says that EA's funders and employers — not its arguments — determine which problems actually get worked on. Stack them and you get one mechanism: EA outsources its moral judgment to a ledger, and then outsources the ledger to whoever is paying for it.

Here's the part I actually want to defend. The interesting failure isn't at the level of individual interventions. Cost-effectiveness estimates for bed nets or deworming are probably fine in the weak sense that they're better than nothing and the errors partly cancel. Even the deworming fight, which is the nastiest dispute in the space, is a fight about an estimate — that's a healthy kind of fight to have. The failure is one level up, at cause selection, where numbers are doing rhetorical work they can't support.

Every estimate needs a counterfactual baseline: what would have happened otherwise. At the intervention level the baseline is usually defensible. At the cause level the baseline is basically a story about what the world looks like without you, and nobody ever audits it. And the stories all lean the same way — they're pessimistic about everyone else. The marginal dollar is assumed to be wasted. The marginal talented person is assumed to be replaceable. The neglected problem is assumed to stay neglected forever unless EA shows up. Each assumption is individually plausible, but they compound multiplicatively, so EA's estimate of its own marginal impact gets inflated by something like the product of them. I'm calling this counterfactual inflation.

Second thing: cause prioritization is presented as a portfolio. Global health, animal welfare, AI risk — three bets, diversify across them. But they aren't independent. All three depend on the same underlying condition, namely that there exist institutions capable of absorbing large amounts of money and talent without wasting most of it. When that condition fails, they fail together. So the diversification is partly cosmetic, and the honest description is that EA has made one large bet on absorptive capacity, sliced into three pieces for display purposes.

Which brings the institutional criticism back around. A movement that replaces moral judgment with measurement has to hand the measuring to someone, and that someone turns out to be a small number of donors plus the organizations those donors fund. That's not corruption, it's just what happens when you build a decision procedure that requires a scorekeeper. The scorekeeper's priors become the movement's priors, and because the priors are expressed as numbers, they're much harder to argue with than a rich person's whims would be. Legibility is the mechanism, not the bug.

The obvious counterargument is that you could say all this about any philanthropic tradition, and EA's only real vice is that it publishes the reasoning everyone else keeps in a drawer. Maybe. I genuinely can't tell if that's a defense or a cope.

4. If you think the whole essay collapses, tell me which premise kills it. I'd rather find out now than in the comments section.
