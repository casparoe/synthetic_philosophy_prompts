In our philosophy reading group, someone defended a citizens' assembly — a few hundred randomly selected laypeople deciding a technical policy question — with the line 'two heads are better than one': more heads, he said, so more likely right than a handful of experts. I'm fairly sure the proverb is doing several different jobs at once, and that only some of them support that inference. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims the sentence could be making — from the merely motivational (two people keep each other alert and catch each other's slips) through information pooling and error cancellation, through the aggregation results showing that majorities of independent, better-than-chance voters converge on the truth, to the idea that two solvers with different heuristics can beat two copies of the best one — and give a verdict on each: platitude, substantive but defensible, or false as stated. I'd particularly like the places where the arithmetic bites: my impression is that the tidy aggregation result needs independence, and that two voters with a fair coin to break ties come out exactly no better than one, while if individual competence falls below one half aggregation actively makes things worse; and that a shared briefing, which makes the errors correlated, may do more damage than head-counting can repair. Tell me which readings would actually license his inference, and what further case would separate a competence-based reading from a diversity-based one. Someone else mentioned that averaging a single person's two separate estimates of a quantity beats either estimate alone; say whether that is the same claim or a different one. Please answer in British English.
