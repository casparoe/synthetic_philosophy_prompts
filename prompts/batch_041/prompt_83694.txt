I'm in a grad seminar on the regress problem and I have to run the movie session next week. We've done the standard taxanomy — foundationalism, coherentism, and the third option, infinitism, where the chain of reasons just never terminates and we're told that's actually fine, it's not vicious — plus the Agrippan trilemma framing where the three options are the only exits and each one looks bad. My plan is to argue that Memento isn't just the usual "memory is unreliable, so skepticism" thing everybody says it is, but a machine for generating trilemmas: Leonard's tattoos and Polaroids and scribbled notes form a chain where every link borrows its authority from another link, the Sammy Jankis story is the one item he treats as bedrock even though the film quietly suggests it might be his own story displaced, and the last scene shown — where he destroys the one piece of evidence that doesn't fit and deliberately writes himself a note he knows is false — has the chain terminating not in anything self-evident but in an act of will. A friend of mine keeps pushing back that this is a cheap trick, that the movie is just about grief and self-deception, and anyway that infinitism only looks absurd if you assume the chain has to be walked through in time — if the infinite thing is just a set of propositions you could in principle keep producing, nobody ever has to traverse it, so the notebook is less an infinite regress than a filing cabinet. He also says the real question the film raises isn't about reasons at all but about retention, since Leonard can produce a further reason at any moment and still can't hold a chain together, and that might be a problem for all three views rather than one.

So: read Memento through the regress problem and tell me what you actually think. Is Leonard a foundationalist who keeps mislaying his foundations, a coherentist whose entire self-image is a mutually supporting web with no bottom, or an infinitist in the set-of-available-reasons sense — and does the film's structure (color scenes backward, black-and-white scenes forward) put the audience in one of those positions, given that we only find out what justifies a scene after we've already accepted it? I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: where the epistemology genuinely illuminates a scene or a choice, say so, and where the film resists the reading and refuses to be an illustration of anything, say that too. Which scenes would you point the group to, and does the movie add anything to the debate that argument alone doesn't — specifically about whether a justification chain that has to be retained rather than merely available changes which of the three options is even humanly possible for a creature like us. No reading list or citations needed, I just want the criticism. American English please.
