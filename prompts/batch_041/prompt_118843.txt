I'm assembling a session for an undergraduate course that pairs philosophy of mind with science fiction film, and for the week on mechanism and free will I've settled on Blade Runner — the 1982 film, in the cut that includes the unicorn daydream and the origami unicorn. The standard reading of the film (do the replicants count as persons; is Deckard himself a replicant) is well worn, and I do not want another version of it. What I want instead is a reading through the idea Hofstadter named sphexishness: the thought, drawn from the digger wasp Sphex, that a creature can produce elaborate, even impressive behavior while being locked into a fixed routine it cannot step outside of. The wasp drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, inspects the burrow, and if the cricket is moved a few inches during the inspection, drags it back and begins the inspection over — apparently without limit. Dennett leaned on this as a picture of behavior that looks thoughtful but is mindless, and others have pushed back that the repetition may be adaptive rather than stupid, and that the example has been made to carry more philosophical freight than the ethology can bear. I want the pushback taken seriously: treat sphexishness as a matter of degree and of description rather than a binary, and treat flexibility as something that has to be earned rather than assumed.

Read the film through that lens, working from scenes and structures rather than dialogue (I'm not asking you to reproduce any). The Voight-Kampff test is itself a procedure: a fixed set of questions in a fixed order, measuring involuntary responses. It is a sphex-detector, in effect — it looks for the wrong kind of responsiveness — and it is administered by people who are themselves running a script. Deckard's job is called retirement, and it is a loop: identify, test, kill, report. Roy Batty is the obvious candidate for genuine flexibility; he improvises, negotiates, seeks out his maker, and at the end does something his designers had no reason to program, sparing the man who has been hunting him, in a scene whose whole force depends on the fact that his four years are nearly up. But I want the harder question asked rather than the sentimental one: is Roy's final act a break in the loop, or the highest-order expression of one — a being built to value its own survival and its own kind, behaving exactly as such a being would once survival is off the table? And is there a level at which the film's humans — Gaff with his origami, Bryant with his threats, Tyrell with his chess game and his thick glasses — are the rigid ones, while the replicants are the ones who can revise? If it helps to have a name for the thing I'm worried about, call it second-order sphexishness: a creature that can only follow rules is not free, but a creature that can always revise its rules may simply be following a higher-order rule, and I want to know whether the film offers any image of a mind that escapes that regress rather than merely moving up a level.

So: what does the film show, complicate, or get wrong about the difference between mechanical rigidity and genuine flexibility? Which scene should I put on the board to make the point land, and which scene resists the reading, where an honest critic has to admit the film is trading on an ambiguity or where the replicants come out looking more sphexish than the humans? Close by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the wasp and the arguments about it do not — whether seeing a mind run down, with a borrowed memory and a body that fails on schedule, tells us something about freedom that the abstract arguments cannot.
