I am trying to understand a passage from Frank Ramsey that is routinely cited as the origin of the idea that the probability of an indicative conditional is the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent, and I want to know how much of that equation the passage actually licenses. The passage reads:

"If two people are arguing 'If p, then q?' and are both in doubt as to p, they are adding p hypothetically to their stock of knowledge and arguing on that basis about q; so that in a sense 'If p, q' and 'If p, ¬q' are contradictories. We can say that they are fixing their degree of belief in q given p. If p turns out false, these degrees of belief are rendered void."

Two things puzzle me. First, the passage describes a procedure two people follow when they argue about a conditional, yet the thesis everyone extracts from it concerns a single person's degree of belief in a proposition; how is that transition supposed to work, and does the passage require the conditional to have a truth value at all? Second, Lewis's triviality results appear to show that no conditional can have its probability equal to the corresponding conditional probability except in trivial cases, so if the passage does commit one to that equation it commits one to something false, and if it does not, I want to know what weaker thesis it does commit one to. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the passage is best read as a semantic thesis, a thesis about how to evaluate a conditional under supposition, or a thesis about assertion — and then give your reasoning, explaining what work the clause about degrees of belief being rendered void and the claim that the two conditionals are contradictories in a sense are doing in your reading. Present the triviality result only so far as it bears on which reading survives, and say what a defender of the equation would have to give up.
