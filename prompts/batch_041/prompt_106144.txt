I'm designing a ninety-minute graduate seminar session in the philosophy of the human sciences on how — and whether — one may infer beliefs and institutions from mute material remains. The room is split: the philosophers arrive convinced that reading meaning off an object is either trivial (the thing simply means what it looks like) or impossible (objects are silent, so all such inference is projection), while the archaeologists arrive convinced they already know how the inference works and that the philosophers are naive about the evidence. I want you to plan how I should explain this, not to explain it to me.

Take the role of an expert in archaeological theory and in the philosophy of the human sciences, and work carefully: before you propose a sequence, brainstorm the conceptual dependencies — write out what must be understood before what, and why — and only then commit to an order.

The plan should cover the following.

First, the order of ideas, with the dependencies made explicit: which distinction or result has to be in place before the next step can even be stated intelligibly. I have a working scaffold that I want you to test rather than accept. I propose to organize the session around three gaps — a trace-to-behavior gap (what survives is the residue of processes, not a frozen moment of life), a behavior-to-practice gap (getting from a single act to a repeated, socially patterned way of doing things), and a practice-to-institution gap (getting from a practice to the norm, office, or conviction it is supposed to express). Tell me whether that three-gap scaffold is the right skeleton or whether two of the gaps are really one; if two collapse, say which, and say whether the collapse changes the order.

Second, the carrying example. I am inclined to build the session on a hoard of standardized metal weights together with a few sealings, using it to show that the very same assemblage supports two incompatible institutional readings — a central authority enforcing a standard, and a decentralized network of traders converging on a convention — and that the objects cannot choose between them without an added bridging assumption. Assess that choice: does this carrier do the work, and what does it have that a burial or a set of figurines lacks? If you think a different carrier is better, name it and say what it must have and must lack. Do not use the grave-goods-to-afterlife example as the carrier; tell me briefly why it is the wrong choice for this audience even though it is the one they will expect.

Third, the misconceptions, in the order they will actually surface in the room, and the precise point in the sequence at which each must be headed off: that the object speaks for itself; that a lavish burial implies a belief in an afterlife; that anything not obviously utilitarian is "ritual," treated as a residual category; that a material pattern which is underdetermined is therefore evidentially worthless; and that the absence of a material correlate is the absence of the institution. Add any I have missed, and tell me where in the sequence the underdetermination lesson must land so that it deepens the students' confidence rather than collapsing it into "so anything goes."

Fourth, the single question I should ask at the end that would show the idea has landed rather than merely been admired — something that makes them perform the inference on a fresh case and expose the bridging assumption it requires.

Fifth, what I should deliberately leave out at this level, and how I should say so out loud.

End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" that states, in one sentence, the single dependency that most constrains the order and the one misconception most likely to wreck the session.
