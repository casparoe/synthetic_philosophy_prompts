I'm a reporter working up a piece about moral dilemmas — Sophie's choice, the trolley, all that — and I interviewed a philosophy professor who handed me the tidy version, which I was about to drop stright into the piece. Here's what he said:

'A genuine moral dilemma would be a case where you're required to do A and required to do B and you can't do both, so you're damned either way. That can't happen. For one thing, if you couldn't have avoided doing wrong, then you weren't obligated not to do it — ought implies can. For another, morality has to be consistent; it can't tell you both do A and don't do A. So the cases people call dilemmas — Sophie, the trolley, whatever — are really just cases where we don't yet know which obligation outweighs the other. The conflict is in our heads, not in the morality. Once you work out which duty is stronger, there's a right answer and the dilemma evaporates.'

Is that a fair statement of the "there are no genuine dilemmas" position, and does his argument actually get him there? Go through it claim by claim — what someone who thinks genuine dilemmas are possible would nod along with, what they'd call a cheat, and whether his last two sentences follow from anything true in the rest. Start with a one-sentence verdict, then the breakdown. No reading list needed; I just want to know where the load-bearing move is and wether it holds. 400 words max.
