I'm a science journalist — mostly public health and environment stuff — and I'm deep in a feature about a city water utility that knew its pipes were leaching lead and just never replaced them. Not a cover-up exactly; more like a hundred non-decisions stacked on top of each other over fifteen years. Everybody wants a villian, and the more i report the more it looks like nobody did anything. Which sent me down a rabbit hole on causation by omission and now I'm out of my depth. Hoping you can help.

Role for you: a philosopher of science who works on causation — the kind of person who's spent years on the doing/allowing distinction and on whether absences can be causes at all. Please think carefully, and start by brainstorming: lay out for yourself what a really good answer would need to cover, sketch the competing positions, and only then write the explanation. Skip the bibliography, I'm not going to read it, just the ideas.

What I want is the actual state of the debate, not the cocktail-party version. Why is "an omission caused it" supposed to be strange in the first place — what's the worry about absences as relata of the causal relation, and why do some people think the counterfactual story ("it wouldn't have happened if she'd acted") settles it while others think it's hopeless? Then walk me through how the main accounts handle omissions — the counterfactual/dependence one, the interventionist one, the mechanistic or powers/"production" one — and where each of them actually breaks.

Three cases I came up with that I'd like you to run through the accounts, because they're the ones that matter for the story:

1. Redundant omission: my guy never signs the work order, but the budget office was going to block it anyway and his successor wouldn't have signed either, so even if he'd acted, nothing changes. Counterfactually he's clean. Is he? What does each account say?

2. The profligacy problem: I didn't fly to that city and fix the pipes myself, so by the counterfactual test my omission is also a cause of the kids' lead levels. So is everybody's. How does anyone ever pick out the one omission that "caused" it — is that a fact about the world, or about our norms and expectations? And what do I do with the experimental work suggesting people's causal judgments shift with moral valence?

3. Double prevention: the guy who quietly got the inspector reassigned before the inspection. He didn't poison anyone; he just stopped someone else from stopping it. Does that count as causing, and if the accounts disagree here, why?

Last thing, and it's the part i care about most: even if omissions are causes, we don't blame omitters the way we blame doers. Is that asymmetry a seperate moral fact, or is it smuggled into the metaphysics? I have a half-formed hunch i'd like you to attack — that "omission causation" isn't a kind of causation at all, it's just how we redistribute causal credit for moral and rhetorical reasons. Tell me where that's wrong, and where it's uncomfortably right.
