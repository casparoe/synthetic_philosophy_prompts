Me and some friends have been going back and forth for weeks about "coherent extrapolated volition" and "idealized preferences" — the whole family of ideas that says what you really want, or what your good actually is, is whatever you'd want if you knew more, thought more clearly, and were less of a mess internally. Trouble is, every time one of us leans on it, someone else says "idealized how?" and we just go in circels, so before we fight about which version is right I want the spec written down. Give me the desiderata that any adequate theory of a person's "real" or idealized preferences has to meet: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse, the stuff it has to stay consistent with, and the reason behind each requirement. Then tell me which of those requirements are non-negotiable and which are just house rules of one camp, which single one does the most to kill off the versions people actually defend, and flag any two that I probably can't have at the same time.

I care about this for a concrete reason: I'm trying to work out when it's legitimately OK to override what someone says she wants, for her own good, so the theory needs to be good enough to license or forbid real paternalism, not just to look nice on a chalkbord. So please make it survive the four cases I keep getting stuck on. (1) The person whose idealized self would want to be someone else entirely — different values, different projects, even a different name — is that still her preference, or did the idealization quietly swap in a stranger? (2) The procedure problem: the answer comes out different depending on whether you idealize by adding information, by demanding internal coherence, by demanding reflection, or by simulating decades of therapy, and there doesn't seem to be a fact of the matter about which one is the real idealization. (3) The person whose actual, non-idealized preference is to not be idealized at all — she thinks the whole exercise is creepy and violates her; does the theory have to respect that, and can it? (4) The cult member or the addict whose "better-informed, more coherent" self still endorses the life we're all sure is ruining her. Anyway — lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and don't restate my question.
