I teach a mid-level undergraduate seminar in moral psychology, and next week I have to introduce the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the question of what, if anything, all pleasures have in common. My students will have read Bentham and Mill and will arrive confident that they already know what pleasure is. Do not explain hedonism or the problem to me; I know the terrain. What I need is a teaching plan: the order in which the pieces have to come so that the problem feels forced rather than contrived, the single case that should carry the whole session, the misconceptions the students will bring or form and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off, and one closing question that will tell me whether they have felt the force of the problem rather than merely memorized a taxonomy.

Build the session so that it begins from the students' own introspection rather than from a definition. I do not want to announce that pleasure is heterogeneous and then defend the claim; I want them to discover that their confident report of what pleasure feels like collapses on their own cases. Make each step forced by the one before it: the moment the naive sensory-quality answer is stated, the cases that break it should already be on the table, and the attitudinal response should arrive as a repair the students themselves propose rather than as a position I hand them. I am tempted to carry the session on the pleasure of relief — the scratch finally scratched, the headache that stops — but tell me whether that is the right carrier or whether something else does the job better, and in either case say what the carrying case must contain and what it must not contain. In particular, it has to include at least one pleasure the students will resist calling a pleasure at all and at least one they will insist is not a bodily sensation, and it must not be a case so exotic that they treat it as a curiosity rather than as a test of their own view.

I expect three misconceptions in particular: first, that pleasure is simply a sensation with a characteristic felt quality; second, that the attitudinal view is trivially true because liking something is obviously part of enjoying it; third, that the whole problem is merely verbal. Tell me where in the arc each of these surfaces, which of them the students will voice and which will stay silent, and how I should handle each at that moment rather than in a general cleanup at the end.

Finally, tell me what to leave out — which disputes I should explicitly set aside at this level and name as set aside, rather than let them leak in and muddy the session — and give me the one closing question whose answers would reveal the difference between a student who can recite the positions and a student who has understood why the problem is a problem.
