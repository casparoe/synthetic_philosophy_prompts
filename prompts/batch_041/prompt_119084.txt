I'm an undergraduate in a course on early modern philosophy, and we have just spent three weeks on Berkeley: the Principles, the Dialogues, and the interpreters' dispute over the so-called master argument. I'm now writing a short precisification essay, and the thing I keep running into is the tag "to be is to be perceived." In seminar it was used in two incompatible ways. One student invoked it to shut down a question about what an unperceived tree is like: since the tree's being just is its being perceived, he said, the question presupposes something incoherent and there is nothing further to investigate. Another student, defending Berkeley against the charge of losing the external world, used the very same tag to say that the tree persists as a standing possibility of perception — which the first student rejected on the grounds that on that reading the tag does no work at all. A third pointed out that if being is being perceived, the minds doing the perceiving had better not be things whose being is being perceived, or the position eats itself.

I would like the tag broken down rather than either defended or attacked. Give me the menu of distinct precise claims it could be expressing, ordered from the nearly trivial to the genuinely radical, and for each one say: what exactly the claim is; what kind of claim it is (a proposal about the meaning of the word "exist," an identity or constitution claim about the things themselves, a dependence claim, a claim about what we can conceive, a claim about the phenomenology of sensory experience, or something else); whether it is plausible as a reading of Berkeley and whether it is plausible as a standalone thesis; and whether it would actually support the use each student put it to — in particular, whether it licenses dismissing the question of unperceived objects, or instead supports the standing-possibility reading.

Several distinctions strike me as doing hidden work, and I would like each reading located with respect to them. Does the claim apply to minds as well as to ideas and sensible things, or are minds supposed to be known in some other way — the idea/notion contrast, if I have that right? Does "perceived" mean actually perceived by some mind at some time, perceivable, or perceived whenever anyone happens to be looking? Is the tag a one-way implication from existence to perception or a biconditional, and what would the reverse direction commit one to (does hallucinating or merely imagining something give it being)? Are the objects in question the immediate objects of sense or the ordinary things one is said to perceive mediately? Does the tag extend to mathematical objects, which Berkeley seems to treat as ideas too? Must the perceiver be divine, and if so is the appeal to God a consequence of the tag or an extra premise? And is the tag a premise in the master argument, a restatement of its conclusion, or just a summary of it?

Finally, tell me which reading the tag's repeaters most likely have in mind when they use it to end a discussion, which readings are simply false or incoherent, and which single reading is the strongest available if the tag is going to carry the weight of the first student's dismissal rather than collapsing into something a realist could happily accept. I want a numbered menu with verdicts, not a survey of Berkeley interpretation.
