I report on science and ideas, and I keep encountering a position that I can neither dismiss nor state cleanly. It is a lexical threshold version of negative utilitarianism: mild pains and pleasures are commensurable on a single scale and can be traded off against one another, but suffering above some intensity — torture, say, or the worst forms of chronic illness — is lexically prior to any amount of positive experience, so that no finite quantity of pleasure can outweigh one such episode. I want a number between 0 and 1 for your credence that there exists a version of this view that is both internally coherent and consistent with considered moral judgments after reflection, and I want the number decomposed rather than asserted. Break it into at least: (a) the probability that pleasure and pain are commensurable at all, in the sense that comparisons of quantity are meaningful; (b) the probability that a lexical threshold can be specified non-arbitrarily, given that any line will look like a convention; (c) the probability that lexical priority survives the standard aggregation problems — a being who gains enormous utility from others' suffering, the conclusion that a vast population of barely positive lives is better than a smaller flourishing one, the risk of paralysis before any action that might bring about extreme suffering — without collapsing into ordinary negative utilitarianism or into an absolute prohibition on action; (d) the probability that, even if coherent, the view matches considered moral judgment rather than merely codifying a bias toward the worst case; and (e) whatever other term you think belongs. Give a number and one line of justification for each, show how they combine, and tell me which term your final credence is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move it most. I am not asking for a survey of the literature; I am asking where the view's load-bearing assumptions are.
