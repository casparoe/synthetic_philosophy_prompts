I'm working up a position for a side project and I keep going round in circles, so I want the criteria sorted before I go any further. The position is roughly this: the badness of suffering and the goodness of enjoyment don't sit on one common scale, at least not all the way up. Pain has priority over pleasure — a certain amount of agony is never bought off by any quantity of mild delight, however large the quantity; but the priority is threshold-shaped rather than absolute, so below the threshold (small aches, small pleasures) ordinary weighing up applies, and there's some level of suffering above which enjoyment stops counting as compensation at all. Call it a lexical threshold view in a negative-utilitarian mood. I'm not asking wether the view survives; I want to know what it, or anything else in the area, would have to do to be in the running.

So: not a defence and not a demolition, the spec. What does any adequate theory of the pleasure/pain trade-off have to deliver — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the formal and empirical results it can't contradict — with the consideration behind each requirement? And be clear about which requirements are genuine constraints on any view here and which are just prejudices of one camp; I'd rather be told that half of what people demand is a stipulation than get a padded list. Then flag any pair of requirements you think can't be jointly satisfied, because that's the bit I actually care about. My suspicion is that the interesting cases are where a view is asked to be both action-guiding under risk and faithful to the intuition that no amount of pleasure compensates for certain suffering.

To make it concrete, the cases I want it to handle are at least: the torture-versus-mild-pleasures intuition; the pinprick-versus-paradise counter-intuition, where the same priority principle seems to license any tiny pain trumping any amount of joy; a long life of mild contentment weighed against a short sharp agony; two-person versions, where one person's ache is traded against another person's bliss; and prospects, where a tiny chance of something terrible is pitted against a certainty of moderate good. I'd also like the distinctions it has to respect set out — lexically infinite weight versus mere priority within a range; the ordering of outcomes versus the ordering of lotteries; intrapersonal versus interpersonal comparison; and the different things 'commensurable' might mean (one cardinal scale, one ordinal ranking, bare pairwise comparability). The purpose it has to be good enough for is decision-making under uncertainty, so a view that's only defined for riskless comparisons doesn't meet the spec. Tell me which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out, and feel free to close with a few lines on how the two obvious shapes — plain negative utilitarianism, and ordinary additive utilitarianism — fare against the list.
