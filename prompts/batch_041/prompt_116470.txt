I'm a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist binds his future self with written instructions he can no longer interpret or revoke, and Memento is the film I keep measuring the draft against. I want a reading of it as a precommitment problem in its hard form — not the familiar Ulysses-and-the-sirens case of weakness of will, but the case where the self who writes the rules and the self who must live under them no longer share a will, and the man who feeds the bound self his facts, Teddy, has an agenda of his own. The constitutional analogy is what I'm really after: a precommitment is only as binding as its entrenchment, and Leonard holds the pen, so nothing he writes is entrenched against him; whether a rule can genuinely govern its own revision is a real puzzle, not a plot device. Tell me where the film dramatizes something the arguments about precommitment and entrenchment handle badly, and where it cheats — buying pathos with amnesia that the underlying problem hasn't earned. Give me one definite reading with reasons rather than a survey of options, name the scenes that carry it, and say plainly which scene resists it.
