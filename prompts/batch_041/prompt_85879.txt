I'm trying to wrap my head around Mill's System of Logic, specifically the bit in Book VI where he lays out the inverse deductive method and says it's the right way to do history. As I understand the gist of it: instead of starting from general laws and deducing particular events, you start with empirical generalizations about how societies change (things like despotism tending to decay into anarchy or whatever), and then you try to verify those by showing they can be deduced from more fundamental laws — laws of human nature, laws of mind, and what he calls the laws of social statics. He seems to think that without this deduction you're just stuck with low-level regularities that might be coincidences, so history isn't really a science yet.

What I don't get is how the deduction is supposed to actually go. If the fundamental laws are laws of individual psychology, how do you get from people seeking pleasure and avoiding pain to something like the fall of the Roman Empire or the Reformation? It feels like there's a massive gap, and Mill just sort of waves at the state of society as an intermediary without really spelling it out. Is there a step I'm missing, or is he hand-waving? And does this connect to the later debate about whether historical explanations need covering laws at all, or whether narrative explanation is a seperate kind of thing? I'm not looking for a reading list, just help understanding the logic of the position.
