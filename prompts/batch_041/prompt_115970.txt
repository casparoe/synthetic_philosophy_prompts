I'm putting together a session for a 16-student upper-level seminar in philosophy of mind — 75 minutes, a room where I can clear one long table or a patch of floor — and I want to run the group as an ant colony rather than lecture at them about one. What I'm after is the uncomfortable middle of a question my students keep collapsing to one side or the other: a colony forages efficiently, sorts its dead into piles, relocates when conditions change, and yet no single ant represents the colony's goal or has any inkling of the pattern it is producing; meanwhile the quickest way to predict what such a colony will do is to treat it as one agent with wants, even though nothing inside it plays the role of a controller. I want them to be the ants for twenty minutes, watch a pattern appear that nobody in the room intended, and then find they cannot say cleanly whether the colony minded or only seemed to. So: design me the activity. I have sketched one rule and you should feel free to keep, fix, or replace it — the sort of local rule that simple models of ant corpse-gathering use, where an empty-handed participant who bumps into an object picks it up, and a laden participant who bumps into another object puts down what they are carrying. Tell me the materials (tokens, rule cards, a cleared surface, anything else), how many rounds and how long each runs, whether anyone gets a role other than ant, and how you would stage the moment where the group confronts the interpretive question without my having said "emergence," "intentional stance," or "colony mind" at any point before the debrief.

Two things I specifically want built in. First, a prediction phase before anyone moves: each participant writes down what they think the final configuration will be, and I want the room to discover that simulating every ant step by step is hopeless while "the colony wants to gather the things together" gets them there instantly — I care about that asymmetry landing as a felt experience, not a slide. Second, a version of the contrast where the same behaviour is then repeated by people who now know the goal, so they can see how differently it sits with them when it is deliberate.
