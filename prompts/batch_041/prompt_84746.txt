I'm not a philosopher -- I did a business degree and now I work in operations -- but I've been reading a lot about AI alignment in my spare time, and I keep coming back to the question of whose values a system is actually supposed to be aligned to. A newsletter I follow (tech commentary, not accademic) ran a piece on this last month and I've been chewing on it ever since, because it felt like it cut through a lot of hand-waving but I also have a nagging sense that it was cheating somewhere. The gist was: "Everyone in AI safety talks about aligning models to human values as if there's a single neutral thing called human values. There isn't. Aligning a system is just aggregating the preferences of everyone it affects, and social choice theory killed that idea off decades ago -- Arrow's theorem shows there's no fair way to add up people's preferences, so whichever method you pick is smuggling one group's values in and calling the result neutral. Utilitarians at least bite the bullet: they say just maximize total welfare, which means the bigger group always wins and how strongly anyone feels about it doesn't matter. Liberal political philosophy used to offer an escape hatch -- the state stays neutral between competing visions of the good life and lets people pursue their own -- but even liberals have mostly abandoned that, since critics showed neutrality is impossible: a state that refuses to rank ways of living is already ranking them, and neutrality is itself a value that has to be imposed on people who don't share it. So the honest version is that alignment is either covert utilitarianism or whoever has the compute decides, and I'd rather we just admit it's the second one."

Is that a fair summary? I'm not looking for a reading list (I know I should read Rawls, I'm not going to) and I don't need cites -- I just want to know which parts a philosopher who works on this stuff would agree with, which parts are true as far as they go but are being made to carry more weight than they can, and which parts are just wrong. The bits about Arrow and about utilitarianism are the ones I'm most suspicious of, because they sound like the kind of thing people say after half-reading a Wikipedia page. And separately: does his conclusion actually follow from the parts that survive? Because I'll admit I half agree with him politically, but I don't want to agree with him for a bad reason.
