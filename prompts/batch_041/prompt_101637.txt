Among contemporary moral philosophers there has been recent discussion of a practice sometimes called moral trade. The idea is this. Two people hold different moral priorities: one holds that relieving the suffering of factory-farmed animals is the most urgent task in the world, the other that preserving wilderness and biodiversity is. Neither will be argued out of her view. But each can do something the other regards as valuable while regarding it herself as only mildly costly — or even, by her own ranking, as mildly bad. The first might take on a job that involves some habitat destruction, on the condition that the second devote a substantial share of her income to animal welfare, and vice versa. Judged by her own lights, each may come out ahead: the arrangement is an improvement over no trade for both parties, even though each performs an act she would not otherwise have performed, and even though the underlying moral disagreement remains entirely unresolved. Defenders describe this as a way of lowering the costs of moral disagreement; critics describe it as putting a price on what should not be for sale, and as corroding the integrity of those who engage in it.

Suppose that Henry Sidgwick — the Victorian utilitarian whose Methods of Ethics appeared in 1874 and who died in 1900 — had lived long enough to encounter this discussion and to understand it. Write an essay predicting what his considered view would have been. Do not merely summarize what he wrote; extend his position to a problem he never faced. To do this well you will need to work from the specific machinery of his ethics: his three methods and the unresolved dualism of practical reason between egoism and universal benevolence; his practice of treating common-sense morality as data to be systematized and corrected rather than as authoritative; his distinction between the criterion of right action and the motives from which agents should act; and his willingness to consider whether a utilitarian morality might have conclusions that should not be proclaimed to everyone. Address at least these questions. Would Sidgwick regard a moral trade as permissible? Would he think a utilitarian should encourage or discourage such trades? Does the practice threaten the integrity of the traders, and if so, does that give Sidgwick a reason to oppose it? And how, if at all, does the phenomenon bear on the dualism of practical reason — does it offer a partial reconciliation, leave the dualism untouched, or make it worse? Where his texts genuinely underdetermine the answer, say so plainly and explain which way his wider commitments point. Assume your reader knows Sidgwick's corpus well; there is no need to cite editions or page numbers.
