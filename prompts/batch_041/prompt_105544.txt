I cover psychology and the law, and there's a cluster of courtroom practices around emotion that I can't get out of my head. Here's the setup. If someone kills in a sudden rage after what the old common-law cases called adequate provocation — catching a spouse with someone, a serious blow, mutual combat; insults on their own usually didn't count — most US states will knock the charge down from murder to manslaughter. And the doctrine comes with a literal stopwatch attached: if enough time passed for the passion to cool, no mitigation. So the law is treating an emotion like weather, or like a wave — something that happens to you, crests, and recedes on its own schedule, and you're less answerable for what you do while you're inside it. Ok, fine. But the same system, at sentencing, orders the guy into anger management, which only makes sense if anger is a trainable skill he's responsible for cultivating. And at trial the prosecutions use of his flat calm is evidence he planned it, while his fury is evidence he meant it — which makes emotion sound like a reading of a situation, an appraisal of an insult, rather than a feeling in the chest.

So which is it — are emotions judgments, perceptions, or feelings? I'd like someone to reconstruct the theory the doctrine actually needs, not the one lawyers think they have. Does provocation-as-mitigation definitely require a non-cognitive, hydraulic picture where the emotion is just a force that overrides reason and carries you off? Could an appraisal view — where the rage is itself a judgment that you've been wronged — do the same work, or does it destroy the excuse, since we hold people answerable for their judgments but not for surges of adrenaline? Is there a version of the perceptual view, emotions as perceptions of value, of the insult as an insult, that keeps the mitigation, and what does it cost — we don't excuse someone just because their perception was distorted, do we? Tell me where the practice is actually underdetermined: which rival theories would license exactly the same rules, and which one it quietly rules out. And then the fun part: if we swapped in the view you think is right, what would have to change on the ground — the stopwatch, the adequate-provocation list, the anger-management orders, the whole weird apparatus?
