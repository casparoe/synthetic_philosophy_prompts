I am preparing a graduate seminar on early modern philosophical theology, and I want to set my students a question that forces them past the standard caricature of Leibniz's optimism. I would like you to act as a historian of early modern philosophy with a strong command of Leibniz's metaphysics and of the live disagreements in the secondary literature, and to think carefully before settling into an answer. The thesis I want you to work from is the claim that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds, understood in Leibniz's own terms: there are infinitely many possible worlds in the divine understanding, a world being a maximal set of compossible things; God's choice among them is determined by the degree of perfection each contains; and perfection is a matter of maximal variety combined with maximal order, or, as Leibniz also puts it, the richest phenomena produced by the simplest laws. I am particularly interested in the doctrine's less obvious consequences, and I want you to identify and rank the most important of them.

Among the implications I would like you to consider, though you should not feel bound by my list: first, what follows from the fact that not every possible good is compossible with every other — that is, that some genuine goods cannot coexist in a single world — for how we should understand the necessity of trade-offs and the place of evil in the best world; second, what follows from Leibniz's insistence that the universe is in a state of perpetual progress towards greater perfection, given that this world is supposed to be the best possible — is the best a maximum attained at each moment, or a limit approached only by the whole infinite series, and does the difference matter for how we should assess the present state of things; third, what the doctrine implies about merely possible beings and whatever claim to existence they might be thought to possess, given Leibniz's suggestion that possible things have a right to exist in proportion to their perfection; and fourth, what it implies for practical reason — in particular, whether it drains the point from efforts to improve the world, or whether Leibniz can consistently hold both that this is the best possible world and that we ought to work towards a better one.

Please distinguish, throughout, between implications Leibniz himself drew or would clearly have accepted and implications that follow from his premises but that he may not have noticed or may have resisted, and be explicit about which of his assumptions each implication depends on. Where an implication rests on a contested interpretation — for instance, on how the degree of perfection is to be measured, or on whether God's choice can be free if it is determined by the best — say so, and explain what turns on it. I do not need citations or a reading list; I know the literature and will supply references myself, so concentrate on the arguments and on the conceptual structure of the position. Aim for a rigorous, self-contained treatment that a strong graduate student could follow without having read the primary texts.
