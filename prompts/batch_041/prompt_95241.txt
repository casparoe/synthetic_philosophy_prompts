I am writing a paper whose thesis is that the dust-specks debate has mislocated its own crux. The disagreement is standardly described as being about whether harms may be summed across persons, but I suspect the verdicts people give are driven by something else: whether a grave harm to one person may be traded against a mass of negligible harms to others. I need a case built to order that prises these two things apart by reversing the usual rhetorical roles. The aggregating theory should end up selecting the single grave harm; the anti-aggregating theories should end up selecting the mass of negligible harms. The case is for separating aggregation from the direction of the verdict, so a case that simply stipulates the verdicts, rather than generating them from the principles, will not do.

Build the scenario to the following specifications.

- Exactly two options, one of which must be chosen. Both outcomes are certain: no risk, no chance devices, no unforeseen side effects, no harms to anyone outside the two groups, and no person who appears in both options.
- Option A imposes one grave harm on a single person. It must be grave in absolute terms, the kind of thing the original debate would call torture and not a mild inconvenience dressed up. Nothing anyone suffers under Option B may approach it in intensity or duration, so that the worst individual outcome in the whole case is the one person in A.
- Option B imposes one negligible harm on each of a very large but finite number of people. Each of these harms must be genuinely experienced and unmistakable, so that no one can be told they did not really suffer it, yet so small that no one would treat it as a reason to alter their day. Each person receives exactly one, so that nothing compounds within a life.
- You may stipulate the magnitudes, but the verdicts must then follow from the principles rather than being built into the stipulation. State what magnitudes and what population size are required to produce the reversal.

Then, in order:

1. Totalism: show that summing harms across persons selects Option A on your numbers.
2. Two anti-aggregating theories: a threshold view, on which harms below a certain magnitude count for nothing, and a lexical or priority view, on which the fate of the worst-off person takes absolute precedence. Show that each selects Option B, and check whether they select it for the same reason or for different reasons.
3. Prioritarianism: locate it between the two, distinguishing the version that applies a concave weighting within the sum from the version that gives the worst off lexical priority, and say whether the two versions land on different options here.
4. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens if it is removed. In particular, say what the verdicts become if the population is reduced so that Option B's total falls below Option A's, and whether any disagreement about aggregation survives that change.
5. Say how a defender of the view that comes out worst would attack the case. Can they claim the numbers were gerrymandered to force the result, and if so, what would count as a non-gerrymandered choice of magnitudes? Can they claim that the population required is so large that Option B is not really the cheap option? Answer honestly, including where the answer weakens the point I want to make.

Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph; the analysis may run longer. Draw the harms from a sensory domain other than the original example's, and do not restate the original case. Argue from the principles themselves; I do not need references.
