For some time now I have been bothered by a contrast between two things the law does, and I would like you to help me work out what each of them is implicitly committed to. In most common-law systems a witness takes an oath whose wording is much broader than a promise to avoid falsehood: the witness undertakes to tell the whole truth and nothing but the truth. And yet the crime of perjury has been construed so narrowly that a witness who gives answers that are literally true but deliberately arranged to mislead cannot be convicted of it. In the leading American case, a man was asked under oath whether he had ever had a personal Swiss bank account and answered by describing an account belonging to the company he owned; the statement was true, the questioner was plainly misled, and the conviction was still set aside, the court reasoning that the remedy for an evasive witness is a sharper follow-up question rather than a criminal charge. Now contrast what the same legal system does elsewhere. Securities regulators forbid a statement that is literally accurate but that omits a fact needed to keep it from misleading, and consumer-protection authorities treat an advertisement that is technically true yet creates a false impression as deceptive. So one corner of the law effectively insists that misleading is not lying, while another corner treats a half-truth as a lie. I am not a lawyer and I do not have the vocabulary for the underlying dispute, but the apparent inconsistency is what I want to understand.

Begin with the perjury rule. What theory of what is wrong with lying does its narrow definition presuppose? Does it presuppose that the wrong consists in the falsity of the assertion, a mismatch between words and world; or in the deception of the hearer; or in the breach of an implicit promise, or of a conversational expectation, to be informative and responsive? Be careful to separate what the rule actually requires you to believe from what it merely happens to fit comfortably, and say where the rule is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories would equally license it. Then turn to the half-truth rule that governs securities and advertising, and ask the same question of it: does it presuppose a genuinely different theory of the wrong of deception, or can a single theory accommodate both practices — for instance by distinguishing the act of asserting something false from the act of knowingly bringing about a false belief?

Finally, tell me whether the legal system's overall stance is coherent or whether it merely equivocates between two different things we call lying. And if we replaced the perjury rule's implicit theory with the one the regulators seem to hold, what concretely would have to change? Would the whole-truth clause of the oath become enforceable, would the literal-truth defense disappear, and what would that do to the practice of cross-examining a witness in an adversarial system that expects each side to pin the other down with its own questions?
