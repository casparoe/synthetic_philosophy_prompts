I'm a Year 12 student and next Thursday I'm giving a talk to my school's environmental society. The title is something like The Suffering We Don't See: Why Wild Animals Matter Too. My thesis is that if you take suffering seriously, you can't treat the suffering of wild animals as morally invisible just because no human caused it, and I want to defend a limited duty to intervene -- things like vaccinating against diseases, rescuing animals from fires and floods, maybe helping with starvation in a drought -- while saying no to the big stuff like wiping out predators or gene-driving whole species. The real distinction I want to push is between a suffering-centred ethic and a wildness-centred one: I think the wildness-centred view has a hard time explaining why wildness is worth more than the suffering it contains. The thing I'm most worried about is what I've been calling the 'ledger problem': we have no real baseline for how much suffering is normal in a wild population, and we can't compare a rabbit that dies fast to a rabbit that dies slow, so any intervention might be moral gambling. I definately want to argue that uncertainty cuts both ways, but I know that's shaky. The audience will be: our biology teacher, who's an ecologist and thinks conservation is about protecting processes and ecosystems, not individual animals; three or four vegan mates who think wild animal suffering is a distraction from factory farming; a religious studies teacher who's into Buddhism and thinks suffering has a meaning we shouldn't try to engineer away; and a couple of other sixth-formers who've read some utilitarian stuff and will probably ask why I'm not going all the way to abolishing predation. There might also be a few people from the school's conservation volunteering group who spend their weekends clearing invasive species and will hate the idea of intervening in nature.

Tell me what I should concede up front to buy credibility, what I should refuse to give up, the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss, and the one question I'm really hoping nobody asks. Please write it in British English, and don't give me a reading list or citations -- I can't use them in a school talk anyway. I don't want a general essay on wild animal suffering; I want the prep sheet.
