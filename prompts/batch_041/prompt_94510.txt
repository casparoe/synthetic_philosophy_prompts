Been working through Marx's Grundrisse on and off — the 1850s notebooks, the long stretch on fixed capital and machinery — and there's one paragraph I keep bouncing off of. Here it is:

"The exchange of living labour for objectified labour – i.e. the positing of social labour in the form of the contradiction of capital and wage labour – is the ultimate development of the value-relation and of production resting on value. Its presupposition is – and remains – the mass of direct labour time, the quantity of labour employed, as the determinant factor in the production of wealth. But to the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time, whose 'powerful effectiveness' is itself in turn out of all proportion to the direct labour time spent on their production, but depends rather on the general state of science and on the progress of technology, or the application of this science to production."

What I can't get straight is the logic of the second sentence against the third. He says the presupposition of the whole value-relation "is – and remains" the mass of direct labour time as the determinant of wealth, and then in the very next breath says that as large industry develops real wealth depends less and less on labour time and more on the agencies set in motion during it — the general state of science. Is that a contradiction he's attributing to capital (capital has to keep measuring wealth in labour time even as it erodes that measure), a claim about what's actually happening to value, or a prediction about what eventually has to give? Walk me through how the inference is supposed to run, and tell me what work science is doing in it — is the general state of science a productive force in the same sense labour is, or something capital can only annex from the outside?

And then the thing I actually care about: this passage gets quoted alot these days in arguments that automation makes labour-time accounting obsolete — value tracks socially necessary labour time, machines drive that toward nothing, so the measure falls apart. Does the paragraph really support that, or is he making a narrower point about capital's own bookkeeping and its crisis tendencies? If the popular reading overshoots, say exactly where it overshoots. No need for citations or reading lists, I just want the argument itself.
