I teach a third-year seminar on knowledge and the record, and next session is the archives-and-power one -- who ends up in the record and who doesn't. It's 90 minutes, 16 students, one room, a whiteboard, no budget, and last year's version died because everyone just said 'keep everything' and the whole thing collapsed into a lecture from me, which is exactly what I'm trying to avoid. What I want them to walk out with: (i) the sense that what a later person can know was settled in advance by people who had no interest in those later questions, (ii) that the silences are patterned -- they fall along lines of who had the resources to write things down, keep them, and hand them over -- and (iii) that they themselves will make the classic move of reading 'nothing in the sources' as 'nothing happened'. So design me an activity where that lands in the doing rather than in my mouth.

Here's the shape I've been sketching, but change it if something better occurs to you. Phase one: they're the records committee of a made-up municipal body -- say a mid-century city housing department that ran a slum clearance and rehousing programme -- with a fixed amount of shelf space, a fixed processing budget, and a set of perfectly sensible retention criteria that I hand them (legal requirement, evidential value, cost, redundancy, does anyone actually want it, privacy). They decide, item type by item type, what gets accessioned, what gets destroyed, and what goes into a backlog of boxes nobody has time to describe. Nothing in those criteria tells them to favour the department over the tenants, but the department's minutes, plans and press releases are voluminous and cheap to keep, while the tenants' association's damp ledger and a few residents' complaint letters look like ephemera. Phase two: they swap roles and become historians with one specific question -- what happened to the residents of a particular street -- working only from what survived phase one, and they have to write a short account with a confidence marker attached. Then I reveal that the thing they couldn't find was never written down in the first place, and that the committee's own choices are the reason. Give me the concrete rules, roles, materials and timings, the exact question I should put to the historians, the twist that shows the loss isn't random, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the different places a silence can enter: at the moment something is or isn't written down, at the moment records get selected, at the moment somebody searches, and at the moment a story gets told. They shouldn't hear any of that vocabulary until the debrief.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- I already know one: some one will say 'this is rigged', and I need a way of handling that which doesn't defuse the point. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce. My suspicion is that the big one is this: in the activity they get to see what's missing, but a real historian never does -- the silence is invisible from inside the sources -- so that part will have to come from me. Don't bother with citations or a reading list, I'll supply those myself.
