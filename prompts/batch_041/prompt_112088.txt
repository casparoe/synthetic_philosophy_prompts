I am the founding director of a new research centre for the study of consciousness, and the opening ceremony is in three weeks. I have to give the address — eight minutes, about a thousand words — and the room is really three rooms at once.

The first is our funders and their guests: the philanthropist whose money built the place, a handful of donors with personal reasons for caring, and a number of well-read laypeople who expect to be told, before the evening is over, what the mystery of consciousness is and roughly how we intend to crack it. The second is a delegation from the phenomenological institute across town: trained in first-person methods, skeptical of our programme as behaviourism in a better suit, and already on record that our methodology begs the question it claims to be neutral about. The third is science journalists and our own graduate students, who will quote me and will then have to live with what I have said.

I want the address built on heterophenomenology — the method Daniel Dennett proposed, in which the subject's utterances are treated the way a critic treats the text of a novel: taken down in full, worked up into a coherent world of what it is like for that subject, and then treated as something to be explained rather than as a set of premises to be trusted. The hinge of the speech should be an asymmetry I want stated as the centre's own methodological rule: the subject has complete authority over how things seem and no authority whatever over how things are, so the laboratory's business is to explain the reports, neither to certify them nor to correct them.

Here is the difficulty, and I want you to work through it rather than around it. My own view — mine, not the centre's — is that experience is not what it seems, that the phenomenal properties our reports appear to be about are not really there, and that the science of consciousness should end by explaining why we are so unshakably convinced that they are. The delegation across town suspects that heterophenomenology's neutrality is a fig leaf for exactly this view, and they are not being stupid: a method that explains every report without ever needing to invoke phenomenal properties has made an argument, whether or not it meant to. So the address has to hold three things at once. It must not promise the funders a solution to the hard problem of consciousness. It must not concede that there is nothing here to study. And it must not smuggle my illusionism in as the centre's official doctrine. Nor may it manage this by being vague: the funders will forgive candour about difficulty and will not forgive evasion, and the delegation will forgive almost anything except a method quietly emptied of its neutrality.

Requirements:

- Roughly a thousand words, in my voice throughout. No citations, no names of researchers, no quotations, no borrowed formulas. If you need a distinction, coin it in plain words and then use it consistently.
- Any technical term that survives into the final text must be introduced in a clause the donors can follow without losing the thread.
- The seem/is asymmetry should carry the architecture of the speech, not sit in a single paragraph of methodology in the middle of it.
- Somewhere, say plainly why the neutrality of the method does not guarantee the neutrality of its results. I would rather I said it first than be accused of it afterwards.
- Give the centre's public rationale as a research question about why creatures like us are so powerfully disposed to find experience mysterious, rather than as a concession that it is.
- Include one concrete, ordinary example of the kind of result we are after: a single sentence, no invented numbers, in which the gap between what the subject reports and what the mechanism is doing is the whole point.
- Close with a commitment the centre can be held to, phrased so that the funders and the delegation can each hear it as addressed to them, and so that it binds us to something we could actually fail at.
