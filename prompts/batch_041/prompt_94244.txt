So a bit of background: I took early retirement from a career in atmospheric chemistry about three years ago and have been making up for lost time on philosphy ever since — I've got a small reading group that meets in the back room of a pub, twelve of us or so, a real mix (two nurses, a retired magistrate, a couple of grad students who heard the free crisps rumour, my old labmate). The discussion is genuinely good but it has a bad habit of staying abstract. Last month we did Scanlon's contractualism and everyone nodded along at the formula — a principle is one that nobody could reasonably reject — and then I realised that not one of us actually felt why that formula causes trouble. In particular I want them to feel the aggregation problem: the thing where, because the test is applied to each person one at a time, you're apparently not allowed to add up the many small complaints of a crowd and throw the total against the one big complaint of a single person. On the page it reads like a technicality. I want them in a room where it bites them personally.

Could you design me a 90-minute activity for that group of twelve that does this? My own instinct is some kind of staged disaster or triage convention where each participant is a delegate obliged to protect one particular claimant, and a rule only passes if no delegate can reasonably reject it — but I'm not wedded to that, you'll know better than me what shape actually works. I want the rules, the materials (keep it to index cards, sticky notes, maybe a whiteboard), the roles or payoffs, a rough minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes, and a debrief that maps what just happened onto the actual parts of Scanlon's view and onto the aggregation problem — including the difference between the aggregation problem proper and the separate worry about whether numbers count at all, because I gather those two get conflated in casual discussion and I'd like the group to leave seeing the seam between them. Show me where in the run of play each piece of the problem is meant to surface, so I can tell whether it landed. And please be straight with me about two things: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it in the moment, and which part of the idea the activity simply can't reproduce, so that I know what I have to supply myself in the debrief rather than pretend the game did it. No lecture up front — I want them to walk into the wall before I put a name on it.
