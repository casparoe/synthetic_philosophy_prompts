For a project on the theory of letterforms, I want the specification before I look at any candidate account. The Letter Spirit project set itself the task of building a program that, shown a handful of letters drawn in some particular style, would then design the remaining letters of the alphabet in that same style, so that every shape it produced was unmistakably a token of its letter and unmistakably in the style of the seeds. Whatever one thinks of that program, it forces the underlying question into a sharp form: what makes a shape a token of the letter a, and what is left over as style? I need the answer to serve two ends at once, so keep both in view: a list that a program could actually be held to, and an honest statement of what the philosophical question — whether letterforms have essences at all — really turns on.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of letter identity must deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. I want the list driven by cases, and I want it to reach outside the usual examples. It should say what the account must do about the single-storey and the double-storey a, about cursive, blackletter, and children's handwriting, about the a of a typeface no one has yet designed, about the degraded, rotated, or partly occluded a that a skilled reader still identifies at once, about the shape that is an a but badly drawn, and about the shape drawn in the a slot that is not an a at all; about letters whose identity is not carried by a visual shape, as in Braille, Morse, and fingerspelling; and about the fact that letterforms themselves drift across centuries and across scripts, so that the a of an old manuscript is the same letter under a shape no modern designer would draw. State also the distinctions the account may not collapse: type and token; glyph, allograph, and letter; identity and style; membership and typicality or goodness of example; the shape as generated and the shape as perceived.

Then tell me which of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and say plainly which are genuine desiderata and which are the prejudices of a particular camp — whether the demand for a decision procedure, for mind-independence, or for a criterion a machine could implement is something the subject actually owes us, or a stipulation of one side. Finally, name the single requirement that excludes the most popular answers, and say what it excludes. Three well-defended requirements will serve me better than fifteen, so keep the weight on the criteria and the cases that motivate them, and skip the survey of the literature; I can supply that myself.
