I need a eulogy for the man I worked for: the mayor of a mid-sized city, who died last month at seventy-eight. I was his aide for eleven years, and in the late 1990s I was the one who carried the envelope. Here is what the room will not say and the eulogy cannot pretend away. During the worst year of the war between the two organizations that then controlled the city's drug trade, he authorized payments and safe passage to the people doing the killing. It worked: the killing stopped, and nothing replaced it. He then lied to the public about the arrangement for four years, and he allowed the police commander who executed it to absorb the blame and resign in what the papers reported as a breakdown. That commander's widow will be in the third row. So will the mayor's two sons — one of whom has spent a decade defending his father in public and has never seen the file — and his old chief of staff, who knows every page of it and will be listening to my sentences rather than grieving. There will also be reporters, because nothing I am about to describe has ever been said in public before, and after Sunday it will be.

What I need from you is not an essay on the old problem political philosophers call dirty hands. It is a eulogy, and the problem has to do the structural work: it should decide what I can say, and, more importantly, how I say the things I cannot avoid saying. The genre is working against me and I want you to feel that pressure rather than dissolve it. A eulogy wants to return a verdict on a person; this problem is about an act. If praise of the man stands in for judgment of what he did, the speech has failed. If the judgment stands in for the mourning, it has also failed, and two hundred people go home with nothing.

The constraints are hard:

- No sentence may weigh the good he did against the harm he authorized — no balance struck, no net, no reckoning, not even a denial that a reckoning is possible. If a listener could extract a total, the speech is wrong.
- There must be one sentence that states plainly what he authorized — the payments, the passage, the lie, the commander — in the past tense, without euphemism and without a softening adjective. That sentence may not be surrounded by material that excuses it; the sentences on either side have to stand on their own without the excuse. This is the hardest sentence in the speech and it should read that way.
- The guilt is spoken of in the present tense. He did not finish paying it, and the speech should not let his death be mistaken for a settlement.
- I may not speak for his conscience: no claim about what he felt at the end, what he would have wanted, whether he was at peace, whether he forgave himself. No one in that hall has standing to know, and I have less than most.
- I may not ask the room to forgive him on his behalf. The words sacrifice and necessary are not available to me.
- My own part in it gets exactly one sentence, and it must not turn into a confession. The speech is not about me.
- The problem itself may not be explained. Most of the room has never heard of any of this, and the moment the speech begins to teach, it stops being a eulogy. Whatever the philosophy accomplishes, it has to accomplish through what is said and what is left unsaid.

One thing you should have, because it is the only thing he ever said to me about it and I have never known what to do with it: he said the wrong was not a debt he could pay off but a thing he would carry, and that a man who believed he had paid it had already become the kind of man who could do it again.

Aim for six to eight minutes — roughly nine hundred words — in a register that can be read aloud to a room. It should leave the two hundred people who came to be told he was good able to grieve, and it should leave the six or seven who know the file unable to say I lied to them.

End with a single line, after the speech, in exactly this form:

Verdict: LABEL

where LABEL is one of UNPAID, PAID, or UNPRICEABLE — whichever the speech actually enacts: UNPAID if the wrong remains outstanding and the speech asks the room to leave it outstanding; PAID if the speech releases it; UNPRICEABLE if the speech holds that it cannot be converted into anything payable or carried at all. The label must be earned by the speech rather than announced in it, and there is no hedging: choose the one the speech does.
