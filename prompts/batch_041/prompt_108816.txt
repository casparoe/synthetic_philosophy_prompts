For the spring fundraiser, my school's philosophy club ran an auction of a one-dollar bill that I had bought out of my own pocket, under the rules such auctions are usually given: the highest bidder pays what she bid and receives the dollar, and the second-highest bidder also pays what she bid but receives nothing. Two seniors bid it up to $4.25, and both looked genuinely ill about it afterward. I understand the standard explanation, which is that they were fooled by sunk costs and by the feeling of having come too far to quit. What troubles me is that at every point in the sequence the bidder who continued was better off continuing than stopping. When the runner-up is standing at $2.00, quitting costs her $2.00 for certain, whereas raising to $2.10 and winning costs her only $1.10. That is not the arithmetic of someone who believes spent money can be recovered; it is correct arithmetic about a game that punishes quitting. So I am no longer sure the escalation is a mistake at all.

Here is the claim I would like a number on. Call a bid irrational when, at the moment she made it, the bidder had available some other action that she would have preferred to that bid, given her own preferences and a correct understanding of the rules. Please give me your credence that the escalation in an auction of this kind — with bidders who understand the rules and are not being deceived — is irrational in that sense.

I do not want a verdict; I want the number taken apart. Give me a rough probability for each sub-question it rests on, with one line of justification for each, and then the combination:

- Whether a single bid is the right unit of evaluation at all, or whether the proper unit is the decision to enter the auction, the plan the bidder arrived with, or the whole episode — and whether rationality is a property of choices, of plans, or of lives.
- How much of real escalation is the sunk-cost illusion, treating money already paid as though it could still be recovered, as against the genuine strategic structure of a rule that makes the runner-up pay for nothing.
- Whether loss aversion, and the unwillingness to be the person who quit, are genuine preferences of the bidder, in which case honoring them is not an error, or distortions of the preferences she would endorse on reflection — and who has standing to decide which of a person's preferences are the real ones.
- Whether anything changes once the money is going to the school rather than to a stranger, since a bidder who loses money may still be bringing about something she endorses.
- Whatever else you think belongs in the decomposition.

Then give me the single combined credence, name the sub-question the final number is most sensitive to, and say what argument or piece of evidence would move it most. Finally, tell me whether your answer changes under a modified rule in which any bidder may at any time pay a fixed withdrawal fee, say twenty-five cents, to be released from her bid entirely with no further obligation. That seems to remove the trap without removing the sunk cost, and I would like to know which of your sub-questions it bears on.
