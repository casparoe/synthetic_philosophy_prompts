Next month I will present the central chapter of my dissertation, on the semantics and pragmatics of slurs, at our department's works-in-progress seminar, and I want to walk in prepared for the discussion rather than improvising my way through it. The audience is unusually split, and I expect the two halves to press me in quite different directions.

About half are formal semanticists and philosophers of language. They will want to know how my machinery composes, they will run the standard projection and embedding diagnostics against my examples, and they are allergic to contextual parameters that are not independently motivated. The other half are sociolinguists and experimentally minded researchers working on language and identity; they care about actual usage and about variation within and across communities, they are suspicious of armchair intuitions, and they will object to any account that treats a community as speaking with a single perspective.

The thesis I will defend is this. The derogatory dimension of a slur is a presupposition rather than a truth-conditional entailment or a conventional implicature, but it is a presupposition of a distinctive, shiftable kind: it is anchored to what I call a perspective parameter — the set of social identities and attitudes that the context assigns to the speaker. Because the anchor is a parameter of the context rather than part of the word's lexical entry, the derogation can be re-anchored by any expression that explicitly invokes a standpoint ("from the standpoint of…", "in their terms", "as it was used then") and, I claim, can be bound by quantifiers over perspectives in a way that conventional implicatures cannot. My three headline predictions are: (i) the derogation resists ordinary denial and cancellation — "I said nothing derogatory" does not neutralize it — and yields only to an explicit perspective shift; (ii) in third-person reports ("he called her…") the slur is evaluated relative to the reported context's perspective rather than the reporting context's, which is what separates my account from expressivist ones; and (iii) reclamation is genuine re-anchoring, so a reclaimed use and a slurring use share their lexical content but differ in the value of the parameter.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet. I want the objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that half of the room would phrase it, with a note on which camp it comes from. For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is soft, and a recommendation about what to concede up front and what to hold. Tell me which single reply has to land for the chapter to survive the seminar, and finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
