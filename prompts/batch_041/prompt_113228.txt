I'm partway through a philosophy of mind module and I keep tripping over the original-versus-derived intentionality business, so what I want is a proper conceptual explanation rather than the usual summary. Here's the picture I've assembled. Searle draws a firm line between the intrinsic intentionality that minds have — aboutness that's there in virtue of the mind itself, grounded for him in consciousness — and the derived intentionality of words, pictures, traffic lights and the like, which manage to be about things only because a mind has assigned them a job or reads them a certain way. Dennett then produces the two-bitser, that vending-machine coin-slot gadget that accepts a US quarter, and asks: when a Panamanian balboa — which the machine can't distinguish from a quarter — drops in, has it gone into a state meaning quarter, and so misrepresented things, or into a different state meaning balboa, and so got things right? His line, if I've got it, is that there's no fact of the matter here over and above what a designer's intentions or our own interpretive interests put there, so that derived is doing all the work — and then he wheels on Mother Nature as the designer of our own states, so that nothing ends up with original intentionality.

What I can't get straight is whether Dennett and Searle are really disagreeing, and if so about what. My hunch is that two quite seperate moves are being run together: Searle's looks like a relativity claim (the content is genuine but owed to an assigner), Dennett's like an indeterminacy claim (grant the assigner and there's still no privileged answer), and those seem to me to come apart — you could have perfectly determinate content that's nonetheless derivative, or indeterminate content in a mind. So could you explain what the two-bitser is actually meant to establish, how it bears on the derived/original distinction rather than just restating it, and whether the Mother Nature step survives the obvious objection that natural selection isn't a designer at all — that it has no intentions to confer and can't mean anything by what it builds?

I'd also like it pushed past the textbook telling, with a case the standard version skips. Suppose a coin detector of this kind was built by someone who sincerely believed that balboas were quarters and thought he was building a balboa-detector; or suppose the gadget was sold on and now sits in a different machine using it to sort washers from bolts. What does each view say the device represents in those cases, and does the Dennett/Searle clash still look like a disagreement about intentionality, or has it quietly turned into one about how to fix the reference of an artefact's states? I don't want a reading list or references — just the concepts, and definately in British English.
