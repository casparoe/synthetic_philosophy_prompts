I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on knowledge and technology, and for the past month I've been collecting my verdicts on cases of reliance on AI, in the hope that a principle would emerge from them. So far I have a tangle rather than a principle. I'd like you to tell me what principle, or small set of competing principles, best captures the verdicts below, what each implies about cases I haven't listed, and which of my verdicts I should be prepared to give up.

My verdicts, roughly in the order I recorded them:

1. A student obtains a proof of a theorem from a chatbot, then works through it line by line, checks each step, and a week later can reconstruct it and explain why the fourth step follows. Fine; he understands the theorem. Understanding is a state he is in, not a pedigree he has to have earned.

2. The same student produces the same proof, glances at it, and a week later can reproduce it only by asking again, and cannot say why the fourth step follows. He does not understand it. I don't think he has necessarily done anything wrong if he never claimed otherwise.

3. A physician orders an assay and trusts the laboratory's interpretation of it without knowing how the assay works. Fine, and not merely permitted — I don't think anyone would call this an erosion of her understanding of medicine.

4. A patient with a persistent cough asks a chatbot, is told it is nothing to worry about, and accepts that because the chatbot said so. She has no sense of what would make the answer more or less likely to be right. Not fine, and I want the reason to be something other than that the chatbot might be wrong, since so might the physician in case 3.

5. Someone who can no longer do long division in her head, or navigate her own city without GPS. Fine. Losing a skill is not losing an understanding.

6. A student whose essays are drafted by a model, which she then reads and endorses sentence by sentence, defending any of them on request — but who after a year cannot write a first paragraph unaided. Bad. What puzzles me is that each essay, considered on its own, looks fine to me.

7. A researcher who lets a model triage the literature, and after three years cannot tell a relevant paper from an irrelevant one without it. Bad.

8. A beginner who uses a model as a tutor, asking it to slow down and re-explain until she can do the next proof on her own. Fine — better than fine.

9. Someone who adopts a moral or political position because a model endorsed it, and would switch if the model switched. Bad, and the clearest case on the list.

10. A cohort in which every individual passes the test of being able to check an output if asked, but in which no one ever checks unprompted, so that an error the model repeats would propagate unchallenged. I want to say something has gone wrong, yet I cannot name a single individual who fails any test I have stated.

My tentative principle, which I already suspect is too crude: reliance on a tool is compatible with understanding when the competence it replaces remains recoverable, in the sense that the person could at some cost do the thing herself, or at least recognize and repair an error; and it erodes understanding when the reliance is opaque to her, uncheckable by her, and self-reinforcing. The physician in case 3 seems to sink this, since she cannot recover the assay competence and the process is opaque to her, yet I want to call her case fine. Whatever separates her from the patient in case 4 is therefore doing more work than my principle admits.

The seminar handout suggested a distinction between scaffolded competence, which the learner could in principle shed, and ventriloquized competence, which she could not, along with a notion of query-depth — how many follow-up questions a person could survive before she had nothing of her own left to say. I found both useful but cannot get them to line up with cases 6 and 10.

What I'd like from you: the principle or principles that come closest, stated precisely enough to apply; for each clause, the case in my list that motivates it; what the principle predicts for at least three further cases of your own construction that I have not considered, especially cases at the boundaries between 3 and 4 and between 6 and 8; and an honest accounting of which of my ten verdicts it cannot accommodate and what it would cost — in added complexity, or in abandoning a verdict — to save them. The case I keep going back and forth on is a colleague who drafts everything with a model, edits it heavily, understands the final product better than I understand mine, and strikes me as a sharper thinker than she was before; I want to call her case fine, but I cannot say what separates her from case 6 except degree.
