I teach a third-year seminar called Consciousness and Cognition, 14 students, roughly half philosophy and half psych, and next week I have the full 80 minutes for the split between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness. Every year I do the same thing: access/phenomenal up on the board, a bit of the global workspace material, students nod, and then in the essays I get 'access = what you can report, phenomenal = what it's like' and not one word more. I want them to feel the pinch this time instead of hearing about it. What I've been kicking around is making the seminar itself the workspace: a whiteboard in the middle of the room that is the only channel that counts, each student holding a private card with a stimulus on it, and a hard rule I've been calling broadcast-or-nothing, meaning if it never made it onto the board the group isn't allowed to treat it as existing — not in the discussion, not in the final answer. Some of them would be reporters who can write to the board, and the rest would be silent modules whose cards never get broadcast but who are sitting there looking at them the whole time. I think there's something in that but I can't see how to run it so the interesting thing happens by itself rather than because I stand up and explain it afterwards. If you think the framing is wrong, say so and change it — I'm not precious about it.

Act as someone who knows this literature well (access vs phenomenal consciousness, global workspace theory, the overflow debate, no-report paradigms) and has also actually run strange seminar games and watched them flop, so I care about the pedagogy working, not just the philosophy being right. Before you design anything, take a few minutes to think through what the students need to be doing at each stage for the reveal to land, and where this could fall apart. Then give me the thing itself: rules, materials (cheap, photocopiable, no tech), how you'd assign roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes, any scoring or payoff scheme that mechanically enforces the broadcast rule rather than relying on me policing it, and the debrief. I want at least one moment where the room gets the feeling that there is more in front of them than the group can ever say — and then I want you to undercut it, because the whole reason this topic is hard is that the feeling can also be read as an artifact of memory and cueing, not as evidence of unreportable experience. So build in whatever the group needs (a wiped board, a filler task, asking people afterwards what was on their own card) to produce both the pull and the deflation.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the actual positions: the claim that being broadcast is just what it is to be conscious, the reading where reportability and phenomenality come apart, the deflationary reading where the apparent surplus is fragile trace rather than experience, and why the no-report idea was supposed to break the tie and why it doesn't fully. It should also land on the bind I keep failing to get across, that the only evidence anyone can give for content that was never broadcast is itself a report. Finish with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be straight with me about which feature of the idea the activity simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by talking — I'd rather know that up front than discover it in the room.
