I am assembling a teaching anthology on epistemic injustice, and I need help deciding which candidate cases belong in the central chapter and which belong at the periphery. Please rate each case below on a 0–100 scale for how squarely it falls under the concept of epistemic injustice as that concept is standardly understood in analytic epistemology (100 = a paradigm instance; 0 = not an instance of the concept at all). Then arrange the cases into three or four ordered tiers, from most to least central. For each case, supply one or two sentences identifying the specific feature that does or does not make it an instance of the concept, and flag any case whose placement you take to be genuinely contestable rather than a matter of clear judgment. Where it bears on your rating, say explicitly whether the case requires a culpable agent or can be counted as a purely structural wrong.

The cases:

1. A pain specialist repeatedly discounts a Black woman's reports of chronic pain, treating her as a drug-seeker, and she goes years without adequate treatment.

2. Before a term for the phenomenon existed, women in a workplace had no shared vocabulary for a set of experiences that were nonetheless systematically damaging to them.

3. A jury gives more weight to a confident, fluent witness than to a hesitant one who is equally reliable.

4. A patient cannot afford the subscription to the journal in which the trial results relevant to her condition are published.

5. A man persistently undermines his partner's confidence in her own memory of events, until she comes to doubt her grasp of what actually happened.

6. A speech-recognition system performs markedly worse on the accents of a minority group, so that the requests of those users are frequently misconstrued by the institutions that deploy the system.

7. A student is marked down on an essay for using a grammatical construction that is standard in her home dialect.

8. A hiring committee passes over a candidate after a referee describes her, in what is meant as praise, as "surprisingly articulate."

9. A researcher's findings are ignored by her field until a more senior colleague publishes substantially the same results and receives the credit.

10. A speaker's testimony is accepted almost without scrutiny, and with more weight than the evidence warrants, because she belongs to a dominant group.
