In a seminar discussion last week about a drunk driver who ran a red light and, only because a child happened to be on the sidewalk, killed someone, while a second equally drunk driver ran the same light and hit no one, I suggested that our reactions to the two should differ. A colleague closed the exchange by saying 'it's the thought that counts' and moved on. Before I decide whether that saying does the work she asked of it, I want a full account of what it could actually be claiming.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu rather than a single favored reading, the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing. I want the spectrum from the trivial to the radical, and I want at least the following axes kept separate rather than run together:

- what is being assessed — the act, the intention behind it, the agent as a whole, or the agent's quality of will;
- whether the claim concerns what grounds desert, what makes blame and resentment fitting, what makes regret fitting, or what makes legal liability appropriate;
- whether 'the thought' denotes the occurrent intention at the moment of acting, the agent's settled character, or the effort of deliberation she expended;
- whether the sentence is a descriptive report of how we in fact apportion praise and blame or a normative claim about how we ought to;
- and whether it is a claim about causal contribution (the thought being the only part of the outcome the agent supplies), an epistemic claim (the thought being the only part we can reliably access in assessing her), or a claim about the value of a gift or gesture that has nothing to do with morality at all.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or simply false, and flag which readings are in genuine tension with one another.

Two further matters I want addressed, because they are where I suspect the slogan comes apart. First, suppose the reading that only 'the thought' counts is correct: does that eliminate luck from moral assessment, or merely relocate it, given that a person's intentions, her character, and even her having been in a position to form any particular intention at all are themselves things she did not choose? State which readings survive that objection and which collapse into it. Second, several of the readings make divergent predictions about a pair of cases: two agents who share an intention but whose actions turn out differently, and an agent whose intention is itself the product of an upbringing she had no hand in. Use those cases to sort the readings.

Close by telling me which reading my colleague was most likely relying on, and whether on that reading the slogan really licenses treating the two drivers alike — or whether it only appears to, because the reading she needs is quietly doing work that another reading would refuse to do. Keep the deliverable to the menu and the verdicts; I do not need citations and will locate the literature myself.
