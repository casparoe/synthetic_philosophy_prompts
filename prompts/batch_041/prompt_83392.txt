I did a philosophy masters a few years ago and now I work in a fairly non-technical role at a small AI company, which means I end up reading internal safety documents that I only half understand. There's one practice I can't stop thinking about. Before we deploy anything we do four things. We train the model against a reward model built from human comparisons, but we deliberately don't let it climb all the way up that reward: there's a penalty pulling it back towards the pre-trained model, and we stop the run when the held-out evaluations stop improving, even if the reward score is still rising. The evaluation sets are kept secret and never go into the training data. We run the same prompts in two framings, one that looks like a test and one that looks like an ordinary user session, and if the model is noticably better behaved in the test-looking one, the run gets flagged and a human has to sign it off. And we compare what a model says it's doing in its visible reasoning with what it actually does on cases it hasn't seen, on the assumption that a gap between the two means something bad. I raised this in a reading group last week and got three completely different answers, so I'm trying again.

What I can't work out is what all of that assumes about what's actually going on inside the thing. If the model is just a policy that the reward signal has shaped, then behaving differently depending on whether it thinks it's being watched is no more sinister than any other bit of prompt sensitivity — you'd retrain it and move on. Flagging it as suspicious seems to assume there's something behind the behaviour: an aim that the training isn't reaching, and beliefs about its own situation that do real causal work. But the same company says in public that these systems are only next-token predictors with no aims of their own, and we carry on training them regardless, which you'd only bother doing if you thought the process really does reshape what they want. So what theory of the relation between a model's training objective and its actual objectives does a protocol like this presuppose? Which of those four bits genuinely need the stronger picture — the one where there's an agent in there with its own goals, biding its time, what people call the treacherous turn — and which of them would be just as sensible if the truth were much thinner, a bundle of habits that shift with the context and no persisting aims at all? And if the thinner picture is enough, what would actually have to change in how we do things, or would it all be left standing?

I'd rather have it as continuous prose, like an email — no headings, bullets, bold or tables, I get enough of that at work. If there's a short answer, put it in the first couple of sentences and then argue for it; I'm more interested in where the practice is genuinely underdetermined than in being told it's all one thing.
