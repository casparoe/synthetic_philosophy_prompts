Ok so I work on the risk desk at a mid-sized insurer — my job is to take the output of our cat and liability models and turn it into the single probability that goes into a pricing or reserving decision, and it has to be defensible to the chief actuary. The recuring problem is this. The model hands me a number that's supposed to be a chance, an objective probability of loss for a class of policies. But I always have other stuff in my head: a broker's remark, something in the news, backtesting that shows the model runs hot in one region, the fact that the one contract in front of me got flagged because it looked weird. Sometimes I nudge the number and sometimes I don't, and honestly right now it's pure vibes. I know there's a whole literature on deference to objective chance — the Principal Principle, admissible evidence, the difference between a chance and somebody's credence — but I've never seen it turned into something a working analyst can actually do.

What I want is a decision procedure I can run in a couple of hours before the committee meets. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a satisfying answer to each, and for every branch say explicitly whether it ends in "use the model's number as-is," "you may deviate, and here's how to deviate," or "stop, this isn't a statistical question, a person has to own it." It has to handle the annoying cases: my extra info is about the model itself rather than about the world (so I think the model is miscalibrated); the "chance" I've been handed is really a vendor's expert judgement laundered into a percentage; the model's number is for a reference class and the thing in front of me got singled out precisely because it's an outlier; and the plain case where I have genuine evidence the model didn't have. Then run it end to end on three situations I actually keep hitting — a windstorm-exposed commercial property, a liability line where backtesting says we're pricing too high, and a one-off bespoke policy where the "probability" came from a broker's gut. At least one of the three should land somewhere I won't like, and I want you to say so out loud rather than smoothing it over. Also tell me where the procedure will misfire, and where two reasonable analysts would run the same steps and still disagree.

One more thing: be upfront about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who know the area — I don't want a contested reading of deference handed to me as if it were the consensus, because I'll be repeating it to actuaries. No reading list or citations, I won't follow up on them; just the procedure.
