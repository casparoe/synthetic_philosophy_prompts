And now I don't entirely trust my own sense of what I'm feeling, which is honestly kind of destabilizing.

So I want to practice arguing the other side. Play a defender of introspection for me. Strongest version, not a strawman, and not someone who just waves the confabulation stuff away. Specifically: someone who thinks introspective report is a skill, like reading or wine tasting -- genuinely reliable when you're trained, unreliable when you're not, and the lab results are mostly about untrained people being asked leading questions in weird rooms. Hold that line. You can concede small things but never the main one. One or two paragraphs a turn so it feels like a conversation, stay in character until I say stop, don't ask me clarifying questions, and don't cite studies at me -- I can't check them and neither can you, really. Argue from cases, analogies, and stuff I can check in my own head.

Here's my opening: the skill analogy needs a feedback signal. When I learn to read, the world corrects me -- the word was dog, not god; the recipe works or it doesn't. Whats the feedback signal for introspection? The only one I can find is whether my self-report hangs together with the rest of my self-report, which is the exact thing under suspision. And if I can't tell when I'm confabulating, then "trained introspection" is just confabulation with better manners -- more confident, which is worse, not better. So how does the skill story even get off the ground?
