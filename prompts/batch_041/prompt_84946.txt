I am a third-year PhD student in a mechanistic interpretability group, and for the past few months my main job has been building an anomaly detector — not one that watches what a model says, but one that watches how it computes, raising a flag when the route taken on some input looks qualitatively unlike any route the model takes elsewhere in the distribution. The point is to catch the episode whose output is unremarkable and whose internals are not. The flags arrive as episodes, and the project turns on a question we cannot answer cleanly: when we say that two flagged episodes are the same mechanism, what exactly have we claimed? A postdoc who joined us from an evolutionary developmental biology lab eventually lost patience with us and put the difficulty in her own vocabulary. The camera eye of a vertebrate and the compound eye of an insect are the standard example of convergence, arrived at separately rather than inherited from a common ancestor with eyes, and yet the mouse version of the gene that sits at the top of the vertebrate eye cascade can set off an eye in a fruit fly, because a far older regulatory switch was inherited by both; the term for this is deep homology, and it means the two structures count as the same at one level of description and as different at another, depending on whether you are tracing ancestry or tracing function. She then asked which of the two we were doing, and we had no answer. Something similar happens in the philosophy of science, where a part is held to belong to a mechanism roughly when you can intervene on the part and the mechanism's behaviour changes, and intervene on the behaviour and the part changes — a criterion that says nothing at all about whether two token mechanisms, in two systems or two training runs, are tokens of a single type.

I would like you to write a short story, of about two thousand words, in which this disagreement is dramatised rather than explained. Put it in a specific place and a specific week: a small group, four or five people at most, in the last days of a mechanism-audit sprint, sharing a room that is too warm and a whiteboard that is too small, with a funder visit or a paper deadline closing in, and two flagged episodes on the board whose status is precisely what is in dispute — the obvious story about the first being a familiar copying circuit doing its ordinary job on an unfamiliar input, the obvious story about the second being two unrelated circuits happening to agree on an output, and nobody's output being anything anyone would notice. Give the disagreement bodies: one character reasons in the style of the evo-devo postdoc, one is a functionalist who cares only whether the same intervention produces the same change, one is impatient to ship, and the narrator is somewhere in the middle. Include at least one concrete attempt to settle the matter by experiment — patching an activation from one run into another, ablating a component, comparing the same behaviour across checkpoints or random seeds — and let it fail to settle the matter, not because the experiment was botched but because the two sides disagree about what the experiment was a test of; and let the characters notice that the answer shifts depending on the level at which they draw the boundary of the mechanism, and that whichever criterion they adopt will quietly determine what counts as a new anomaly next quarter and therefore what they will spend the next three months doing. If it suits the story, the detector itself can turn out to be a mechanism of the same disputed kind, though I would rather that landed as an observation than as a punchline. I do not want an ending that declares the question merely relative or a matter of taste; I want an ending in which someone commits to a working criterion and the reader can feel what that commitment buys and what it costs, with the tension still live.

Two constraints. There should be no citations, no reading lists and no real papers, labs or researchers named — invented tools and labs are welcome so long as they are plausible rather than magical, and no technique should amount to reading the model's mind. And the philosophical content should be carried by the events, the dialogue and the objects on the desk rather than by a character delivering a lecture: a reader who works on mechanisms should recognise the problem in the story, and a reader who does not should still find it a good story.
