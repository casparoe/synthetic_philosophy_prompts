I am a novelist — my current book is about the dead and the uses we make of them — and I also teach a graduate seminar on historical fiction. I am setting the final examination, and I want one essay question, and nothing else, on what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them. The question must not be answerable by reciting the familiar opposition between fidelity to the record and the novelist's licence to invent; every student in the room can produce that opposition in a paragraph, and it decides nothing. What I want instead is a question that forces a student to decide whether our obligations to the dead have any content of their own, or whether the phrase quietly dissolves into two obligations we can state more plainly: what we owe the living — descendants, communities, the custodians of an archive — and what we owe the reader, who is owed a story that holds together.

To keep them from reaching for the standard test cases (the invented romance, the composite character assembled from two real people, the villain given a sympathetic childhood), build the question around a case of my own devising, and state that case inside the question. A novelist writes a first-person novel from the point of view of a woman who left no writings at all: in the archive she survives only as a name in a ledger, a line in a coroner's report, a signature on a marriage bond. The novelist supplies her with an interior life — a lover, a private heresy, a grief she never spoke aloud. In the same season, and with the same confidence, the novelist writes a first-person novel about a man who left forty years of letters, and in one scene of her invention has him commit an act that the letters explicitly and repeatedly disown. Neither book injures any living person in a way a court would recognise; both become, for a generation of readers, the images by which these two people are known.

The question should then ask the student to decide two things. First: does the novelist owe these two dead people different things, and is any difference between the cases a difference in what is owed to the dead themselves, or only a difference in the warrant the novelist has for the portrayal — the one case being underdetermined by the record and the other contradicted by it? Second, and more sharply: if the dead cannot be harmed, cannot consent, and cannot be asked, is the whole language of owing them anything a confusion, a sentimental way of talking about our duties to the living and to the record? The student must reach a verdict and defend it; a non-committal survey of the difficulties should not be able to earn full marks.

A strong answer will have to say what would have to be true for the side that takes our debts to the dead seriously to survive. It will have to say whether counterfactual consent — what she would have wanted, had anyone thought to ask her — can carry that weight, and whether an obligation to keep faith with a person's own understanding of herself can outlast the person who held it. It should also engage the possibility that the real injury is neither to the dead nor to the living but to the future: that a vivid invention forecloses the record, and the harm is done to readers not yet born, who will take the novel for the life. Students may refer to ideas by description; the question must not require any citation, reading list, or bibliography, and must not turn on knowledge of any particular scholar's position.

Please write the examination question, a model answer of roughly 650 words that takes a definite position, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points worth twenty marks in total, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how candidates typically miss that point. The marking scheme should separate an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain.
