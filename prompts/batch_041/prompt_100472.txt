Something's been bugging me about how rules work where I live. When they pass a new building code — sprinklers, insulation, whatever — it only applies to new buildings. The old ones get "grandfathered in," even when they're exactly the kind of death trap the rule was written about. Same story with rent control, same with pollution limits for old factories. But cars are the opposite: my state makes basically every car pass emissions no matter how old it is, and when they decided lead paint was poisoning kids, they eventually made landlords deal with the paint that was already on the walls. So sometimes we freeze the past in place and sometimes we go back and clean it up, and nobody seems to think thats weird.

What is grandfathering actually assuming? That when a thing got built makes a real moral difference — you can't be held to a rule that didn't exist when you acted — or just that ripping out old buildings costs more than it's worth? And does the practice assume we're stuck with what we've already got, that the only way forward is to build new stuff and wait for the old stuff to die off? Or does it assume the opposite, that everything turns over eventually so the timing doesn't matter much — and if we did start over from scratch today, would we really end up somewhere different, or basically the same place?

The part that really gets me: a freind of mine does HVAC work and he says people keep their ancient furnace limping along, because putting in a new one means meeting the new code. So a rule meant to phase out the old junk ends up keeping it alive. Does that mean the whole "we're locked in" thing is something we're doing to ourselves? And is "it's already there, we can't undo it" ever a real argument, or is it always just "we don't want to pay" with extra steps?
