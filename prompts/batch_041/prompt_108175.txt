I chair the theory review panel at a research foundation that funds foundational computing. Our hypercomputation track receives about a dozen proposals a year, each asking for two to five years of support, and every one of them gets a written verdict from me that will be read by the proposers, by my board, and occasionally by a logician who will enjoy catching us out. The panel is five people: four computer scientists and me, with a background in the philosophy of computation. We have ninety minutes per proposal, we cannot redo anyone's mathematics, and we are in no position to settle open questions in physics or in computability theory. What I need from you is not an essay on the Church–Turing thesis but a decision procedure I can actually run in that ninety minutes.

Please write the procedure as a sequence of questions asked in order. For each question, say what would count as a sufficient answer, which answers stop the review with a no, and which answers mean the question is not ours to settle and must be handed off — naming the recipient in each case (a physicist, a mathematician, the board as a question of what we want to fund rather than what is true). It has to be runnable by panel members who are not logicians, and it has to be candid about which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of a procedure.

The procedure must survive the fact that the thesis itself is contested. It is not a theorem, and a claim against it can be read as a claim about what is effectively calculable in principle, as a claim about what physical systems can do, or as a proposal about how the word computable ought to be used. I do not want the procedure to adjudicate that dispute. I want it to force each proposal to declare which of these it is attacking, and to say what the panel should do when a proposal refuses to declare, or declares one thing and argues another.

Two things I want the procedure to do that our current informal process does not. First, it should keep three failure points distinct and name them: whether the device's behaviour is merely described by a non-computable function rather than being the computation of one; whether the non-computability survives the fact that a real device realizes its idealization only up to tolerance, so that the hardness may sit entirely in the preparation of the input; and whether, even granting that a non-computable value appears somewhere in the device's behaviour, that value is available as the output of a procedure we could run on inputs we could prepare and read, and could run more than once. Second, it should separate a genuine counterexample to the thesis from a claim about speed, cost, or the widening of what we call computation, and say what the panel does with the latter, given that we have no track for it and will have to send it somewhere.

Then run the procedure on three proposals. (1) A mechanical device in which a ball reaches a singularity in finite time; the proposers encode the index of a Turing machine in the initial angle and claim that the direction in which the ball escapes encodes whether that machine halts. (2) An analog network with real-valued parameters, said to compute a function outside the Turing-computable class in the limit of exact parameter values. (3) A device exploiting a hypothetical spacetime in which infinitely many operations can be completed in a finite time along one worldline while a signal arrives later on another, claimed to decide the halting problem by inspection.

At least one of your three verdicts should be one I will not enjoy delivering, and you should say which it is. Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do and where you expect it to misfire on the proposals we will actually receive.
