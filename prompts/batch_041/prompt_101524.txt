I am a high school student writing an independent-study essay on how we ought to think about the future, and I have encountered a pattern of argument for which I cannot find a settled name.

The pattern appears whenever someone claims that our own era is the most consequential in human history. The claim is usually supported in the following way: we now command powers — to annihilate ourselves, to redesign our biology, to build machines that think — that no earlier generation commanded, so the decisions made in our lifetimes will fix the fate of everyone who comes after us, and therefore our moment is the pivot on which the whole of history turns. I find the conclusion moving, but I do not trust the argument, and I believe I can say why without merely being cynical. The same form of argument could have been, and I suspect was, made in many earlier generations: by those who expected the world to end within their lifetimes, by those who lived through the first use of a weapon capable of ending a war in a single day, by those who first learned to write or to farm. If the argument's form can be run at any moment, then the bare fact that I can run it now tells me nothing about whether now is truly the pivot. Some further premise seems to be required to break the symmetry between our moment and every other, and I cannot see what that premise would be.

I have three questions. First, does this pattern — an argument for the uniqueness of the present that seems to prove too much, because it could be repeated in any present — have an established name? Second, what are the nearest concepts to which it should be compared, and precisely where does each fall short? I am aware of the style of reasoning about how many people will ever live, and of the general human tendency to treat one's own age as special, but neither appears to be the same thing, and I would like to understand why. Third, if there is no established name, please coin one, define it carefully, and mark its edges: a case that clearly falls under it, a case that clearly does not, and a borderline case. The label matters less to me than the carving, so please be explicit about what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and whether the premise that would break the symmetry can be supplied at all, or whether the argument is simply broken as it stands.
