I have a set of gut verdicts about when something is a reason for an attitude rather than merely a reason to want the attitude, and I want to know what principle, if any, unifies them.

A demon threatens to torture me unless I believe the moon is made of green cheese. I take that threat to be a reason to want the belief, and even to try to induce it, but not a reason that bears on whether the moon is made of green cheese — if anything it is a reason to distrust my own believing.

The same demon threatens to torture me unless I admire him. I think that threat gives me no reason to admire him; it does not make him admirable, and admiring him on that basis would not really be admiring him.

But when the same demon threatens to torture me unless I fear him, the threat seems to be exactly the right sort of thing to ground fear, since it presents him as dangerous. So one and the same fact is the wrong sort of reason for admiration and the right sort for fear.

A doctor I trust tells me that patients who believe they will recover recover more often. Now the usefulness of believing seems to give me a reason of the right sort to believe I will recover, because it is also evidence about how things will go.

A benefactor offers my art class a large donation if we admire his paintings. I feel no pull to admire them for that; if they are mediocre, his money does not make them less so. But suppose the same benefactor offers me a large sum if I merely intend to visit him in the hospital, where I have no interest in visiting and no intention of going. I feel no pull toward the intention there either — only toward the money and perhaps toward pretending. Yet when a friend offers me the sum if I intend to visit her, and I would genuinely go, the offer does seem to give me a reason to intend, because it gives me a reason to do the thing.

My tentative principle is that a consideration bears on an attitude just when it bears on the question that attitude answers — truth for belief, admirability for admiration, danger for fear — and that this yields all the verdicts above. But I suspect the principle is partly just restating them, and that it mishandles at least one case. Can you give me the principle, or the best pair of rival principles, that actually delivers these verdicts, state it precisely and say which case motivates each clause, tell me which if any of my verdicts I have to give up, and say what the winner predicts about a case I have not mentioned — someone offering me money to be amused by a joke I do not find funny?
