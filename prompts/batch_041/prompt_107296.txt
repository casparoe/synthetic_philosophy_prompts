I have no academic training in philosophy, but for a few years I have been circling a pattern in my own thinking and in arguments I overhear, and I have never found a name for it. I would like to know whether one exists, and if it does not, what a careful definition would have to look like.

The shape of it is this. Sometimes a consideration seems to count in favor of an attitude — a belief, an emotion, a desire — but counts in a way that strikes me as the wrong way, and I cannot say precisely why. Suppose a stranger offered me a large sum of money to believe that some country's population is under ten million. The offer does seem to give me *something* — at minimum a reason to want to hold that belief, or to take steps that might bring it about — and yet if I ended up believing it because of the offer, I would feel there was something defective about my belief, even if the belief happened to be true and well supported. The money bears on how good it would be for me to hold the belief, not on whether the belief is the one the evidence supports. I notice the same shape when someone threatens me unless I admire them, or when a colleague tells me I should be angry about a slight and the reason offered is not that the slight was grave but that my anger will help me negotiate a better settlement.

Two complications keep me from being sure this is one thing rather than several. First, in some cases the consideration seems to reach the attitude only sideways, through a side effect: I have no evidence either way about whether I will finish a difficult project, but I am told that believing I will finish makes it more likely, and so the practical reason appears to be the only reason available for the belief. I cannot tell whether that is the same phenomenon or something else wearing its clothes. Second, in some cases the consideration is not merely available but seems to be the *only* thing there is to go on, and I do not know whether "wrong kind" is a verdict about the reason itself, about the attitude it is aimed at, or about what a person would be doing if they formed the attitude on that basis — whether they would be responding to the consideration or merely exploiting it.

What I want from you is not a label. I want the boundary drawn. Does the pattern have an established name? If it does, I want to know how it is standardly defined, and — more useful to me — which of my cases fall inside it and which fall outside, and on what principle. If it does not have a name, I would like you to coin one and define it, with the cases at the edges used to fix where the line goes: the self-fulfilling belief, the case where no other reason is available, the case where the consideration is a genuine reason for something else (a reason to want the attitude, or to try to acquire it) rather than for the attitude itself. I have run across phrases like reasons to want to have a belief, the fittingness of an emotion, and the idea that reasons for attitudes differ in kind from reasons for actions, but I do not know whether any of these is the thing I am describing or merely a neighbor of it, and I would like that sorted out rather than gestured at.

Please also separate what is settled in this area from what is genuinely contested among people who know it, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
