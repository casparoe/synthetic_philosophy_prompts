Four years into retirement from a career in metallurgy and materials engineering, I have been reading philosophy in a disorganized way, and this term I have been sitting in on an advanced seminar on vagueness; the instructor has invited the auditors to contribute a question apiece to the final examination. I have been given the job of drafting one essay question together with a model answer and a marking scheme, and I would rather it not be the sort of question a student can answer by reciting two labelled positions. What I want is a question that forces candidates to say precisely where the epistemicist and the supervaluationist part company, and whether the parting amounts to anything.

The case that started me thinking about this comes from my old trade. Specifications in my field routinely impose a sharp numerical cutoff on a property that varies continuously — a batch conforms if a measured value falls on one side of a line — and everyone involved knows both that the measurement is noisy and that the underlying property is graded. Practitioners, in my experience, cheerfully assert two things that I now realize pull in opposite directions: that a batch sitting on the line is one the standard does not decide, and that of course there is a fact of the matter about it, which we simply cannot establish. The first sounds like the view on which the standard's language admits many equally good ways of drawing the line; the second sounds like the view on which the line is already there and our trouble is ignorance. I would like the question to use a case of that kind — a specification, a certification decision, a borderline batch — as the occasion for the analysis, and to require all of the following: whether the two views can be told apart by what they say about a borderline sentence's truth value and about whether an omniscient inquirer could in principle settle the matter; the semantic price each side pays, including the one that gives up the principle that a compound's truth value is fixed by the truth values of its parts, and the one that has trouble with the thought that a sentence is true exactly when things are as it says they are; what becomes of each view once the vagueness of the borderline is itself taken seriously, so that no sharp line separates the borderline from the clear cases; and finally whether the whole dispute has any practical or even theoretical upshot, or whether the two sides are describing one situation in two idioms. Please write (i) the examination question itself, pitched for a 90-minute written paper in an advanced course and entirely self-contained — it should tell candidates that no outside reading is required and that they will be marked on the argument rather than on names — (ii) a model answer of roughly 700 words, and (iii) a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth five marks each, out of 25, where each point states what a top answer must contain and, separately, the specific error that should cost a candidate marks; close with two or three sentences saying what separates an A answer from a B. Since I have already embarrassed myself once by confidently attributing a view to the wrong philosopher, please keep the model answer free of attributions and have it describe the positions rather than cite them.
