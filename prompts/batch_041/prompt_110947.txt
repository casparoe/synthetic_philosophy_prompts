This week, right in the middle of us grinding through fission cases and the 'what matters in survival' stuff, one of them waved the whole thing away: 'Buddhism sorted this out ages ago — there is no self, so there's no fact of the matter about whether it's you tomorrow, we can stop now.' I didn't want to just say 'go read more Buddhism' and I also didn't want to hand her the entire answer, so I fudged it. Help me do better before next week.

What I want is a menu, not a lecture. Lay out the different precise things 'there is no self' could be claiming, running from the readings that are almost trivially true up to the ones that are genuinely radical, and for each one tell me (a) what it actually commits you to, (b) whether it's plausible, and (c) most importantly, whether it would actually support the move she made — that there's no fact of the matter about personal identity over time, so the whole debate is idle.

The readings I've half-formed so far: something about there being no unchanging essence or substance (which sounds very different from there being no person at all); something about 'self' being a word that just doesn't latch onto anything; something reductionist where persons are real enough but not 'further facts' over and above the parts (the Parfit-ish line we covered); something merely practical or therapeutic about not identifying with your thoughts and feelings; and maybe something about the self as a story or construction we keep telling. Tell me which of these are genuinely distinct, which collapse into each other, which are true but useless for her argument, and which one is really doing the work when someone talks like she did. And flag any reading on which no-self would actually undercut rather than support caring about your future self — I have a hunch she'd find that uncomfortable and it might make a good teaching moment.

Keep it plain and please don't recite sutras or hand me a reading list at the end — I'll never remember them and I just need the argumentative map.
