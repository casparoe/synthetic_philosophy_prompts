Me and a mate have been running a reading group on the big-history stuff all term — Harari, Diamond, and then the Graeber-and-Wengrow side of things as the rebuttal — and it's basically split into two camps that have spent three months talking past each other. Camp one: the macro-story is real science, geography and energy capture and information technology are doing genuine explanatory work, and most of the criticism is politics dressed up as method. Camp two: the macro-story is a projection, its categories are retrospective folk categories, and the archaeological and ethnographic record is so various that there's no single trajectory to tell at all.

I want to end the term by making them produce the document they should have written in week two: a joint statement of an adversarial collaboration that both camps could actually put their names to. Please draft it.

What I want in it:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps agree on — and not weasel ones. Where do they genuinely overlap? What would both accept about what a macro-narrative would have to do to be respectable, about what the critics have actually established, about what the defenders have actually established, and about which of the standard objections to each side are weak or beside the point?

2. Then the residual disagreements, each phrased the way its own side would want it phrased, rather than the way the other side would caricature it.

3. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling one would actually move somebody. I reckon a few of them are: (a) whether "the state" picks out a natural kind or is just a label we slap on a loose cluster of features; (b) whether the emergence of agriculture and large-scale hierarchy is convergent — many independent origins with similar outcomes, so something law-like is going on — or contingent, in which case the macro-story is survivorship bias with a nice cover; (c) whether a claim like "geography drives institutions" is falsifiable at all, and if it is, what would falsify it; (d) whether macro-history needs directional laws or only ratchet mechanisms, i.e. things that are hard to un-invent once invented, which would give the appearance of direction without any law.

4. For each crux, the case or piece of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. And please invent at least one test that neither side has run yet but both would sign off as fair, and make it concrete — e.g. something like coding a large sample of excavated settlements for population size, presence of a central store, and inequality in house sizes, and then seeing whether scale and hierarchy co-vary tightly or come apart. Also worth a look: what the ancient-DNA picture of large-scale Bronze Age population replacement does to each side's story.

Two things to keep in mind. Don't let it collapse into "both sides have a point" mush — the whole value is in separating genuine disagreements from ones that were only ever about words, so if part of the fight is verbal, say which part and why. And I don't want citations, reading lists or references; I want the argument itself.

Format: numbered agreed-claims section first, then the disagreements as a table with columns for the crux, each side's own formulation of its position, the shared test or case, and each side's prediction.

Oh, and write it in British English, please.
