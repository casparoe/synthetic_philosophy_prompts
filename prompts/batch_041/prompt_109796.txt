Fourteen years ago I joined a small club that plays a game of self-amending rules: you begin with a bare rulebook and a table of players, and each turn someone proposes an amendment, the table votes, and the rulebook grows under its own hand. I am a novelist — my books are about families and the small institutions they build around themselves — and I have spent most of my membership keeping the minutes and staying out of the quarrels. This year the club has asked me to give the after-dinner speech at the annual dinner, which falls three weeks after the worst quarrel we have ever had, and I would like you to write that speech.

Here is the quarrel, in the four rules that matter. Rule 1, adopted at the founding meeting of six players: "Any rule may be amended or repealed by a simple majority of the players present." Rule 4, added years later: "No amendment may be adopted at a meeting at which fewer than ten players are present." Three years ago, at a meeting of fifteen, the table voted nine to five to strike Rule 1 and replace it with: "Any rule may be amended or repealed only by a two-thirds majority of the players present." Last year the same table added Rule 12 — "Rule 12 may never be amended or repealed" — together with a companion clause confirming that the two-thirds rule and Rule 12 are validly in force whatever defects attended their enactment. Since then a member has been demanding a ruling on whether the two-thirds rule was ever validly adopted (nine of fifteen is not two-thirds), whether the confirming clause can cure a defect it presupposes, and which of Rule 1 and Rule 12 governs where they contradict; the club has no rule for resolving conflicts between rules, the meeting dissolved without a vote, and our retired judge, who has played since she was a girl, has stopped coming. Rule 9, which nobody has ever disputed, provides that the after-dinner speech at the annual dinner is entered in the minutes as a proposal to amend the rules and is voted on at the next meeting.

I want eight hundred words or so, in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, I will be reading it aloud — and in the register of a person talking rather than a brief being filed. Three constraints, and I care about all three. First, the room is split: half of them play the game and will hear every word as a move in it, and half are spouses, children and one baffled cousin who have come for the dinner and will be lost the moment I sound like a tribunal. Second, because my remarks are entered as a proposal and voted on under rules whose authority is exactly what is in doubt, the speech must not stake its standing on either rulebook; anything that presupposes which rule governs will be heard, correctly, as an attempt to legislate my own validity, and I will lose the logicians before the second paragraph. Third, it must be worth the logicians' time anyway: I want them to leave with at least one distinction they did not have before, about what is genuinely at issue between a rule that was never validly enacted, a rule that is no longer in force, and a rule we are no longer obliged to keep playing by, and about why telling everyone to start a fresh game solves nothing, since a fresh game is either a continuation of this one and inherits the defect, or a founding — and the founding of this one had no rule behind it either. No real philosophers, no books, no titles, no citations; this audience would hear any of that as decoration, and the cousin would hear it as an insult. End on a line that could stand in the minutes as the text of the proposal itself, and that a person could also raise a glass to.
