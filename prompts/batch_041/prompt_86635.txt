I took early retirement from analytical chemistry about three years ago -- thirty-one years of it, mostly alloy certification and reference materials, which is a fancy way of saying I spent my career arguing about whether two samples were the same stuff -- and since then I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars at the university near us, mainly because my wife got tired of watching me reorganize the garage. I've gotten hooked on the natural kinds question: are the sciences really carving nature at its joints, or just cutting it into convenient chunks and then forgetting they did the cutting? My problem is that everyone in that room argues for their favorite theory by waving one pet case at it, and I never know what the target is supposed to be. So before I read another word of theory, I want the specification. You're a philosopher of science who works on classification and metaphysics and actually knows the chemistry and the biology, so start by brainstorming the cases and tensions out loud for a few sentences, then write me the desiderata -- the full list of things any adequate theory of natural kinds has to deliver, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, not a summary of anyone's position and definately not a reading list.

The cases I keep tripping over, and I'd like each of them to generate at least one requirement (add others if I've missed something a theory has to survive): the word jade covered two completely different minerals, an amphibole and a pyroxene, that nobody could tell apart by eye for centuries, yet "jade is tough" was reliable the whole time -- so what was that old term tracking? Isotopes, where the atomic number is identical and the nuclear behavior is wildly different, so it's not obvious whether the kind is the element or the nuclide. Diamond and graphite, both pure carbon, one kind or two? Heavy water, same formula with deuterium in place of hydrogen, different melting and boiling points and bad for you in quantity -- water or not? Species, where biologists tell me a species is a particular historical lineage rather than a class of organisms at all, and where "fish" turns out not to be a group once you insist on ancestry, even though the fish market runs fine. Kinds of people that seem to shift when you classify them, the way a psychiatric diagnosis can go into the world and come back out changed. And the old inductive payoff that any child can use -- that's a such-and-such, so it will probably do such-and-such -- including the puzzle case of a predicate rigged to flip meaning halfway through, so that all the evidence so far supports it and the very next observation refutes it.

For each requirement tell me whether it's a genuine demand on a theory or just a prejudice of one camp (the essentialists, the pluralists, whoever), and mark which are non-negotiable and which are negotiable given what the theory is for -- I'm a scientist, so I'll accept "good enough to license prediction and explanation" as the purpose. Then, and this is the part I most want, flag any pair or subset of the requirements you think can't be satisfied together, with the argument for why; I have a suspicion that the inductive payoff and the jade and looping cases are pulling in opposite directions, but I'd rather be told I'm wrong than have it smoothed over. Finish by naming the single requirement that does the most excluding work, and if a table helps, use one. American English, please.
