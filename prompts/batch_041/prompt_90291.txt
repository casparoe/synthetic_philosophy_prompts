I got into a stupidly long argument with my friend Priya last night about Arrow's impossibility theorem — she thinks it's basically a proof that the general will is a incoherent idea, I think it's more like a design constraint that tells you which things you have to give up, and by the end we were talking past each other because we kept sliding between "the theorem is about procedures" and "the theorem is about the very idea of a social good." For a reading group I'm running next week (mix of econ grad students and philosophy people, none of whom have read much social choice beyond the theorem's statement), I want to make a one-page decision tree / taxonomy that starts from "I accept that you can't have unrestricted domain + weak Pareto + IIA + non-dictatorship all at once" and ends at named positions. The point is to sort people by what they actually think the theorem shows, not by whether they call themselves a "Borda person" or an "epistemic democrat." So each branch node should be a concrete commitment or case, e.g., "is the theorem a result about the formal properties of aggregation rules, or about the legitimacy of any procedure that claims to speak for a group?", "would you accept a procedure that can rank A above B differently depending on whether C is on the ballot?", "do you think preferences should be taken as given or can deliberation legitimately transform them?", "is a lottery/random dictator an acceptable way to break ties or does it fail to produce a genuine collective decision?", "is the problem that we demand a single social ordering, or that we demand too much information from individuals?", etc.

I want the leaves to include at least: (1) accept all four conditions and treat the theorem as a reductio of the very idea of a collective will; (2) drop IIA and go positional/scoring; (3) drop unrestricted domain and restrict to single-peaked or value-restricted preferences; (4) drop weak Pareto or accept a dictator; (5) move to cardinal/interpersonally comparable utility; (6) move to deliberation that changes preferences before aggregation; (7) move to an epistemic/jury-theorem model where the goal is tracking the truth rather than aggregating preferences; (8) deny that we need a collective ranking at all (exit/market/secession). Also include any unnamed or unoccupied combinations you find — and say whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If two leaves are indistinguishable by your questions, flag them instead of pretending the tree separates them. Keep it to about 10-12 nodes and a page of prose, and don't just list names — the whole thing should be answerable by someone who only knows the theorem's statement. Also, can you make the very first branch distinguish an "internal" reading (it's a theorem about formal aggregation rules) from an "external" reading (it's a theorem about the legitimacy of collective decision-making), and if possible add a branch that separates a "burden-shifting" reading (the theorem tells you which assumption now needs defending) from a "modus tollens" reading (the theorem refutes the whole project), since those are the distinctions Priya and I kept missing.
