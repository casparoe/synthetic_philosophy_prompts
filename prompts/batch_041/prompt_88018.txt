You are a philosopher specializing in free will and moral responsibility, with a firm grasp of the incompatibilist tradition and of the standard objections to it, and I need you to work as an auditor rather than an advocate. I am a graduate student finishing a dissertation on the metaphysics of responsibility, and I present the central position at a work-in-progress seminar next week. Before I commit to the framing, I want it stress-tested, because I have a nagging sense that parts of it are held together with tape and I cannot see where. Please start by setting out, in your own words, what each of my commitments amounts to and what it appears to demand of the others — a short brainstorm, no verdicts yet — and only then move to the audit itself. No citations needed; I know the literature and will attach references myself.

Here is where I have landed. (1) I take Galen Strawson's Basic Argument to be sound: to be ultimately responsible for what you do, you would have to be responsible for being the way you are, and the regress this generates cannot be stopped short of literal self-creation, which is not a coherent option; so nobody is ultimately responsible for anything. Determinism strikes me as beside the point — indeterminism does not break the regress, it merely substitutes one impossibility for another. (2) Consequently I do not believe in desert-entailing blame: nobody deserves to suffer for what they have done, and while some system of state coercion is probably necessary, it must be justified entirely by what it does for the future, never by what was done in the past. (3) I am nonetheless a moral realist — I think there are mind-independent truths about what we owe one another, and that some actions are simply wrong. (4) I also think we cannot stop having reactive attitudes, and should not want to: resentment, indignation, gratitude, forgiveness, and the whole practice of being addressed by another's will are constitutive of any life I would recognize as human, and the ideal of never taking anything personally strikes me as a kind of death. (5) I am drawn to the episodic picture of the self: what exists at any moment is a thin, short-lived subject, and the I of now is not literally the same thing as the I of five years ago. (6) I accept that ought implies can: nobody is obligated to do what they genuinely cannot do. (7) And I think deliberation is causally real — when we weigh reasons, the weighing makes a difference to what happens; we are not merely systems through which the past passes.

What I want is an audit of this package, not a survey of the field and not a defense of any part of it. For every pair of commitments that pulls in two directions, tell me how severe the tension is, whether it is a genuine inconsistency or only an apparent one that dissolves once the terms are disambiguated, and, where it is genuine, the cheapest revision that would repair it together with what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the package. Be specific about which commitment does the damage and why, and be willing to say that a tension I have not noticed is the important one. Order the tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally bad. Then tell me which single revision, if I made it, would clean up the most at the least cost, and which one I should refuse to make because it would gut the rest of the position. I would much rather be told that I am quietly helping myself to something I officially reject than be reassured that it all hangs together.
