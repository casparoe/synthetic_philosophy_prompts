I've been reading a bunch about simultaneous discovery lately — Newton and Leibniz both inventing calculus, Darwin and Wallace both landing on natural selection — and I want to write an essay on a specific controversy inside that topic rather than just a survey of the history.

Here's the case I want the essay built around. It's a made-up scenario, but it's the kind of thing that's basically happening now, so treat it as live. Two grad students in different countries post a proof of the same open conjecture to the arXiv in the same week. Neither saw the other's work. Student A got there through a clever new argument that took her three years and that, as far as anyone can tell, no one else was anywhere close to finding. Student B got there by running a search over a huge space of formal proofs, so in some sense anyone with enough compute and patience would have landed in the same spot. The result was obviously "in the air" — somebody was going to get it soon either way.

What I want you to settle is this: does the inevitability of a discovery reduce how much credit the person who made it deserves, and should we treat A and B differently given how they got there? Write the essay weighing the main positions — the great-person view that originality and difficulty are what count, the zeitgeist/inevitability view that discoveries are basically social products and the individual is mostly a conduit, and some middle position. But don't end on a wishy-washy note. Defend a verdict on the specific question, make the case for why it's right, and deal head-on with the strongest objection to it.

Couple of constraints: keep it in prose, no bullet points, maybe 1500 words or so. And try not to lean on the usual canned examples — Newton/Leibniz and Darwin/Wallace can show up once for setup, but the actual argument should stand on the logic of the case itself. I also dont want a reading list or citations, just the reasoning.
