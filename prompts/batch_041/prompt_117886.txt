Sweden criminalizes the purchase of sex while leaving the seller unpunished; Germany and the Netherlands license and regulate prostitution as a taxable trade with health and safety obligations; New Zealand has decriminalized it and folded it into ordinary labor and employment law. All three regimes claim to serve the interests of the people who sell sex, and each camp describes its rivals as either abandoning those people to exploitation or driving them into it. I want the implicit theory of each. What does each regime presuppose about where the wrong of prostitution lies — in the buying, in the working conditions, or nowhere intrinsic at all? What does each presuppose about consent: is a paid sexual agreement treated as continuous with consent to any other job, or as something the law cannot trust? About agency: are sellers workers exercising autonomy the state should support, or a class whose choices the state should override or redirect for their own good? And about what law can achieve — deterrence through stigma, or harm reduction through visibility and regulation?

Then separate what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Could the Nordic model be defended without any claim that buying sex is intrinsically wrong, or does its deliberate asymmetry between buyer and seller require that claim? Is regulated legalization coherent if it simultaneously treats the trade as legitimate and its participants as presumptively damaged? Where are the regimes philosophically underdetermined, such that several theories of harm, consent, or autonomy would license the same statute? Finally, say how much of the dispute is genuinely empirical — whether criminalizing buyers reduces demand or trafficking — rather than conceptual, and what each regime would have to change if its governing view of harm or consent were swapped for one of the others.
