I'm in a grad seminar on early modern metaphysics and I've gotten into a rediculously long argument with a friend about Leibniz, so I want you to act as a Leibniz specialist for me — someone who knows the Theodicy, the Monadology, the correspondence with Arnauld and Clarke, all of it — and do some careful, disciplined historical-philosophical extrapolation. Please start by brainstorming out loud for a bit: which commitments in Leibniz's system are actually load-bearing for a claim like "the basic axioms of arithmetic are inconsistent," and which ones would he be most willing to sacrifice first? Then give me your best reconstruction of his likely response. I'd rather have a committed reading than a survey of options.

Here's the setup. Leibniz died in 1716, so he never saw any of the modern foundational material. Suppose you could tell him: there's a live and respectable worry among contemporary logicians that the ordinary axioms of arithmetic (or of set theory) might be inconsistent — that some contradiction might be derivable from them, and that by Gödel we can't even establish from the inside that they're consistent. And suppose he actually took the hypothesis seriously instead of brushing it off. I want you to work through three specific things. First, the principle of contradiction is supposed to be what makes a truth necessary — a necessary truth is one whose negation is a contradiction. But if the axioms are inconsistent, classical logic lets you derive anything, so "2+2=5" ends up as provable as "2+2=4," and the principle of contradiction stops doing any work at all. Does Leibniz have any resource for resisting that collapse short of giving up classical logic? Second, his possible worlds are, on one modern reading at least, maximal consistent collections of propositions, and God selects the best one. If arithmetic is inconsistent, do any worlds survive at all? I have a half-formed argument I'd like you to evaluate rather than just agree with: there obviously is an actual world, and Leibniz holds that whatever is actual is possible, and a world has to be consistent — so the bare existence of this world looks like it gives him an a priori proof that arithmetic is consistent, meaning he could just declare the hypothesis refuted without doing any mathematics. Is that argument genuinely available to him, or does it cheat, e.g. by helping itself to the reliability of the very reasoning under suspicion? Third, he had this dream of a universal characteristic plus a calculus that would settle disputes by computation. If arithmetic is inconsistent, does that dream become the ultimate dispute-settler (everything gets proved, so everyone must agree) or does it self-destruct?

Don't worry about citing chapter and verse, and please don't just list things Leibniz said — I want you to reason your way to an actual verdict on each of the three points, flag clearly wherever you're extrapolating past anything he ever wrote, and finish with a short paragraph on which of the three he'd have found most disturbing and why. Also, try not to hedge everything into mush; if you think one of my framings is anachronistic or just wrong, say so and tell me what the better framing is.
