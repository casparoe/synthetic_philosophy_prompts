I am a retired experimental physicist — condensed matter for most of my career, then several years in geophysics — who has spent the last while reading philosophy of science on my own, with more enthusiasm than discipline. I would like you to take the role of a philosopher of science who specializes in causation and causal modeling. Please think carefully before answering, and open with a short brainstorm in which you set out, in your own words, what each of the two accounts of causation actually claims.

My puzzle is this. In the laboratory I always understood an experiment as an intervention: one varies a single factor, holds everything else fixed, and reads off the consequence. When I later encountered the notation do(x) in the causal-inference literature, I took it to be nothing more than the experimenter's intervention written down, and when I saw the terms exogeneity and modularity attached to the interventionist notion, they seemed to me to say only that the manipulation must not itself be produced by its own effect — which is just sound experimental hygiene. So when I read that there are two rival theories of causation, a counterfactual theory and an interventionist theory, I assumed that the interventionist account is the counterfactual account with a practical gloss attached, the two differing only in vocabulary, rather as Lagrangian and Hamiltonian mechanics differ only in bookkeeping. If that is right, then no experiment or observation could ever discriminate between them, and a disagreement that no possible observation could settle strikes me as a dispute about words rather than about the world. Hence my question: what experiment or observation would distinguish the two theories, and if none can, am I right to conclude that the choice between them is a matter of convenience rather than of truth?

A related worry, from my geophysics years. We say without embarrassment that the Chicxulub impact caused the end-Cretaceous extinction, and that the collision of the Indian plate with Asia raised the Himalayas. No one can intervene on an asteroid impact or on a continental collision; the events are singular and unrepeatable. Does the interventionist account therefore oblige me to stop speaking of causes in these cases, on pain of inconsistency?

I would be grateful if you would separate what is settled in this area from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on it, and if you would refrain from presenting a contested position as though it were the consensus. Please answer in American English.
