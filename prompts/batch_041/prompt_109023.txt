I sit on the ethics comittee at a rehab hospital — I'm a physiatrist, not a philosopher, so forgive me if I fumble the theory part. We meet Thursday and I have to circulate a written opinion beforehand on a case that's turned into a fight about our pain policy. The patient is a man in his sixties, four weeks out from a stroke, here for shoulder rehab. Neurology says the lesion clipped the insula and they mentioned "pain asymbolia" on the way out the door and left me to look it up myself, which wasn't super helpful. What I can tell you is what he does: if you ask him to rate his shoulder on the 0–10 scale he says 7 or 8, every time, no hesitation; he says it's "burning" and puts one finger exactly on the spot. But he doesn't grimace, he doesn't guard the arm, he'll let the therapist move it through range without so much as a wince, he never asks for anything, and twice he's told the nurse it "doesn't really bother me." Once he asked her whether he was "supposed to mind." Our protocol — Comfort Protocol 4.2, which I helped write, so this is partly my mess — keys everything to the number: 4 or above, escalate the opioid, reassess in an hour; 3 or below, chart "pain controlled, continue." So the nurses are charting "severe uncontrolled pain" and pushing opioids at a guy who keeps refusing them, pharmacy is flagging sedation risk, and the surgeon wants him off everything so he'll actually move the arm.

Meanwhile we have the mirror case two doors down: a woman on the same shoulder protocol who says "two out of ten" while she's crying, not sleeping, and refusing therapy because she dreads it, and her chart says "pain controlled, continue" and she gets nothing. So the working group is split three ways: some want to add a second documented axis for how much the pain bothers or distresses the patient and trigger escalation off that instead of the number; some say that's just giving clinicians a license to discount self-report, which is exactly how pain in women and Black patients got undertreated for decades, and that our one protection is the patient's own number; and a couple of people think the real question is whether the first patient needs treating at all, since if nothing about his pain is unpleasant then maybe there's nothing bad left to fix — though the PT says he's still destroying the joint. I need a memo I can circulate: a concrete recommendation (change the policy, don't, or change it with conditions), with a time frame and with what actually goes in the chart and what the nurse does at 2am, plus an honest pass at the strongest version of the argument I'm rejecting.

Lead with the recommendation itself in the first sentence — what we should do and by when — then the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate my email back to me, and skip the part where you explain to me what pain asymbolia is.
