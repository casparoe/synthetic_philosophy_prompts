I host a small philosophy-of-physics podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher who's spent her whole career defending Gödel's line that time, in the everyday "it flows" sense, isn't really part of the physical picture, and she leans hard on the rotating-universe solutions to get there. I want eight to ten interview questions that don't let her just do the usual "relativity of simultaneity kills the present" routine -- they should press on whether the argument actually works. A few things I specifically want covered, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give:

- whether the existence of rotating models with closed timelike curves through every point is doing any real work here, or whether it only shows that GR doesn't have to include a preferred cosmic time, plus the follow-up for when she says the possibility alone is enough;
- the step from "there's no physically distinguished simultaneity" to "there's no objective becoming," which looks to me like it assumes the thing it's trying to prove, with the follow-up in case she says I'm the one begging the question;
- whether she's quietly running a Kantian/idealist reading of time, and whether she'd actually own that label;
- the obvious reply that you can always single out a global time by something like the mean motion of matter, or the frame in which the CMB looks isotropic -- so what's supposed to be wrong with that;
- and whether the argument proves too much: if it kills objective time, does it also put pressure on objective space, or on there being any frame-independent facts at all.

To close, I'd like her to say what would actually change her mind -- is there some cosmological observation, or some revision to GR, that would make her drop the whole line -- and, if she had to bet, where she thinks the argument is most likley to be wrong.
