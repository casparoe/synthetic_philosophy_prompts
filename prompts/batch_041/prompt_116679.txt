I am a programme officer at a small foundation that funds interdisciplinary research, and I have recently been put in charge of our mechanisms stream, which supports projects that try to explain complex human phenomena—religious belief, depression, moral judgement, aesthetic experience—by lower-level mechanisms such as genes, neurotransmitters, neural circuits, or evolutionary game theory. My background is in cognitive science, and I am temperamentally sympathetic to reductionism; I would rather fund a bold reductive project than a vague humanistic one. But our board has become sceptical, and at the last meeting we spent an hour arguing about two proposals without resolving whether either was a genuine scientific advance or a piece of what one trustee called neuro-babble. I need a reusable decision procedure that a panel of intelligent non-specialists can run in a twenty-minute slot, using a one-page summary and, if necessary, a short interview with the applicant. The procedure should be grounded in the distinction between cranes and skyhooks and the charge of greedy reductionism, but it must do more than repeat those labels: I want it to tell us what to look for in a proposal, in what order, and when to stop. I have also been experimenting with a distinction of my own, and I would like the procedure to incorporate it if it is any good. I call it explanatory debt. A reduction, on this picture, borrows explanatory credit from a lower level—genes, neurons, selection pressures—and must repay that debt at the level it is trying to explain. A crane pays in instalments: it identifies the lower-level mechanism, shows how that mechanism actually produces the higher-level pattern, and then says what the higher-level description adds that the lower-level one does not. A skyhook borrows the same credit but never shows the exchange rate; it asserts that the lower level must be doing the work without displaying the transaction. Greedy reductionism, on this way of putting it, is the special case where the debt is paid in a currency the lower level does not issue—for example, explaining a norm by a hormone, or a symphony by a synapse. I am not certain the metaphor is sound, so part of the job is to say where it breaks down and what it obscures.

Please write the procedure. It should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers trigger an immediate no, which answers allow funding but only with a changed description of what the project is doing, and which questions cannot be settled by the panel at all and must be referred to a philosopher, a domain expert, or the board as a matter of values. It should be honest about where the procedure is a judgement call dressed up as a checklist, and about the cases it will misfire on. Then run it on three proposals, giving the panel’s likely verdict and the reasoning in each case: (1) a project explaining religious belief as a by-product of agency-detection plus a common gene variant, with fMRI and twin data but no account of how the mechanism generates doctrinal content; (2) a project explaining depression by serotonin deficiency, with a biomarker and a small trial, but no account of the psychological level; (3) a project I am personally drawn to, explaining moral judgement by oxytocin and a simple game-theoretic model, with elegant experiments but no account of normative reasoning. For the third, I expect the procedure to tell me something I will not want to hear, and I want you to say plainly what it is. End with a final line in the form \answer{...} containing a short name for the procedure and the single test that does the most work in distinguishing a crane from a skyhook.
