I run a lunchtime reading group in my department — six or seven of us, mostly economists plus one historian, all comfortable with formal arguments but nobody's done philosophy past a first-year intro course. Next month I'm on the hook for the session on whether 'better than' is transitive, and I want to actually teach it rather than just report the state of the debate. What I want them to walk out with isn't "transitivity is false" — it's the shape of the choice: once the spectrum is in front of you, you have to give up either transitivity, or the comparability of the adjacent steps, or one of the intuitions that generates the spectrum in the first place, or the assumption that better-than is a single relation with fixed application conditions, and each of those costs something diffrent. I've been reading Temkin's Rethinking the Good and it's exactly the kind of book where I havent been able to keep hold of what depends on what, which is my real problem.

So don't explain the argument to me — plan the session: sixty-five minutes, whiteboard, no slides. I need the order the pieces have to go in and why each depends on the one before it, in particular when they need the idea of a relation having formal properties at all (transitive, complete, and so on), and whether that should be built on a non-evaluative example like taller-than or whether that's a trap that makes them think transitivity is a law of logic rather than a substantive claim about this particular relation. Then the carrying example. I was going to use the lives-and-populations spectrum — one person with a very good life, then two people with lives a hair worse, and so on down to an enormous population whose lives are barely worth living — but tell me whether that's the right carrier or whether it smuggles in population ethics and does the work for the wrong reasons; if it's wrong, tell me what the carrier has to have and lack. I also want to know where the money-pump worry goes (the idea that intransitive preferences let someone trade you in a circle until you end up worse off), because the economists will grab for it in the first ten minutes and I don't want it eating the middle of the session.

Misconceptions I'm braced for: that the spectrum is just the sorites with extra steps; that intransitive means incomparable; that intransitive means there's no fact of the matter; that the fix is that adjacent steps are roughly equal so the chain never really gets going; and that better-in-a-respect and better-all-things-considered behave the same way. A colleague also keeps insisting two things can be comparable without either being better, which I gather is Chang's parity idea, and I can't tell whether that belongs in the session or is a distraction. Tell me which of these actually matter, which are my own projection, and where in the sequence each has to be headed off. Finally, give me one question to ask at the end that would show me they've got the structure of the problem rather than just a taste for paradox, and tell me what I should openly leave out at this level — the full population axiology, the impossibility results, whatever — and why.
