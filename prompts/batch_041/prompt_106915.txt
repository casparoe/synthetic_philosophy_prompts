I teach a grad seminar on population ethics and last week we did Benatar's Better Never to Have Been, and a student cornered me in office hours with something I couldnt answer on the spot. Her line was roughly: the whole asymmetry only gets off the ground if you secretly treat not existing as a state of the person that can be better or worse for them — you line up the bads and goods of existing against the blank of not existing and declare nonexistence the winner. But there's no subject around to be better off when there's no existence, so isn't the asymmetry just trading on a comparison between two things where only one of them can even be a "state of" anyone? She put it more colorfully — you can't be better off never having been born. I nodded and said "well, Benatar anticipates that" and then completely failed to reconstruct the move at speed, which was a bit embarrassing.

I think part of why it rattled me is that I've always taught him as claiming each of us is worse off for having been brought into existence, and I've written it on the board that way for years, so her objection landed like it was aimed straight at the heart of the argument. What I want from you is the substance, not a reading list: what does the asymmetry actually assert, where does the "better never to have been" conclusion sit relative to any claim about someone being better off, and is her objection a real problem for Benatar or is it pointed at the wrong target? If it's the wrong target, tell me where a critic should actually be pushing instead. I'd like to walk back into seminar with something better than a nod.
