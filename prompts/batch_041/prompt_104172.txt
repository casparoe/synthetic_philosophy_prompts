I teach a seminar on value conflict and hard choices, and I want to use William Styron's Sophie's Choice next term without letting it collapse into a trolley problem. I don't need quotations — I need a reading. The novel is narrated by Stingo, a young would-be novelist looking back on the Brooklyn summer when he began his first book and fell in with Sophie and Nathan, and the choice that gives the book its title — a camp doctor, on her arrival at Auschwitz, makes her pick which of her two children will be gassed at once and which will be allowed to live on in the camp — arrives late, secondhand, and only after we have spent hundreds of pages loving her. I want the philosophical apparatus for hard choices taken seriously, and then I want to be told where the novel defeats it.

Some distinctions to work with, since they do not carve the same joint. Incommensurability in the strict sense: no common measure on which the options can be ranked. Parity: the fourth comparative relation some philosophers posit, in which two options are neither better, nor worse, nor equal, so that the difficulty is genuine rather than merely psychological. The dilemma proper: not a failure of ranking but the absence of any permissible option, so that whatever one does, one does wrong. And the remainder: the thought, bound up with agent-regret, that a justified choice can leave a residue no justification cancels — that choosing well does not put the agent back where she was.

Sophie's case is usually filed under the first two, but it looks more like a coerced selection than a comparison, and Holocaust testimony has taught us to speak of choiceless choices — decisions that cannot be judged by ordinary moral categories because the options were never alternatives in any recognizable sense. Yet the novel refuses to let her off that hook, and so does she: she cannot inhabit the justification anyone would offer her, and she ends by returning to the violent man she loves and dying with him. That looks less like a ranking problem than like moral injury — damage not to a principle but to the self's standing as a source of reasons, a notion developed for those who perpetrate, witness, or fail to prevent terrible acts, and here applied to someone who was both victim and, under coercion, agent.

So adjudicate. Which of these vocabularies, if any, is the right instrument for this novel, and where does the novel expose what the vocabulary misses — for instance, if injury is a matter of identity and ranking a matter of acts, no theory of comparison could reach it. Say also what the novel's form does: does the delay, and the mediation through a narrator who loves her and means to turn her life into literature, serve the moral seriousness of the case, or is that framing itself a kind of appropriation — turning a person into an example, which is precisely what my seminar will be doing to her? And end by saying what the fiction adds that argument alone cannot; or, if you conclude that the novel and the theory are answering different questions, say which one has the better claim on the truth of the matter.
