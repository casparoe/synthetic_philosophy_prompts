Right, so — I'm a novelist by trade, but the university ropes me into running a little final-year seminar called 'Blame, Guilt and the Novel', and I've got to set the exam. Last year was a disaster: I asked something broad about moral responsibility and got back twenty-five near-identical essays all reciting the same thing about Strawson's participant attitude versus his objective attitude, as if they'd all copied it off the same revision site. Which rather misses the point of the man, I think.

So I want a question this time that actually makes them feel the difficulty rather than name it.

Here's the knot I want them to worry at. Strawson's basic move (the 1962 essay, the one everyone half-remembers) is that blaming and resenting aren't propped up by some prior metaphysical fact about free will — they're grounded in the attitudes we actually take towards one another in ordinary life, and the bare thought that everything is caused simply doesn't (and shouldn't) shake those attitudes loose. Fair enough. But I want them to notice the thing they always skate past: he *does* let particular facts about a person's history dissolve the reactive attitudes — we stop resenting someone once we learn the act came out of a delusion, or that we're dealing with a small child — and yet the *general* fact of determinism is meant to be powerless to do the same. Why the asymmetry? That's the whole game, and I want the question to force them to say what's doing the work there, rather than gesturing at 'different standpoints'.

And to stop them just regurgitating, I want to spring a case on them out of fiction — a character I've been drafting. He's read all the determinist arguments, swallowed them whole, and has deliberately adopted what Strawson calls the objective attitude towards everyone, himself included, as a sort of ethical programme — treats people as things to be managed rather than to be met. Then, in the scene that matters, he can't hold it: someone betrays him and he's simply flooded with ordinary resentment. So the question should ask whether Strawson's account can make sense of this character — is the resentment a *vindication* of the participant attitude, or just a fact about human psychology that might have been otherwise — and what that in turn tells us about whether we're 'responsible' in any sense that survives all of this.

So: please write me the exam question itself (final-year undergrads / MFA students, answerable in about an hour), a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme. Build the scheme on five must-hit points, give the marks each is worth out of 20, and add a one-line note on how students typically fluff each one. I want the scheme to genuinely seperate an A from a B, so name the specific moves a top answer has to make — e.g. getting the distinction between excusing someone and exempting them right, and being able to say what it would even *mean* for a reactive attitude to be fitting or unfitting — and name the specific errors that should cost marks.

One last thing, and it matters to me: I'll be the one reading these, and I'm a bit of a pedant, so write the whole thing in British English. Ta.
