I teach a first-year seminar on the First World War and its aftermath, and I have stumbled into something I am not equipped to resolve. My students arrive holding two beliefs that pull in opposite directions, and I suspect I have been teaching them both without noticing. The first is that responsibility for 1914 is a live moral question about a nation: the 1919 settlement obliged Germany to accept responsibility for causing the loss and damage of the war, and although the clause's own words are those of a legal liability rather than a moral verdict, it was read at once, in Germany and outside it, as a judgment on the German people — and the great postwar quarrel among historians over whether the German leadership had deliberately aimed at a war of European domination, the quarrel that made Fritz Fischer's name in the early 1960s, was understood by his critics and his defenders alike as a quarrel about the German present at least as much as about 1914. The second belief, which I teach in the same term, is the Nuremberg principle that responsibility under international criminal law attaches to persons and not to states or nations, and that only persons can be tried and punished for it. Yet the same order that rests on that principle holds states liable for reparations, receives apologies offered by governments on behalf of their peoples, and speaks without embarrassment of a nation's responsibility to remember what it did.

What I want from you is not another verdict on 1914 — I have colleagues for that, and my students can read them. What I want is an account of what these practices presuppose, and of what follows from that. Does a treaty clause of that kind presuppose that a nation can be responsible in the strict sense, or only that it is useful to write as though it could? Does the Nuremberg principle contradict it, or can the two coexist because they divide the labour between them — individual guilt in criminal law, collective liability in the political settlement? Is there a single theory of collective responsibility that licenses both, or is the appearance of consistency merely the truce between two institutions that never had to agree with one another? I would also like you to be explicit about where these practices are philosophically underdetermined: which rival theories of collective responsibility would fit the evidence equally well, and where the practice is doing work that no theory is really needed to underwrite. And if I were to tell my students what would have to change under a thoroughgoing individualist account of responsibility, and then under a thoroughgoing collectivist one, what should I say about each element — the peace settlement, the criminal tribunal, the apology, the reparations payment?

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning in full, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
