My friend is a weird hybrid of art historian and compiler nerd, and she applys this one framework to every self-referential thing she runs into. Her terms: a tangled hierarchy (two levels that each feed back into the other) and an inviolate level — the part that isnt in the loop, the thing that fixes what the loop is doing and that the loop itself doesn't get to touch. She thinks a tangle is only legitimate if it's got one. Here's my collection of her verdicts, which I've been writing down because we keep argueing about them:

- Escher's Drawing Hands: real tangle, inviolate level = Escher. The hands don't draw themselves into being; someone was outside the frame.
- The liar sentence, the one that says of itself that it's false: vicious, no inviolate level. Nothing outside it.
- A quine, i.e. a program that prints its own source code: real tangle, inviolate level = the language and the interpreter running it.
- A compiler that compiles its own source, bootstrapped from an earlier build: real tangle, inviolate level = the machine underneath and the earlier stage.
- A court whose jurisdiction is defined only by that court's own past rulings, with no statute or charter above it: vicious, no inviolate level. She has some line about it looking like a loop but actually being a hole.
- Two states that each recognize the other as sovereign and each cite the other's recognition as the source of its own authority: vicious, same reason.
- A dictionary that defines all its words using only other words in the same dictionary: not a loop at all, in her book. Nothing in it represents anything else at a higher level, it's one flat level with a lot of mutual dependence. She gets genuinely annoyed when people call that self-referential.
- Two mirrors facing each other: also not a loop. An endless corridor of copies isn't a loop, she says; nothing in it is about anything.

(She's adamant that not-a-loop and vicious-loop are different verdicts, in case that turns out to matter.)

Now here's the one I want a prediction on. Take a rulebook — a club's bylaws, a wiki's policy pages, whatever — that contains the rule for amending the rulebook, and that amendment rule can itself be amended, so the book can change the very procedure by which it changes. Nothing outside the book authorizes any of it: no charter, no founder, no external law, just the members and their standing practice of following the book. The wrinkle is timing. Every amendment has to be carried out under the procedure that was in force when that amendment process started, and it only takes effect once it's done. So the rule licensing the amendment is frozen for the duration of the amendment, then the loop moves and the next amendment runs under the new frozen rule.

Does she call that a real tangle with an inviolate level, or a vicious circle? Don't just give me the verdict though. I want: what rule, or rules, she could be running that fit every verdict above, what each of those rules predicts for the rulebook, which one I should bet is actually hers and why, and what further case would force her to pick between them. If two of the rules happen to agree on the rulebook, tell me what case splits them instead. And just to make it worse: same rulebook, but with a clause letting an amendment reach back and rewrite the very procedure that passed it — does that move anything for her?
