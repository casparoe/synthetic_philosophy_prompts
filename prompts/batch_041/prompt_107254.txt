Write an FAQ and support page, approximately 700 words, for a fictional consumer device: the Meridian L-3 Field Translator, a handheld earpiece-and-microphone unit marketed to anthropologists, documentary crews, and aid workers as rendering any human utterance into English "without loss of meaning." Write it in the deadpan, relentlessly reassuring register of a real consumer-electronics support site — a page title, numbered Q&As, a warranty section, the occasional cheerful aside. The page is a work of sustained irony, and the irony must come from the philosophy rather than from winking. The manufacturer never mentions Quine, never uses the phrase "indeterminacy of translation," and never admits a limitation; instead it converts each genuine feature of Quine's argument into a selling point, a feature toggle, or a warranty exclusion. A reader who knows the argument should recognize every move and find the satire fair; a reader who has never encountered it should still be able to follow what is happening from the device's described behavior alone. The device is not broken — it works exactly as promised, and that is the joke.

The page must, over the course of its entries, do each of the following, and must do so as a feature or a piece of fine print rather than as an explanation:

- Ground the device's evidence in stimulus meaning: it is trained only on what speakers will assent to or dissent from when prompted, and the copy insists that this is "all the data there is" or "the only signal that survives translation."
- Concede that many different translation manuals fit that evidence equally well, and sell the surplus as a customizable "translation pack" — the user may install any manual consistent with the recordings, with no way to say which one is correct.
- Offer a "reference" setting letting the user choose whether a term tracks a whole object, a temporal stage of it, or an undetached part of it, together with a support answer explaining that the unit declines to answer "which object does this word stand for?" because, the company says, that question is not well-posed.
- Include a customer complaint that two units, fed identical recordings, return different yet equally adequate translations, and a support reply that treats this as expected behavior rather than a defect.
- Address holophrastic indeterminacy: a note that word-by-word glosses may be fully determined while the translation of a whole sentence remains unsettled, and that the unit therefore ships with a "sentence-first" mode.
- Include a warranty clause that voids coverage if the user attempts to extract a determinate referent from the device.

Do not resolve the tension; the page should end as smoothly and reassuringly as it began, as though nothing has been conceded. Do not quote or cite any real text, and do not name any philosopher or work. Keep the jargon light — prefer concrete device behavior and marketing language over technical vocabulary, though you may use a technical term where it earns its place.
