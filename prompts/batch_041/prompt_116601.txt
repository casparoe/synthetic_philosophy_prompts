I sit on the technical advisory board of a state hazard-assessment unit — we publish an annual regional volcanic and seismic risk report, and the numbers in it come from a structured elicitation we run every fall with about forty outside specialists. Each specialist gives us subjective probabilities for a fixed list of events, and we feed the median of each cell into a decision model that the emergency-management people use for budgeting. At the last meeting our newest member, a statistician, moved that we stop accepting incoherent submissions. Her argument was the Dutch book one: if someone's degrees of belief violate the axioms of probability, then there's a set of bets, each of which she'll regard as fair, that together guarantee her a net loss; so incoherent credences are irrational, and a unit like ours shouldn't be laundering irrational numbers into a public document. She has cases to point at, too — last year one expert put 0.20 on "eruption at site X" and 0.18 on "eruption somewhere in the region," and another gave 0.4 for an event and 0.7 for its negation. Our chair wants a memo from me for the February meeting, and she wants the reasoning to be something the statistician can actually live with, not a procedural fudge. I'm a seismologist, not a probabilist, so I need help getting this right.

Please draft that memo. I want a concrete recommendation with a bottom line the board can vote on — e.g. discard incoherent submissions outright, keep them but flag them, run some kind of repair step with the expert, weight them differently — plus a time frame for implementing it and an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the other side (the statistician isn't being silly; if we're publishing a single probability per cell, something has to be coherent, and she's right that we currently have no policy at all). Two other members are worth anticipating: one thinks we should just take medians and stop moralizing about people's inner lives, and one wants to drop any expert who won't revise on the spot.

A few things I'd like the memo to work through, because I know they'll come up. Does the sure-loss argument actually show the expert is *irrational*, or only something weaker — say, that her credences can't be represented as a single probability function, or that she's exposed to exploitation if she bets? What does the argument quietly assume: that she'll accept every bet she regards as fair (what if she just declines to gamble with us?), that money is linear in utility for her, that the bookie knows her credences and can offer the whole package at once? Does it matter that we're not actually betting with anyone — we just want a number to plug into a model? And is there a gap between the finite version of the argument and things like countable additivity, i.e. should our threshold for "incoherent" be stricter than what the betting argument itself delivers? Keep it to roughly two pages, formal enough to circulate but readable by the four board members who aren't statisticians.
