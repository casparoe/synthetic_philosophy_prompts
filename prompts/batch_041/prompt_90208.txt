Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about heritability, and neither of us could produce an argument the other would accept. He is a data scientist rather than a biologist, and he holds three claims that I could not knock down to my own satisfaction: that a heritability of 0.8 means a trait is 80 per cent genetic; that when a trait is highly heritable within each of two separate populations, the difference between those populations must be largely genetic as well; and that a high heritability estimate means there is not much anyone can do to change the trait. I told him that each of these was a misreading of the statistic, but I could not say precisely where the misreading lay, and I am no longer confident that all three of his claims are wrong.

This is on my mind because I am a teaching assistant for a 300-level seminar called Genes, Environment, and Behaviour, and I am in the middle of grading the midterm. One answer reads very well — it is fluent, confident, and well organized — and I want to be certain that the fluency is not doing work that the reasoning should be doing.

Here is the question I set:

A large study of adults in a single country reports three heritability estimates for that population: 0.80 for adult height, 0.60 for educational attainment, and approximately 0 for the number of limbs a person has. Two further results are reported. First, the average adult height in this country exceeds that of a neighbouring country by about six centimetres. Second, the study was repeated on a cohort born after a reform that made secondary schooling universal and equalized its quality across the country, and the heritability of educational attainment in the later cohort was higher than in the earlier one.

(a) What does the figure of 0.80 for height entitle you to say, and what does it not entitle you to say, about the height of an individual?

(b) What, if anything, does the six-centimetre difference between the two countries tell us about why they differ?

(c) A commentator said that the rise in the heritability of educational attainment shows that genes matter more for educational attainment than they did before the reform. Is the commentator right? Explain the mechanism that produces the pattern.

(d) The estimate near zero for the number of limbs is sometimes used to argue that how many limbs a person has is not a genetic matter. Assess that inference.

And here is what the student submitted:

(a) Heritability is a population statistic, not a fact about individuals, and it is a ratio of variances rather than a breakdown of the causes of any one person's height. So 0.80 does not mean that 80 per cent of my height comes from my genes and 20 per cent from my environment; it means that 80 per cent of the variance in height among the people in that sample is associated with genetic differences among them. Since the genetic differences are what make people differ, and the environmental differences contributed comparatively little, the figure supports the conclusion that height is a strongly genetic trait. It is a claim about the causes of the trait, not merely a description of statistical spread in one sample.

(b) This is the error of reading a within-population statistic as if it explained a between-population difference. The 0.80 was estimated on the variance inside one country and says nothing directly about why two countries differ. The average difference could be entirely environmental — diet, disease, and so on — even if height is highly heritable in both countries. The claim that the gap is genetic is therefore unsupported. And since the heritability estimate gives us no reason to attribute the gap to genes, the sensible default is to treat the six centimetres as an environmental difference until someone shows otherwise.

(c) The commentator is wrong. The reform equalized schooling, which removed a large part of the environmental variation in educational attainment. Heritability is a ratio — genetic variance over total phenotypic variance — so if the environmental share of the variance shrinks, the genetic share of what remains rises automatically, without anything about the genes changing. The genes were doing whatever they were doing before; only the denominator moved. The rise therefore tells us that the remaining differences between people are more attributable to genetic differences than they were before, not that genes became more important.

(d) This inference is a confusion. A heritability near zero means that, in that population, genetic differences between people account for almost none of the variation in limb number. It does not mean that genes are irrelevant to having limbs: limb development plainly depends on genes, and with the relevant genes absent or altered the trait would not appear at all. What the near-zero figure shows is that genetic variation for limb number is nearly exhausted in that population, while environmental and developmental accidents — injury, chiefly — supply almost all of the variance. Nor does it follow that limb number is highly malleable, since a heritability of zero says nothing about how the trait would respond to a change in environment.
