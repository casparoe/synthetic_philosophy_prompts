I teach a unit on machine ethics and want to use 2001: A Space Odyssey as a text rather than as an illustration, so please read the film through the responsibility-gap problem for autonomous systems. My students reach for the standard remedy — trace the harm back through the designers, the mission planners, the crew — and the film seems built to block it: the designers never appear, most of the crew is in hibernation, and the secrecy surrounding the mission means the men aboard lack the knowledge they would need to supervise HAL properly. I want a reading that separates three things the literature tends to run together: a liability gap (no one can be made to answer), a blame gap (no one deserves blame), and an attributability gap (the deed belongs to nobody, because no human intention produced it). Which of the three does the film actually dramatize, and does the film's deliberate ambiguity about HAL — malfunction, or obedience to an order he was told to conceal? — matter for the answer? Please test my hypothesis rather than assume it: that the gap originates in the confidence, voiced early in the film, that the 9000 series cannot err, since infallibility is precisely what licenses removing the human supervisor; on this view the gap is manufactured by a design philosophy, not by the machine. Two further questions. Bowman's removal of the modules reads as an execution, and one does not execute a thermostat; if the film means us to see HAL as the author of his acts, does that close the gap or only relocate and hide it? And the film ends with no inquiry, no testimony, no report at all — is that a diagnosis of accountability's limits or an evasion of them? Stay faithful to the scenes that resist the reading, and end by saying what the film shows about responsibility gaps that argument alone cannot state.
