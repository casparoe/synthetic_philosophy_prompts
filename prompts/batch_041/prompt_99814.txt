I've been lurking in the LessWrong-adjacent corners of the internet for years now and I keep having the same argument about whether a text-only language model means anything by its words, and I've finally worked out why nobody ever gets conviced: we're all answering different questions and then comparing answers. So I want to build a proper decision tree — something I can actually draw up and paste into a blog post as an indented outline — that takes a reader from one starting commitment to a named position on the symbol grounding problem, in the specific case of a model that only ever sees text. The starting commitment should be something everyone in the argument grants, like 'it produces fluent, task-appropriate output, and it has never had a sense organ'.

Each branch node should be a question they can answer without knowing where it leads, and I'd much rather they were phrased as claims about cases than as abstract labels. So not 'do you think meaning requires grounding?' but things like 'suppose it says "there's a cat on the mat" and there's no cat and never has been — does it have to be possible for it to be wrong about that, in a way that matters to it, for "cat" to mean anything in its mouth?' The distinctions I think actually matter here: whether meaning needs a causal-informational link from word to world at all; if it does, whether any old link will do or whether the link has to be one the system is *supposed* to have (i.e. whether you need function or normativity in the story); whether that 'supposed to' can be inherited from the designers, the training corpus, the users or the wider speech community, or whether it has to be the model's own; whether the thing that gets grounded is the individual symbol, the whole system, or the practice the system is embedded in; and whether grounding is all-or-nothing or comes in degrees and kinds.

My flatmate has a distinction I quite like and I want the tree to be able to place it: he says there's referential grounding (a word hooks onto an object) and practical grounding (a word hooks onto something the system can do, and can be held to), and he reckons a text model can have the second without the first, and that only the second is what anyone should really care about. I'm not sure he's right but it's a good test case. I'd like roughly eight to ten leaves, each carrying the name of the position (or 'unnamed' if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and I want you to be honest about the cells of the space that nobody occupies — say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive or just unexplored — and to flag any two leaves the tree can't actually separate with these questions (I suspect a couple of them only come apart on something the tree doesn't ask about). One constraint: no leaf may be 'the model is conscious', that's a separate argument and I want this one kept clean. Write it in British English, and don't bother with citing papers or authors — just say 'the standard X-ish line' wherever a name would otherwise be needed.
