You are advising a philosopher who has three years, no co-authors, a strong grounding in decision theory and social choice, and no appetite for empirical work, and who means to spend those years contributing to the debate around Harsanyi's aggregation theorem rather than commenting on it from outside. Assume the standard apparatus is already in hand: von Neumann–Morgenstern utility, the Pareto condition extended to lotteries over social states, and the representation of social welfare as a weighted sum of individual utility functions — together with the two principal routes to that representation, one treating society as a single rational agent choosing among lotteries, the other deriving social preferences from the impartial preferences of a single individual behind a veil of ignorance.

What I want is not an account of what the theorem says or of who has written what about it, but a defended judgment about which open questions in this area are most worth three years of this person's effort, and why. Frame the answer as an agenda: a short ranked set of questions or lines of work; the criteria you are applying, stated explicitly; the trade-offs among those criteria; and an account of which questions are upstream of which. Where you rank something low, say whether that is because it is settled, because it is intractable with the tools available, or because its resolution would not change much downstream.

Treat the following as a menu to be reordered and supplemented rather than a list to be worked through:

1. Whether the Pareto condition over lotteries should be weakened or rejected, and what becomes of the aggregation result if it is — including the objection that expected-utility aggregation licenses a lottery that is fair only ex ante while leaving identifiable people worse off ex post.
2. How much of the argument survives the recognition that the formalism does not by itself supply interpersonal comparisons: whether the theorem establishes utilitarianism or only an additive representation whose weights and zero points remain arbitrary, and which further premise — equal weighting, anonymity, or equiprobability behind the veil — would be needed for the stronger conclusion.
3. Whether the two routes to the result genuinely converge, or whether their agreement is an artifact of axioms they share.
4. Whether the extended preferences the impartial-observer route requires — an individual's preferences over occupying the positions of others — can be constructed in a well-defined way, and what follows for that route if they cannot.
5. Whether the result can be extended beyond expected-utility preferences to agents who are ambiguity-averse or who otherwise depart from expected utility.
6. Variable-population extensions, and whether additivity commits one to anything in the vicinity of the repugnant conclusion.
7. The contrast with Rawlsian maximin: whether the disagreement is substantive or turns on how the veil of ignorance is interpreted.

For each item you retain, say what result or development over the next two or three years ought to move it up or down the ranking, and close by naming the two or three things you would actually tell this person to start on. There is no need to supply citations; positions and arguments may be referred to without them.
