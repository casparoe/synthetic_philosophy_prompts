I'm a postdoc in a vision-science lab, and next month I have to open our joint lab meeting — normally a psychophysics journal club, occasionally hijacked by visitors — with a twenty-minute talk defending something my own PI thinks is a category mistake. The room will be mostly psychophysicists and EEG people: people who spend their weeks fitting models to contrast thresholds, arguing about whether an effect survives a change of criterion, and measuring the latency of early visual components, and who are allergic to anything that smells like philosophy. Two philosophers of mind will come for the coffee; they will be the only people present who have read the timing-of-experience literature, and they will want to talk about multiple drafts and the specious present. I'd like to be ready for them too, but I mainly need to be ready for the other twelve.

The thesis I want to defend is this. The familiar Orwellian-versus-Stalinesque distinction — did the brain undergo the experience and then falsify the record, or falsify the record before any experience was ever published — is usually criticized as empirically underdetermined. I want to say something stronger: it presupposes that experiences are dated, datable events, and the colour phi phenomenon should have killed that presupposition. When two differently coloured dots are flashed in quick succession at different positions, observers see a single dot travelling between them and changing colour en route; asked to describe what they saw, they place the colour change before the second dot could have been processed at all. The usual temptation is to conclude that the colour information was written back into the earlier part of the record. I want to argue that the interesting conclusion is not the Stalinesque one but a deflationary one: there is no fact of the matter about the time at which the observer experienced the change, not because our instruments are too coarse but because the question presupposes a single finished narrative with a determinate temporal order, whereas what the brain actually produces is a set of partially independent processes reconciled into a story only at the moment of report. The real question, on this view, is not when the reconciliation happens but what work it does — and the Orwellian/Stalinesque taxonomy turns out to be a distinction without a difference. I don't want to deny that experience happens; I want to deny that it happens at a time, and I'm aware that this is exactly the sentence that will get me into trouble.

Prepare me for the question period as it will actually go in that room, not as it would go at a consciousness-studies conference. Give me the objections in the order they will arrive, ranked by how likely each one is and how much damage it does to my position, each phrased the way the person who will raise it would phrase it — the psychophysicist's demand for a dependent variable, the EEG person's latency argument, the philosopher's vehicle-versus-content complaint, the colleague who thinks I've simply restated the standard point about the specious present — and for each one a reply I can deliver out loud in a minute or two, followed by an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front before anyone has to extract it from me, which single reply has to land or the talk is over, and the one question I am quietly hoping nobody asks. No reading list and no references, please: I have to be able to say all of this from the podium without a slide of citations, and I would rather know the shape of the fight than be handed things to look up afterward.
