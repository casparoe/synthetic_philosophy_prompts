I've spent twelve years in conservation at a mid size museum -- I'm the person who decides how much of a damaged object to intervene on, so provenance, reversibility, original surfaces, later accretions, all of that is my daily vocabulary and I know it cold. A colleague pushed Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life at me and its been rattling around my head for a month.

As I understand him: history is only ever worth anything relative to life -- a person or a culture has to be able to act, and the past either serves that or it doesn't. He splits our ways of relating to the past into three. Monumental: you look back at the great ones and treat them as proof that greatness is possible, as models to copy. Its rot is that it idealizes and so falsifies, and it makes the present feel like a decline; you get imitators and fanatics. Antiquarian: you love the particular past you came out of, you revere it and keep it, you feel continuous with it. Its rot is mummification -- you end up guarding and collecting instead of living, and the past becomes a house you can't leave. Critical: you put the past on trial, condemn it, and try to break with it. Its rot is that you're made of the thing you're condemning -- you can't get outside it, so the break is either a fantasy or it leaves you with nothing under your feet. Running through all three: the idea that health needs a bounded horizon, some things kept out of sight so you can act at all, and what he calls plastic power -- the capacity to grow out of yourself, to take in what's alien and past and turn it into yourself rather than being crushed by it. Too much history makes people into spectators: knowing, ironic, unable to want anything.

So: translate that into my vocabulary, properly. Take the three modes and the horizon/plastic-power business one at a time, and for each tell me what carries over cleanly, what I'd have to distort to make it fit, and what has no counterpart at all in how I actually talk and think about objects. I want the residue report as much as the translation -- and for each piece of residue, say whether it's my vocabulary being too narrow for something real, or Nietzsche sounding deep about something he never had to make precise.

Two more things. Flip it: is there anything in my vocabulary that his doesn't have, that actually matters -- something about objects, evidence, or who the future belongs to that his frame can't say? And then the practical bit. On my desk right now is a badly overpainted panel: either I take it back toward something like its original surface, or I stabilize it as it stands, accretions and all. Tell me which mode each option really is (not which one I'd like it to be), and whether the vocabulary I've got even gives me a third option.
