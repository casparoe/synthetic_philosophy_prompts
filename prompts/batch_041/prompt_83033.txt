I teach an upper-level seminar on collective harm, and the session on the inefficacy objection is the one I find hardest to plan. I want to build it around a case on which my co-instructor and I disagree, so before I commit to it I need a prediction of what she will say. She is careful and consistent; I have never caught her contradicting herself, and she gives reasons rather than verdicts alone.

Her verdicts on the cases I have put to her so far:

- A person who knowingly buys and eats a beef burger acts wrongly, even though she accepts that the probability her purchase causes one more animal to be reared and slaughtered is on the order of one in a hundred thousand. The smallness of the increment does not move her.
- She holds that verdict even in a society in which almost nobody else is cutting back on meat, so there is no scheme of restraint she could be accused of free-riding on.
- A person who takes one long-haul holiday flight acts wrongly.
- A person who abstains in a national election in a district that is certain to be won by a wide margin does not act wrongly.
- A person who declines to contribute to a fund that will provide a modest benefit to many people, where the fund will reach its target whether or not she contributes, does not act wrongly.
- A person who stands up at a concert to see better, in a hall where everyone else is seated, does not act wrongly, even though if everyone did it nobody would see any better.

The new case. A person takes one long-haul holiday flight in a jurisdiction whose total emissions of aviation fuel are fixed by a legally binding cap, set and enforced by a body independent of the fuel industry, that will not be relaxed. Every permit is in use. She knows all of this, and she knows that the total quantity of greenhouse gas emitted that year is identical whether or not she flies: someone else emits correspondingly less. She also knows that aviation is a serious contributor to a serious harm, and she is not flying for any urgent reason.

What I want from you:

1. The rules that fit her six verdicts, stated as precise conditions on when an act is wrong rather than as the names of positions, and a note on which of them her verdicts actually eliminate.
2. What each surviving rule predicts about the new case, and your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring that guess over the alternatives.
3. The further case I should put to her to settle which rule she is running independently of the new case — ideally one that separates the surviving rules along a different axis from the one the new case exploits — together with what each rule predicts about that case.

If her verdicts leave the answer genuinely underdetermined, say so rather than manufacturing a single reading. I do not need citations or reading lists; I need the rules stated sharply enough that I can see exactly where they come apart.
