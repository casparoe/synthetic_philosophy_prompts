You are a historian of late antiquity who also works on the philosophy of historical explanation. Please think carefully before answering, and begin with a short brainstorming pass on what the analogy below is being asked to do.

In arguments about Rome I keep meeting a picture of the empire as an organism that lived and died: it was born, grew, reached a vigorous maturity, then aged and finally expired, with 476 as the death certificate. The point of the picture is to settle a dispute — if Rome aged and died the way a body does, then there really was a fall, and talk of "transformation" is a euphemism for refusing to name a death.

Audit the analogy. Give me an explicit table of correspondences: what plays the role of birth, growth, maturity, senescence, and death. Then say where the mapping breaks — which features of biological aging the empire lacks, and which features of the fifth-century evidence have no slot in the picture. Then tell me what it smuggles in: for instance, that Rome was one organism with a single lifespan, that 476 is a terminal event, and that decline names a cause rather than redescribing the data. Finally, separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that don't, and say whether the claim that there really was a fall can stand on its own once the analogy is set aside — and if it cannot, what a better analogy would be.
