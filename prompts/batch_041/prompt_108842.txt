Okay, legal-philosophy thing I keep flip-floping on, and I'd rather you just pick the side I hand you and defend it hard. You know the Hart/Fuller thing. Hart: law is law if it was made and recognised the right way, full stop, and keeping validity seperate from morality is exactly what lets us say plainly that an evil regime's statutes were valid law but morally monstrous. Fuller: no — law has an inner morality, and to count as law at all a system has to be general, public, prospective, intelligible, non-contradictory, actually possible to obey, stable over time, and applied by officials the way it was announced; flout enough of those and you don't have bad law, you have something that isn't law. So here's the position I want you to argue: Fuller is right that there's an inner morality, but wrong about where it lives. Those requirements aren't conditions on validity — they're conditions on a system's ability to guide conduct, and guiding conduct is the entire basis of law's claim to authority. Put them there and Hart's positivism comes through untouched, which means the two of them were mostly talking past each other and the whole debate is a bit of a category error.
