I have been reading about biological signaling and about the way anthropologists and economists borrow the handicap principle, and I find that I make snap judgments about which cases are genuine signals and which are not. I would like to know what principle, if any, lies behind them — and I would rather have a principle that is honest about what it costs me than one that saves every verdict by growing epicycles.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

- The peacock's tail: an honest signal of the male's condition, and honest precisely because a sickly male could not grow it. A signal.
- A wasp's black-and-yellow banding: an honest warning of a sting, though the colors cost the wasp nothing to produce. Still a signal, still honest.
- A man's low vocal pitch: tells a listener something real about his size and strength, but it is no handicap in any sense. Honest — and yet I resist calling it a costly signal at all.
- Blushing: an unfakeable sign of embarrassment, with no cost and no intention behind it. Honest, but a different kind of thing from the peacock's tail.
- Gazelle stotting: costly, honest, and directed at predators — yet the predator benefits from the information, which makes me uneasy about calling the stotting a handicap to the gazelle.
- A flat tax that every adult must pay, rich or poor, able or infirm: costly to all, informative about none. Not a signal.
- A potlatch in which a chief gives away or destroys wealth before rivals: a genuine signal of standing, and the waste is part of why it works.
- A large anonymous donation to a famine-relief fund: costly, but not a signal of anything, because no one knows about it.
- The same donation with the donor's name on a university building: a signal, even though the money does real good and nothing is wasted.
- A painful initiation rite performed before the whole community: a signal of commitment. Yes.
- The same painful discipline practiced alone and disclosed to no one: not a signal — except that I have a stubborn sense that a private discipline does signal something to the person who undergoes it, and I do not know what to do with that.
- Saying "I will be there at eight": not a signal, just cheap talk.
- A degree from an expensive university: signals something about the graduate, but I do not think the tuition is what does the work; it feels more like the wasp's stripes than the peacock's tail.

My tentative principle: a signal is honest when producing it costs the sender something a lower-quality sender could not afford. That gets the peacock, the potlatch, and the initiation, and it correctly excludes the tax and the anonymous donation. But it seems to exclude the wasp, the voice, and the blush, none of which I am willing to give up, and it says nothing obvious about the degree or the private discipline.

What principle — or small set of principles — actually captures the pattern? I would like any two principles that are doing distinct work to be kept separate and named, the principle's verdicts on cases I have not listed, and an explicit statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. If the price of a single principle is overturning one of the verdicts above, tell me which one I should give up and why that is the cheapest loss.
