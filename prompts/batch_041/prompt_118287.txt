A working group I sit on has spent three meetings trying to specify when a risk model may report a number as "the probability that this particular applicant will default," and I would like a dialogue that gets underneath the procedural argument we keep having. The group's draft rule is homemade, so treat it as stipulated rather than something to be looked up: begin with the whole population, and repeatedly subdivide the current class by any available covariate whose subdivision both yields a sub-cell of at least a hundred cases and moves the outcome frequency by more than five percentage points; when no covariate satisfies both conditions, stop and report the frequency of the class you have reached. On our data the rule turns out to depend on the order in which covariates are considered. There is a covariate A and a covariate B; the cell of people having both contains eighty cases at a 90% default rate; the cell with A but not B contains 420 cases at 60%; the cell with B but not A contains 420 cases at 30%; the cell with neither contains eighty cases at 62.5%. The base rate is therefore 50%, subdividing on A alone gives 64.8%, subdividing on B alone gives 39.6%, and the cell that actually fits the applicant in question is at once too small to be used and nothing like either marginal. I gather this is a domesticated version of the old reference-class problem, but the numbers are ours.

Write the dialogue between the two people in the room who care most. The first is the modeler who designed the rule and wants to keep it; she suspects the order-dependence is a feature rather than a defect, since it tracks which covariates we actually have reliable data on, and she holds that what matters is which number produces better decisions, not which number is "true of" the applicant. The second is a colleague who takes the episode to show that there is no fact of the matter about this applicant's probability at all, and that the rule is a device for laundering a practical choice into the appearance of a measurement. Let them press each other at the real joints: whether the hundred-case floor and the five-point threshold are epistemic constraints or conveniences; what the modeler can say when the other points out that the maximally specific class — 90%, eighty cases — is the one that best describes the person standing in front of them; whether the skeptic's position is stable, or whether it spreads to every single-case probability and so proves too much; and what each would have to concede if the other turned out to be right. Neither should be a strawman, and each should answer the objection actually made rather than restating a position. End with the disagreement narrowed to one question, even if neither of them wins it.
