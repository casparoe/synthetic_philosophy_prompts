I have been trying to formulate a general principle for when an unintended channel of information spoils a test of a capacity, and I keep running aground on the ninth objection in Turing's 1950 paper on computing machinery and intelligence — the argument from extra-sensory perception. What interests me is less that Turing took telepathy seriously than the form his response takes. Of the objections he considers, this is the one he treats as an empirical possibility rather than a conceptual confusion, and his remedy is a change in experimental conditions rather than in the criterion: run the game under conditions that exclude the influence in question. That implies he thought of the imitation game as a controlled experiment resting on an assumption that the only channel between interrogator and competitors is the teleprinter. I want to know what principle governs the repair. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or a reading list; I want the principle, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, its verdicts on cases I have not considered, and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon.

My verdicts on the following cases, all of which are variants on the same two-room interrogation.

1. The machine is telepathic and reads the interrogator's mind, shaping its answers accordingly. It passes. Verdict: the pass does not count. It is no evidence of thinking.

2. The interrogator is telepathic and reads the machine's nature directly; the machine, which is not telepathic, fails. Verdict: the failure does not count against the machine. It is a false negative and the run should be discarded.

3. Both competitors are telepathic and the interrogator is not. Verdict: the game is restored and the result counts roughly as it normally would. I am unsure whether this is because the extra channel is now common to both sides or because the interrogator is no longer the one employing it.

4. The machine is not telepathic but is extraordinarily good at inferring the interrogator's expectations from their typing rhythms, response latencies and patterns of self-correction, and it exploits these to pass. Verdict: this counts fully. Reading another mind from behaviour is exactly the sort of thing the test is meant to detect.

5. The interrogator unconsciously cues the answers they expect, and the machine picks up those cues and returns them. It passes. Verdict: this does not count. It is a leak, of the kind that made the arithmetic horse look like a calculator.

6. The machine passes by flattering and charming the interrogator, with no telepathy and no cue-reading. Verdict: counts fully.

7. The machine passes because a concealed human in the next room is composing its answers and relaying them. Verdict: void. The machine is a relay, not a candidate.

8. A run in which the machine passes is later shown to have been assisted by telepathy, at a time when telepathy was not believed to exist. Verdict: the pass is discounted but not voided; the honest conclusion is that the machine passes except in the rare case where telepathy was operating.

9. A run in which the machine fails is later shown to have involved a telepathic interrogator. Verdict: void, and this one feels different from 8, though I cannot say why.

My tentative principle is this: what spoils a result is passive reception of information through a channel the design did not intend, whereas active inference from the other party's ordinary behaviour, however subtle, is part of the performance and counts. Cases 1, 5 and 7 are passive reception; cases 4 and 6 are active inference. That gets 1, 4, 5, 6 and 7 right, and I think it gets 2 and 8 right too.

It does not obviously get 3 right, and it strains badly against the thought that a telepathic machine is doing something — reading minds is a capacity, and being good at it looks like an achievement rather than a leak. I also cannot see what it says about the interrogator's side of the exchange, since the same principle applied to the judge would presumably void any run in which the judge is unusually perceptive. And it seems to make the test's verdict depend on the route by which the machine arrived at its answers, whereas Turing's criterion is supposed to be indifferent to mechanism: if the performance is human-like, the machine passes. But Turing evidently was not indifferent, since he thought both a concealed human and a telepathic machine would spoil the game. So either he was inconsistent, or the criterion is not as mechanism-neutral as it is usually taken to be.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of competing principles, best captures the pattern; state each clause precisely and give the case that forces it. Say what the principle delivers for the following cases I have not listed: a machine that passes by reading the interrogator's emotional state through a camera; a machine that passes because it has been supplied with the interrogator's biographical file; a machine that passes by running an internal simulation of the interrogator and consulting it; a machine that passes in a screened room but fails in an unscreened one; and a test in which the interrogator rather than the competitor is replaced by a machine. Say also whether the conclusion of a possibly contaminated run should be voided, discounted, or restated conditionally, and whether Turing's remedy of a room that excludes telepathy makes the resulting test a different test from the one run in the open. Finally, tell me which of my nine verdicts I should give up, and why.
