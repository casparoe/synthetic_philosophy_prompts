I'm three weeks into a metaphysics seminar on causation, and I've been defending a deliberately austere line: for one thing to be a cause of another is simply for the second to counterfactually depend on the first — had the first not happened, the second wouldn't have either. A friend in the seminar, who works on omissions, says this austerity has a consequence I won't accept, and she has set it out as a chain I haven't managed to break. What I want from you is not a verdict on who wins but a diagnosis: which link to cut, why it looks so obviously right until the moment you cut it, and what cutting it costs. I'm attached to the counterfactual line and would sooner sacrifice almost anything else, so I want the price in advance.

Here is the chain. (1) C is a cause of E just in case E counterfactually depends on C: had C not occurred, E would not have occurred. (2) An omission can bear this relation as readily as a positive event can — had I watered the plant, it would not have died, and I did not water it, so the plant's death counterfactually depends on my not watering it. (3) My omission, therefore, is a cause of the plant's death. (4) Nothing in (1) through (3) is special to me, to watering, or to plants; for any outcome E and any agent A who could have prevented E, had A prevented E, E would not have occurred. (5) So for any outcome E and any agent A who could have prevented E, A's failure to prevent it is a cause of E. (6) Yet nearly anyone could have prevented nearly anything — I could have given away my savings, gone elsewhere, spoken sooner, been faster, been kinder — so my omissions are causes of an enormous range of outcomes. (7) Causation is one univocal relation, the same one whether the cause is an act or an omission, and nothing further has to be added before a cause counts as a cause. (8) So I stand to the famine, the murder, and the accident in the very causal relation in which the person who actively produced each of them stands — and he stands to it no more than I do. She calls the conclusion monstrous and says the fault must lie in my premise (1); I would rather the fault were anywhere else. Which link should I cut, and does cutting it oblige me to abandon the counterfactual account outright or only to amend it — and if I amend it, what must the amended account say about the plant?
