You are a philosopher who works on pragmatism, antirepresentationalism, and the philosophy of language, and who is unusually good at the unglamorous business of separating the distinct theses packed into a single sentence. I want a careful analysis, not an essay on Rorty's place in twentieth-century philosophy. Take your time with this, and keep the readings genuinely distinct from one another; most bad treatments of a slogan like this one fail because the author quietly merges four claims into one.

In the reading group I belong to — mostly philosophy of science people, a couple of historians of science, one card-carrying scientific realist — a senior member has a habit of settling arguments with the line "truth is what your contemporaries let you get away with." Last week she used it to close down a question about whether our current physics is any closer to the truth than Newton's was. Her move was that "closer to the truth" is just a compliment we pay to whichever theories survive our present standards of justification, that there is nothing else for it to mean, and that any realist who says otherwise is covertly invoking a God's-eye standpoint no one can occupy. I could not tell whether she had said something so obviously true that it cannot do the work she wants it to do, something substantive and defensible, or something that quietly collapses into "truth is whatever the powerful can enforce."

Begin by brainstorming in rough notes every distinct thing the sentence could be claiming. Aim for breadth, include readings you suspect are uncharitable or silly, and do not evaluate anything yet. Then produce the following sections, in this order and under these headings:

1. Brainstorm (rough notes, about 200 words): the full spread of candidate readings, one line each, no verdicts.

2. The readings, stated precisely (about 900 words): a numbered menu. For each reading, state it as a precise thesis rather than a paraphrase of the slogan — say what it asserts about the relations among truth, justification, and a community's standards; what it would commit its holder to regarding disagreement, the history of science, and the possibility of being right against everyone; and whether it is trivial, contentious but defensible, or false. Be explicit about which readings are compatible with one another and which exclude one another. If a reading is so weak that no one who says the slogan could actually hold it, say so.

3. Which readings carry her use of it (about 250 words): identify which of the readings would actually support dismissing the question about progress in physics, which would not, and which reading she would have to accept in order to reach the conclusion she wants — noting whether that reading is one she could hold with a straight face.

4. Bottom line (about 120 words): the reading the slogan's users most likely intend, the one I should probably grant, and a sentence I could say back to her that does not merely restate the realist's assertion.

Throughout, be precise about the difference between (a) a claim about what the word "true" means, (b) a claim about what makes a belief true, and (c) a claim about what entitles someone to call a belief true. The value of the analysis will depend on not running these three together. Do not pad the answer with historical background or name-dropping; I want the menu and the verdicts.
