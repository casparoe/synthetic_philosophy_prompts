I'm a TA for a second-year seminar on mass violence, and next week is the week on perpetrator motivation. Honestly every year it collapses into the same thing: someone says Browning, someone says Goldhagen, everyone agrees it's situation versus culture, and we run out of time. I want to bust that open, so I'm making a one-page decision tree (flowchart, basically) that starts from a reader's own hunches and walks them to a named position on why otherwise ordinary people take part in genocidal killing. Can you build it? Five to seven branching nodes at most, six to eight leaves, and every node has to be a concrete question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. So not "do you think it was the situation or the culture?" but more like "two men from the same village, same age, same upbringing: one joins the killing squad, one hides a family in his attic — which of the two most needs explaining, and what kind of fact would explain it?" (that one's off the top of my head, use it or don't).

Please make sure the tree can actually separate the things I think get mashed together: explanations that locate the cause in the immediate situation (orders, peer pressure, not wanting to look like a coward, diffusion of responsibility); in what the person already believed before anyone put a rifle in his hand; in the wider culture or ideology he was marinated in; in the institutional role or bureaucratic machinery he got slotted into; in a slow brutalization or desensitization that changes the person over time; and in plain self-interest (loot, career, status, dodging punishment). I also want at least one leaf for the view that there's no general answer at all — that the question is malformed, or that every case is sui generis — because I suspect some of my students are secretly parked there. Test the branches against cases the seminar already knows: Reserve Police Battalion 101, the Milgram obedience experiments, the Stanford prison experiment (and flag that its status is contested), Abu Ghraib, and the Rwandan interahamwe — plus at least one case where most people refused or rescued, like the Danish rescue, so the tree has to explain the negative case and not just participation. Where a real position lives, name it; where a combination of answers is logically available but nobody seems to defend it, mark it unnamed and tell me in one line why you think it's empty (incoherent? merely unappealing? or maybe just unexplored?). And if two of your leaves can't really be pulled apart by your own questions, say so instead of pretending they can.

When you're done, be honest about where the thing is weakest. Wich branch would you redraw or cut if you learned something specific, and what argument or piece of evidence would actually change your mind about which leaf is best supported? I'd much rather have a tree that admits its soft spots than a tidy one that oversells itself, so don't sand down the parts you're unsure about.
