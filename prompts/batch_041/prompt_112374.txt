I'm putting together a handout/quiz for my reading group on underdetermination and reference — the cluster of problems where you've got all the information there is to get about a system, and you still can't say which of two incompatible ontologies it has. I need you to be the expert here: take the role of a philosopher of science who works on theoretical equivalence, reference, and structural realism, and who is good at building diagnostic instruments rather than just summarizing the literature. Before you write anything, brainstorm quietly: list the axes along which positions in this area actually come apart, and check that each axis is one where real people disagree rather than one where everybody nods along. Then build the thing. Show the brainstorm briefly, then the instrument.

What I want is a decision tree (flowchart style), not prose. It should start from a commitment that most people in the room would accept and end at named positions. Build it from the logic of the debate first — the questions and the combinations of answers they allow — and then go back and plant the actual positions on the tree, noting which branches nobody occupies and why (incoherent? unattractive? just unexplored?).

Start point: the person accepts that two theories can agree on every piece of accessible information — all observable predictions, all inputs and outputs of the system, everything any conceivable measurement or inspection could turn up — and still differ in what they say exists.

Branch nodes should be questions someone could answer without already knowing where the branch leads. I want at least these distinctions somewhere on the tree, though you can add others if they earn their place:

- whether the underdetermination is epistemic (there is a fact of the matter, our evidence just doesn't reach it) or metaphysical (there's no further fact beyond the accessible ones, so the apparent disagreement is verbal or pragmatic)
- whether the missing information is inaccessible in principle or only in practice, and whether the person thinks that distinction itself does any work
- whether reference is determinate: does a term in the theory latch onto one thing in the world, or is reference inscrutable/indeterminate, or is it fixed by something outside the theory (causal contact, use, charitable interpretation, the world's own joints)
- whether going structural helps or just relocates the problem — i.e. the objection that any structure you specify is satisfiable by just about any domain of the right size, so saying the world is structural says almost nothing until you say which relations count
- whether treating a theory's theoretical vocabulary as existentially quantified placeholders (Ramsey-style) dissolves the question or merely postpones it
- whether the person thinks the whole thing is a pseudo-problem that should be dissolved rather than answered

Cases I want used as the concrete probes, since these are the ones my group will argue about:

1. Quine's radical translation: the rabbit runs by, the native utters one word, and the evidence is compatible with it meaning rabbit, undetached rabbit-part, or a temporal stage of a rabbit. Use the case, dont cite anything.
2. Two formulations of the same physics that differ over whether there are fields or only particles-with-dispositions, with identical predictions.
3. A trained neural network whose weights you can inspect completely: does it have a determinate ontology of internal representations, or is that question underdetermined by everything the network does and everything inside it?
4. The mereological case: a world of simples arranged tablewise versus a world of simples plus tables, with all the same observable facts.
5. Something like the problem of other minds — a system that behaves identically to a conscious one, so the accessible information is complete but the ontology question seems to survive it.
6. A case where two descriptions are related by a systematic relabeling or permutation of the underlying objects, so every accessible fact is preserved.

Leaves I need named, at minimum: Quinean indeterminacy of reference; a Putnam-style model-theoretic / internal realist conclusion; epistemic structural realism (structure knowable, intrinsic nature not); a Newman-flavoured objection to that structuralism; a Carnapian or deflationary line where the question is internal to a framework and has no external answer; a verificationist or stronger anti-realism that calls the extra ontology meaningless; and a stubborn metaphysical realist who says there is a fact of the matter and we simply cant have it. If you think some of these collapse into each other, or that the tree can't separate a pair, say so at the leaf and explain what further question would be needed — don't fake a separation.

Audience: smart grad students and a couple of stubborn physicists, so keep the node questions free of jargon where you can, and put the jargon in the leaf names. Size: aim for 8-12 branch nodes and 8-10 leaves; it's fine if some leaves are reached by only one path.

At the end, add a short section: which branches are empty or nearly empty, and for each, whether that's because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive once stated plainly, or because nobody has bothered. And flag any two leaves that the tree only separates by a question that's doing suspiciously heavy lifting.

Think carefully about the Newman-style branch especially — thats the one people usually get wrong.
