I am setting the final paper for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Aristotle's ethics and his scattered remarks on exchange, money, and household management, and I need one essay question that cannot be answered by reciting the standard summary of the topic. The paper will carry four questions; candidates choose one, have sixty minutes, and are told to write about 800 words. No books, no notes, no bibliography, so nothing may depend on candidates naming modern commentators.

The crux I want the question built on is a tension inside Nicomachean Ethics V.5. In the translation set for the course, the chapter contains, a few sentences apart, both of the following: 'Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality.' And: 'Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes.' I want candidates to have to say what work money is doing in the first sentence and what the concession in the second does to it, rather than to reach for the usual contrast between use value and exchange value.

To keep them off the secondary literature I have been teaching them a distinction of my own devising, which I set out in the lecture notes in roughly these terms. On the scaffolding reading, money is an instrument that lets us read off and act upon ratios that hold independently of the practice of measuring: the goods are comparable in themselves, the currency merely registers a comparability that is already there, and a currency that failed to track those ratios would be simply a bad instrument. On the constitution reading, goods are not comparable until there is a measure in circulation: comparability is an achievement of the exchange practice, and what money does is turn a heap of heterogeneous goods into a single field of magnitudes, so that outside the practice there is nothing for a currency to be accurate or inaccurate about.

I also give them two cases of my own construction to test the readings against. Case A is a community with no currency at all in which one ox is regularly exchanged for ten measures of grain, and everyone expects the same terms next season. Case B is a community with a single official currency whose prices drift from month to month with no pattern that anyone can detect or rely on. The candidates should be made to say what each reading predicts about each case, and in particular whether the scaffolding reading has any room left for the concession in the second sentence I quoted, and whether the constitution reading can make sense of Case A without smuggling in a measure it has not admitted.

Three things the question must not reward: summarising the long quarrel over whether Aristotle anticipated a labour theory of value or a marginalist one; glossing the passage as an early statement of the use value / exchange value distinction; and any answer whose weight rests on what a named commentator is supposed to have held. Everything needed should be in the two sentences, the two readings, the two cases, and what the course covered on the difference between acquisition that is natural and bounded by need and acquisition that is unlimited. Candidates who bring that difference in should be rewarded, but nobody should need it in order to pass.

Please give me: (1) the question as it will be printed, with the extract reproduced, the time and word limit noted, and the two readings and two cases stated compactly enough for an examination paper; (2) a model answer of about 800 words; (3) a marking scheme built on five must-hit points, each with its mark, the five totalling twenty, and for each point a one-line note on the characteristic way candidates miss it; and (4) a short paragraph addressed to the second marker setting out what separates an A answer from a B answer.

The model answer must reach a verdict: either it says which of the two readings the passage better supports and why the other cannot be made to fit, or it says precisely why the passage leaves the choice open and what further feature of Aristotle's account would decide it. It should also confront the fact that Aristotle names need, not money, as what really holds an exchange together, and should say whether need can serve as a common measure at all when needs are as heterogeneous as the goods. Please do not put any further passages in quotation marks: the extract above is the only verbatim quotation we can check, so any other appeal to Aristotle should be paraphrased.

Write the whole response in American English, including the wording of the examination question, the model answer, and the marking scheme, with the extract standing as printed.
