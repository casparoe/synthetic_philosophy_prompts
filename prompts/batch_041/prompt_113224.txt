Ok so I'm a junior and I peer tutor in the math resource room two mornings a week before first period, and somehow I ended up as the student rep on the district committee that's rewriting our "demonstrated proficiency" rules, which everyone is suddenly freaking out about because of AI and because kids can now get right answers without (maybe) knowing anything. Here's my problem. My cousin who's a philosophy major told me about this guy Ryle who said knowing how to do something and knowing that something is the case are two completely seperate kinds of knowledge, and that the people who think knowing how is just knowing a fact in disguise are wrong, partly because if you needed to know a rule before you could do anything, you'd need a rule for applying the rule, and you'd never get started. But then my cousin also said that in the last 20-30 years a bunch of philosophers pushed back and said no, actually knowing how IS a kind of knowing that, it's just knowing a fact about a way of doing the thing, and the argument is more subtle than Ryle made it sound. Meanwhile our committee keeps writing rules that quietly assume one side or the other — like "students must be able to explain their reasoning" (that's assuming you can't know how without being able to say it) or "a correct answer is sufficient evidence of mastery" (that's assuming the opposite) — and nobody will admit it. I want something I can actually use.

It has to be usable by teachers and administrators who have never read any philosophy and never will, so it should be plain steps in order, and for each step it should say what counts as a sufficient answer and what I do when I can't get evidence for that step at all. It also has to flag the places where the step is secretly a fork in the philosophical road, i.e., where the procedure only "works" if you've already picked a side about whether knowing how reduces to knowing that — I'd rather see the fork than have it hidden in a checklist. And it should say where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a person, because it's turned into a values question about what we're willing to give credit for, not a factual one about what's in the kid's head. Then run it on three cases from my actual life: (a) a girl in my algebra II group who can recite the quadratic formula steps and explain completing the square perfectly well but freezes the second the numbers are different from the ones in the book; (b) a boy who rips through problems fast and gets them right but genuinely cannot say why any of it works, and whose teacher wants to mark him down for "no understanding"; (c) a kid who turns in nice work with an AI thing open in another tab and can't do it when it's closed.
