My friend and I are both longtermists of the annoying kind: we think the far future is where nearly all the value is, and that even a one-in-a-trillion shot at a future with 10^38 or so happy lives beats any sure thing on offer now. I'm also pretty convinced by the simulation argument, the three-horned one: either civilizations almost always die out before they get posthuman, or posthumans almost never run ancestor simulations, or we're almost certainly in one. My friend thinks the second of those commitments eats the first, and she gave me this:

(1) Fanaticism is just expected value: a tiny probability p of securing a far future with N lives of high value dominates any cost we could pay, for any p > 0 we can defend.
(2) The simulation argument gives a substantial probability, maybe better than even, that we're in a simulation.
(3) In the simulated branch, the far future of our world is a finite computation. How long it runs and how big it gets is set by the simulators' budget and their interest, and it can be switched off at any moment for reasons that have nothing to do with us.
(4) So in that branch we aren't buying p·N, we're buying at most p·q·M, where q is the chance the simulators keep us running and M is the largest future they'd bother to compute. Neither q nor M is large.
(5) The un-simulated branch doesn't rescue the fanatic either. Even there our reach is finite and any plan for the far future is hostage to ordinary extinction risk, so that term gets discounted to.
(6) So the astronomical tail never dominates. Fanaticism is dampened, and near-term goods deserve roughly the same weight as the far future.

I'd rather keep (1) and (2) — I'm not giving up expected value and I'm not giving up the simulation argument. So which of the rest goes, why did it look fine when I first read it, and what does dropping it cost me? And if the argument is actually fine and I just have to swallow (6), say so, and tell me what else I've bought.
