I've been trying to think through the Quebec secession business and I keep getting stuck, so I want you to do the mapping work for me. Here's the shape of the case. Quebec held sovereignty referendums in 1980 (lost by a wide margin) and 1995 (lost roughly 50.6 to 49.4, on a question that asked voters to approve Quebec becoming sovereign after making a formal offer of an economic and political partnership to the rest of Canada). The federal government then referred the legality question to the Supreme Court of Canada. In 1998 the Court said a province could not secede unilaterally under either Canadian or international law, but also said that a clear vote on a clear question would trigger a duty on all sides to negotiate, and it grounded that in four underlying principles of the constitution: federalism, democracy, constitutionalism and the rule of law, and the protection of minorities. Parliament then passed the Clarity Act in 2000, which says the House of Commons gets to judge whether a referendum question is clear and whether the majority is clear enough before negotiations begin. Quebec's National Assembly passed its own reply asserting that the people of Quebec can determine their own future. Meanwhile the Cree and Inuit of northern Quebec intervened in the reference arguing that if anyone holds a right of self-determination in that territory it's them, and that their land shouldn't just be handed over.

Now the lens. In the normative literature the big split is between theories that give a group a standing right to secede whenever it chooses (choice or plebiscitary theories, nationalist theories of self-determination) and theories that grant it only as a remedy for serious injustice, like Buchanan's. What I want:

1. Take the remedial-only view first. On that view, what is Quebec's case actually made of? Is the 1982 patriation without Quebec's consent, or the language and cultural situation, or the economic grievance, an injustice of the right kind? Be honest if it isn't, and say what follows for the moral claim.

2. Then take the choice/nationalist view. What does it say about the fact that the unit whose choice is supposed to count is a province whose borders were drawn by the very state it wants to leave? That's the nesting problem, the Cree and Inuit claim. Does the choice theory have a principled answer here, or does it just stop at whatever borders happen to exist?

3. The Court's four principles look like a theory, but maybe they're a way of not having one. Do they generate a determinate answer to any of the hard questions — who counts as the people, what majority is clear, what makes a question clear — or do they just relocate the decision into the political process? Same question for the Clarity Act handing the judgment to the federal House of Commons: is that a plausible reading of democracy or a self-serving one?

4. Now go the other direction. What does the episode show about the theories? A remedial-only theorist has to explain why negotiations were even on the table after a close vote on a vague question, and a choice theorist has to explain why the courts and the international community were so cold to a unilateral declaration. Which theory does the case actually support, and which one does it embarrass?

I want a real verdict at the end, not a survey. And keep separate the things that are actually settled among people who work on this — say, that international law doesnt recognize a general unilateral right of secession for substate groups — from the things that are genuinely contested, like whether fifty percent plus one should suffice, or whether a right to secede can coherently belong to a group defined by the territory it happens to occupy. Don't sell a contested position as the consensus, and flag anywhere you're leaning on a reading of the case that a Canadian constitutional lawyer would fight you on.
