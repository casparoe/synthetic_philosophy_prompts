I'm a first-year PhD student in philosophy of science and last weekend I got into a long, slightly drunk arguement with my brother-in-law, who's an actuary at a mid-size car insurer. He prices policies: a 23-year-old guy with a two-year-old hatchback in a certain postcode pays one number, a 45-year-old woman with a Volvo pays another, and he swears the whole thing is just "objective probability, straight from the data" — millions of rows of claims, loss ratios, the lot. The more we talked, though, the less I could see what the number on any single policy is even supposed to *be*. There isn't a big pile of identical versions of him out there; he's one guy, and the premium is one number attached to one person for one year. He also let slip that they add a loading for expenses and profit on top of the "technical" price, and that when they enter a brand-new market they basically pick a figure and adjust as the data comes in. That last part sounds to me like a degree of belief, or just a price, not a frequency of anything.

Here's what I want from you. You're an expert on the foundations of probability and the philosophy of statistics, so please be rigorous and don't just hand me the textbook three-way split with a bow on it. Take the practice itself — how insurers actually price individual policies — and reconstruct the view, or views, of probability it presupposes. Start by brainstorming the candidate readings before you commit to one, then tell me: does the practice strictly *require* a frequentist picture, where the probability of an event is its long-run relative frequency in some reference class — and if so, which reference class, and how is that choice made? (I have a hunch this is where it gets bad, but show me.) Or is the practice equally well, or better, explained by a subjectivist reading, with the premium as a coherent betting quotient / degree of belief, or by a propensity-style view on which the individual case has a genuine chance? Be careful to separate what the practice logically presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with — I suspect it's underdetermined and that several interpretations would license exactly what he does, but I want you to *argue* that rather than assume it.

And then the part that's really bugging me: the apparent tension. He insists the numbers are objective and data-driven, yet the profit loading and the "we'll guess and adjust" move in a new market look thoroughly subjective. Is that a genuine inconsistency in the practice's implicit philosophy, or are the two pieces answering to different questions? Finally, suppose we replaced whatever view is presupposed with a thoroughgoing subjectivist one — or, the other way, with a strict frequentist one. What would actually have to change in how premiums get set and defended? Anything practical, or is the philosophy here just window dressing? Think it through carefully and don't hedge your way out of giving me a verdict.
