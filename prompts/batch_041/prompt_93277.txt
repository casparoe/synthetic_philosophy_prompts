I am a postdoctoral fellow in early modern social history, and in six weeks I am presenting a pilot project at the annual meeting of our regional archives association. The project takes a language model fine-tuned on one district's surviving records — parish registers, manorial court rolls, tax assessments, and a run of poor-law correspondence — and uses it to produce ranked hypotheses about the people who appear in those records only once: probable kin links, likely migration paths, and short life-course sketches for servants, laborers, and unmarried women whom the sources name and then drop. My thesis, which is the thing I will be defending, is that this kind of probabilistic reconstruction is a form of repair rather than fabrication. The silence in the record is itself a datum about who had the power to be written down, and a hypothesis that wears its uncertainty openly can serve the people the record discarded without pretending to be a source. Nothing the model produces enters the catalogue; it lives in a separate shadow index with its own provenance notes and confidence scores. On a held-out sample of two hundred people whose kin I could confirm independently, the model's top-ranked guess held up about three times in four.

The room will not be the room I would have chosen. It is mostly career archivists, formed in appraisal, arrangement, description, and custody, several of whom hold as a matter of professional identity that the record is the record and that the archivist's duty runs to the document rather than to the gap; the association's ethics chair will be there, and has spoken before about descendant communities and who owns the past. Two early modern social historians who have worked this same district for decades will be present, and both are allergic to counterfactual reconstruction. The one digital humanities colleague likely to be on my side will ask the most damaging technical question of the session. A local genealogist will want to know whether she may use the shadow index in her own research.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this room will actually raise — I expect eight or so, but include anything with real force even if the list runs long — ordered by how likely each is and how much damage it would do, phrased in the objector's own vocabulary rather than a philosopher's. For each, give me a ninety-second reply, say what I should concede outright instead of defending, and flag where the reply is thin. Tell me the concession I should make before anyone asks for it, and the question I am dreading. Say how confident you are in each claim you make, and at the end identify the single judgment in the whole sheet you are least sure of.
