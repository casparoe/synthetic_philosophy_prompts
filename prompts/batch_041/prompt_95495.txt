I am a high school student, and I would like to understand a problem in the philosophy of logic by translating it into the one formal vocabulary I actually possess.

That vocabulary is the two-column proof as it is taught in geometry. On the left are numbered statements; on the right, for each statement, a reason. The reasons are of two kinds, and this is the distinction I care about: some are propositions that have already been established or stipulated — postulates, definitions, previously proved theorems — and some are rules of inference, such as modus ponens, which are never themselves numbered lines. A rule is what licenses the move from one line to the next; it is not one of the things the proof asserts. I have also noticed that the whole format presupposes someone outside the proof, my teacher, who checks each line against the rules and grants the marks, and that the teacher's own entitlement to apply the rules is not written down anywhere in the proof.

The problem I would like translated is Lewis Carroll's dialogue in which the Tortoise accepts the two premises of a valid argument and the conditional joining them, and still declines to accept the conclusion, asking Achilles to enter the conditional as a further premise; when Achilles does, the Tortoise asks for the further conditional that licenses drawing the conclusion from all of it, and so on without end.

Please restate the Tortoise's position, Achilles's successive replies, and the regress itself in the vocabulary of the two-column proof — premises as numbered lines, rules of inference as the reasons that license steps, a proof as a finite object checked line by line from outside. Be as faithful as you can, and then give me the residue report: which parts of the Tortoise's demand and of Achilles's replies carry over cleanly, which have to be distorted to fit, and which cannot be expressed in that vocabulary at all. I am especially interested in three questions. First, when the Tortoise asks that the rule of inference itself be entered as a numbered line with a reason, is that a request the proof format can even entertain, or is it a demand for something the format has no column for? Second, does the proof vocabulary let me distinguish the claim that a step is licensed by modus ponens from the claim that modus ponens is valid, and if it does not, is that a defect of the format or a sign that the Tortoise's question was never about proofs in the first place? Third, the geometry textbook simply stipulates that postulates are not proved; is that a real answer to the Tortoise or only a refusal to answer him?

Finally, I would like you to tell me what would change your mind. If you conclude that the Tortoise's challenge either survives translation as a genuine problem or dissolves in it, say which argument or consideration would lead you to the opposite verdict, and identify where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be mistaken. I would rather have an honest account of where the translation fails than a tidy resolution.
