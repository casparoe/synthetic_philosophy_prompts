You are a philosopher of mind who specializes in panpsychism and the combination problem, and you also happen to have spent years writing corporate communications. I am assembling a teaching unit on the combination problem for an advanced undergraduate seminar, and I want a piece of sustained deadpan satire that will do double duty: it should be funny to students who already know the debate, and it should teach the shape of the problem to students who do not. Please write a mock internal integration playbook and FAQ, roughly 800–1000 words, circulated by the fictional Office of Phenomenal Synergy at “Panqualia Holdings” to employees ahead of a corporate merger. The merger is not of companies but of micro-subjects: the firm claims it will combine vast numbers of tiny experiential units into a single, unified corporate consciousness. The document should be written in the bland, confident voice of an HR and change-management memo—headings, bullet points, a Q&A, a “frequently expressed concerns” section—and it should never wink at the reader. The satire should target the temptation, common in panpsychist writing, to treat the combination problem as an engineering or logistics problem that can be solved by the right integration protocol, while also making clear why the problem is genuinely hard.

Use the actual philosophical terrain. The memo should presuppose or debate: whether micro-subjects can literally sum into a macro-subject; whether the resulting unity is an emergent phenomenon, a phenomenal-bonding relation, or a matter of the whole being prior to its parts (cosmopsychism); what happens to the boundaries and grain of experience; whether proto-phenomenal properties are enough; and whether the new corporate subject has its own point of view or is merely a committee of the old ones. At least three of the following should appear as substantive positions the memo has to manage: constitutive subject-summing, emergent panpsychism, cosmopsychism, panprotopsychism, and illusionism about phenomenal unity. To keep the piece from being a generic parody, invent and use a fictional “Combination Readiness Level” (CRL) scale, with levels such as CRL-1 “mere co-occurrence,” CRL-2 “phenomenal bonding,” CRL-3 “emergent unity,” CRL-4 “cosmopsychic inversion,” and CRL-5 “single post-combination subject.” The memo can treat this scale as an ordinary compliance tool. Include at least one dissenting note from a cosmopsychist auditor or illusionist compliance officer who thinks the whole merger is either backwards or empty, and make that dissent philosophically respectable rather than a strawman. The jokes should come from getting the positions right—for example, what it would mean to owe fiduciary duties to sub-experiences, whether a post-merger subject can be liable for pre-merger qualia, how one audits the unity of a point of view—not from mocking panpsychists as silly.

Before writing, brainstorm briefly in a short planning section of no more than 150 words, which I will not count toward the word limit; identify which real positions and objections you will dramatize, then write the memo. Do not include citations, reading lists, or references to actual books or articles; the piece should stand on its own for a reader or model without web access. Keep the prose polished and the irony sustained: a specialist should recognize that you understand the debate, and a newcomer should come away knowing what the combination problem is and why it is not solved by a merger consultant’s slide deck.
