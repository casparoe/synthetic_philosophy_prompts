I'm a third-year student in a joint philosophy and computer science programme, and I'm currently taking a seminar on complexity theory and its philosophical consequences. One thread we keep returning to is the thought—raised, in different forms, by Gödel in his 1956 letter to von Neumann, where he asks whether theorem proving might be feasible in something like linear or quadratic time and suggests that if it were, mathematical discovery could be automated—that the P versus NP question tells us something deep about the limits of mechanized reasoning, or about whether human mathematical insight is doing something a machine couldn't. Our instructor also had us read a taxonomy of possible worlds, attributed to Impagliazzo, in which the space of outcomes is carved more finely than by P versus NP alone: a world where NP problems are easy; a world where they are hard in the worst case but easy on average; a world with problems that are hard on average but where one-way functions still do not exist; a world where one-way functions exist but public-key cryptography does not; and a world where full public-key cryptography exists. I want to write my final position paper on this, and I'd like you to argue a thesis for me rather than just survey the options.

Please argue that the widespread philosophical framing—treating P ≠ NP as the crux of whether mathematical discovery can be mechanized, and treating the existence of hard problems as the crux of whether privacy and secrecy are possible at all—rests on a confusion between worst-case and average-case hardness, and that once that confusion is cleared up, the scenario worth taking most seriously philosophically is the one in which there are problems that are genuinely hard on average yet no one-way functions exist. The paper should make the positive case that this scenario, if actual, would sever the link that is usually assumed between intractability and the possibility of cryptographic secrecy, and should explain what follows for how we should think about the relationship between computational limits and human cognitive limits. Because I'll be presenting this to classmates who know the taxonomy but haven't thought hard about its philosophical upshot, please make the argument self-contained rather than leaning on references I'd have to go look up—I'd rather you reconstruct any position you need to engage with in your own words, and I don't need a bibliography.

Two things I'd like you to take seriously rather than brush aside. First, the strongest objection to my thesis: someone could insist that worst-case hardness is exactly the notion that matters for the logical point about mechanization, since a single unmechanizable problem type is enough to show that no algorithm can subsume all mathematical reasoning, and that shifting to average-case hardness changes the subject rather than answering it. Second, the worry that average-case difficulty is not a natural fact about a problem but is relative to a distribution over inputs, and that distributions of the kind that actually arise in mathematical practice or in an adversary's choice of keys may be artefacts of our interests rather than features of the world—so that my preferred scenario may not even be a stable possibility. Address both directly, and be honest about how much of the original philosophical interest survives once the average-case framing is in place.
