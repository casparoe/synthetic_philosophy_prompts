I recently sat in on a colleague's graduate seminar in confirmation theory, and his handout opens with a characterization of Jeffrey conditionalization that I found lucid on first reading and rather slippery on the second. He writes that the rule is the natural generalization of Bayesian conditioning to cases of uncertain evidence: where ordinary conditioning demands certainty in some proposition E, Jeffrey's rule lets the evidence redistribute your probabilities across a partition {E1, ..., En}, and you respond by holding your conditional probabilities P(H | Ei) fixed — what he calls probability kinematics — and then taking a weighted average of those conditionals, the weights being your new probabilities for the cells. That much, so far as I can tell, is broadly right. But the handout then goes on to say that the maneuver is ultimately idle: any piece of uncertain evidence can be represented as the output of a noisy observation, so one may simply condition on that observation in the usual way and recover exactly the same posterior; on this telling, Jeffrey's rule is just ordinary conditioning on a richer sample space, its chief historical service was to dissolve the problem of old evidence, and its one genuine blemish — that applying it twice in succession makes the order of the updates matter — is why most Bayesians regard it as defective and prefer to condition on the total evidence. Since I mean to assign the handout next term, I would rather not propagate its mistakes.

Please give me a careful audit, claim by claim: tell me which parts a working Bayesian epistemologist would endorse as stated, which are defensible only with qualifications, and which are simply false, and wherever something is false or loose, say what the correct statement is. I am especially interested in three points. First, whether the advice to represent uncertain evidence as a noisy observation and then condition ordinarily really does deliver the same posterior as Jeffrey's rule in general, or only under special conditions — and if the latter, what those conditions are and what goes wrong outside them. Second, whether the rule's dependence on the choice of partition (I have heard it said that the very same shift in your probability for H can be induced by different partitions, with different resulting posteriors) is as damning as the handout's closing sentence suggests, or whether it is a manageable feature. Third, whether the non-commutativity is a defect of the rule or simply a fact about sequential learning from uncertain evidence that a defender would accept without embarrassment. And finally, tell me whether the handout's practical conclusion — that one might as well always condition on the total evidence — actually follows from any of the true claims about the rule, or whether it is doing work the premises do not support.
