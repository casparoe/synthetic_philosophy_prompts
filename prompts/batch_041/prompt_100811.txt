My cousin rides for a semi-pro cycling team and a couple months ago she got one of those Therapeutic Use Exemption forms approved so she can keep using her asthma inhaler, and honestly it's been bugging me ever since. The way the anti-doping system seems to work is that it bans a substance and then hands out permission slips for the same substance, and the only thing that changes is whether you've got a diagnosis on file — two riders, same inhaler, roughly the same dose, one of them is a patient and the other's a cheat. And once I started noticing that shape I saw it everywhere. Health insurance pays to fix a wrecked knee but not to upgrade a good one. A kid with an ADHD diagnosis gets extra time on the SAT and a kid who just wants extra time gets told no. Sports will let you get laser eye surgery to get to 20/20, but a lens that takes you past 20/20 is cheating. I'm in a medical ethics class right now and we read the standard therapy-versus-enhancement stuff, but the readings always present the line as if everyone already agrees where it sits, and I really don't think they do.

So here's what I'd like you to do. Take the anti-doping exemption system as the main case (the other examples are just there for comparison) and tell me what philosophical commitments it actually presupposes. Does it need some notion of "normal" or species-typical functioning as the baseline — restoring you to normal is medicine, pushing you past it is doping? If so, whose normal is it: the individual athlete's own pre-treatment level, or a statistical average for humans? Because those come apart badly. Treating an asthmatic can leave her with better lung function than a rival who was never sick, and yet nobody strips the genetically gifted rider of her huge aerobic capacity, so the baseline clearly isn't equal-for-everyone. Is the practice committed to the idea that disease versus enhancement is a biological fact a doctor can just read off the body, or is it a social and normative judgement dressed up in clinical language? And does leaning on a diagnosis as the switch presuppose that medicine has a clean notion of "disorder" — and does it survive the fact that something like asthma is weirdly common among elite endurance athletes, which makes you wonder whether we're treating a disease there or just a body doing exactly what a body is supposed to do under that kind of load?

Then, please, separate what the system strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit comfortably with — it might turn out to be compatible with several different accounts of where the therapy/enhancement line falls, and I want to know which ones and why. And tell me what would actually have to change in the rules if we swapped the presupposed view for a rival one: say a purely welfarist view that only cares whether the athlete is better or worse off, or a flat conventionalist view where "enhancement" just means whatever the rulebook says it means that year. Don't worry about citations, and don't sweat whether I've got every sports detail exactly right — I care about the philosophy, not the paperwork.
