I am leading one ninety-minute session of a general-interest reading group — adults, no philosophy training — on the short passage that closes the second chapter of the Zhuangzi, in which Zhuang Zhou wakes from dreaming he was a butterfly and cannot tell whether he is Zhou who dreamed of being a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming of being Zhou, and which ends by granting that there must be a difference between the two and calling this the transformation of things. Don't explain it to me; plan how I should explain it. What has to be in place before the passage can do any work — I assume it is not the problem of how we know we are awake, but tell me what it is and why it must come first. Which single case should carry the session, drawn from their own nights, in which the dream contains its own check and returns a verdict, so that the ordinary test for being awake fails visibly from the inside — and what must that case not be or do? Then give me the misconceptions in the order this group will form them (my guess: they arrive at Descartes and leave as skeptics), each with the exact point in the sequence where it gets headed off, including the member who reports that she is a lucid dreamer and so knows. Partway through I want them to meet the earlier moment in the same chapter where one speaker tells another that both Confucius and his listener are dreaming, and that his saying so is itself a dream, so that the doubt looks self-consuming rather than merely unanswered; say where that belongs. End with one question that shows whether they have got the question the text is asking rather than a Western one it declines to answer, and with a short list of things I should tell them outright I am not going to resolve. No readings, no citations, no textual history.
