I work as a caseworker at a housing-justice clinic, and for the past few months I've been helping draft what we've been calling a "freedom audit" for the residents of a new social-housing scheme that the council has handed over to a private operator. The scheme is managed rather than owned by the council: the operator sets the house rules, runs the inspections, and holds a bundle of discretionary powers under the tenancy agreement. My job is to turn residents' grievances into something a regulator or a court could act on, which means I constantly have to decide what makes a grievance a grievance about *freedom* rather than about inconvenience, bad luck, or bad service. My drafting partner and I keep having the same argument, and I've begun to suspect that my own verdicts on individual residents don't hang together. I don't want to defend a theory I half-remember from a course; I'd rather hand you the verdicts and ask what, if anything, they add up to. Please act as a political philosopher who works on the positive, negative, and republican conceptions of liberty — someone comfortable moving between the formal statements of those views and messy human cases. Take your time, and before you answer, brainstorm the candidate principles openly rather than committing to the first one that fits most of the list. No reading lists or citations, please: I want the reasoning, and I want you to flag the points where you're genuinely uncertain rather than papering over them.

Here are my verdicts, going by gut, on the residents I've been dealing with. A live-in domestic worker on the estate whose employer keeps her passport locked in a safe: unfree, obviously. A resident whose tenancy agreement gives the operator the right to enter without notice at any hour, but who in four years has never actually been entered upon: unfree — the standing power is what does it, not its exercise. That same resident getting an annual letter reminding her of the entry right: I don't think the letter changes her freedom at all. A resident who is scored by a proprietary "behaviour" system whose rules are never disclosed to her, and which has never once been used against her: unfree, and I want to say *more* unfree than the first resident, though I can't yet say why. A resident with a gambling addiction who loses her housing benefit within days every month: I want to call her unfree, my colleague says she's merely weak-willed, and I notice that the thing constraining her isn't a person. A resident born on the estate who says she has never wished to live anywhere else and would choose this life again: I'm reluctant to call her free and equally reluctant to call her unfree. A Deaf resident who can't use the building's intercom and so can't receive visitors: misfortune, not unfreedom, I think. A resident who could move out tomorrow, but every available alternative is worse than staying: my colleague says she's free because she can leave; I say no. A resident who formally needs the operator's permission for guests, pets, and paid work, though permission has never been refused: unfree. A resident of a neighbouring scheme run by a mutual housing co-operative — binding rules, regular inspections, a right of appeal she helped design — whose life contains far more actual interference than anyone else on this list: free. And two cases I keep circling: the "sleeping dominator," where the operator holds an unused right to relocate any resident to a distant estate on thirty days' notice, a right exercised twice in a decade — I say everyone under it is unfree even in the quiet years; and the "collective dominator," where no individual at the operator decides anything and a proprietary algorithm allocates repairs that no one can explain or overrule — I say that's unfreedom too, and my colleague says you can't be dominated by a process. My own tentative formulation keeps coming out as something like: you are unfree to the extent that another agent's choice, rather than your own or mere chance, fixes the terms of your life. I can see that it breaks on the addict and the Deaf resident, and I suspect it also mishandles the co-operative.

What I want from you is a small menu of precise principles — the negative-liberty reading, the positive-liberty reading, the republican reading, and any hybrid you think is doing the real work — each stated sharply enough that I can tell which of my verdicts it licenses. For each candidate, tell me which clause of it is motivated by which case, what it predicts about cases I haven't listed (please invent at least two new residents designed to pull the candidates apart), and — this is the part I actually need — an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would force me to give up. If it turns out that no single principle survives, say which of my judgments is the one I should abandon and why that one rather than another. Finally, tell me whether "free" here is a single predicate at all, or whether my list is really tracking a family of different questions that I've been wrongly forcing into one column.
