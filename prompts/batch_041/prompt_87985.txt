I keep running into a convention among people who study algorithms that I can't make sense of, and I'd like help working out what it quietly assumes. By the convention, an algorithm counts as efficient, or feasible, if its running time grows no faster than some polynomial in the size of its input, and as intractable if it grows exponentially. So an algorithm whose work grows like the thousandth power of the input is officially efficient, while one that grows like 1.0001 raised to the input is officially intractable — even though for any input I could actually hand to a computer, the exponential one would finish first. I understand it's a convenient idealization, but I want to know what it presupposes. Does calling this a sharp line commit its users to there being a real division in nature between tractable and intractable problems, or only to a useful convention, or to the thought that feasibility is relative to the input sizes we happen to care about? Does it presuppose that tractability is a property of the problem itself rather than of us and our machines? And if someone rejected the picture and held that feasibility is irreducibly about concrete resources at concrete sizes, which parts of the practice would actually have to change, and which parts could survive untouched because the convention never really depended on that picture in the first place?
