My old stats professer had a line he was weirdly proud of: "probability doesn't exist." He'd trot it out any time someone asked about the "real" or "true" probability of something, and last week he used it to shut down an arguement in our reading group. Two people were going back and forth about whether a coin that gets flipped exactly once — or tomorrow's rain, or the chance that this specific lopsided handmade die comes up six on its very next throw — even has a probability, in the sense of something that's actually true or false about the world regardless of what anyone thinks. He cut in with "it doesn't exist, it's just in your head, there's no fact of the matter," and everyone nodded like it was profound. I have a nagging feeling that little line is doing like five jobs at once — some boring, some genuinely radical, and at least one maybe just plain false.

Can you break down the different precise things "probability doesn't exist" could be claiming, ordered from the trivially-true to the wild, and for each one say (a) whether it's actually defensible and (b) whether it would really get him his conclusion that there's no fact of the matter about a single case? I know there are supposedly different "interpretations" of probability — the long-run-frequency one, the degree-of-belief one, the disposition/propensity one — but I've never seen anyone line them up against this one slogan. I'd also like to know which readings secretly collapse into each other, which are just false, and which one he probably actually meant. Lay it out as a menu I can bring back to the group, so I can point at the exact reading someone's using when they say it.
