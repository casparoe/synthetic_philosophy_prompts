I have no academic training in history — I spent thirty years as an actuary and now read history and time-series statistics for pleasure — but for the past two years I have been attending the interdisciplinary seminar series at my local university as an outsider, and the convenor has offered me a forty-minute slot next month, followed by about twenty-five minutes of questions, to present a thesis I have been working up on my own. The thesis is this: the large agrarian states of the last two thousand years run a recurring cycle of roughly two to three centuries, and that cycle is a real dynamical feature of those societies rather than a pattern imposed on the record after the fact. The engine, as I see it, is mundane. Population grows against a fixed stock of good land, so rents and land prices climb while real wages sag; at the same time the number of educated aspirants to elite positions grows faster than the number of such positions, so competition within the elite sharpens; state revenue lags behind the state's obligations, so it can neither buy off nor repress the contenders; and the endgame is elite fragmentation on top of mass immiseration, which shows up as civil war, revolution, or collapse. Then the population falls, land is cheap again, and the whole thing restarts. My proxies are deliberately blunt: entries per decade in national biographical dictionaries as a stand-in for the number of aspirant elites, divided by the number of salaried administrative posts, with grain prices and land values as the immiseration proxy. I make two methodological commitments that I expect to be the parts of the talk that get me in trouble. First, I refuse to trust a cycle that appears only after the data have been smoothed; I insist that the period show up on its own in the unsmoothed series and with the phases staggered across those series in the order the mechanism predicts. Second, the mechanism makes its sharpest prediction about dispersion rather than about the average: as a boom matures, the spread of outcomes within the elite should widen, with a few houses consolidating while most aspirants fail, so the signature I am looking for is rising variance rather than a rising mean. I am dimly aware of an old result in the time-series literature to the effect that summing or averaging independent random shocks can manufacture a smooth wave that looks periodic — the name for it escapes me every time — and I want the sharpest available version of that charge aimed at my own series.

The audience will be about twenty-five people, and it will split into two camps that will not use the same words for the same complaints. One camp is social historians and historical sociologists, mostly medievalists and early-modernists, who treat each case as a particular and are allergic to covering laws, to functionalist homeostatic pictures of society, and to anything that sounds like a law of history with error bars attached. The other camp is three or four applied statisticians and econometricians from the economics department who work on time series; they will not care at all about the historical substance and will go straight for the inference. There is also one archaeologist who does radiocarbon dating and will have views about whether any of this survives dating error in the first place. What I want from you is not a defence of the thesis but a preparation sheet for the question period: the eight to ten objections I will actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do, phrased the way the person who will raise them would phrase them, with the historians' objections in the historians' vocabulary and the statisticians' in theirs; for each one, a two-minute reply, an honest note on where that reply is soft, and a clear statement of which points I should concede up front rather than fight. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me why it is the one I am hoping nobody asks.
