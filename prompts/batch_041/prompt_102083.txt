Last week my reading group discussed the Platonia Dilemma, and two members, Mira and Hal, had an argument that the rest of us could not settle. The setup is this: twenty people are each offered a chance to send a telegram bearing only their own name to an institute. If exactly one person sends, that person wins one billion dollars. If two or more send, the prize is voided and nobody wins. If nobody sends, nothing is awarded. None of the twenty can discover who the others are, contact them, or share the prize afterward.

Mira argued that the case vindicates superrationality. Since all twenty are in an identical position, she said, and all are equally rational, they will all reach the same decision; so the only live outcomes are that all twenty send or that none do. Both outcomes pay each person nothing, but only one of them reflects a correct grasp of the situation, and so the rational thing is to abstain. The whole point of recognizing the symmetry, she said, is to see that the reasoning "I'll send, since it can't hurt me" is the very reasoning that guarantees the prize is voided.

Hal said this was wishful thinking. My abstaining does not cause the other nineteen to abstain, he said; each person faces the same individual question, and for each, sending weakly dominates abstaining — it never costs you anything and it sometimes wins you a billion. Worse, he added, universal abstention is not even stable: any one of the twenty could break ranks and send, and would then collect the billion if the rest held to the plan. A recommendation that any single participant has an incentive to abandon is not a solution, he said; it is a fantasy.

Mira replied that the dominance argument is precisely what superrationality rejects. In a symmetric problem, she said, your choice is not independent of everyone else's — it is the same choice, computed by the same reasoning. If "send" is the answer you arrive at, it is the answer all twenty arrive at, and then nobody wins. Recognizing that is what makes abstaining correct; it is not a claim that your telegram moves anyone else's hand.

Hal answered that this shows superrationality is either too strong or too weak. Too strong, because it lets you solve a game by treating your own decision as though it decided everyone else's. Too weak, because here it gives you no reason to prefer abstaining: universalized sending and universalized abstaining pay exactly the same, namely nothing. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, he conceded, superrationality earns its keep, because mutual cooperation beats mutual defection; but in the Platonia Dilemma there is no cooperative payoff to be had, so the machinery has nothing to grip. Calling abstention the superrational answer, he said, is just picking one of two equally worthless outcomes and dignifying it.

Mira said that even so, the analysis clarifies something: the panic that "I must send or forfeit my chance" is an illusion, since sending in the world where everyone sends is worthless. Hal said that if all the analysis shows is that the game has no interesting solution, then that is a defect in the example, not a vindication of superrationality. He added that Hofstadter ran an actual lottery through his magazine column in connection with this discussion, and Mira countered that no experiment about how people happen to behave could settle what it is rational to do.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication. For each of the moves above, say whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or partly right, and why. In particular: is Mira right that symmetry settles what to do here, or is Hal right that it only gives the appearance of settling it? Is Hal right that the instability of universal abstention is decisive? Does the Platonia Dilemma vindicate superrationality, embarrass it, or neither? And does either of them have the better overall position?
