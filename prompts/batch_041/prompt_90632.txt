A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether you can take someone's word for it that a film is good, and we ended with neither of us having moved. His position was that criticism is testimony like any other: if the critic is reliable and the circumstances are ordinary, you can come to know that a film is good without having seen it, and the acquaintance principle — the thought that aesthetic judgment has to rest on first-hand experience — is professional vanity dressed up as epistemology. Mine was that what his reliable critic transmits is at most a prediction about my experience, or a reason to go and have one, and that calling that knowledge of the film's value quietly changes the subject. We also kept sliding between three different claims without ever separating them: that you cannot know the thing is good, that you cannot be justified in believing it, and that you cannot appreciate it or grasp why it is good. I suspect the real debate is almost entirely about the third, and that the first two are downstream of it.

It occurred to me afterwards that the film we should have been arguing about is Ratatouille, and I'd like you to read it through the aesthetic testimony debate — not as an illustration of a position, but as a case that might embarrass one. Some threads I'd want pulled on:

First, Anton Ego's conversion: the meal, and the flashback to his childhood that it triggers. What exactly is transmitted to him there, and by whom? If the ground of his judgment is a private memory that nobody else could have, the film looks like it sides with the pessimists. Or is the scene better read as taste being taught rather than reported — he is brought to notice something, not handed a verdict?

Second, the film's own predicament. Cinema can show us a dish and cannot let us taste it; the whole climax asks us to accept on the film's authority that a plate of vegetables is sublime. Is that an act of aesthetic testimony about a sense the medium cannot deliver, and does it therefore count against pessimism, or is it an unwitting confession of the pessimist's point, that we are being asked to take the value on trust?

Third, Ego's professional life is verdict testimony with causal power: his earlier notice is said to have hastened Gusteau's death, and his later one changes what people will order. The film treats that power as both real and hollow. Does it distinguish testimony that transmits a verdict from testimony that transmits a way of attending — criticism that tells you what to think versus criticism that teaches you how to look? If that distinction holds up, does it rescue the optimist? If it collapses, say so.

Fourth, where the film resists the reading. Remy's authority rests on a better nose, not on anything he could tell anyone; the fantasy seems to replace testimony with a superior perceptual apparatus, and it is worth asking what that substitution implies about the debate. And Gusteau's motto that anyone can cook is a claim about who is entitled to judge, which may be a different question from whether judgment can be passed along.

I'm not writing anything formal, so I don't need references or a reading list — I want the reading itself, with the scenes you would rest it on, and an honest account of where the film cuts against it.

Please push back where I've set this up badly. If my three-way separation of knowledge, justification, and appreciation is the wrong way to carve the debate, or if the acquaintance principle does not claim what I think it does, correct me rather than humoring me; I would rather go back to my friend with a better question than with a polished version of the wrong one. And if you think Ratatouille is simply a poor test case for this, say that plainly and tell me what would be a better one.
