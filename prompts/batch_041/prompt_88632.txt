Whenever the question comes up of how we can be moved by the fate of people who never existed, the same comparison gets used on me: watching a horror film is like riding a roller coaster, because both give you the thrill of danger with none of the danger, and nobody claims the fear on a roller coaster is not real. It is usually offered as the end of the argument — if the coaster fear is genuine, then our feelings about invented characters are genuine too, and the puzzle dissolves.

I would like the comparison assessed rather than repeated. Lay out what corresponds to what — the track, the harness, the drop, the danger, the person riding — then say where the correspondence holds, where it breaks, and which of the breaks actually matter for the conclusion it is being used to support. If it cannot carry that weight, offer a better comparison.

Three things make me suspicious. On a roller coaster something really is happening to me: I am falling, and the danger is real even if it is managed, whereas with a novel nothing whatever is happening except my reading. A jump scare and the top of a coaster's drop both seem to work on the flinch, which is not obviously the same thing as the pity I feel for a character across two hundred pages. And two of my own reactions pull in opposite directions: I felt genuinely cheated when a memoir I had loved turned out to have been invented, which suggests my feelings had been resting on my taking the people to be real, yet my feelings about a novel do not weaken on a second reading, when I know exactly what is coming.
