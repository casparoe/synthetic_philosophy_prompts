I have no academic background in this — I read history and archaeology on my own, mostly in the evenings — and I keep noticing two comparisons that do a great deal of quiet work in arguments about whether societies can really collapse, and I would like you to audit them for me.

The first is biological: a society is like an organism. It is born, it grows, it matures, it hardens, it ages, and it dies. On that picture the end of the Bronze Age states, the abandonment of the Maya cities, and Easter Island are not accidents but the last stage of a life cycle, and the conclusion people draw is that collapse is natural and expectable rather than a scandal. The second is financial or ecological: a collapse is like a bankruptcy, or like a fire that clears a field. The old arrangement is liquidated, the debts are discharged, the ground is cleared, and something new starts up on it, often quickly. Here the conclusion is that nothing really failed — the society changed shape — and that the word collapse does little more than express the regret of whoever lost out.

I want an audit, not a verdict on which comparison is nicer.

First, give me the correspondences explicitly, one comparison at a time. For the organism: what plays the part of the body, the body's boundary, the birth, the aging, the death, the corpse. For the bankruptcy and the fire: the debtor, the creditor, the court, the discharged debt, the cleared ground, the new growth. Where does each comparison already strain against itself, before we even reach the cases?

Second, tell me what each comparison quietly imports. The organism brings a single boundary, a single lifespan, an internal clock of aging, and a death that is terminal and leaves a corpse. The bankruptcy brings a legal order that survives the failure, creditors whose claims are enforceable, and a restart that is genuinely a restart rather than the same people with new stationery. Which of these does the target — a Maya city, a Bronze Age kingdom, an island — actually have, and which are being smuggled in?

Third, separate the mismatches that matter for the conclusion from the ones that are merely colorful. Does the organism comparison survive without its clock and its corpse — that is, can it still support the claim that the end was inevitable? Does the bankruptcy comparison survive without its court and its enforceable claims — that is, can it still support the claim that nothing failed?

Fourth, consider the possibility that the disagreement is not about the cases at all: that failed and merely changed are two descriptions of the same events from two different baselines, and that the comparisons are there to make one baseline feel obvious. If that is so, say what would have to be true of a particular case — who was worse off, how quickly, compared to what — for one description to be the better one, and whether the comparisons can help settle that or only decorate it.

Finally, if you think one side would be better served by a different comparison altogether, propose it and say what it gets right that the organism and the bankruptcy do not.

Please don't give me citations or a reading list; I would have no way to check them, and what I want is the argument from the structure of the comparisons themselves. Write your answer in American English.
