I am a novelist at work on a book whose pivotal scene turns on a parole board hearing, in which a character must decide what to make of a statistician's evidence about the risk that a particular prisoner will reoffend. To get the texture right I have been reading around the philosophy of probability, and a friend of mine — a competent populariser, though not a specialist — has offered me the following summary of something he calls the reference class problem, which he says is the deep reason such evidence is always shaky. His account runs roughly like this: 'To assign a probability to a single event — whether this prisoner reoffends, whether this patient recovers — you must first place the individual in a class of similar cases and read off the frequency. But every individual belongs to indefinitely many classes: all prisoners, all prisoners of that age, all prisoners of that age with that offence history, and so on; and these classes yield different frequencies, so the choice of class, and therefore the probability itself, is arbitrary. This shows that frequentist probability simply cannot be applied to the single case and ought to be abandoned, and that all genuine probability must be subjective — a personal degree of belief. It is also why statistical evidence is always weaker than direct evidence about the individual, as the prosecutor's fallacy illustrates. The remedy, when one must proceed, is to choose the narrowest reference class available — the most specific one — so that as many relevant features as possible are held fixed.'

I should be grateful if you would audit this for me. I am not a philosopher and cannot tell how much of it I may safely put in the mouth of a character who is meant to know what he is talking about. Please go through it claim by claim: tell me what a philosopher of probability would accept as fair, what they would wince at as a common simplification, and what is straightforwardly mistaken; say which of the claims are doing independent work and which have been run together; and tell me whether the conclusion he draws — that frequentism must be given up and that only subjective priors can rescue us — actually follows from the parts that survive. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question, and please use British spelling throughout.
