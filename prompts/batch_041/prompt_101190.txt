For some years now I have been reading philosophy in earnest. I am a retired experimental biologist of sixty-eight, and I came to these questions late, so I am old enough to distrust my own amateur enthusiasm and stubborn enough to keep asking anyway. What has preoccupied me lately is the hinge-of-history hypothesis. Stated plainly, it is the claim that history does not matter evenly: that there are particular moments at which a small number of people, making particular choices within a short window, determine a disproportionate share of the value of the whole subsequent future; and, in its stronger form, the further claim that we ourselves may be standing at just such a moment. I want to test this hypothesis rather than affirm it, and I want to test it against a real episode rather than an invented case.

The episode is the Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA, held in February 1975 on the Monterey Peninsula, together with the moratorium that preceded it. The bare facts, in case you need them fixed: after Paul Berg and others had worked out how to join DNA from different organisms, a group of biologists—Berg and Maxine Singer among the organizers—called publicly, in 1974, for a voluntary halt to certain experiments until the hazards could be assessed; roughly a hundred and forty scientists, with a few lawyers and physicians, then met at Asilomar and produced a set of containment guidelines that were subsequently adopted and that shaped the regulation of biotechnology for decades. The actors were, by any ordinary standard, a small group; the window was short; and they were plainly aware that they might be deciding something large.

I would like you to analyze that episode through the hinge-of-history hypothesis and to run the analysis in both directions—the hypothesis upon the episode, and the episode back upon the hypothesis. I am asking for a disciplined case analysis, not a narrative of what happened, and not a sermon either for or against longtermism.

To keep the analysis honest, I want you to use two distinctions I have been turning over, which you should treat as my own proposals and feel free to criticize. The first is between a moment that is pivotal and one that is merely prominent: a moment is pivotal only if the counterfactual divergence is large—the world that follows from the branch not taken differs enormously in value from the world that follows from the branch actually taken—and only if the untaken branch was genuinely live, that is, had a non-negligible probability of being taken. A decision that could never realistically have gone the other way is prominent but not pivotal, however dramatic it looks in hindsight. The second is the distinction between an open hinge and a closed hinge: an open hinge is a juncture whose participants knew they stood at a choice and could deliberate about it; a closed hinge is one that can be identified as pivotal only from the far side, by historians, and that no one at the time experienced as a decision point. I suspect the hinge-of-history hypothesis, as usually stated, quietly presupposes open hinges, and that this is a weakness.

With those tools in hand, I want you to answer the following. Who are the players, on the hypothesis's own terms, and what were the actual options and payoffs—was there a live alternative branch, an indefinite or permanent moratorium on one side or unregulated research on the other, and how live was it? Run the pivotal-versus-prominent test on the episode and give me your verdict, with reasons. Say where the hypothesis illuminates the episode—what it makes salient that an ordinary history of science would not—and where it misdescribes or overreaches, forcing the episode into a shape it does not have. Say what the episode in turn shows about the hypothesis: whether it confirms it, counts as an anomaly against it, or reveals a case the hypothesis never anticipated—for instance, that the actors were less exercising power over the future than deferring to an unknown one. And attend to the epistemic gap that troubles me most: the hypothesis evaluates moments by the magnitude of what was at stake, while the actors could know almost nothing of what was at stake. Tell me what follows for whether such moments can be identified and acted upon from inside them, which is the only place anyone ever stands.

Close by saying whether the episode leaves the hinge-of-history hypothesis stronger or weaker, and be willing to say that it is silent on something rather than forcing it to speak. You may draw on whatever you know of the episode, but do not pad the answer with citations, dates, or reading lists; I want the argument, and I would rather you reason carefully from the facts I have given than gesture at a literature.
