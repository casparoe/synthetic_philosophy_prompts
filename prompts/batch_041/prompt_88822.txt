I'm prepping a grad seminar on explanation in history, and I've got a colleague who's turned into a bit of a bore about the chain-of-causation picture. His line goes: causes are links in a chain, each link pulls the next, you trace it back to the first link, and if any link had been missing the whole thing falls apart. From this he concludes there's no such thing as a genuinely overdetermined historical event -- when something looks like it had three seperate causes, each sufficient on its own, that just means we haven't traced the chain properly, and there's always one link doing the real work.

I want to knock this down without retreating into the "web of causation" hand-wave, which always sounds to me like giving up while claiming to have said something deep. So: audit the chain picture for me. Start with the correspondence table -- what's meant to play the role of the links, the first link, the weakest link, the load -- then where the mapping breaks. And be explicit about which of the breaks actually matter for the arguement he's using the picture to make, and which are just decorative.

Here's a case to test it on. A regime collapses inside a fortnight because (i) the harvest failed and bread riots spread, (ii) the garrison refused to fire on the crowds, and (iii) the finance minister fled with the treasury and the state couldn't pay anyone. Take each of those in turn and say what the chain picture can and can't say about it. What I really want to know is whether the metaphor can even represent a case where none of the items on the list was necessary but the outcome was still causally produced -- my suspicion is that a chain is a conjunction of necessary links by construction, so it's rigged from the start and his "conclusion" is just the metaphor read back out to him. Tell me if that's right, or if I'm being unfair.

Second thing: is there a better picture? If the honest answer is that you need two or three different ones -- something like redundant systems for one kind of case, converging tributaries for another, and maybe a third for whatever's left over -- because "overdetermination" gets used for structurally different things, then say so and tell me which case needs which. Bring in the standard distinction between two causes that each suffice and one cause that pre-empts another if it earns its keep, but only if it earns its keep.

Don't bother with reading lists or who said what, I know the literature. I want the argument. British English, please, and don't be shy about telling me where I'm wrong.
