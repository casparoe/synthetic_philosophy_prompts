I've spent the last few months reporting on a fight in a mid-sized city — call it Fairbrook — where the county historical society and a neighborhood association have been going at each other for years over what happened at the old foundry back in 1919. The historical society has the paper: payroll ledgers, a coroner's inquest, two newspaper write-ups that basically agree with each other. The neighborhood's account, handed down orally for four generations, has a different death toll, a different date, and a different villain. The city council, trying to keep the peace, passed what everyone here just calls the Records Parity Ordinance: on the historical marker and in the eighth-grade unit, the archival account and the oral account get equal billing, and neither one gets labeled "the true one." A philosopher I interviewed for the piece loved the whole thing and gave me a phrase I can't get out of my head — "mnemonic sovereignty," the idea that a community's memory is the final authority over its own past, and that the archive is just another interested party with better filing cabinets. A historian I interviewed called that same phrase the most high-minded way anyone has ever found to say "whatever we say is true."

I don't want to write a he-said/she-said piece — I've definately done enough of those. I want to write the thing they should have written together. So: draft the joint statement that an archive-first documentary historian and a memory-studies scholar could both actually sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration, over this case and over the general question behind it — when a community's memory and the documentary record conflict, which one gives way? Give me the claims both would put their names to, including the hard ones: is there a fact of the matter about 1919 at all, or only competing constructions; does the provenance of a claim (who carried it, and why, and for whom) matter to whether it's warranted, or is it only the evidence; does a community have a kind of epistemic standing over its own past that outsiders lack; and what the marker and the curriculum should say given whatever they settle. I suspect two of the cruxes are whether a group can be flatly wrong about its own past, and whether "memory" and "history" are even answering the same question — if part of this fight turns out to be verbal, say which part and why. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section, then a table of the surviving disagreements with each side's position written in wording that side would accept for itself, then the cruxes; under each crux, give a case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction about it in advance, so neither side can move the goalposts after the fact.

Also — please don't hang any of this on citations. I don't need a reading list, and honestly I'd rather have the shape of an example than a possibly-misremembered name. Types of cases are fine: the kind where an oral tradition later got corroborated by a document or a dig, and the kind where a vivid communal memory turned out to be a late invention. That contrast is exactly what I want them to fight over productively.
