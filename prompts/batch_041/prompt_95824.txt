Ok, bit of an odd brief for you. I'm a journalist — I write features about science and ideas, not an academic — and I've somehow ended up agreeing to run a 75-minute workshop at a small ideas festival. About 25 people in the room, mixed crowd, a handful have read some philosophy, most are there for the free wine and are allergic to being lectured at. The organsers have asked me to do the Jain idea that reality is many-sided, anekāntavāda, and I want to build the whole session as a game or a structured deliberation rather than me standing up and explaining it for twenty minutes. I've watched that format die too many times.

Here's the stuff I want them to actually feel rather than just hear, because I don't trust them — or honestly me — to already have it straight. The Jains weren't saying it's all a matter of opinion. They thought there's a fact of the matter, but that any single statement about it is true only from a standpoint and only in a respect. So they'd have you hedge every claim, and they had a scheme where you run through the options: in some sense it is, in some sense it isn't, in some sense it both is and isn't, and then versions of those with "can't be put into words at all" tacked on. There's also a classic split between looking at a thing as the persisting stuff and looking at it as the passing shape — the clay vs. the pot, the riverbed vs. the water — where each angle hands you a different and apparently contradictory truth. And the bit I find most interesting and hardest to get across: the Jains thought their own view was the complete one, not just one more partial take sitting alongside the rest. So it isn't relativism, and the obvious heckle — fine, then your own doctrine is only partly true too — has an answer I want people to feel the bite of.

So design me the activity. It should (a) get them into a genuine disagreement over some concrete case where both sides are right in a respect — I keep thinking about whether a neighbourhood is still the same neighbourhood after it gentrifies, or whether a river is the same river once it's dammed, but use whatever case you think will actually land; (b) force them, by the rules, to hedge every claim and to try to work all the way through the is / isn't / inexpressible combinations, so they feel how the scheme strains; and (c) leave them with the uncomfortable realisation that "everyone's a bit right" is a cop-out the view itself doesn't license.

Give me the rules, materials, roles if you need them, a rough timeline, and the debrief — mapping what happened onto the actual ideas, what'll predictably go wrong the first time I run it (my guess: someone says it's all subjective and everybody nods, or they treat the hedging as weasel words and get annoyed), and an honest note on which part of anekāntavāda the activity simply can't reproduce and I'll have to say out loud myself. No reading lists or citations please, I'm not putting a biblography on a handout.
