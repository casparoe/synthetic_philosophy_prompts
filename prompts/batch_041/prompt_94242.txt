I work as an analyst at a small conflict-monitoring organisation — five researchers, no lawyers, no library — and my main product is a weekly note, for people in foreign ministries, on what the leadership of one particular armed group believes. My raw material is recordings and transcripts of that group's public statements, sermons and interviews, in a language I read and hear at an intermediate level, almost always with a partner from the region who translates and who is often, though not always, right; my editor gives my draft twenty minutes and rewrites my hedges into headlines; the note goes out under my name. I have no access to the group's own doctrinal texts and no time to read up on the scholarly literature. But I read enough of Davidson years ago to believe that his account of radical interpretation is the closest thing anyone has written to my actual job: I must assign meanings and beliefs simultaneously, to people whose language and situation I only partly share, under deadline, and I cannot fix one side of that pair before the other. What I want from you is not a summary of Davidson. I want the account turned into a procedure I can run every Thursday afternoon on whatever is in the folder — the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, and the point at which the procedure stops and tells me that I cannot yet say what they believe, rather than producing a verdict anyway.

The procedure has to survive four things that come up constantly and that I have no principled way of handling now. First, denials I believe are false: a spokesperson says an operation did not happen and the satellite imagery says otherwise — I write 'the group denies', my editor writes 'the group falsely claims', and I want to know whether the charitable constraint bears on this case at all, and specifically whether treating the words as coherent is the same thing as treating the speaker as sincere; I suspect it is not, but I cannot say why in a way I could defend to my editor. Second, apparent contradictions across time: the same leadership says one thing about women in public life in March and something that looks like its negation in June, and I cannot tell whether to record a change of belief, a change of audience, or my own mistranslation of a term whose extension I have not pinned down. Third, content I cannot bring myself to believe: statements about the efficacy of a curse, or about what the blood of a martyr secures, where taking the speaker at her word seems to require me to hold a great many of her other beliefs false. Fourth, and worst, the case where my partner's translation and my own hearing of the recording both fit everything I know and differ in what they make the leadership believe — I have been writing the one that fits the story my editors already tell, and I am not sure that is a decision I am entitled to make silently. The procedure should also say what to do about the asymmetry of information: on local matters the source is usually better placed than I am, and I want to know whether that should pull my interpretation toward her claims even when they conflict with the briefing pack I have been handed.

Run the procedure on three of these cases — the denial, the apparent contradiction, and the curse — and be explicit about the one where it gives an answer I will not like, since I would rather learn that from you than from a diplomat. It should also say where it cannot be run at all: the point at which I should stop interpreting and simply predict, and the point at which it hands me a choice that is mine rather than the material's. Be committed rather than even-handed: pick one procedure, commit to an ordering of the steps, and where the procedure underdetermines the case, say which way you would go, why, and how confident you are. Structure the answer in four named sections. First, The Procedure: the ordered steps, each with what counts as a sufficient answer, each marked as either a constraint on interpretation or a judgement call, and ending with a short list of sentences I should be forbidden to write in the note, with the reason for each — roughly 800 words. Second, Worked Runs: the three cases above, roughly 300 words each, and end each run with the exact sentence I should be permitted to publish. Third, Where It Gives Out: the cases the procedure cannot settle, the misfires I should expect, and what changes when the source is deliberately deceptive or when what looks like assertion is ritual — roughly 250 words. Fourth, Bottom Line: a short committed statement of what the procedure implies for how I should write these notes, with your confidence stated plainly.
