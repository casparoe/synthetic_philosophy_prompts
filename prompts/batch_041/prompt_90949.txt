I have no background in philosophy or economics, but I've been thinking on my own for a while about how people weigh the present against the future, and I've arrived at a distinction that I suspect is doing a lot of hidden work. When I say I care less about something the further ahead it is, I think I'm actually blending two different reasons. One is simply that the thing lies further away in time, and I'm impatient. The other is that the thing might never happen at all, or might never happen to anyone, so it makes sense to weight it by how likely it is to occur. I've been calling the first reason impatience and the second uncertainty, and I've convinced myself that they are not the same kind of reason: uncertainty can be argued about by pointing to evidence and probabilities, whereas impatience looks like either a raw preference that cannot be criticized at all or a mistake that can be corrected.

I've also noticed something about my own impatience that puzzles me. It seems to attach to whether my own experience continues rather than to time as such. If I imagine a benefit landing on me in thirty years, I discount it heavily, and part of why is that I am not sure the "me" who receives it is really me. But if I imagine the same benefit landing on a stranger in thirty years, or on people who have not been born yet, I do not feel the same pull to discount it. That makes me wonder whether impatience is really about time or about the continuity of a particular self, and what happens when the question is scaled up from one person to a whole society.

Please describe the most important implications of taking impatience and uncertainty to be genuinely separate reasons, both for how I should weigh my own future and for how a society should weigh policies whose costs fall on people alive now and whose benefits fall on people who will not exist for thousands of years. I am most interested in the implications that would change how someone thinks who has only ever heard the whole issue discussed as "the discount rate," a single number to be chosen. In particular, I want to know whether the far-future side of such a policy debate really does collapse into nothing once any impatience at all is allowed, and whether my suspicion that impatience is about self-continuity rather than time means the social case is not simply the personal case writ large. I do not need a reading list or names to look up; I want the substance of the implications themselves. Please answer in American English.
