I chair the clinical ethics committee at a teaching hospital, and next week we must decide a case that I expect to divide us. What I want from you is not a general treatment of advance directives but a recommendation that keeps faith with our own record, and, if that record cannot settle the matter, a clear statement of the principle we would be adopting by going either way.

Here is the record. A man with advanced ALS who had lived with the diagnosis for three years executed a directive refusing mechanical ventilation; when he came in with respiratory failure, unable to speak and not in evident distress, we honored it and he died on the ward. A man of twenty-nine, in ordinary health, had signed a directive refusing all life-sustaining treatment whatever his condition; five years later he was admitted with a treatable meningitis, we overrode the directive and treated him, and he thanked us afterwards. A woman with early Alzheimer's, who had spent six years nursing her mother through the same illness, wrote a directive refusing antibiotics, hospitalization, and artificial feeding once she no longer knew her family; when she reached that point, comfortable and making no objection, we declined to place a feeding tube, over the objection of her brother. A man with a long history of severe depression, who had undergone electroconvulsive therapy before and knew what it involved, inserted a clause instructing us to disregard any later refusal of that treatment; when he later refused it, we treated him, and he was glad of it. A woman on dialysis for end-stage renal disease, who detested the treatment, refused further sessions; we honored that.

The case before us is a woman of seventy-one with moderate dementia. At sixty-two, after nursing her sister through Alzheimer's to the end, she executed a detailed directive refusing antibiotics, hospitalization, and artificial nutrition and hydration once she stopped recognizing her husband, whom she named as her proxy. She added a sentence in her own hand: that if she ever seemed to want to eat or drink, we should remember that this was the illness speaking, and that her real wishes were the ones written in the document. She is now on our unit, no longer recognizes her husband, and has aspiration pneumonia. She appears to be in no pain; she eats with apparent pleasure when fed, smiles at the aides, and seems at home in the place. Twice in the past month, during a cold, she said clearly that she did not want to die, and once asked to be taken to the hospital; her capacity to make this particular decision is disputed. Her husband, the named proxy, holds that the directive governs and wants comfort care only. Her son, the alternate proxy, holds that a course of antibiotics would return her to a life she is visibly enjoying, and that it would be wrong to let her die of something so easily treated.

Tell me how we should decide if we mean to be consistent with ourselves, which of the earlier cases is the closest analog, which other rules fit our record just as well as the one you favor, and, if the record genuinely underdetermines this case, what principle each course of action would commit us to. I would also like to know which further case, one we have not yet had, would best tell those rules apart. I am not asking for case law or a review of the literature; I want the structure of the argument, in continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
