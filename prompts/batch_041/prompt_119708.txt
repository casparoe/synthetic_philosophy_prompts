The paradox of deontology is usually put like this. Deontology says you must not violate a right even if doing so would prevent more violations of the same right. But deontology also seems to say that more violations are worse than fewer. So if you can violate one person's right to prevent five violations, the constraint tells you not to, while the badness of violations tells you to. The view looks either inconsistent or fetishistic -- it treats the one as if she were more than one.

I want to try a different move. The paradox arises because we treat constraints as if they were rules in a maximizing system -- rules that tell an agent what to do with the world's stock of violations. But constraints are not about the world's stock. They are about the agent's jurisdiction. A constraint marks the boundary of what an agent is authorized to do in the name of the good. It does not say never let a violation happen; it says never be the one who does this. So when I refuse to violate one to prevent five, I am not saying that one violation is worse than five. I am saying that the five are not mine to prevent in that way. The numbers still matter, but they matter for other agents, or for me in other roles -- as a legislator, a rescuer, a planner -- not as the bearer of this constraint.

This is not just a relabeling if we take jurisdiction seriously. Jurisdiction has two features. First, it is role-relative: the same person can have different permissions as a doctor, a citizen, a friend. Second, it is bounded by what I call the renegotiation condition: if my jurisdiction is too narrow to address a catastrophe, I may have a duty to try to change my jurisdiction -- to ask for authorization, to create new institutions, to step into a different role -- rather than to simply violate the constraint. The paradox then becomes a practical problem about institutional design, not a contradiction in the moral theory.

The hardest case is the one where no renegotiation is possible: a lone agent, no institutions, five lives versus one. Here I want to say the constraint still holds, but the agent may be permitted to act if the alternative is that the constraint itself becomes a suicide pact. That sounds like a threshold, and maybe it is. But the threshold is not a maximizing threshold; it is a threshold of role collapse -- the point at which the role that carries the constraint can no longer be sustained. I'm not sure this works. It might just be a fancy way of smuggling in consequentialism at the limit.
