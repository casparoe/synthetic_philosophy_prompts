I have read rationalist blogs for long enough to have absorbed one suspicion in particular: when a procedure needs to know who counts, and the answer is that whoever counts decides who counts, the procedure has smuggled its conclusion in at step zero. The boundary problem is that suspicion in its purest form, and until this year I was content to enjoy it from a safe distance.

I now chair the registration appeals panel of a newly created combined authority: a city-region with a directly elected mayor, a standing citizens' assembly with a capital budget of its own, and, since April, a registrar of members. The registrar's initial policy is to inherit the parliamentary register, which nobody defends and everybody uses. We sit weekly, hear about forty written appeals a month, and must dispose of each in half an hour on the papers; three of us decide (myself, a solicitor, a lay member drawn by lot), a precedent binds us for a decade unless we overturn it unanimously, and everything we do has to survive judicial review and a hostile local paper. The assembly has asked for a general procedure rather than case-by-case improvisation. Please write it: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the matter to the assembly because the question is one of values rather than facts.

Three commitments I want the procedure to take seriously, because each is where I think the usual treatments cheat. First, the franchise is not one thing. Being counted in the constituency, voting, standing for office, petitioning, triggering a referendum, having standing to challenge a decision, being taxed, and being bound are separable incidents; a test that is defensible for one may be indefensible for another. Build the procedure incident by incident, and say where one test can honestly serve several. Second, two principles are in play and they disagree: the affected-interests principle, on which anyone whose interests are significantly affected by a decision has a claim to a proportionate say, and the subjected-persons principle, on which only those bound by the authority's rules have a claim to make them. Say which principle each step is using, and what we are to do when they conflict at the level of the answer we give an appellant, not in the abstract. Third, I have a half-formed test I would like you either to operationalise or to kill: give the vote to whoever bears the residual risk of the decision going wrong, the party left holding the loss once the specific beneficiaries have been paid. It has the pleasing property of tracking who actually pays for a mistake. I suspect it also hands the vote to landlords and creditors and takes it from the transient poor, which I will accept if it is right and not otherwise. Tell me whether it survives contact with the cases, and if it does, where it enters the procedure.

The cases that keep arriving, which the procedure must handle without special pleading:

- a man with a British passport who has not lived in the region for twenty-two years, pays no local tax, owns a flat here which he lets out, and votes in national elections;
- a woman who has lived and worked here for fourteen years, pays council tax, has two children in local schools, and holds a visa with no route to settlement;
- commuters from the neighbouring district who use the region's hospitals and trains, are governed elsewhere, and whose council has asked to be represented;
- a sixteen-year-old who turns eighteen before the assembly's term ends;
- an adult under guardianship whose capacity her brother disputes;
- a developer who owns about a third of the land in one ward and lives abroad;
- the people who will be born here during the decade over which the assembly is issuing debt;
- an association of former residents who have moved away but still own property and meet once a year.

Run the procedure on three of these, at least one where the answer will annoy me, and finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do and where I should stop pretending it is one. I do not need a survey of the literature; I have read enough of it to know that the honest answer is a procedure with the value judgements left visible.
