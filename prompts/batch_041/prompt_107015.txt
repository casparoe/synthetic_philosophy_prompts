A friend, working from the asymmetry in the antinatalism debate, has handed me the following argument, which I cannot refute and which seems to prove far too much. (1) If a being had never existed, the absence of its pains would be good and the absence of its pleasures would not be bad, since there would be no one deprived. (2) If a being does exist, its pains are bad and its pleasures are good. (3) So for any being, non-existence is at least as good as existence, and usually better. (4) Now imagine a world in which each sentient being's existence is renewed instant by instant, so that at every instant there is a fact about whether it exists at the next. (5) Applying (1) through (3) to each renewal: at each instant, the being's not existing at the next instant is at least as good as its existing then. (6) So at every instant it is at least as good, and usually better, for the being to cease to exist; hence in that world, and by parity in ours, every sentient being has at every moment a reason to die, and the happiest life is a mistake. (7) But that is absurd: the death of a happy person is a loss. Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I would most like to keep (1) and (2). Answer with a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, and no introduction or conclusion.
