I need help with an argument, and the context matters, so I will give it first.

I recently left a philosophy PhD program for a role at a small research nonprofit, where I have been helping draft our internal charter. The charter is deliberately indirect. It does not commit us to a list of values. Instead it says that when we face a hard call, the right answer is the one we would endorse if we were fully informed about the relevant facts, free of self-serving bias, and fully coherent in our reasoning, with as much time to deliberate as the question requires. The indirection is the point: we do not trust ourselves to state our commitments correctly in advance, so we let the idealization state them for us.

A colleague who thinks the whole approach is bankrupt has been circulating the argument below, and I have been asked to write our reply. I have read it a dozen times and every step still looks fine to me.

(1) An act is right if and only if it would be endorsed by a deliberator who is fully informed and fully coherent.
(2) Idealizing a deliberator along those dimensions improves her beliefs and makes her attitudes mutually consistent. It does not give her final ends. A deliberator's final ends are inputs to her deliberation, not outputs of it.
(3) So an ideal deliberator's verdicts are fixed by her final ends together with the facts. Idealization tells her which means serve those ends, and which ends can be held together, but not which ends to have.
(4) Nothing in the idealization privileges one set of final ends over another, so any coherent set of final ends could be the end-set of a fully informed, fully coherent deliberator.
(5) Hence for any coherent set of final ends E there is a fully informed, fully coherent deliberator whose verdicts are fixed by E; and by (1), whatever such a deliberator endorses is right.
(6) Consider, then, a deliberator whose final ends are appalling but internally consistent — say, whose only final end is that a particular group of people suffer. She is fully informed and fully coherent.
(7) By (1) and (5), what she endorses is right; and since there is equally an ideal deliberator with the opposite ends, what she rejects is right too.
(8) So (1) leaves nothing right or wrong except relative to an arbitrary end-set, and an account on which nothing is right or wrong is not an account of rightness. Therefore (1) is false, and our charter with it.

I am committed to (1), and I cannot accept (8): the indirection is the only reason the charter works at all, and I do not believe that a genuinely ideal deliberator could endorse atrocity. So which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me?
