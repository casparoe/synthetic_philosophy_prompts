Last week our council's environment committee voted down a pilot scheme for a new biodegradable pesticide on the allotments. The chair's main reason, repeated about six times, was 'better safe than sorry'. I have to write up the minutes, and I'm definately not happy with just recording that as if it settles anything. Can you seperate out the precise claims that phrase could be making in this context? I want the full menu, from the trivial don't-take-stupid-risks reading, through the weak precautionary principle (lack of full certainty isn't a reason to delay), to the strong one (lack of evidence of safety is itself a reason to prohibit), to something like a maximin rule or a presumption in favour of the status quo. For each reading, say what kind of claim it is -- epistemic, decision-theoretic, moral, procedural -- and whether it's plausible. Then tell me which of those readings would actually justify blocking this particular pilot, and which are just smuggling in a preference for not changing anything. Also, is the chair's move just ambiguity aversion in disguise -- treating an unknown chance as if it were a known bad chance -- and is that a bias or a rational response to genuine uncertainty? And one councillor keeps saying the precautionary principle is self-defeating because being too cautious can cause harm too; she calls it the precautionary paradox. Is she right, and if so, which reading of 'better safe than sorry' does that undermine? I need this as a list I can bring to the next meeting, not an essay. Please write it in British English.
