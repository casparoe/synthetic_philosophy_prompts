Hegel's master–slave dialectic treats self-consciousness as something achieved only through a struggle for recognition between two self-conscious beings, and it is commonly read as showing that the master's victory yields a hollow, one-sided recognition while the slave, through labor and the shaping of the object, attains a self-relation the master cannot. Set against this a notion I will call impersonal recognition: recognition conferred not by another subject but by a system that is not itself a subject — a market that prices your labor, a bureaucracy that certifies you, a scoring algorithm that rates you. Compare the two. Which moving parts of the dialectic — the life-and-death struggle, the asymmetry of dependence, the constitutive role of labor and the object, the defectiveness of one-sided recognition — carry over to impersonal recognition, and which presuppose a recognizing subject and so cannot? Is impersonal recognition a coherent notion at all, or does it collapse into a mere description of how a system happens to treat you? Assess whether it adds explanatory power that the dialectic lacks or is already subsumed by it, and be explicit about where you are extrapolating beyond Hegel rather than interpreting him. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among readers of Hegel, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
