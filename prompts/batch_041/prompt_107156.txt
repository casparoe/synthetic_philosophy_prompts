I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and this autumn I'm running a seventy-five-minute session at a small ideas festival — roughly two dozen adults, no philosophy in their backgrounds, seated at four or five tables. I want the session to be an activity rather than a talk: I have one shot at making a room of intelligent strangers feel the force of the old puzzle of choice without preference, and I would much rather they walk into it than be told about it.

Design a game or structured deliberation in which the participants themselves get stuck on a decision that is, by construction, perfectly symmetrical — and stuck in a way that follows from the incentives I have set rather than from a trick of my staging. The stakes should be real enough to matter (a prize, a title, bragging rights, a small token) but never money. Nobody should hear the phrase "Buridan's ass" or the word "equipoise" until the debrief. The activity should run in three movements: first, an exact tie in which the payoffs are identical and the participants discover that nothing in the rules tells them what to do; second, a chance for the group to supply its own resolution — a coin, a rule, a convention, a fiat — and a moment to notice that whatever they supply comes from outside the ranking of outcomes; third, a round in which the arbitrary resolution they invented has hardened into a stable convention that a newcomer would simply have to obey. Give me the rules, the materials, the payoff scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the specific things I should say (and refrain from saying) at each transition. What I want them to come away seeing is that a complete ranking of outcomes plus a rule to maximize is still not enough to act, and that every real resolution comes from somewhere other than the ranking itself.

The debrief is where the ideas have to land, and I want it to map what they just did onto several genuinely distinct positions rather than resolving to a single moral. Work these in where the moments fit instead of announcing them as a list. Buridan's own view was not the starvation story that later writers pinned on him; he held that when two courses are judged equal the will cannot break the deadlock and can only suspend judgment until circumstances change — so one thing the group should be able to see is that their paralysis is a position, not a failure to think. Al-Ghazali, considering a man offered two equally good dates, thought the will could break the stalemate through a faculty whose office is precisely to differentiate between things that are alike. Leibniz denied that perfect equipoise ever occurs, on the grounds that we are always moved by perceptions too faint to notice, which is worth raising only after the group has watched itself find a reason it did not know it had. Spinoza's reply is deflationary and worth keeping in reserve: someone who really did starve in such a situation should be counted an ass rather than a man. Bayle's point is that the artificial examples miss the mark, because we make choice-without-preference decisions all day and think nothing of it.

Two further elements belong in the debrief, because they are the parts a general audience will not have met. One is the engineering analogue: a digital latch given an input that is neither high nor low can hang in a metastable state, and designers treat the hazard as real and handle it with synchronizing circuitry that gives the undecided state time to settle, rather than pretending it cannot occur — a good way of showing that the tie is a practical problem with practical fixes and not merely a philosopher's fiction. The other is that the two classic escapes from the paradox, stopping at the first option that is good enough rather than the best, and choosing by an explicit randomizing device, each buy the resolution at a price; I want the group to feel what that price is.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this in a real room — the table that refuses to treat the options as truly equal, the participant who announces the answer in the first three minutes, the group that will not accept a coin — and how to handle each without tipping my hand. And be honest with me about what the activity cannot reproduce: what part of the problem I will have to supply myself, in words, once the game has ended.
