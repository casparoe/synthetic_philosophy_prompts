So I'm a novelist — few books out, midlist, the kind of career where the advance just about covers a nice rug — and I've started doing ghostwriting on the side to pay the bills. Here's the thing that's been naging at me.

Last year I wrote an entire memoir for a guy you'd recognize from TV. I did the interviews, I wrote every sentence, I probably know more about his childhood than his own mother does. My name appears nowhere on the book. The copyright page says the book is his. Fine, that was the deal, I signed it, and there's a clause in the contract saying the thing was made for hire and that I waive any claim to being its author.

But then a friend who works at a publisher told me they can't get copyright on a book written by an AI, no matter how good the output is, because there's no human author behind it — and no body can tell me where the line is, except that if a human "substantially edits" the AI draft then maybe there's a copyright in the human's contribution. Meanwhile there are these book packagers who've been churning out series novels for decades where the writer is basically an employee and the company is the author of record.

So the same industry that won't let a machine be an author will happily let a TV presenter, or a corporation, or a brand be one, even when they didn't write a word. What view of authorship is that whole arrangement actually resting on? Is "author" in publishing just a legal slot for whoever owns the thing, with the romantic idea of the writer-as-creator as basicly decoration? Or is there a real theory underneath that I'm missing? And does the practice actually need that theory, or would a few different theories license exactly the same set of arrangements?

Then the part I really can't get my head around: if you swapped in a labour view — the person who does the writing owns it — what breaks? Does the industry fall over, or is the current setup just one way of doing it? And is the AI thing a genuinely different problem, or the same problem wearing a hat?
