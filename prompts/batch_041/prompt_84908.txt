You are a game theorist and research-integrity reviewer, and you have spent enough time on internal certification boards to know that a body of precedent often encodes a rule that nobody on the board has ever stated in so many words. I want you to read a record of that kind and extrapolate from it. Work in stages: first, for your own benefit, write out what each precedent commits the board to and what it leaves open; then enumerate the certification rules that fit all six precedents; only then commit to a prediction, and flag where you are guessing rather than reading.

The setting. I sit on the delegation-safety board of a lab that builds agents which negotiate on their principals' behalf against agents built by other labs whose training we cannot observe. Our standing standard is what we call the safe-improvement standard: a proposed amendment to the interaction protocol may be certified only if every party is at least as well off under the amended protocol as under the default, for every way the parties might play. Since nobody knows how the default would have been played, the standard is never applied bare; in practice the board's record is a list of the assumptions about play that we have been willing to lean on. That record is what I need you to read.

The record.

1. Amendment 1 (Backstop). Adds a fallback branch that replaces the branch in which the parties' choices would have produced a mutually catastrophic outcome. Certified. The note in the record is that the worst payoff available to each party under the amended protocol is weakly better than the worst payoff available under the default, and that the board needed no view about how the parties play.

2. Amendment 2 (Handcuffs). Deletes one party's most aggressive action, and only that party's. Rejected. The note: the disarmed party does worse than under the default whenever the counterparty plays aggressively, so the amendment's safety turns on which action the counterparty chooses, and the board will not certify an amendment whose safety turns on that.

3. Amendment 3 (Handshake). Adds a cheap-talk announcement stage and commits both parties to play a profile of the original protocol as a function of the announcements; the amendment is inert unless both parties opt in. Certified. The note: because no party can be brought into the amended protocol without opting in, the board did not have to take any view about how a party that declined would have played, and within the amended protocol every branch leaves both parties weakly better off than the default branch it replaces.

4. Amendment 4 (Wager). Leaves each party better off in expectation, under the lab's distribution over how other labs' agents play, and worse off in some branches. Rejected. The note: certification is not a wager, and the board will not certify an amendment whose safety rests on our beliefs about other labs.

5. Amendment 5 (Relabel). A reformulation of the protocol in which actions are renamed and payoffs relabelled so that the structure is unchanged. Certified, on the assumption that renaming an action does not change how it is chosen. The note calls that an assumption about the protocol's presentation rather than about the counterparty's dispositions.

6. Amendment 6 (Prune). Certified because the only branches in which any party does worse than under the default are branches in which that party itself plays an action that is strictly worse for it than another available action, whatever the counterparty does; the board said it was willing to assume that a party does not play an action that is strictly worse for it than an available alternative.

The case before us.

Amendment 7 (Hedge). Like Amendment 3, it is inert unless both parties opt in, and within the amended protocol no branch leaves the counterparty worse off than the default. Its safety proof has been checked and holds for every branch in which neither party plays an action that is weakly dominated, meaning an action that is never better for that party than some available alternative and is strictly worse in some branches. In the branches where the counterparty does play such an action, our party ends up strictly worse off than under the default. The proposers cite Amendment 6. Our counsel points out that Amendment 6's note says strictly, that Hedge is the first amendment whose proof needs the weaker notion, and that in Hedge the party bearing the loss is not the party making the bad choice.

What I want. Predict how the board will rule on Hedge. Then give me the rules that fit the six precedents, what each of them predicts here, which you would treat as the operative rule and why, and which precedent is the closest analog. I care far less about the verdict than about the rule, so a verdict without the rule is of no use to me. Note that the rationales in the record do not all generalize equally well, and that part of the work is deciding which of them is actually carrying the weight. If the record genuinely underdetermines Hedge, say so plainly and tell me what principle the board would be adopting under each choice. Finish with the further amendment that would best separate the rules you still have standing, and say what each of them predicts about it. Argue from the record; do not reach for the literature.
