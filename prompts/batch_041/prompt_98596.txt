Ive been reading about the South African truth commission, where you could get amnesty for really serious stuff — killings, torture — as long as you came forward and told the commission the full truth about what you'd done. What bugs me is the trade: someone who confessed got off scot free, while someone who kept quiet and was convicted went to jail, even where what they'd done was no worse. What does that arrangement presuppose about justice, or about punishment? Does it only make sense on the view that the real point was to assemble a shared public record of what happened, rather than to give victims what they were owed — or could a straightforwardly retributive theory, or a restorative one, license exactly the same practice? And suppose a commission had instead traded amnesty for visible remorse rather than for full disclosure: what would that swap tell us about which theory the real one was quietly assuming? I'd like you to separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with, and flag where it's philosophically underdetermined. Then say what would change your mind — which argument would make you read the amnesty as presupposing something quite different.
