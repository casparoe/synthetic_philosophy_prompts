I am preparing a seminar on Plato's Meno, and I want to use Christopher Nolan's Memento as a test case for the problem Meno raises near the end of the dialogue. The philosophical apparatus is this. Meno asks why knowledge is more valuable than mere true belief, and Socrates answers with two images: the road to Larissa, which a man who has been there can guide others along just as well as a man who merely has the right opinion about it, and the statues of Daedalus, which run away unless they are tied down. True belief, on this picture, is a runaway statue; knowledge is the same statue tethered, and the tether is working out the reason why the belief is true. The trouble, which I want the seminar to feel, is that this answer threatens to make knowledge only instrumentally better than true belief -- more stable, less likely to be lost -- which seems to concede that what we value is reliability of action rather than anything about knowing as such. In the contemporary literature this is pressed as the swamping problem: if truth is already what confers value on a belief, the added property of being tethered seems to add nothing of its own. I also want a distinction on the table, which I am not sure the literature keeps cleanly apart: the tether as truth-tracking, where the believer's reason is the reason the belief is true, versus the tether as answerability, where the believer can give and defend a reason regardless of whether it tracks anything.

Read the film through this. Leonard Shelby cannot form new enduring beliefs, so his notes, tattoos and Polaroids are beliefs stored outside his head; each one is a Daedalus statue, true or false as it happens, and always liable to run off. I want a reading that asks, first, whether the film vindicates Plato's image by making us feel the difference between a true belief that stays put and one that does not. Second, and more importantly, whether the closing sequence breaks the answer: Leonard destroys the evidence that would correct him, manufactures a reason he knows to be bad, and then commits the resulting claim to his body as a fact. That is a tethered belief with answerability and no truth-tracking -- a statue glued to its pedestal by someone who wants it to stay there. If a tether can be fabricated at will and hold a falsehood just as firmly, what becomes of the claim that tethering is what makes knowledge better than true belief? Third, treat Sammy Jankis as a control case rather than a plot device: a man who cannot be tethered at all, and whom the film seems to present as barely a person. Is that the film answering Meno prudentially, by showing that without tethering there is no agency to speak of, or is it offering a different and stronger claim, that the tether is constitutive of the self rather than a means to reliable action?

Be faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the film to an illustration of the Meno, and do not bend the Meno to fit the film; where the film is really about self-deception and the stories we tell ourselves rather than about the value of knowledge, say so plainly, and where the reading forces a scene to carry more than it can, admit it. End by saying what the film contributes to the debate that argument alone does not -- in particular, whether it shows that we sometimes prefer a tethered falsehood to an untethered truth, and if so, whether that refutes Meno's question or merely relocates the value of knowledge somewhere other than truth. Commit to a verdict, tell me which parts of your reading you take to be genuinely contested interpretations of the film, and say which consideration or argument would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
