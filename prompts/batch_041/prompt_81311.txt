I'm a third-year psych student and I'm doing a placement at a community mental health clinic's intake desk (and I do one shift a week on a peer warmline, which is where most of my actual confusion comes from). My supervisor is trying to get me to stop writing things like "query: rule out depression" and instead write a one-page formulation for every new referral before the psychiatrist sees them, and the first line of that page has to commit to one of three things: (a) this is a disease process and we're treating it, (b) this is a problem in living and we're helping with it, or (c) we have nothing to offer and we say so. We just read Szasz's The Myth of Mental Illness (1961) in my philosophy of psychiatry seminar and I mostly buy his line that the label is a moral judgment wearing medical clothes, that what people actually have are "problems in living," and that his real target is coercion rather than consensual help — he insisted he wasn't anti-psychiatry so much as anti-forced-psychiatry. So I want a procedure that takes his two central claims seriously and makes them runnable by a 21-year-old on placement: first, that there's no illness there in the disease sense unless there's neurological damage or disease; second, that a diagnosis never erases a person's responsibility for what they do. What I need from you is a decision procedure — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each before I move on, where it stops with a hard no, and where it hands the call to the psychiatrist because what's at stake is a value and not a fact I can go look up. Keep whatever is operational in it: I want a meaning paragraph on every formulation (what the behaviour is doing for the person, in their own words, if it's doing anything) and a responsibility line (what we're going to hold them to, and what we are explicitly not excusing). Tell me which distinctions I have to keep intact to stay faithful to him and which ones I can flatten without wrecking the point.

Then actually run it, on three intakes: a nineteen-year-old who hears voices and is terrified of them and wants them gone; a man in his forties whose opioid use has cost him his job and his flat and who came in mainly because his sister made him; and a woman with severe anorexia whose weight is getting dangerous and who has refused treatment, with her family pushing us to hold her anyway. For at least one of these I expect the procedure to spit out an answer I won't like — if it does, don't soften it, just tell me why it falls that way and what I'd have to change about the procedure to get the other answer (and whether that change is cheating). Also say plainly what the procedure can't do: I assume the risk/coercion question is exactly where the facts run out, but I want to see where you think that line actually sits, because my supervisor signs off on anything landing in box (c) and the county contract obliges us to document risk, so I can't just write "this is a values call" and go home.

One more thing: a friend in the seminar thinks Szasz is just a libertarian ideologue who'd tell a terrified kid to pull himself together, and I don't think that's fair, but I also don't want you smoothing over the disagreements. So please seperate what's actually settled about him and about this domain from what's genuinely contested among people who know it, and don't present one reading of Szasz as the consensus — if you think the whole exercise is incoherent because "problems in living" can't be operationalised without turning into the thing he was attacking, say that and say why, instead of building me a procedure anyway and burying the objection.
