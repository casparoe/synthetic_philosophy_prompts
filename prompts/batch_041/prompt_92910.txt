I have no academic training in philosophy. I have read a good deal of it on my own, and for some time I have kept a notebook of philosophical thought experiments — the ones that are supposed to settle something — recording what happens to my judgment when I change details that the accompanying argument calls irrelevant. Out of that notebook I have arrived at a position, and what I want from you is not reassurance but a map of how exposed it is.

The position is this. A thought experiment does legitimate philosophical work only when it removes an option: it shows that some escape route I had assumed was open is in fact closed, so that the remaining alternatives are forced. Everything else a thought experiment does is rhetoric — the concrete detail, the vividness, the first-person or third-person framing, the number of people involved, the tense. Where a verdict about a general principle depends on any of that, the verdict has been manufactured by the description rather than found in the case. My suspicion is that most celebrated thought experiments are of the second kind, and that wherever one seems decisive, a premise has been smuggled in that does not appear in the case itself.

I would like the following.

First, what would count against this? Give me the observations, arguments, and possible findings that ought to move me, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with your reason for each ranking.

Second, which of these would I have to concede on my own terms — which count against me even if I grant nothing to my critics?

Third, what does my position predict that a rival does not? The rival I have in mind holds that at least some intuitions about cases are responses to the content of the case rather than to its framing. If I am right, what should we expect to see when irrelevant details are varied? What should we expect if the rival is right?

Fourth, has my position been qualified into something unfalsifiable? Is there anything that could happen which I would refuse to count as a counterexample, and if so, is that a defect in a position of this kind?

To give you something to work on, here is a case I constructed myself, and which I cannot classify. A person is offered a pill that will give them, for exactly one hour, an intense concern with something they now find trivial — the outcome of a match between two teams they have never heard of. They will remember the hour, will be unchanged by it, and no one else will ever know. I find I judge that taking the pill is a waste and that refusing it is not. Then I strip the case down to a sentence — a person is offered a pill that will give them an hour of strong feeling about nothing that matters — and my verdict does not move. My position says the verdict should have been carried by the vivid detail and should weaken when the detail goes. Either my position is wrong, or the bare sentence is itself a description doing the pumping, and I cannot tell which. Please use this as a test.

Two final things. I cannot look anything up, so do not send me to the literature or cite anyone; if a point can only be made by appeal to what some philosopher wrote, make it from the case instead, or tell me plainly that it cannot be made. And tell me what would change your mind about your verdict on my position: which single consideration would move you off it, and where in your reasoning you are most likely to be wrong.
