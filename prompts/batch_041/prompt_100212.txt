ok here's a credence question I keep going back and forth on and I'd like to see how you decompose it. Take the strongest version of the inefficacy objection. A single person's choice to eat a burger, take one flight, or vote in a safely-blue state makes no difference at all to the outcome: assume for the sake of the question that this is literally true, the world is exactly the same in both branches, no threshold crossed, no animal reared differently, no election flipped. In that case is there still a genuine moral obligation on the individual to refrain? I want your credence in [0,1] that the answer is yes, and I want the decomposition rather than a bare number.

Here's the terms I'd want you to price seperately, but add or drop terms if you think the question actually hinges on something else:

1. Whether 'obligation' has to bottom out in causal contribution to harm at all, or whether complicity / participation in a harmful practice can ground it on its own.
2. Whether the literal-no-difference premise is even the right model of these cases -- i.e. whether the right move is to say the act raises expected harm by some tiny real amount, so the objection never gets off the ground.
3. Whether fair-play or rule-consequentialist reasoning can generate an obligation to do your part even when your part does nothing.
4. Whether the substitution point matters: the question is arguably not 'refrain vs. do the thing' but 'do the thing vs. do something better with the same resources', and the obligation might live entirely in that second comparison.
5. How much your answer to (2) contaminates your answers to the rest -- if you think the no-difference premise is a fiction, do terms 1, 3 and 4 even need to do work?

Give me a number for each, a line of justification each, and then how you combine them into the final figure. Then tell me which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and what argument or piece of evidence would move that term the most.
