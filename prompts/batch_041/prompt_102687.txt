Got into a long argument with a friend last night about humanitarian intervention — Kosovo, Iraq, Libya — and we kept going in cirles about something we couldn't quite name. Here's the thing that bugs me. In practice we treat Security Council authorization as what makes an intervention legitimate. Kosovo in 99 had no authorization and everyone sort of shrugged and said it was the right call anyway, just not legal; Iraq in 03 had none and we treated the lack as basically damning; Libya in 2011 had the resolution, so it got a legitimacy pass even after NATO clearly blew past what the resolution actually authorized. So what does that whole pattern presuppose about legitimacy — that it comes from being signed off by the right body, or from the intervention actually being morally justified, or some hybrid of the two? Can it even be consistent to hold that Kosovo was fine despite being unauthorized and that Iraq wasn't fine partly because it was unauthorized? And my friend wants to scrap authorization altogether and ask instead whether the people being rescued would have consented if you could ask them. Does that rest on a different theory, and does it actually resolve the Kosovo/Iraq/Libya mess or just relocate it? I want the implicit theory spelled out, which rival views it quietly rules out, and where the practice is just underdetermined — like, would other theories license the exact same record?
