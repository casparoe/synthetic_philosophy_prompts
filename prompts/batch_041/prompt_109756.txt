I'm in a reading group that spent the whole term on philosophy of biology — we meet Thursdays in the back room of a bike shop, terrible coffee, somebody always brings their dog — and for the last session the rule is that instead of a summary you have to bring a poem about one concept from the syllabus. I pulled "function," which is the one topic where after fourteen weeks I genuinely can't say where I landed. So here's what I want: a pantoum, sixteen lines, four quatrains — lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next one, and the closing quatrain folds back into the lines that opened the poem. No philosophers' names in it, no footnotes, and definately no "on the one hand some theorists hold..." It has to be a poem, and the argument has to be doing work in the form.

The speaker should be the trait itself, first person, and it should be a case where the two accounts I keep going back and forth between give different verdicts, so that the repeated lines carry the disagreement without either side winning. Give it this life: it starts as an accident — some ossification that turned up for no particular reason, whatever's biologically plausible — then it gets kept for ages because it happens to do job A, then the population ends up somewhere that makes A pointless and it stops doing A altogether, and now, for a few hundred generations, which is nothing, it's under strong selection for a completely different job B. On the history-first account, where a trait's function is the effect it was selected for, its function is still A — the thing it was kept for — even though it hasn't done A in a very long time, and it's the account that can still call it broken; even the history people split among themselves over how much history you need before B counts, so my trait is a live dispute inside that camp too. On the other account, where a function is just what a part contributes to some capacity the organism currently has, its function is obviously B and the history is neither here nor there — except then you owe me an answer to why it isn't equally my function to make a bump you can feel through the skin, since that's a contribution to something too. One guy in the group insists both are wrong and that a function is what the trait *would* be selected for if the current setup kept going, which gets you B for a third reason; don't turn this into a survey of three views, but if that reading can slip into a line without clogging things, let it.

What I actually care about: someone who knows this literature should be able to point at the line that's the historical verdict and the line that's the present-tense verdict and see why the form won't let them stay apart, so make those two the lines the pantoum keeps handing back to you. Open on the present-tense one (what I do now) and make sure the last line of the poem is the historical one (what I was kept for), so the whole thing ends looking backwards even though the closing stanza has just been about the present. And don't resolve it — if it ends with a tidy reconciliation I've failed the assignment, I want both verdicts left standing over the same body. It's due Thursday and I've been avoiding it for a week, so anything you've got.
