I am in a political theory seminar that keeps circling back to populism, and I have noticed that every argument I get into -- about whether a referendum really expressed the popular will, whether a leader can legitimately claim to speak for the people against the courts or the press, whether a policy that the public would endorse only after deliberation counts as something the people want -- goes wrong at the same place. Each of us arrives with an unstated theory of what "the will of the people" even is, and we spend the whole hour discovering that we were answering different questions. So before I read another word of Rousseau, of the neo-republican deliberative literature, of the aggregative-majoritarian camp, or of the Schumpeterian proceduralists, I want the specification written down first. Please give me the desiderata that any adequate theory of the will of the people must meet. I want the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot be allowed to collapse, and the results it has to be consistent with -- with the particular case or consideration behind each requirement, so that I can see why it is a genuine demand rather than a prejudice of one camp. Among the cases I would insist any such theory handle: a referendum in which a majority votes to strip rights from a minority; a situation where preferences cycle so that no majority preference exists at all; a case where the preferences being aggregated were themselves produced by advertising, agenda-setting, or manipulation, so that it is unclear whether the "will" is discovered or manufactured; and a case where a leader or movement claims to embody the true people against the actually-existing institutions that claim to represent them. As for the distinctions, I am thinking of ones like the difference between the people's will and mere majority preference, between an empirical reading of the will (what people in fact want) and a normative one (what they have reason to want, or would want under suitable conditions), between the people as a single corporate agent and the people as an aggregate of individuals, and between the will being something determinate and pre-existing versus something constructed in the very act of asking for it -- but tell me which of these are load-bearing and which are red herrings.

I would also like you to say which of the requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely commitments of one camp, and, most of all, to flag any subset of the desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied -- because if, say, one requirement demands that the will be independently determinable while another concedes that preferences are endogenous and framing-dependent, or if the demand for a single corporate will runs into the impossibility results that plague any aggregation of individual preferences, then the list itself is the result and I want that stated plainly rather than buried. Then, briefly and only at the end, run the main contenders against the list: the general-will tradition that reads the will as what citizens would will as a body, aggregative majoritarianism, the deliberative view that the will emerges from suitably structured public reasoning, the populist embodiment view on which a leader or movement is the authentic voice of the people, and the proceduralist view that the will is simply whatever the established decision procedure outputs. Keep the weight on the criteria and the cases that motivate them rather than on the scoring, since I can do the scoring in class -- the criteria are what I cannot currently get anyone to state. One practical constraint: the theory is meant to be adequate for adjudicating concrete disputes of the kind we keep having, so the requirements should be pitched at whatever level of precision that purpose actually needs, no more.
