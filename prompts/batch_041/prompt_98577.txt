I produce a small history-of-philosophy podcast, and for an upcoming episode I have lined up a historian of early modern philosophy whose recent work defends a revisionist reading of Hobbes's mathematics. Her thesis, roughly stated, is that Hobbes's geometry was not the embarrassing sideline of a great political thinker who fancied himself a geometer, but a project continuous with the rest of his system. On her account, because Hobbes treats reasoning as a kind of computation — adding and subtracting names — and because he treats the objects of geometry as figures we ourselves generate by motion, geometry becomes for him the paradigm of a demonstrable science: the one domain where we grasp the causes of what we study precisely because we make it. She reads the notorious attempts to square the circle not as simple incompetence but as the visible working-out of a nonstandard method, and she argues that the long public quarrel with John Wallis was as much about theology, university authority, and the reception of Leviathan as it was about mathematics. I want the interview to take this reading seriously and then lean on it hard, rather than allowing her an easy twenty minutes of refuting the charge that his math was just bad.

Please draft nine or ten questions, each followed by a one- or two-sentence follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, so that I can keep the conversation moving when she reaches for a stock reply. At least one question should press on whether her reading can distinguish a bold nonstandard method from plain error: if every failed construction is reinterpreted as a feature of the method, what would it take for her to conclude that Hobbes's geometry really was just bad, and does her thesis have any falsifiable edge? At least one should press on the nominalist side of the story: if universals are only names and geometrical objects are constructed rather than discovered, does the computational view of reasoning collapse geometry into a verbal game, and if so, how does Hobbes retain the demonstrative status she attributes to it? At least one should probe the boundary between geometry and the other sciences: why is geometry demonstrable while the science of natural bodies is not, and does the fact that we are said to make the commonwealth buy civil philosophy the same demonstrative standing, or does that analogy strain under pressure? And at least one should press on the historical and political stakes of the Wallis quarrel, including what her reading implies about how the new experimental circles of the period received Hobbes and why it mattered to them. I would also like a closing question that asks her to say what is actually at stake for how we tell the story of the Scientific Revolution if she is right — but I do not want a reading list or suggested sources; just the questions and their follow-ups.
