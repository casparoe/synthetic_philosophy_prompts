For the past several months I have been part of a small adult-education group at my parish that has been working through questions of political obligation, and the question of tyrannicide is the one we cannot get past. The occasion is not merely academic: several members of the group are involved in advocacy on behalf of Christians in a country whose government most of us would describe as tyrannical, and we disagree sharply about whether the removal of such a government — and, in the limiting case, the use of lethal force against its ruler — is something a Catholic moralist can endorse. I volunteered to report on what Aquinas actually holds, since both sides in the group claim him. Before I do, I want to know whether the note that has been circulating among us is a fair account of his position. It reads:

Aquinas's teaching on tyrannicide is simple and consistent. Sedition, he says, is always a mortal sin; therefore any uprising against a ruler, however wicked, is forbidden. He makes one exception: a tyrant who has seized power illegitimately may be killed, and Aquinas even says that the person who kills such a tyrant to free his country deserves praise and a reward. But this permission is granted only to the community acting through its lawful authorities; it is never granted to a private individual, who has no authority to kill anyone, tyrant or not. And where a ruler holds power legitimately but governs oppressively, Aquinas says the subject has no remedy but endurance: the ruler is God's instrument, and only God may judge him.

Several of us are inclined to think this is roughly right, though the concluding sentence seems to prove too much, since it would leave the tradition with nothing to say to people living under an intolerable regime. What I need is an audit of the note, sentence by sentence: which claims are accurate, which are subtle misreadings, and which rest on conflations — of sedition with resistance, of the private individual with the public authority, of the tyrant by usurpation with the tyrant by practice. Where Aquinas's texts pull in different directions, I would rather know that than have the tension smoothed over. Please also say where the note, once corrected, leaves the group's actual question: whether Aquinas's texts give any determinate guidance for a case like ours, or whether they are silent in a way that the note conceals.
