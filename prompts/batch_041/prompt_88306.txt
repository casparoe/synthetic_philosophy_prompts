So I got roped into sitting on the advisory board of this small humanities foundation -- we hand out maybe $2M over three years, and the trustees have all read the same two books about forgeries and are now obssessed. The theme they've picked for the next funding cycle is "originals, copies, and what we owe the difference," and they've asked me (because I'm apparently the only one with a philosphy background) to come back with a short list of the conceptual questions we should actually be paying people to work on. I keep splitting the value of an "original" into three things that I think come apart more than people admit: the contact value (you're standing in front of the actual thing the maker's hand touched), the informational value (the original is a physical record -- brushwork, materials, mistakes, all the stuff a copy flattens out), and the relational or ritual value (the original anchors a practice -- pilgrimage, patrimony, the auction, the family heirloom). And then there's the old line about works where the original just IS the work (a painting, a hand-thrown pot) versus works where any accurate copy is as good as the first (a novel, a score, a proof), which makes me suspect "the original" isn't even one concept. Two puzzles keep nagging me and might be worth building questions around: a forgery that's hung in a museum for eighty years and has its own exhibition history and its own fans -- is its "relational value" real, or is that just a category error? And a painting restored so heavily that almost none of the original material survives -- is it still the original, or have we quietly made a very good copy?

What I need from you: which open questions in this area are most worth funding, ranked, and more importantly WHY in that order. Assume the money is real but small, the people we'd fund are mostly philosophers plus maybe one or two conservators or museum theorists, and the horizon is three years. For each candidate question I want to know whether it's upstream of the others (does answering it change what the other questions even are?), how tractable it is for the kind of person we'd fund, and what would move it up or down the list -- some finding, or some change in the world (deepfake-level image synthesis, say, or a big authentication failure at a major museum). Also tell me which of the questions everyone reflexively brings up are overrated or just wouldn't resolve anything, because half the trustees' suggestions sound like they'd produce essays about "authenticity" that argue in circles. I'd rather fund two or three things that will still look like the right call in ten years, and I care a lot less about the list itself than about the reasoning that puts one question above another -- including what evidence over the next couple of years would make you swap them.
