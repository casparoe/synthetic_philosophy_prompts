For the past three years I've spent one afternoon a week cataloguing works on paper at a small regional auction house — sketches and drawings, mostly, by minor nineteenth- and twentieth-century hands. It began as volunteer work and has quietly become the thing I am best at. I have no training. What I have is a great many hours, and somewhere in there I acquired what I can only call an eye: a sheet by a particular hand has a certain weight in the line, and I can usually tell that something is wrong before I can say why.

The trouble is the file. When I read the consignor's paperwork before I look, and it says the attribution is solid, the drawings look like the artist. When the paperwork records that a previous dealer doubted it, the same drawings look thin, and the tics I would have called characteristic read as imitation. I do not mean that I reason differently from the same evidence. I mean that the sheet in front of me looks different. It has got bad enough that I now do my first pass with the folder closed, but I cannot tell whether that is discipline or superstition.

A friend with a philosophy degree told me the interesting question is not whether I am biased — plainly I am — but whether the bias reaches all the way down into what I see, and that if it does, something I have been assuming about my looking counting as evidence has to give.

Two things I could actually try. First, shuffle everything and look twice, once with the file and once without, and count how often my verdict flips. Second, have a colleague slip me a sheet they know to be a modern copy, tell me only that it came in with the rest, and see whether I begin to see the hand in it.

Would either of those tell me whether my experience is changing, or only my verdict? If not, what would — and is that distinction available to me from the inside at all? Then tell me what to do on Monday. Do I keep looking first and reading second, do I stop giving verdicts, or is there something better? And if the answer is that my eye is being driven by the file, does that make my looking worthless as evidence, or merely not independent of what I have been told — and is a trained eye a different case from an untrained one, or the same ailment with better manners?

I would rather be told that I have posed this badly than be given a fluent answer to a question that does not have the shape I think it does. If both tests are useless, or if there is no fact of the matter about what it looked like to me apart from how I describe it, say so plainly rather than working around it. I am not asking for a reading list; I want the structure of the problem and a course of action.
