Most of my working life I was a field ecologist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science in an attempt to make sense of a methodological embarrassment I kept running into without ever having a name for it. The embarrassment was this: I could show a colleague a dataset in which a species was, by every pooled measure, in decline, then show the very same data broken down by soil type, and in every soil type the species was doing fine. Nothing in the numbers told me which of the two statements was the true one; whichever I reported felt like a choice I was making rather than a fact I was reading off. I have since learned that the phenomenon is called Simpson's paradox, and that a good deal has been written about it, but the discussions I have found either dismiss it as an arithmetic curiosity or dissolve it with causal assumptions whose credentials I do not fully trust.

I would like you to write me a short story that takes this problem seriously as its subject rather than using it as a twist near the end. Let the reversal emerge from the protagonist's own data, and let the story be, in substance, about the difficulty of saying what the data support. I want the protagonist to try the obvious remedies and find that none of them settles the matter: reporting the pooled figure, reporting the disaggregated figures, hunting for the one correct stratifying variable. In doing so the protagonist should come to see that no purely statistical rule picks out the privileged partition, and that the choice between them rests on a causal or evaluative commitment imported from outside the numbers. Include at least one turn in which the natural causal remedy backfires, because the variable the protagonist wants to condition on is itself downstream of the thing being studied — a consequence as much as a cause — so that breaking the data down manufactures the reversal instead of exposing a hidden one. Include a further turn in which adding one more covariate reverses the reversal yet again, so that the protagonist sees there is no resting place, and begins to wonder whether the population whose fate he is tracking is given by nature or chosen by him. He need not resolve any of this; I would much prefer the story to end in honest perplexity than in a tidy moral, and I would rather it dramatize these difficulties through the work and the arguments than explain them to the reader.

Write it as continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; the way one would write a long letter.
