So my roommate and I got into this after a philosophy of mind seminar and now it's 1am and I can't sleep. The prof spent a whole week on personal identity and kept waving his hand at "you could replace the brain bit by bit and nothing would be lost," like it was just obvious, and my roommate, who cannot let anything go, built this argument to show it has to be wrong, and I can't find the loose thread. Here it is: (1) What makes a later being you is that it has the same psychological/functional organization — same memories, dispositions, character, the whole pattern — and not that it's built from the same atoms. That's the standard functionalist line and honestly I'm happy to grant it. (2) If we swap out one of your neurons for a silicon chip that does exactly what that neuron did, you survive. You'd feel nothing, your friends would notice nothing, and we already think people survive when chunks of brain get removed or replaced by prosthetics, so one-for-one shouldn't be special. (3) If you survive swapping one, then you survive swapping them one at a time all the way through, because no single step differs in kind from the last — there's no magic threshold where "you" wink out. (4) So when the last neuron is gone and you're running on silicon, it's still you. Fine, I can live with that; I figure that's what uploading is supposed to be anyway.

But then my roommate says: hold on. Suppose while we're doing the swap we don't destroy the neurons we pull out — we keep each one alive and firing in a nutrient bath, and when the last swap is done we wire the original neurons back together into a working biological brain. That reassembled brain has exactly the same functional organization too — it literally is your original brain, just taken apart and put back together. (6) So by (1) the reassembled biological brain is you. (7) But by (1)-(4) the silicon system is also you, and now the two of them are sitting on different tables, made of different stuff, so they can't be the same being. (8) And one thing can't be two different things. So (1) has to be false, and the prof's "nothing would be lost" is just wrong.

Here's my problem: I'd sooner keep (2)-(4) than give up (1), but I genuinely can't tell which one is doing the work, and I don't trust my gut that something is off around step (3) or step (6). Which premise do I give up, and what do I have to pay for it — like, does dropping it break the ordinary uploading case, or does it break something I care about even more?
