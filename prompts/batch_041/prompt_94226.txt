The question (12 points: 3 for each part):

Consider two cases. Case A: Devi and Marco are both careful, well-trained historians working on the same archive; each knows the other to be at least as competent and as thorough as herself, and each knows that they have examined exactly the same documents. They reach opposite conclusions about whether a disputed charter is a forgery, and each has read the other's write-up and finds the other's reasoning as careful as her own. Case B: Devi, before writing up her verdict, receives a note from her physician — the medication she has been taking for the past month is associated, in a minority of users, with a subtle tendency to find ambiguous patterns more probative than they are, and there is no way for her to tell whether she is one of those users. Marco has not looked at the archive at all, and no one else has. Devi's own reasoning still strikes her as compelling.

(a) What is higher-order evidence? Say precisely how it figures in each case, and whether the two cases raise the same question. (b) What does the conciliationist say about Case A, and what does a steadfast or total-evidence view say? What turns on the difference between them? (c) Is it ever rational to believe that p while believing that one's belief in p is not supported by one's evidence? Say what the state usually called level-splitting amounts to, and assess the charge of epistemic akrasia against it. (d) State the self-defeat objection to conciliationism and say how damaging it is.

The student's answer (typed up as written, student's own numbering):

(a) Higher-order evidence is evidence about the epistemic standing of one's beliefs rather than about the subject matter those beliefs are about. The standard case in the literature is the disagreement of a peer: when someone as well placed as I am weighs the same evidence and comes out the other way, that is evidence that something has gone wrong in my own handling of the evidence, and it bears on the disputed question only indirectly. Case A is the paradigm. Devi and Marco have each seen the same documents and each knows the other to be at least as careful as herself, so each now has higher-order evidence about the disputed verdict, and neither can treat her own reading as settling the matter.

Case B looks similar but is not, in my view, a case of higher-order evidence in this sense. Devi's physician's note tells her something about her own cognitive machinery, but there is no second opinion in play and nobody for her to conciliate with. Properly speaking the note is just one more fact about the world for her to fold into her picture of the archive, so the disagreement literature — which is where the higher-order evidence debate lives — has nothing to say about her situation. The two cases do not raise the same question.

(b) In Case A the conciliationist says that because Devi and Marco are peers who have seen the same evidence, the disagreement is itself evidence for each of them, and it is unreasonable for either to remain at her original confidence. In the fully symmetric case the equal-weight view says each should move halfway toward the other's position: suspend judgment if they began equally confident, and in any event end up much less confident than before. The steadfast or total-evidence view replies that disagreement is only one consideration among many. It is real evidence, but it can be outweighed; if the first-order evidence genuinely supports the forgery verdict, Devi may keep believing it, because her total evidence still favours it. What turns on the difference is whether the higher-order evidence can defeat a first-order belief that the evidence otherwise supports, or whether the two are simply weighed against each other.

On the steadfast view, Case B is straightforward: nothing in the note bears on the justification of Devi's verdict, since the total evidence that supported her conclusion is unchanged, and her belief remains exactly as rational, and as warranted, as it was before she opened the letter.

(c) Level-splitting is the state of believing that p while believing that one's evidence does not support p. It is hard to see how it could be rational, since it looks like plain inconsistency: the agent believes p, and she also believes something incompatible with p. Most writers treat it as a form of epistemic akrasia, believing against one's own better judgment, and I think the charge sticks — an agent who splits levels in this way is contradicting herself, and no belief that requires you to contradict yourself can be rational.

(d) The self-defeat objection is that conciliationism cannot survive being applied to itself. Conciliationism is itself a contested view, and there are peers who reject it; so by the conciliationist's own standard its defenders should reduce their confidence in it, and if they follow the standard through they should end up with very little confidence in conciliationism. That is a contradiction in the view itself, so the objection is decisive and conciliationism is refuted.
