Hey — I'm a third-year PhD student in phil of mind (project is on mental causation, though mostly it's me staring at a whiteboard). I'm drafting the part of a chapter where I have to say something about epiphenomenalism, and I keep getting tangled in an arguement I can only half-state cleanly. I think the thing I'm after is sometimes called the paradox of phenomenal judgment, but dont hold me to the label.

The setup: epiphenomenalism says brain states cause experiences, but experiences themselves cause nothing downstream — they're inert byproducts of the neural goings-on. Fine, coherent enough on its face. Now the tangle. Right now I can form the judgment that I'm having a slightly throbbing, reddish-dusk sort of visual experience, and that judgment is roughly accurate. But if the experience is causally inert, it isn't what caused my judgment about it. So either (a) the accuracy of my phenomenal judgments is a staggering coincidence, or (b) the judgments are caused by the brain states that also cause the experiences — in which case my judgments are tracking the brain states and it's unclear what the experience contributes — or (c) the nasty one: the epiphenomenalist's evidence for his own view is precisely his introspective judgments, so if those float free of the experiences, the view eats its own tail.

What I want from you is a dialogue between two philosophers, A and B, where A defends epiphenomenalism and B thinks phenomenal properties are (or must be) causally efficacious, or at least that A's story can't be right. No -isms traded, no name-dropping papers, just the argument in their own voices.

Things I specifically want them to work through, in a real back-and-forth where each one answers what the other actually said:

1. A's best line on the coincidence worry: the judgment is caused by the physical state, the phenomenal property is lawfully correlated with that state, so reliability is inherited from the physical facts and nothing is coincidental. B should then ask what makes such a judgment about the experience rather than about the brain state — and A shouldn't get off with a slogan; he should say something about how the content gets fixed.

2. A scenario I want them to fight about: two people (or one person before and after some change) whose phenomenal lives differ in a systematic way — inverted colour experience is the obvious case — while every physical fact, including every judgment either of them makes, is held fixed. B's question: on A's view, what in the world makes the judgment sensitive to the phenomenal difference at all? Let A give a genuine answer, even a bullet-biting one, and let B say what it costs.

3. The self-undermining worry from (c). Let B press it hard: on A's account, couldn't a being with no experiences at all have produced A's exact book? And let A say whether that's an acceptable consequence and why. I'm not after a knock-down; I want to see whether the position can absorb the blow or has to change shape.

Neither should be a strawman — A especially should be the strongest version of the view, and B should concede where A has a point. End with the two of them stating as precisely as they can what their remaining disagreement actually is: whether reliability needs a causal explanation, whether content can be fixed by correlation without causation, or something else. If they turn out to be disagreeing about more than one thing, say so.
