I write about science and ideas, and for the past several months I have been reporting on how much confidence medical research deserves. A statistician I interviewed — careful, well regarded, not a crank — left me with an argument I have been unable to shake or to refute. He presented it as a proof that the whole enterprise of determining whether a treatment works is incoherent. I have written it out below as faithfully as I can. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling, and what rejecting it commits me to.

(1) To say that a treatment helps is to say that administering it produces a better outcome than withholding it — a claim about what the treatment does, not merely about who happened to receive it.

(2) Take any dataset relating a treatment to an outcome. There is always some further variable on which to condition — some way of dividing the subjects into subgroups — such that the treatment-outcome association reverses. If the treatment looked beneficial when the subjects were pooled, it looks harmful within each subgroup, or the reverse.

(3) So for any dataset there is a partition under which the treatment helps and another under which it harms. The direction of the effect is a function of how the data are sliced.

(4) Nothing in the data itself identifies the correct partition. To prefer one slicing over another is to import an assumption the data do not contain.

(5) Therefore no dataset, however large or carefully assembled, can establish whether a treatment helps or harms.

(6) Therefore a claim that a drug works is never a finding but a convention of analysis, and the movement for evidence-based medicine rests on a mistake. He adds that the argument applies to randomized trials as much as to observational studies, since the arithmetic does not care how the subjects came to be assigned.

He illustrated with a case I will render in invented numbers so that I do not misreport his data. A drug was given disproportionately to the sickest patients. Pooled, those who received it died at a higher rate than those who did not. Within the mildly ill, and again within the severely ill, those who received it did better. His point was that the same maneuver can be run the other way: condition on a different variable and the pooled verdict returns, so the example cuts in both directions. He says step (2) is a theorem, not a hunch.

I would like to keep (4) if I can, because I am wary of smuggling causal assumptions into claims that present themselves as purely about data; but I will surrender it if that is the price. What I cannot do is accept (6), which seems to me to prove far too much — it would dissolve not only medical trials but every science that reasons from data to causes, including the reasoning by which we first learned that the drug in his own example was confounded. I suspect a misstep somewhere rather than a genuine reductio, though I cannot rule out that one of the premises I happen to like is simply false. Which step do I give up, and what must I accept in its place?
