Two philosophers have argued for years about Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition. The first holds that the regress is vicious: if a proposition's constituents are held together by a further relation — a tie, copula, or mode of combination — then that relation must itself be unified with the items it relates, and so on, so that either unity is brute or the proposition is never formed at all; on this view the regress shows that unity cannot be any further relational ingredient. The second holds that the regress is benign: it is an artifact of a mistaken demand that unity be explained by something over and above the constituents being combined as they are, and a correct account either does not generate it or generates a harmless one. Draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want a numbered section of claims both accept — about what the regress is, what it does and does not show, what the shared explanandum is (for instance, why a mere list of entities differs from a proposition containing just those entities), and what would count as a solution — followed by a disagreements table: the precise residual disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording, the cruxes, and for each crux the argument or case both sides agree bears on it with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect one crux is whether unity is a further fact over and above the constituents being arranged as they are, and another is whether this is just the general problem of composition in another guise. Make both sides engage two stipulated cases: an identity proposition in which the same object occupies both argument places, and a notation in which no copula or tie is explicitly represented — does the regress still arise, and if it does not, is that a solution or a change of subject? If any part of the dispute is merely verbal, identify it and say why.
