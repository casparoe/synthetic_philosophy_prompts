My friend Nadia did a philosophy MA and is now the sort of person who will stop a dinner party to tell you whether you have a reason to feel a certain way. She has a running bit: you give her a case where someone's offered something for having an attitude, and she rules on it. Here's her track record, as close as I can recontruct it.

- A demon says it will destroy the world unless you admire it. Nadia: you have no reason to admire it. You have a great reason to want to admire it, and to take a pill or hire a hypnotist if that's what it takes, but none of that is a reason to admire it.
- Your rich uncle offers you $10,000 to believe that the moon is made of cheese. Nadia: no reason to believe it. A reason to want to believe it, sure. She'll also mutter that you can't just decide to believe things, so the offer's kind of a non-starter.
- Your employer offers you $500 a week to be afraid of a mouse that is provably harmless. Nadia: no reason to be afraid of it, though a good reason to want to be afraid, so you can collect.
- A billionaire offers you a bonus if you'll find his favourite comedian funny, and the comedian is genuinely mediocre — not painfully unfunny, just not funny. Nadia: the money isn't a reason to find him funny, it's a reason to want to.
- A friend does you a small real favour and a third party offers you $1,000 to be grateful to them for it. Nadia: the favour is a reason to be grateful. The thousand dollars isn't; it's a reason to want to be grateful.

Whenever I press her on what "no reason" is doing in those verdicts — does she mean there's literally nothing counting in favour of having the attitude, or just nothing that bears on whether the attitude fits its object — she says something like "does it matter?" and refills her drink, so I honestly can't tell wich she's committed to. (I think the lit calls these wrong-kind-of-reason cases; I don't need the literature, just your reasoning.)

Now the new case. An eccentric benefactor will pay Nadia $6,000 if she *wants* to spend her coming sabbatical in a particular small town — he's nostalgic about the place. The town is fine: she has no complaint about it and no affection for it, and if you asked her whether a year there would be good she'd say "sure, that'd be fine, no strong feelings either way."

So: what's her verdict? Does the money make it the case that she has most reason to want to spend the sabbatical there, or is it only a reason to want to want that, or to engineer the want? Would wanting it be fitting/appropriate, or just something she has reason to bring about? Could the money be the reason for which she wants it? Tell me which rules fit everything she's said so far, what each one predicts here, which you'd bet on and why, and name the further case that would actually settle which rule she's running.
