I'm in this informal forecasting group that meets every couple weeks -- mostly people who've read too much history and too many superforecasting books -- and we've been going in circles for months on whether to expect the decline of a major power sometime in the next few decades. The two camps have basically calcified. One side says take the outside view: great powers and empires have a base rate of collapse, the relevant reference class is just 'great powers,' here's the distribution of how long they last, so the prior is that this one falls too, deal with it. The other side says that's numerology, that this particular power has features the reference class blurs over -- reserve currency, demographic profile, whatever -- and you have to forecast from the inside, from what's actually going on. Nobody moves, and both sides keep reaching for different historical analogies, which I suspect is doing alot of the work.

My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement isn't empirical at all, it's about the reference class itself: there may be no fact of the matter about which class of past cases you're supposed to generalize from, in which case the outside view is doing something more like a decision aid than a prediction, and the two sides are quietly answering different questions under one heading. Then there's the reflexivity problem -- if enough people believe the decline forecast it changes behavior and maybe averts or hastens the very thing, which seems to poison the reference class from the inside. So: is my hypothesis right, or is there a genuine empirical or modeling dispute underneath that I'm missing? And is the age-based picture (things of this kind fall after roughly N years) even the same kind of claim as the state-based picture (they fall when some strain variable crosses a threshold) -- because I have a feeling the camps are split along that line too without realizing it. Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is, with reasons, and what would count as progress here: a better reference class, a formal result about when base rates even apply, or just admitting the whole thing is a values-laden choice about how to act under uncertainty.
