For the past few years I've been running an evening reading group that started as an offshoot of a local rationalist meetup: we began with the usual sequences, got diverted into decision theory for a year, and have spent the last two terms stuck on self-reference. I've been reading this corner of the internet long enough to remember when the Liar was treated as a distraction from real epistemology, and I still find it striking how reliably the topic dead-ends — every time we reach the Liar, half the table takes one of two exits, either that the sentence simply fails to express a proposition or that the whole business is an artifact of sloppy natural language, and we adjourn without anyone having had to defend the exit they took. I want to build a unit that makes those exits cost something, and the occasion I have in mind is the final exam for the graduate seminar I teach on truth and paradox. The students are philosophy of language people plus two logicians borrowed from the math department; the exam is ninety minutes, closed book, no notes, and this question is one of three, so it should be worth 25 marks and occupy a good student for roughly forty minutes of the ninety.

The thing I want the question built around is a puzzle I've been tinkering with myself rather than lifting from anywhere. Take two sentences, A and B, where A says that exactly one of A and B is true and B says that exactly one of A and B is false, and assume a truth predicate governed by the ordinary truth schema, so that each sentence is true just in case its content is; begin with classical two-valued logic and then move to the non-classical alternatives. I have convinced myself of three claims about this pair, and I would like the exam to test exactly them: that a consistent classical assignment of truth values exists, that it is unique, and that the assignment delivered by a Kripke-style minimal fixed point is not that one — so that groundedness and determinacy come apart here in a way they do not for the plain Liar or the plain truth-teller. The question should require students to (a) compute the classical assignment and show why each of the three competing assignments fails, (b) say what a Kripke-style fixed-point account says about A and B and whether the pair counts as grounded, (c) say what a supervaluationist who quantifies over fixed points says instead, and (d) explain what the divergence between those verdicts reveals about groundedness, determinacy, and the exactly-one construction — including whether a theory that issues such verdicts can state its own verdict about this pair without inviting revenge.

Please give me the exam question itself, a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme. Build the scheme around five or six named must-hit points, with the marks each is worth adding to 25 and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each wrong; the two errors I am expecting are treating the pair as a plain Liar that admits of no consistent assignment and announcing indeterminate without noticing that the classical analysis pins both sentences to a definite value. Students may bring nothing into the room, so neither the question nor the model answer may lean on citations or on recalling who said what — everything has to be derivable from the puzzle as stated plus what is already in the students' heads. And keep it fair: the point is to separate the people who can compute a verdict and then interpret it from the people who can only recite the menu of theories, so a strong student who has done the reading but is not a logician should still be able to reach a solid answer.
