I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years at the bench, and for the last three of them a serious amateur of philosophy, mostly epistemology and the philosophy of science. I have been working my way through the debate over peer disagreement, and I keep running into the same discomfort. The debate is organised around a picture that does not match how evidence actually behaves in a laboratory. The conciliationist says that on discovering a recognised epistemic peer who disagrees, one ought to move toward that peer's view; the steadfast interlocutor says one may hold one's ground. But in my old field two competent people almost never possessed the same evidence, or weighted the same measurements identically; the disagreements that mattered were asymmetric clashes between people whose grounds differed in ways neither could fully articulate, and the real question was often not what the evidence was but which method or framework deserved to count as evidence in the first place. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher of epistemology — someone who knows this debate deeply and can think about its future rather than merely cataloguing its past — and to help me determine which open questions in the epistemology of disagreement most deserve sustained work over the next five years, and why.

I do not want a survey of the existing positions, and I emphatically do not want a reading list or citations; I want a defended agenda. Please begin by brainstorming — set out for yourself, before you commit to anything, what the genuine candidate open questions are: the correct formulation and scope of the disagreement norm; whether the norm should differ between first-order factual disputes and disputes about which method or framework to adopt; whether the standard idealisations (shared evidence, recognised peers, independence between the disagreeing parties) are load-bearing or merely convenient simplifications; what to do about the awkward fact that disagreement about the correct epistemology of disagreement is itself widespread; and whatever else you think belongs on the list, including lines I have not mentioned. Then rank the candidates and defend the ranking explicitly — which questions are upstream of which, which are tractable for someone working with conceptual tools rather than empirical data, which are neglected, and which look weighty but whose resolution would change little downstream. Where a question rests on an empirical assumption that a working scientist might actually be able to test, say so, and sketch what the test would look like.

Finally, tell me what would move an item up or down your ordering: what a decisive result on one of the top questions would look like, and what developments over the next few years should lead me to revise the list. I would rather have your reasoning than your ranking.
