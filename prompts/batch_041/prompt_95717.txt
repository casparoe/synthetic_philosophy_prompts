I retired two years ago after forty-odd years in geophysics (mantle convection mostly, with a long detour into seismology) and since then I've been reading philosophy alot. Most of it's been a joy. But I've hit an arguement I can't get around, and I'd like you to tell me where it goes wrong, because right now it's telling me my whole career was probably about things that aren't there.

Here it is, in steps, since that seems to be how it's done.

1. Take any scientific theory from the past that was, in its day, as well tested and as useful and as widely accepted as our best theories are now. Phlogiston. Caloric. The luminiferous aether. The steady-state universe. The continents nailed in place. Take your pick.
2. Every one of them was eventually thrown out.
3. And not thrown out because the old data turned out to be bad, but because the theory's central claims turned out to be false. There is no caloric fluid, no aether, no fixed continents. The thing the theory said was there wasn't.
4. So the record is: theories as well confirmed as ours have, every time, turned out to be false in their central claims.
5. We're not special, and the only evidence we have about how this goes is the record.
6. So our current best theories are probably false in their central claims too.
7. If a theory is probably false in its central claims, you shouldn't believe its central claims.
8. So I shouldn't believe the central claims of current physics or biology or my own field. Which is awkward, because I still do, and so, I notice, does just about everyone who argues about this.

I'm told this line of thought has a name, something about a pessimistic induction. Three things about it bother me and I can't tell which is the real problem.

First, step 4 only gets you anywhere if the historical cases are a fair sample, and I have a nagging feeling they aren't — that we remember the theories that got overturned and quietly forget all the core claims that just got kept and extended and never made the highlight reel. But when I try to make that objection sharp it collapses into "I bet it's fine," which isn't an argument.

Second, there's something slippery about the word theory. Step 1 counts a whole theory as one thing; step 6 hands down a verdict on it as one thing. But in my experience what dies and what survives don't line up that way — you lose the picture and keep the equations, or you keep the picture but shrink the domain it's allowed to cover, and then who exactly was the theory that got thrown out? I can feel a problem there and I can't name it without sounding like a lawyer.

Third, and this is the one that keeps me up: if steps 4 and 5 are a good way to reason about theories, then that reasoning is itself a claim about how the world works, and by its own logic I should be suspicious of it too. If the induction is exempt from the induction, I want to know what makes it exempt when nothing else is.

So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd most like to keep 1 through 4 — I was a grad student during the plate tectonics fight and watched a well-established picture get taken apart, so I know that part is true. What I'd most like to lose is 8. But I don't want to just refuse the conclusion because I don't like it; if the honest move is to swallow it, tell me, and tell me what I get to keep. And if the fix is to swap the word theory in one of these steps for something narrower, say which step, what it buys me, and what it breaks.
