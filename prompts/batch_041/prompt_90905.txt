I am a high school student in a philosophy of science seminar, and our class is peer-reviewing written answers; I have been asked to grade a classmate's response and would like a grade with the reasoning behind it. Here is the question and the answer.

Question. Ellsberg's two-urn problem: Urn A is known to contain 50 red and 50 black balls. Urn B contains 100 balls, each either red or black, but you are told nothing about the mix. Many people prefer to bet on red drawn from A over red from B, and also prefer to bet on black from A over black from B. (a) Explain how a model that assigns a set of probabilities rather than a single probability can represent this pair of preferences while still treating the agent as reasonable. (b) Name one serious objection to this approach and explain why it is an objection.

Answer. "(a) For Urn A the credence that a random draw is red is exactly 0.5. For Urn B we cannot pin down a number, so we replace the single value with the set of all values from 0 to 1, since the unknown mix could make red as rare as none or as common as all of them. The model is really a probability distribution over the possible objective mixes of Urn B, and that is what gives us the range. Preferring A to B for red, and also preferring A to B for black, is then just the agent preferring the bet whose probability is settled over the bet whose probability is a spread-out interval; the agent is ambiguity-averse, not irrational. (b) The main objection is dilation. If you have an interval for some event and then learn something you had judged irrelevant to it, the interval can actually get wider rather than narrower. A theory of rationality should not let plainly irrelevant information make you less certain, so dilation shows that reasoning with sets of probabilities violates the probability axioms."
