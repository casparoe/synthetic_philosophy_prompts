I am working through Kant's 1784 essay What Is Enlightenment? and am stuck on this passage: "The public use of one's reason must always be free, and it alone can bring about enlightenment among men. The private use of reason, on the other hand, may often be very narrowly restricted without particularly hindering the progress of enlightenment. By the public use of one's reason I understand the use which a person makes of it as a scholar before the reading public. Private use I call that which one may make of it in a particular civil post or office which is entrusted to him." The labels seem exactly backwards to me: the reason I exercise in an official post is the reason I exercise publicly, before others, whereas the reason I exercise as a scholar is my own private reflection. A colleague suggested that Kant is distinguishing not solitary from social reasoning but role-bound from role-free reasoning: the private use is the reason of someone speaking from within an office, and the public use is the reason of someone addressing the unrestricted reading public. She then proposed a further idea, which she called role-silencing: the claim that occupying a role can legitimately silence certain reasons that the same person may freely press in another capacity. I cannot tell whether this is a helpful reconstruction or an anachronistic distortion. Can you explain what Kant's distinction is doing in the essay, assess whether my colleague's gloss is faithful to it, and say what it implies for the contemporary question whether religious arguments should be excluded from political debate? Does the distinction support keeping religious reasons out of public political argument, or does it protect their public expression?
