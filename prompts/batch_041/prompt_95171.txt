Essay question: Explain Moore's account of the value of beauty and of friendship, and show how the method of isolation and the principle of organic unities function within it.

Student answer:

Moore's ethical theory is best understood as a form of pluralistic consequentialism. Against the hedonists, he argues that pleasure is not the only thing that is good in itself. In Principia Ethica (1903) he claims that the greatest goods we know are the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. These are not good as means to something else; they are good in themselves, and we know this by intuition rather than by inference.

The method of isolation is Moore's way of testing whether something is intrinsically good. We imagine a world containing only the thing in question, stripped of all its causes and consequences, and ask whether that world would still be better than a world containing nothing of value. Moore uses this test to show that a beautiful world with no one to appreciate it is better than an ugly world with no one to appreciate it. This proves that beauty is good in itself, and not merely because it gives pleasure to a spectator.

The principle of organic unities states that the value of a whole is always greater than the sum of the values of its parts. Moore applies this to friendship: the state of affairs consisting of a person's affection for another, together with the existence of the beloved person, is more valuable than the mere sum of the affection and the person taken separately. The same holds for the appreciation of beauty, where the enjoyment and the beautiful object together form a whole whose value exceeds that of the enjoyment alone.

For Moore, then, beauty and friendship are valuable because they are virtues, that is, because they are the kinds of things a good person ought to cultivate. The method of isolation confirms this by showing that we can determine what we ought to do by imagining the consequences of our actions in isolation from everything else. Moore's position is therefore a version of ideal utilitarianism, in which right action is action that maximises the intrinsic goods of aesthetic appreciation and personal affection.
