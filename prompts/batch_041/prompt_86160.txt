You are a philosopher of mathematics with expertise in the epistemology of foundational theories: the status of axioms, the epistemology of consistency claims, and the realism debate. I want you to act as an auditor of my own position rather than as an expositor of the literature.

I am preparing a short position statement for a seminar on the foundations of mathematics, and before I circulate it I want to know whether what I believe actually hangs together. My commitments are set out below. Please begin by brainstorming: for each commitment, state in your own words what it amounts to and what sort of consideration would move someone to hold it, so that you are clear about what each item is doing in the set. Then turn to the audit itself. I am not after references; I want the argument.

(1) Determinacy. Every well-formed mathematical sentence has a determinate truth value, and that value does not depend on which axioms we happen to adopt, on what we are able to prove, or on our conventions.

(2) Necessity. Mathematical truths are necessary. There is no possible world in which arithmetic comes out differently from the way it does.

(3) A priori knowledge of the axioms. The basic axioms of arithmetic strike me as self-evident, and I take myself to know that they are true, not merely to have adopted a fruitful formalism. I know, for instance, that every natural number has a successor, and I know it without appeal to experience.

(4) Openness about consistency. I cannot rule out that the basic axioms — of arithmetic, or of the standard set theory in which arithmetic is embedded — are inconsistent. No consistency proof available to me would really settle the matter, since any such proof would have to lean on principles no more trustworthy than the axioms it certifies. And were the axioms inconsistent, everything would follow from them, so I should have to abandon some of them and doubt results whose proofs depended on them.

(5) Moorean confidence. My confidence that arithmetic is consistent exceeds my confidence in any philosophical account of why mathematics is justified. If a respected colleague announced a derivation of a contradiction from the axioms, I should sooner look for the mistake in the derivation, or in my grasp of it, than conclude that arithmetic is inconsistent.

(6) Holism about justification. No axiom is justified on its own. The standard axioms earn their place as a system, through their fruitfulness, simplicity and indispensability to science; justification in mathematics is comparative and holistic, and there is no privileged foundation.

What I want from you:

(a) Identify which of these commitments pull against one another. Be specific about the mechanism of each conflict — what exactly it is about the two items that cannot both be satisfied — rather than gesturing at "tension".

(b) Separate the genuine conflicts from the pairs that only appear to conflict. Some pairs will be in tension only if a further assumption is smuggled in; where that is so, say what the assumption is and why I am entitled to reject it.

(c) Rank the genuine conflicts by severity. For each, say what would have to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set. Where a conflict admits of more than one repair, say which you would recommend and why.

(d) Tell me which single revision would clear up the most trouble, and what I should be giving up by making it. Finally, say whether the amended set is one a reasonable person could hold, or whether the amendments leave me with something unstable.

Be direct: if a commitment has to go, say which one and why, rather than offering a menu of equally acceptable options. Write your answer in British English.
