I'm a decision scientist, and a fight I lost at work last month has turned into a talk I have to give. The fight was over whether to run one more study before committing to a launch; my colleague's closing line was that information can never hurt. I think that line is a misuse of a real result — the one usually called Good's theorem, that for a Bayesian agent who will act after receiving free information, the expected value of that information is nonnegative, because the agent can always ignore it. The theorem is, I believe, true; the slogan is not. I've been asked to run a forty-five-minute session on this for my team: sharp people, comfortable with basic probability, no decision theory and no philosophy of science. Don't explain the theorem to me. Plan how I should explain it.

Tell me the order the pieces have to come in for the theorem to look like a near-triviality by the time I state it rather than a magic trick, and what each step buys the next — I assume acts, states, utilities and expected utility have to be in place before the theorem is even statable, and that the ex ante/ex post distinction has to be drawn before anyone can hear the caveats as anything but evasions. Name the single example that should carry the whole session and say what it must have and must lack; I am inclined to use the free clairvoyant who tells me tomorrow's weather before I choose what to wear, but if that is the wrong carrier, say so and give me the right one. Then map the misconceptions in the order my team will actually form them — I predict "the theorem says information always helps," then "if information can ever hurt, the theorem is false," then a blurring of the expected value of information with what the information does for you in the world that actually happens — and for each one, tell me the exact point in the sequence where I should head it off. Cover the conditions that bound the result — cost, non-Bayesian updating, preferences that shift when you learn, information that is itself part of the payoff, limits on computing the posterior and re-optimizing — and be explicit about which of these are settled results and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this; do not present a contested reading as the consensus. Finish with one question I can put to the room that would show whether the theorem has landed rather than been memorized, and tell me what I should leave out at this level and say out loud that I am leaving out.

Think this through carefully, and brainstorm the dependency structure before committing to an order. Give the reply in these sections: "The dependency order" (roughly 250 words), "The carrying example" (roughly 150 words), "The misconception map" (roughly 350 words), "What bounds the theorem" (roughly 200 words), "The check for understanding" (roughly 100 words), "What to leave out" (roughly 100 words).
