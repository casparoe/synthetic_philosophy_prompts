I am a professor of philosophy who works on personal identity, and I have been asked to serve as the ethicist on a hospital's neurotechnology review board. The board is about to consider a small number of protocols for what the engineers call serial micro-replacement: a patient's neurons are replaced, one percent at a time, over a period of months, with silicon prosthetics designed to be functionally identical at the level of input, output, and internal dynamics. The replacement is done in weekly sessions, with a full neuropsychological battery every ten percent, and the patient remains conscious and responsive throughout. The board is not asking whether the procedure is safe in the ordinary sense; it is asking how to decide, protocol by protocol, whether the person who emerges is the same person who consented, for purposes such as the validity of the original consent, the standing of an advance directive, the authority of a spouse or parent to speak for the patient, and the patient's own legal and moral claims. The board meets monthly, has no metaphysicians on it besides me, cannot run multi-year follow-up studies, and must give a recommendation to the hospital's ethics committee, which has final say. I want a procedure the board can actually run on a protocol in about ninety minutes, in the order of questions to ask, what would count as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure should stop with a clear no, and where it must hand the question to the ethics committee because it is a values question or a genuine indeterminacy rather than a factual one. It should be usable by a clinician or engineer who has read a little philosophy of personal identity but does not remember the details, and it should not pretend that we can measure psychological continuity with a number. Then run the procedure on two protocols: one for a patient with a progressive neurodegenerative disease who is replacing neurons to preserve function, and one for a healthy adult volunteer who wants to replace all of her neurons as a form of life extension. Include at least one case where the procedure yields an answer I will not like—I am inclined to think that identity is preserved throughout if the functional organization is preserved—and be explicit about what the procedure cannot decide. Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; I want the procedure and your reasoning about it. Write your answer as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—the way one would write a long letter to a colleague.
