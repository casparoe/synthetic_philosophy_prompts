ok, decision theory thing I can't stop chewing on. You know Buridan's ass -- donkey parked exactly halfway between two identical bales of hay, starves because there's no reason to prefer either one. Everyone tells it as a reductio of the idea that you need a reason to act: a rational agent with a utility function doesn't get stuck, because if the options are genuinely equal then either one is optimal, so eat already. I've read enough LW-adjacent stuff to know the standard machinery here (complete preferences, or you get money-pumped).

Fine. But institutions do the thing the ass can't do, as a matter of policy, and they act like it's the *most* defensible option rather than a last resort. If a race for a seat ends in an exact tie, some states settle it by drawing a name out of a bowl or flipping a coin, and nobody thinks the resulting legislator is illegitimate. When there are more applicants than slots for a visa or a school or a hunting tag and there's no principled ranking, we run a lottery and call that fair. Clinical trials randomize patients precisely when nobody knows which arm is better, and we treat the randomization as *more* trustworthy than the doctor's judgment, not less. Meanwhile the law will smack an agency down for deciding something arbitrarily, then bless a lottery for the very same scarce benefit.

So what view of preference and value is all this only making sense under? Heres my confusion: a coin flip looks like the cleanest possible case of choosing with no preference at all. But I cant tell whether the practice presupposes there's a real tie out in the world -- the options are equal, or incomparable, or some in-between thing where neither is better but they aren't equal either (I've seen people argue that state exists) -- or whether it only presupposes that the decider doesn't know which is better, which seems like a completely different condition that just happens to look the same from outside. Those pull in different directions about what arbitrariness even is.

So: reconstruct the theory of choice that this pattern presupposes. Tell me what's strictly required versus what merely fits (could complete-preferences-plus-ignorance, genuine incomparability, real indifference, and a preference for "either one" all license the same coin flips?), say where the practice is underdetermined, and tell me what would have to change on the ground if we adopted one of those readings over the others. And I want an actual verdict on whether the ass comes out irrational on the practice's own terms or whether the practice is quietly assuming no such agent could exist. American English please.
