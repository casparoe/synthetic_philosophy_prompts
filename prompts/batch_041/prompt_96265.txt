I have no philosophical training to speak of. I have spent about fifteen years playing in bands and running a small home studio, so I think about almost everything in terms of a signal path: a source, a transducer, a chain of processors, a mix bus, and finally whatever comes out of the speakers — governed by the rule that the master is the only thing anyone ever hears, and that everything upstream is legible only through it. Lately I have been reading Schopenhauer on my own, in particular the long discussion of music that closes the third book of his main work, and I keep finding that I can almost, but not quite, say what he is saying in the only technical vocabulary I genuinely own. My rough grasp of his claim is this: the other arts hold a mirror up to something that is already a copy, namely the world of appearances; music, by contrast, is a copy of what stands behind the appearances — of the striving that the appearances merely express — so that a melody is not a picture of some particular feeling but a direct rendering of the stuff feelings are made of. He seems to add that music discloses the inner sense of the world rather than its events, which is why it can move us profoundly while leaving us unable to say what it is about.

Could you restate that account, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use — sources and transducers, gain staging, the signal chain, monitoring, the difference between a score and a rendered waveform, the difference between a representation and the thing represented — and then give me the residue report? Which parts of what he says map cleanly onto something in my vocabulary, which parts survive only if I distort them, and which parts have no counterpart at all? I am especially unsure about the central move, the one that makes music unlike the other arts. In my world nothing is ever a copy of anything except by a chain of physical causes, and the notion of a signal that is somehow about the world's inner nature rather than about any particular state of it seems to have no place in that picture at all. So I would also like you to tell me whether the leftover is a defect in my vocabulary — a way of thinking about sound that simply cannot represent something real — or a sign that he was saying less than he supposed, that a word like expression is quietly doing work the argument ought to be doing. Please do not be gentle about this last point. I would far rather be told that the whole thing turns on an elaborate pun than go on half-understanding it.
