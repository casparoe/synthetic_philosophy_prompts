Last night I spent several hours arguing with a friend about the four-level picture of minds that distinguishes Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures — the picture according to which some creatures learn only across generations, some learn within a lifetime from the consequences of their own behaviour, some can test a hypothesis inside the head before acting on it, and some do their thinking partly with external tools: notations, institutions, other people's stored discoveries. My friend is a good philosopher, and he ended the evening with an argument I could not answer to my own satisfaction. I would rather rehearse the defence against a competent opponent than against the version of it I can knock down by myself.

Please play a committed defender of that hierarchy. Not a caricature, and not a historian reciting anyone's texts, but an unnamed theorist who actually holds the view and knows its resources. He should treat the four levels as genuine distinctions in kind, not merely as a scale of how long a creature takes to learn something. He may concede minor points, refine his definitions, and grant that the boundaries are messy in practice, but he must not surrender the claim that there is a real structural difference between the levels, and he must not retreat into saying that the levels are just a convenient taxonomy. He should talk like a philosopher mid-argument: precise, a little impatient, more interested in distinguishing senses of a term than in letting one slide. Keep him in character until I say "stop"; if I ask a factual question, answer it as he would rather than as an encyclopedia. Two or three paragraphs per reply is the right size — long enough to make a real move, short enough that it stays a conversation.

Here is my opening, which is my friend's argument and which I am half inclined to accept. The levels are supposed to be distinguished by where the trial and error happens: in the genome over evolutionary time, in the organism over its lifetime, in the imagination before action, in the culture's shared toolkit. But that is one mechanism, generate-and-test, with a variable for where the test is stored and how quickly it runs. A creature that learns from reinforcement is already keeping a model of which actions pay; a creature that simulates before acting is running the same learning off-line; a creature that thinks with notation and institutions is running it off-line across many heads at once. On that reading the hierarchy has no joints in it, the word "higher" is only a remark about latency, and all the explanatory work is being done by the single idea of generate-and-test, which the four-level picture merely decorates. So which is it — is the tower a structure, or is it a stopwatch?
