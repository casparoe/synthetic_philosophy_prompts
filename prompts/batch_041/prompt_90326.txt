A colleague in our decision-theory reading group has produced an argument that half of us now repeat and none of us can repair. It began from a case we kept running into: a screening test whose false-positive rate I only half remember, so that even the range of probabilities my evidence tolerates has no sharp edge. She argued that when evidence fails to settle a hypothesis, no single probability for it can be the right one, and that a rational doxastic state cannot be one whose correctness turns on a choice I made arbitrarily, since I could have chosen otherwise and nothing in the evidence would have objected. From this she concluded that in such cases the rational state is not a probability but the set of probabilities the evidence permits. That much I can live with. What I cannot live with is what she did next, and I cannot locate the error.

Here is the argument as she sets it out.

(1) If your evidence does not determine a unique probability for a hypothesis, then any single probability you adopt is arbitrary, since some other value fits the evidence equally well.

(2) No rational doxastic state is such that its correctness depends on an arbitrary choice by the agent.

(3) Therefore, where the evidence leaves the probability open, the rational state is the set of probabilities the evidence permits, rather than any one member of that set.

(4) The evidence does not determine the exact membership of that set any more than it determined a single value; for any set that fits the evidence, a set differing by one hairline case fits it equally well.

(5) So by (1) and (2), the rational state at that level is the set of all such sets, and the same reasoning recurs at every level without end.

(6) Hence the requirement that a rational doxastic state be determinate is unsatisfiable at every level.

(7) No agent can hold an infinitely ascending hierarchy of states, and a state that is indeterminate at every level cannot guide action; a theory of rational belief that can be neither held nor acted on is no theory of rational belief.

(8) Therefore the project of representing rational belief by probabilities, whether by one or by a set, rests on a mistake.

I would rather keep (2) and (3) than accept (8): (3) is the whole point of the machinery, and (2) is what makes it more than a preference. Yet the same steps, run on a hypothesis or on a choice of action rather than on a probability, would show that evidence never determines what to believe or do about anything, including what it determines about her own premises, and that cannot be right.

I can see three exits and dislike all of them. Deny (1) and insist that evidence fixes a unique probability after all; deny (2) and concede that rational states may be arbitrary at the margin; or refuse (7) and live with the hierarchy. Tell me whether any of these is right, or whether the fault lies somewhere I have not thought to look. For whichever step you reject, explain why it looked compelling and price the commitment: what must I give up to hold the line there, and what does the rival who takes a different exit have to say against me?
