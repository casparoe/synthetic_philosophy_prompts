For some years I have kept a private notebook of the moments when my mind changes, and I have arrived at a puzzle I cannot settle by thinking about it on my own.

The ordinary case is this. I glance across the room at a clock. I cannot read it, but the glance is not nothing: a moment before, I would have given even odds to its being about half past two or about three; a moment after, I am fairly sure it is near three and barely entertain half past two. Nothing was established — I did not come away knowing the time — but something in me moved. In the same way, I lift a parcel and cannot say what it weighs, yet I would now bet against its being under a kilogram; I hear a sound through a wall and cannot tell whether a word was spoken, yet I am now more inclined to think someone is in the next room.

Two things about these episodes bother me.

First, when I try to say what I actually learned, I find I must choose how finely to say it, and the choice is not innocent. I can take the glance to have told me that the time is probably within a quarter of an hour of three; or that it is probably between a quarter to three and a quarter past, with no opinion inside that interval; or that the minute hand is probably somewhere on the left of the dial. These are not rival theories of what happened to me — they are the same impression drawn with a finer or a coarser pencil — yet they leave me with different opinions about things that have nothing to do with clocks: whether I have missed the beginning of something, whether the soup is ready. The experience does not arrive with a grain marked on it, and I appear to be choosing my own evidence after the fact, in a way that changes what I am entitled to conclude. I should add that this is not the familiar trouble of being unsure what I saw. I am not in doubt that I saw the clock; I am in doubt about how much of what I saw to count as evidence.

Second, these half-learnings arrive in an order, and the order seems to matter. If the half-heard word comes before the half-seen clock, I end somewhere different from where I end when the two come the other way round, though the two impressions are the same two impressions in both cases and neither ever rose to certainty. Worse, the difference does not seem to be erased when I later learn the truth outright: finding out what the time actually was does not put me back where the other order would have left me. If that is right, then what a person ought to believe is not fixed by their evidence at all, but by the sequence in which their evidence arrived — and two people with the same experiences and the same starting opinions could be permanently divided by nothing but the order of their lives.

I do not want a label; a label would leave me exactly where I am. What I want to know is which part of this has actually been thought through, and what my description gets wrong. Is the fineness of description one problem and the order another, or one problem seen twice? Is either of them counted a real defect in the usual account of how an opinion should answer to experience, or are they both thought to be artefacts of describing the situation badly — and if so, what is the right description? Is there an established name for the case where what reaches me is not a fact but a nudge, and a different name for the fact that nudges do not combine in a settled order? And for each of those, what is the nearest familiar idea, so that I can see where the familiar thing stops and my puzzle begins?
