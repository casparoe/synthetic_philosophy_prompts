I am a postdoc working on the foundations of game theory, and next month I am presenting a working paper at a half-day workshop whose audience is split three ways. I want a Q&A preparation sheet for the question period, not a summary or a defense of the paper.

The thesis I will defend is this. The solution concept standardly called program equilibrium — two agents each submit a program, each program is handed the other's source code before it chooses an action, and a pair of programs is in equilibrium when neither agent could have got a better payoff by submitting a different program — is not a solution concept at all. It is a self-reference argument wearing the clothes of an equilibrium argument. My claim is that every one of its striking results, including the mutual cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma that is always the headline, depends on reading program as source code that the opponent can actually simulate, and that under that reading the fixed point is not located in the space of strategies but in the relation between what program A outputs and what program B's simulation of A outputs. Make that explicit and the equilibrium framing looks like a misnomer doing rhetorical work: it lets the results inherit the credibility of Nash equilibrium while the real work is done by shared simulation plus the strength of the agents' proof system, which the standard presentation leaves implicit. My positive proposal is to reformulate the concept as a fixed point in the closure of what each agent can prove about the other's specification. That makes proof-theoretic strength an explicit parameter, and I argue it licenses cooperation in some games where the standard concept forbids it and forbids it in some where the standard concept licenses it.

The audience, whom I need you to model precisely:

- Roughly eight game theorists, most of them working on the epistemic foundations of solution concepts. They think a concept earns its keep only when it can be characterised as a fixed point of beliefs plus rationality, or recovered as the limit of a learning process, and they are unimpressed by concepts that can only be stated.
- Three researchers from an AI safety group who work on multi-agent interaction between systems that are not given access to one another's code. They will care about what survives when the assumptions are relaxed and will be allergic to results that hold only for logically omniscient reasoners.
- One programming-languages person who does program verification, who will care about the difference between a proof existing and a proof being findable within a resource budget.

I want the objections I will actually get from each of these groups, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that group would phrase it. For each: a two-minute reply, and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede at the outset, which objection I should not try to win, and which single exchange has to go my way for the paper to be received as making a contribution rather than re-describing a known trick. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks. I will supply the references myself; you need not cite anything.
