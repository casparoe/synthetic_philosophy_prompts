I write a science-and-ideas column, and I am commissioning a piece for a special issue on the demarcation problem — how, if at all, we distinguish legitimate history from pseudohistory, the question readers know from the science side as why astrology is not astronomy. I want the piece to come at the question from inside the camp we usually laugh at rather than from the pulpit, and I want it to stay funny for a reader who actually knows the literature.

The piece is a keynote address, delivered at the annual meeting of a small society of 'independent chronologists' whose members hold that the conventional timeline of antiquity is a late fabrication: that the classical texts are medieval compositions, that the ancient world was substantially manufactured in the Renaissance, and that much of what we call ancient history is a duplicate of medieval events under other names. Invent the speaker and the society; do not name any living researcher, and do not put words in any real person's mouth. The address is titled something like 'Falsifiability Is on Our Side.'

The conceit is that the speaker argues, without irony, that his programme and not the academic consensus is the genuinely scientific one — and he argues it with the real tools. Have him work the standard apparatus: the falsifiability criterion; the distinction between progressive and degenerating research programmes, and the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses; the Duhem–Quine point that no hypothesis meets the evidence alone; the observation that within the orthodoxy an anomaly is always absorbed rather than allowed to dislodge anything. He should deal with the load-bearing evidence of ancient chronology — radiocarbon and dendrochronology, astronomical records, coin sequences, the synchronisms that tie Egyptian and Assyrian king lists together — not by denying it but by the auxiliary-hypothesis move, and he should be candid, in a way that gives the game away, about what would actually cause him to abandon his chronology.

Keep it deadpan: no winking, no narrator's aside, no sneering. The speaker is sincere, courteous, faintly wounded, and believes every word. The humour has to come from the reasoning itself, so gloss the technical terms in passing, as a speaker would for a sympathetic but non-specialist audience, and let the philosophy do work inside the argument rather than reading as a tour of the demarcation literature. Around 800 words of continuous prose; no footnotes, no citations, no invented quotations from real texts. End on a line that leaves the reader less comfortable than before with the criterion they had been using to dismiss him — and if you can, let that discomfort come from the speaker turning his own test, in passing, on something respectable.
