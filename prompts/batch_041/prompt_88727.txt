I retired three years ago from an experimental physics department, and I have spent the time since trying to repair a long-postponed education in philosophy. I now audit a second- and third-year seminar in the history of science, and the convenor has asked me to take a session of my own. My subject is Hobbes on geometry: not his mathematical results, which by common consent are negligible, but the place geometry occupies in his thinking, which I have come to believe is stranger and more load-bearing than the standard treatment allows. I know the outlines well enough by now — that he came to Euclid only in his forties, by accident, in a gentleman's library, and was converted on the spot; that he held geometry to be demonstrable precisely because we draw the figures ourselves, whereas in natural philosophy we are ignorant of the construction and must argue from effects back to possible causes; that he placed civil philosophy in the same class as geometry on the ground that we make the commonwealth ourselves; that he spent decades attempting to square the circle and to duplicate the cube, was answered at length by John Wallis, and was mocked for it by the mathematicians of his day, never becoming a Fellow of the Royal Society. What I cannot do is make any of this teachable. So I am not asking you to explain Hobbes's philosophy of geometry to me; I am asking for a plan of explanation.

Tell me what has to come first, and what depends on what — in particular, whether the Euclid anecdote should open the session or is mere colour that must wait until the methodological point is already in place. Tell me which single example or case should carry the whole session, and what it must have and must not have if it is to do that work; I have been tempted by the squaring of the circle, since it shows the account of demonstration generating both his confidence and his blindness at once, but I suspect it is illegible until the methodological point is established, and I would like to be told so if that is right. Tell me which misconceptions the students will bring and which the explanation itself will create, where each comes from, and the exact point in the sequence at which I should head each one off. I am already expecting that they will assume the geometry was meant to make politics quantitative or axiomatic in some way it never is; that they will read the circle-squaring as simple incompetence rather than as a consequence of his view of what a demonstration is; and that they will conflate his account of our making the figures with the modern thought that mathematical objects are stipulated conventions. I also want one closing question that will show me whether they have grasped the relation between geometry and civil philosophy, as opposed to being able merely to recite that he admired Euclid.

The session is seventy-five minutes, with about twenty students, some of whom have no mathematics past school level and some of whom have done Euclid properly; I have a whiteboard and nothing else. I would rather have one idea land than four be gestured at, so tell me what I should simply refuse to attempt at this level, and say so explicitly, and whether I should end by opening the political philosophy or by leaving it as a promissory note.
