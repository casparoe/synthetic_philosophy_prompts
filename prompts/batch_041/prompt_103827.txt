Last weekend a friend and I burned most of a dinner arguing about the citizens' assembly our city convened to break a deadlock over a housing ordinance, and somewhere around the second hour I realized we weren't even disagreeing about the same thing: he kept hearing me as defending rule by amateurs, while what actually moves me is that nobody in that room had to raise money or run for anything to get there. That gap between the reasons people give for sortition and the institutions they would really endorse is what I want mapped, so I'm putting together a sorting quiz for the reading group we both sit in, and I'd like you to write it. Please produce eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options labeled A, B, and C so a scoring key can refer to them — no flattering self-descriptions, and no item that asks whether the respondent favors sortition, trusts ordinary people, or values fairness in the abstract. Every question has to be a specific scenario, or a specific claim about one, on which two people who both say "I'm for sortition" will predictably answer differently, and after each item please add a single line saying which two positions it is designed to separate, so I can cut anything that isn't earning its place. I want these cases covered: whether an assembly's recommendation should override a referendum when the two conflict; whether the sample should be stratified to match the population's demographics or left a pure lottery; whether members should be paid and staffed like legislators or kept as amateurs on jury-duty terms; whether deliberations should be televised and minuted or held in private to shield members from pressure; whether an assembly may set its own agenda or must take the question put to it; whether a lottocratic body should be purely advisory, a reviewing chamber with a veto, or a full replacement for one of the elected houses; whether a second, independently drawn lottery should audit the first body's work; whether service should be compulsory with penalties for refusal or opt-in for volunteers; and whether the whole arrangement should be a one-off experiment or a standing institution. Add one or two cases of your own if they cut more cleanly than mine, but stop at ten.

For the scoring key, map answer patterns onto the positions I care about distinguishing, giving each a short name and one sentence on the distinctive commitment it carries: (a) the mirror or descriptive-representation view, on which the point of a lottery is that the body resembles the public it governs; (b) the cognitive-diversity view, on which random selection matters because varied heuristics and lived experience outperform a room of specialists, and demographic mirroring is at most a means to that end; (c) the anti-corruption view, on which the value is that no one had to court donors or voters, so the lottery is a device for removing a bad incentive rather than for improving judgment; (d) the non-domination view, on which being governed by a randomly chosen peer differs in kind from being governed by anyone's will, so the good realized is freedom rather than accuracy; (e) the pure fair-chance view, on which equal probability of selection exhausts the justification and no claim about the quality of the resulting decisions is needed; (f) the advisory-only view, on which assemblies are valuable as inputs to elected bodies and should never hold decision power; and (g) the full lottocratic view, on which elections should be largely replaced. Where a respondent's answers don't land on any single position, say so and describe the mixed position they've built instead of forcing a fit, and if two of the positions above cannot be separated by any of the questions, flag the pair rather than pretending the key distinguishes them — I'm especially curious whether (b) and (d) genuinely come apart on these cases, and whether anyone who picks the stratified option can consistently hold (d), so tell me which items are doing that separating work. Close with a short paragraph on what the quiz measures and a sentence or two each on what a reader should make of an unresolved profile. Write in American English.
