So I'm in this philosophy-of-probability reading group and two of us have been going round in circles for months about the reference class problem, and somehow I've been nominated to write the doc we should have produced ages ago. On one side there's Marta, who thinks that when you ask "what's the probability this particular patient responds to the drug," there is a fact of the matter about the right reference class, and it's fixed by the objective structure of the situation -- roughly, the class you get by asking which features of the case are such that intervening on them would shift the outcome distribution. If the outcome wouldn't budge under an intervention, she says, that feature doesn't get to carve the class, full stop. On the other side there's Dev, a card-carrying subjectivist who says probabilities are just degrees of belief, a reference class is just a feature of your evidence partition, and asking which class is "the correct one" is like asking which coordinate system is the correct one -- the only real constraints are coherence and calibration, and any disagreement that survives shared evidence is a disagreement about priors, not about the world. The annoying thing is that they agree on every toy example and still can't stand each other. Our friend Priya has also proposed a rule she's convinced settles it: when several classes are available, always use the narrowest one for which you have at least, say, a hundred cases; if none qualifies, fall back to the broadest. Marta says that's arbitrary, Dev says it's fine as a heuristic but Priya is smuggling in a truth-claim, and neither of them will say what would actually change their mind.

What I want from you is the joint statement they could both really put their names to. Give me a numbered section of agreed claims -- about what a reference class is, about what the problem is a problem *for*, about whether the same difficulty bites for a Bayesian who only ever conditions on her evidence, about what exchangeability does and doesn't buy you, and about the cases where the two of them do converge. Then a disagreements table: for each genuine disagreement, the exact statement of that side's view in wording that side would accept for itself, plus the crux -- the sub-question such that settling it would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, name a concrete case or argument they'd both agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each of them predicts about that case. And if part of the fight turns out to be verbal -- e.g. if "the correct reference class" is doing different work in Marta's mouth than in Dev's -- say which part and why, but don't cop out by declaring the whole dispute a pseudo-problem. Don't let the agreed-claims section be vacuous (they both already agree a probability is a number between 0 and 1) or slanted (a claim Dev would only sign because it's conveniently ambiguous).
