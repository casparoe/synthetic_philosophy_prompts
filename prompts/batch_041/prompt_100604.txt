I am part of a small interdisciplinary group — two of us come from philosophy and cognitive science, one can code — and we have roughly eighteen months, a modest budget, and no large labeled dataset to work with. We want to lay conceptual groundwork for a problem in the spirit of the old Letter Spirit project, where Hofstadter and McGraw asked what it would take for a program to design a complete alphabet, all twenty-six lowercase letters, in a single coherent new style after being shown only one or two seed letters in that style. We are not attempting the full system; we want a defensible theoretical framing plus a small prototype that tests the framing. The puzzle we keep circling is double-sided. Show a person a pair of letters, say a 'g' and a 'q', drawn in some invented, unfamiliar style, and they can produce a plausible 'a' in that same style — and a third party will agree it belongs to that alphabet — even though no two people would draw the same 'a', and even though the style itself was never described in words. Yet the same person will reject an 'a' that is only slightly off in a way they cannot articulate. We find both halves of this baffling, and we suspect the second half is the more revealing one. Our toy domain is a small grid of line-segments on which letters are drawn, so that every letterform is a finite, inspectable object; we are already unsure whether that simplification is faithful to the phenomenon or quietly changes it.

The questions we keep arguing about, roughly in the order they surface in conversation, are these. What is the essence of a letter-category: is there a defining bundle of features that all 'a's share, is it a prototype or a central tendency, or is it something more relational, so that an 'a' is partly whatever stands in the right contrast to the 'o', 'e', and 'd' of the same style? Is the style of a font a single global transformation that can be applied to any letter, a small set of shared high-level design decisions, or a looser family resemblance among the letters that no one rule captures? How much of our readiness to accept a wild 'a' — a double-storey one, a single-storey one, the 'a' of a blackletter face, a cursive 'a' — is recognition of category membership, and how much is recognition of stylistic kinship, and can these two be separated at all? What work does analogy do here: when someone extrapolates a new 'a' in a seen style, are they mapping the seed letters onto the target, transferring a style-role, or doing something else? Is there any single canonical 'a', or only a spread of acceptable ones, and if only a spread, what bounds it? And how would we even tell when a dispute among us is about the artifact in front of us versus about the perceiver's concepts?

Which of these open questions should we prioritize, and why? I am less interested in the list than in the reasoning. Please rank the questions for our specific situation — three people, eighteen months, no big dataset, aiming at one strong conceptual paper and a small prototype — and defend the ordering using criteria you make explicit: how many of the other questions depend on this one, whether it is tractable for people without large-scale data, whether answering it would actually change what we build or how we evaluate it, and how neglected it is relative to the others. Tell me which questions you would deprioritize because their answers would not ripple downstream, and which single question, if we could settle only one, would leave us best positioned on all the rest. Finally, say what evidence or argument over the next year would move a question up or down our list, and whether there is a question we are not asking that ought to dominate the ones above. I want a defended agenda, not a survey.
