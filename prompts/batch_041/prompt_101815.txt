For the past year I have served as the technical adviser to my city's charter review commission, which is deciding whether to replace plurality with a ranked ballot, and that work has pushed me toward a claim I now have to defend before people who will not be gentle with it: that the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is a red herring for reform of this kind. The theorem needs a universal domain, and real electorates do not have one — preferences over the relevant alternatives are single-peaked along a left–right axis, at least roughly and possibly only single-crossing — so a median-style rule is strategy-proof and onto, and the impossibility never gets a grip. In two weeks I give this as a seminar at our joint economics and political science workshop, and I want to know what I will actually be asked. The room is: two social choice theorists who know the theorem, its proof, and its variants cold; a mechanism design theorist who regards dominant-strategy incentive compatibility as the wrong benchmark and will want to know why I am not working instead with the idea that a mechanism should be transparently, and not merely technically, incentive compatible; an empirical political scientist who studies turnout, has no formal theory, and will ask what any of this has to do with the ballot a voter actually sees; and a philosopher of the social sciences who will press on whether strategy-proofness is worth buying at the price of everything else we might want from a voting rule. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will really get, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that particular member of the audience would phrase it, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is weakest. I want the standard escape routes from the theorem adjudicated rather than recited — restricting the domain, including what happens once we are in more than one dimension; dropping single-valuedness or onto-ness so that some alternative is simply never chosen; dichotomous preferences; and the randomized setting, where I am told that strategy-proofness together with ex post efficiency leaves you with random dictatorship — so check that claim for me and tell me whether it helps my case or destroys it. Finally, tell me what to concede at the outset, what to hold, and the one question I am hoping nobody asks.
