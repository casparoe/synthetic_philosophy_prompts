You are a philosopher of science with expertise in formal epistemology and the epistemic foundations of game theory. I'd like you to act as a careful auditor of my own views rather than as an expositor of the literature.

By way of background: I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I build probabilistic models for a living, and over the past year I've been reading around Bayesian epistemology and the theory of rational agreement in my spare time — including a fair amount on the common prior assumption that seems to sit behind so much of it. I've come out of that reading with the following commitments, and I genuinely cannot tell whether they hang together. Please work through the pairwise interactions carefully before committing to an answer; I'd rather you spend your space on how these claims bear on one another than on restating each one back to me.

(1) Convergence. If two agents are perfectly rational, have exactly the same evidence, and are fully aware of each other's opinions and of the reasoning behind them, they cannot rationally go on disagreeing about a factual matter. Once each knows what the other thinks and why, any surviving disagreement has to be a failure of rationality on someone's part.

(2) Permissivism about priors. A prior is nothing more than a statement of my own degrees of belief. There is no fact of the matter about which prior is correct, and two agents who differ only in their priors can both be perfectly rational.

(3) The ignorance default. When I really have no information bearing on a question, the maximum-entropy prior is the one I ought to adopt, because it is the least committal assumption available.

(4) The group as a single agent. When a team has to produce one credence, the right procedure is to pool all of the members' evidence and then apply a single shared prior to it — the group should end up behaving like one ideal agent rather than like an average of its members.

(5) Calibration. A probabilistic forecast can be objectively good or bad: if my 80% predictions come true 60% of the time, my model is defective, and that is a fact about the world rather than about my tastes.

(6) Partial deference. When a better-informed colleague disagrees with me, I should move some way towards their credence but not all the way; I should keep some weight on my own prior.

What I want is an audit of the set. Specifically: (a) which of these are genuinely in tension with which, and how severe each tension is; (b) which pairs only look tense but are in fact compatible once read charitably, and why; (c) for each genuine tension, what would have to give and what that would cost me elsewhere in the set; and (d) which single revision would clear up the most tension at the lowest price.
