I'm an undergraduate in a course on naturalism and metaethics, and our unit on Quine left me with a question I can't settle on my own. Quine's case against moral truth turns on a contrast: science has observation sentences that serve as intersubjective checkpoints, while ethics, on his account, has no such checkpoints, so moral verdicts come out more like expressions of sentiment than like theoretical claims. I'd like a dialogue that puts pressure on that contrast instead of restating it.

Please write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers. The first is a Quinean naturalist who accepts Quine's rejection of the analytic/synthetic distinction and his insistence that there is no first philosophy, and who argues that the absence of genuine moral observation sentences makes moral discourse noncognitive. The second is a moral realist who also calls herself a naturalist and thinks Quine's own methodological commitments tell against his metaethics.

The exchange should turn on a device I'll stipulate, so that neither party can simply cite the literature. Suppose a cheap, reliable neural implant — call it the consensus electrode — brings any two wearers who consider the same concrete case to the same moral verdict within a minute or two, without altering their other beliefs or their ability to describe what happened. The realist uses this to argue that intersubjective checkpoints for ethics are a contingent engineering problem rather than a conceptual gap, and that the wearers observe moral properties much as we observe colours. The Quinean must then explain what work the notion of observation is doing if the electrode produces consensus with no sensory confrontation with a fact, and should press the realist on whether her view collapses into relativism about whichever consensus device happens to be installed.

Have them go on to dispute the descriptive/normative boundary: whether a Quinean who rejects the analytic/synthetic distinction can still treat the lack of moral observation sentences as a principled line rather than an empirical generalization about human psychology, and whether the realist's appeal to moral experience is compatible with Quine's claim that epistemology is a chapter of natural science. Make the realist say what would count as the electrode failing to deliver a checkpoint, and make the Quinean concede whatever needs conceding. End with the two of them isolating exactly what they still disagree about, stated so that it could be settled by an experiment or an argument rather than by choosing a vocabulary.
