I'm putting together the final for my upper-level phil of science seminar, and the last unit is Popper — we do the demarcation stuff, then the Darwin material, then a bit of Lakatos for contrast. I want one essay question I can actually grade, and I keep failing to write it myself, so heres what I need.

What I want students to do is NOT just report that Popper said Darwinism is unfalsifiable and then said oops never mind. They all do that. It's the wikipedia version, and it makes Popper look like he just got bullied by biologists into backing down. What I want them to get at is what the episode shows about his own position: whether calling something a metaphysical research programme is an insult in his framework or not, which bit of Darwinism he was actually calling near-tautological, what the recantation does and doesnt concede, and why the later move — that selection claims become testable once you specify the situation the organism is in — is or isnt just a patch bolted on to save face.

So: the question should be answerable in 90 minutes, essay form, closed book. Level is senior undergrad / MA crossover, so they know falsificationism, they know the rough Popper–Lakatos difference, and I've told them in lecture that Popper's late view involves something like a situational-logic reading of selection, plus his sympathy for the thought that organisms' own behaviour and preferences can precede and reshape the selection pressures acting on them. That last bit is the one nobody ever talks about and it's honestly the most interesting part of the whole thing, so the question should reward a student who brings it in.

Give me three things: the exam question itself; a model answer of about 600 words, written at the level of a strong student rather than a monograph, because I want to see the moves and not every hedge; and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific moves that cap you at a B or below. For the marking scheme I want five must-hit points, each with a mark value, and seperate out a one-line note for each on how students usually get that point wrong. Also flag the two or three howlers that should cost real marks — e.g. treating metaphysical as meaning meaningless for Popper, or thinking the recantation was about whether evolution happened at all.

One constraint: don't build the question around memorized titles, dates, or page numbers. I haven't given them any, and I don't want to grade people on citation recall. Referring to the Darwin College lecture and to the autobiographical claim is fine, as long as the question itself spells out what the claims were so nobody has to have read the exact text.
