For several years a colleague and I have co-taught a graduate seminar on moral particularism and generalism, and I have come to think the debate is stuck in a way that is more philosophically interesting than the arguments on either side. The generalist holds that moral judgment and moral knowledge depend on true universal moral generalizations that can figure in reasoning: without them there is no way to learn to judge well, no way to adjudicate disagreement, and no way for a verdict to be justified rather than merely had. The particularist holds that reasons are holistic, so that a feature which is a reason for a verdict here may be a reason against it, or no reason at all, elsewhere; from this it is concluded that no finite set of exceptionless principles can capture moral reality, and that competence in morals is a matter of discernment rather than application. The familiar exchanges have not moved either camp in decades. The generalist's worry about learning and justification is met with an analogy to perceptual and practical discernment; the particularist's counterexamples are met with the observation that principles were never supposed to be exceptionless, since a principle can be a defeasible default, a prima facie duty, or a rule of thumb.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the dispute is largely verbal, turning on what counts as a principle and on what principles are for, with two non-verbal residues. The first is methodological: a disagreement about the evidential standing of intuitions about exotic cases, where the particularist needs those cases to be credible and the generalist needs to be entitled to redescribe them as cases in which the principle's conditions were never met. The second is epistemic: a disagreement about whether moral justification must be codifiable, that is, whether one must be able to state what justifies one. I am also struck by a wrinkle I cannot make anything of. Particularists generally accept that there can be no moral difference without some other difference, which looks like conceding a universal generalization of some sort. Either that sort of generalization is not a principle, in which case the quarrel concerns the function of principles rather than their existence, or the particularist owes an account of why it does not count.

What I want from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two groups answering different questions under a single heading, one about what makes an act right and one about how one comes to know it? If a large part of it is verbal, what exactly is left once the terminological debris is cleared, and is the residue worth arguing about? Does the particularist's acceptance of supervenience-style generalizations help, or is it a red herring? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress, whether a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or an empirical result about how people actually reason morally, or should we expect none? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims rather than surveying the literature, and state how confident you are in the diagnosis and which part of it you are least sure of.
