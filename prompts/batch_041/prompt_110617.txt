I'm a software engineer at a small dev-tools company (about 40 people, and I'm the only person here who does anything resembling user research). Every other week I run a think-aloud session with a customer on our CLI and dashboard, and afterwards I ask them why they did what they did — why they went to the settings page, why they ignored the warning banner, why they expected the command to work the way they expected it to. I write these up and they feed straight into the roadmap, and some of our bigger decisions have hung on a single sentence somebody said in a 30-minute call. Lately I've been reading around an idea I don't know how to evaluate: that people might not have much access to the causes of their own judgments, so when someone explains why they clicked something they may be generating a plausible story rather than reporting something they can actually see, and the story gets assembled out of whatever theory they have about themselves and about software like ours. I dont know the literature at all, I'm just an engineer, but if even half of that is right it changes what I'm supposed to do with these sessions. And I've noticed something that bugs me: users' explanations drift. Same person, same task, and the reason they gave me in the session is not the reason they gave in a follow-up email a week later. My teammate says the strict version of this is that a verbal report is a second-order inference about a first-order process, and the only data you can really trust is what you can see on the screen — which sounds right until I remember that I can't see what's on the screen either without asking them what they were looking at.

What I want is a decision procedure I can actually run, either live in the session or when I write the notes up that evening. Something like: the questions to ask, in order; what counts as a satisfying answer to each; when I should treat a verbal report as evidence about what was going on in someone's head and when I should treat it as an artifact that I created by asking; and what I should change about how I run the sessions so I'm not leaning on access I can't have. Constraints: 45-minute sessions, one person, no training, no budget for a second observer, and my PM treats my notes as the priority signal for the next sprint, so a wrong call costs us weeks. It needs to be honest about which steps are actual method and which are just me being careful and writing it down. Then run it on three of my real cases: the user who said the error message told them the config was fine (I'm pretty sure they misread it, they're pretty sure they didn't), the user who genuinely could not say why they picked the dropdown over the flag, and the one who gave me a confident walkthrough of our auth flow that contradicts everything they actually did on screen. I'd rather find out that one of those makes the procedure hand me an answer I don't want than get a procedure that always agrees with me, and I want to know plainly what the procedure can't do. Also, tell me how confident you are in the parts of this, and single out the one point you're least sure about.
