I am a novelist, and for a book I am writing I have been reading the medieval discussions of tyrannicide, especially Aquinas. What interests me is less the conclusion than the shape of the argument, and I want to know whether the shape has a name. Aquinas appears to allow that a tyrant may be killed, but the permission is divided. A man who seizes power without title is, on his account, fair game for anyone; a man who holds power legitimately but rules tyrannically may not be dispatched by private persons, and the remedy lies with public authority, or, if no such authority exists, with God. He also warns that killing one tyrant tends to produce another, and that the upheaval may be worse than the disease. The upshot is a doctrine that seems to license a dramatic act while making the licence almost impossible to use in the cases that actually arise. The permission is real on paper, but it is hedged by conditions that, taken together, leave the ordinary subject with little to do but endure or pray.

That is the pattern I keep noticing, not only in Aquinas: a theory grants a radical remedy, but attaches to it a set of individually sensible conditions — proper authority, no superior to appeal to, a reasonable prospect of improvement, the risk of a worse successor — such that the remedy is available only in a vanishingly small class of cases, and perhaps in none at all. The theory can then be praised for its moral seriousness, because it does not flinch from the hard case, while in practice it counsels submission. It is not simple hypocrisy, because the conditions are sincerely meant; nor is it merely a prohibition dressed up as a permission, because the permission is not a sham — there really are conditions under which it holds. And it is not the same as an ordinary proviso or exception, because the exceptions are not marginal; they swallow the rule.

Is there a name for this? I am looking for the established concept, if there is one, for a permission whose conditions of application are so constructed that it is nearly always inoperative, and which thereby secures the rhetorical advantage of a bold doctrine while producing the practical effect of a conservative one. I would like to know how it relates to the nearest ideas — the lesser evil, the right of resistance, the distinction between a usurper and a tyrant in exercise, the notion of a remedy that is available only when there is no lawful authority to invoke — and where the edges of the phenomenon lie. If there is no name, I should like a term coined and defined, with a clear statement of what it is not. I am less interested in the label than in the carving.
