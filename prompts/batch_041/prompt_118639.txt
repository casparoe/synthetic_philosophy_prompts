I am a junior at a high school with a small philosophy club, and we are assembling an end-of-term zine whose theme is the procreation asymmetry — the argument, associated with David Benatar, that there is a reason not to bring someone into existence if that life would be mostly suffering, but no matching reason to bring someone into existence merely because that life would be mostly happy. Our faculty advisor drew the familiar two-by-two on the whiteboard: pain present, bad; pain absent, good even though no one enjoys that good; pleasure present, good; pleasure absent, not bad unless there is somebody for whom the absence counts as a deprivation. I can follow the table as a table. What I cannot stop noticing is that the two absence cells are the same kind of item — a blank, an unoccupied place, nobody there to be either spared or deprived — and that the whole argument turns on our reading one of those blanks as a good and the other as a mere nothing. I would like a poem for the zine that makes that hinge visible rather than merely asserting it, and that does not settle whether the hinge is the argument's deepest insight or its central sleight of hand. (The page will sit opposite a comic another member is drawing about the repugnant conclusion, so it should hold its own visually as well as argumentatively.)

Please write a poem of four stanzas of four lines each, one stanza per cell of the table, in whatever rhyme and meter you can sustain without strain. The constraint I care about is this: the two absence stanzas should be as close to word-for-word identical as the sense will allow, differing only in the single line that assigns a value to what is absent, while the two presence stanzas should be near-mirrors of one another, since both of them have someone in them to bear the pain or the pleasure. A reader who knows the argument should be able to point at the poem and say which stanza is which cell, and where exactly the asymmetry enters — whether it is smuggled in or earned.

For the ending, I want the obvious objection registered rather than resolved: in the absence stanzas there is no one to be the beneficiary, so it is unclear in what sense an unexperienced absence could be good for anyone at all. Rather than have the poem answer that, I would like it to end with the grammar of benefit coming apart — a verb of benefit with no subject to attach to, a for with nobody after it — so that the reader is left holding the question. Give it a title, keep it under twenty lines, and please do not let it become either a sermon for antinatalism or a joke at its expense; the club is genuinely split on the argument and the poem should be usable by both sides.
