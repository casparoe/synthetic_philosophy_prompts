I'm a novelist — two books out, a third that won't behave — and for about four years I've been having the same fight with the same two friends, and I'd like an outside diagnosis of what kind of fight it actually is, because I don't think any of us knows anymore.

The friends: R., who did a philosophy PhD and then sensibly became a tax lawyer, and M., a cognitive scientist. The fight is about thought experiments — M. insists on calling them intuition pumps, which R. thinks is a tell.

The concrete case I keep bringing to the group is one I'm trying to use in the novel: a woman is offered a pill that erases her grief over her dead son. The forgetting is total and painless. She'll be happy, and she won't know what she lost. The question I put to people is whether she owes it to him — to his having existed — to stay sad. Everyone reacts hard and nobody reacts the same way, and if I change one irrelevant detail (the pill also cures her arthritis; she's 80 instead of 40) the reactions flip. That flip is where the trouble starts.

R.'s position: a pump is not evidence, its an argument with the premises hidden. Spell the premises out and the conclusion is only as strong as they are; can't spell them out and you've got rhetoric, not a reason. The pump works by making you picture the case vividly and then treating your gut reaction as if it were a premise you'd endorsed. The framing flip proves it — if the same case with a cosmetic change gives opposite verdicts, the verdict was never tracking the case.

M.'s position: that's a picture of how nobody actually reasons. The pump does work no argument can: it puts you inside a situation, and your reaction is data — data about what you actually value, or about what you find conceivable, or about the content of the concept. Demanding explicit premises is the fantasy that reasoning is a proof with no imagination in it, and R.'s flip objection is just the old demand that evidence be stable, which it never is.

R.: then your data is about your psychology, not about what's true in ethics or mind. M.: fine, but that's where moral and mental concepts live anyway.

Four years, zero movement. And as far as I can tell it's the same stalemate in the literature — people have been arguing about whether intuitions are evidence since long before I was born.

My own hunch, which I don't trust: it's verbal. 'Evidence' means a premise that supports a conclusion to R., and a datum a theory has to accommodate to M., and once you see that, they're both right and just talking past each other.

So diagnose it. Verbal, substantive, two different questions wearing one word, a hidden empirical question (are intuitions stable across people? does 'conceivable' mean anything?), or a sign the whole thing is badly posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, changing the question — or is there no progress to be had, and why not. Don't hand me a menu of options and let me pick; I can do that myself. Take a side, give me your reasons, and tell me where you'd bet against yourself. And keep it useful to me as a working novelist: I use these things on my editor and on readers, and I want to know if I'm doing something respectable or just being a rhetorician with a better vocabulary.
