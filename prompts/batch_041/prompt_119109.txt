I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, but I spend my working life building simulation models, so Daniel Dennett's idea that a thought experiment is an intuition pump has an obvious professional appeal for me: a device that runs a scenario in your head, steers your attention toward the features the author thinks matter and away from the details that are hard to imagine, and thereby delivers a verdict that feels like a finding. I would like that account tested against a real episode rather than against the examples Dennett himself chooses, and the one I keep returning to is Maxwell's demon: Maxwell's sorting being, Szilárd's one-molecule engine, the century in which the demon looked like a genuine loophole in the second law, the eventual accounting in which Landauer and Bennett charge the demon for the information it must record and erase, and the small tabletop versions of the engine built in recent decades that violate nothing.

Analyze that history through Dennett's account of intuition pumps. I want a disciplined mapping first: what exactly is being pumped, whose intuition, toward what verdict; why the demon was persuasive for so long; and why the way out had to come from a theory of information rather than from a better-tuned intuition. Then tell me where the account is silent or wrong.

Two questions in particular, and I want a definite verdict on each. First: was the demon a good pump or a bad one? By Dennett's own diagnosis a bad pump works by making you skip the details that are hard to imagine, and the demon's difficulty lay precisely in such a detail, namely what the demon itself must know and remember. So the pump misled for a century, and it also left behind a real research program. Does that vindicate the line that pumps prove nothing and are therefore harmless, or does it show the line is too permissive to be any use? Second: what does the episode do to the account itself? Is a pump's persuasive force always parasitic on a background picture it leaves unstated, here a classical picture of measurement and knowledge, and if so does the distinction between pumping an intuition and giving an argument survive at all? Say plainly whether this diagnosis could have caught the demon in advance, or whether the framework can only issue such verdicts after the physics has settled the matter, and if the latter, what would have to be added to turn a retrospective label into a criterion.

Commit to a position throughout, including where I have stacked the deck against Dennett, and give your reasons. No citations or reading lists, please; I cannot use them, and I would rather have the argument.
