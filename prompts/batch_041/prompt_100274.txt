I am enrolled in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on collective decision-making and social choice, and I am running the review session for our final examination. I would like to draft one practice essay question on the discursive dilemma, together with a model answer and a marking scheme, so that my study group can attempt it under exam conditions and then grade ourselves honestly. The question should be pitched so that it cannot be answered by restating the difference between premise-based and conclusion-based aggregation; it should force the writer to work through a concrete case and then say what the case does and does not show.

The case I want the question to turn on is the following. A five-member appointments committee must decide whether to promote a candidate, and its charter ties the decision to two reasons. Let A be "the candidate's teaching record is excellent", B be "the candidate's research record is excellent", and C be "the candidate should be promoted", with the charter stipulating that C is correct exactly when both A and B are. The members vote as follows:

- Member 1: A yes, B yes, C yes
- Member 2: A yes, B yes, C yes
- Member 3: A yes, B no, C no
- Member 4: A no, B yes, C no
- Member 5: A no, B no, C no

Every member is individually consistent with the charter's rule; the inconsistency appears only at the collective level, which is what I want students to discover for themselves rather than be told.

I have also invented one institutional wrinkle for the exercise. Suppose the charter contains a further clause requiring that the committee's recorded verdict be answerable to the reasons that carried the day, meaning the verdict must be derivable from the reasons the committee endorsed rather than from a head-count on the verdict itself. I want students to notice that this clause seems to settle the dispute in favor of aggregating the reasons and then deriving the verdict, and then to press on whether that settlement has a cost — in particular whether it commits the committee to a verdict that a majority of its members rejected and that no single member's own combination of reasons supports.

Please write three things.

First, the examination question itself. It should be a single essay question for a fifty-minute paper worth twenty-five marks, broken into lettered parts that build on one another, and it should ask the candidate to: (a) demonstrate, using the case above, that premise-based aggregation and conclusion-based aggregation deliver opposite verdicts, showing the arithmetic rather than merely naming the procedures; (b) explain what each procedure preserves and what each sacrifices, framed as a conflict among desiderata such as collective consistency and logical closure, responsiveness to the members' judgments, and some form of fairness or anonymity across members and propositions; (c) assess the reasons-tracking clause, saying whether it resolves the dilemma or merely legislates one horn of it, and what the losing side could fairly say in reply; and (d) say whether the dilemma is a defect of majority voting as such or a consequence of the fact that the agenda is logically connected — that is, of the verdict being tied to the reasons — by describing what would happen to the case if A, B and C were logically independent of one another.

Second, a model answer of roughly 650 to 750 words that a strong candidate could produce in the time allowed. It should not merely summarize the topic: it should work the case, take a position on the clause in part (c), and give a clear verdict in part (d) about where the difficulty really lies.

Third, a marking scheme totalling twenty-five marks, distributed across the four parts, that names the specific moves a top answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks — for instance, confusing the discursive dilemma with a Condorcet-style cycle of preferences, assuming the dilemma presupposes that individual voters are irrational, or treating premise-based aggregation as obviously correct merely because it preserves consistency. For each part, indicate the difference between a full-credit answer and a merely competent one.

Please write the model answer in continuous prose suitable for a candidate to emulate, and keep the marking scheme in a form a study group could apply without an instructor present.
