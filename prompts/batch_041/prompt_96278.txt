I'm leading a session on the intentional stance and the attribution of beliefs to AI systems for a graduate reading group in philosophy of mind. They can all repeat that we predict a system by treating it as a rational agent, but none can say what the stance licenses and what it does not. Plan the session rather than give it: the order in which the components must be introduced and why each depends on the one before; the single case that should carry the session; the misconceptions to expect and the exact point at which to head off each; and the closing question that would show whether they have understood the stance or only memorized the formula.

Two constraints. First, the session must culminate in a distinction I have been using and have not seen stated: between an ascription of belief that does predictive work the system's design does not already do, and one that merely redescribes behavior the design already explains — call the first load-bearing and the second idle. I want the distinction to be forced by the material rather than announced. Second, the carrying case must not be the chess program or the thermostat; it must be a system whose behavior is best predicted by modelling it as a rational agent that is systematically wrong about its own situation, while the system itself contains no state that represents anything. Tell me what that case needs and what it must lack, where the students will wrongly reach for inner representations, and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level. End by telling me how to pose the question of whether ascribing beliefs to a present-day language model is load-bearing or idle.
