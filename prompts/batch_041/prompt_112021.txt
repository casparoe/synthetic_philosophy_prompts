I convene a comparative penology seminar, and for the past year the same argument about Norway and the United States has returned each term without either side giving ground. The descriptive facts are not what is in dispute: everyone accepts that the two countries imprison at very different rates, that the Norwegian establishments we discuss look and function nothing like a large American state prison, that the reconviction figures both sides cite come from the same sources, and that the history of the American punitive turn is common ground. What is not common ground is what any of it shows.

One camp holds that Norway is a genuine exception, produced by an egalitarian political culture and a generous welfare state, and that American imprisonment is harsh because American politics places no comparable constraint on the appetite to punish. The other camp holds that the contrast is overdrawn and misleading: that Norwegian leniency is real only in the narrow dimension of everyday conditions inside a sentence, that Norway also imposes long terms and holds large numbers of people on remand, and that the rehabilitative apparatus is itself an exercise of power — that a state which undertakes to remake you is doing something to you, not refraining from doing something to you. The first camp replies that this is an evasion: whatever one calls it, being treated as a person with a future is better than being warehoused, and a conceptual point cannot make that false.

What I want from you is not a verdict on which side is right about Norway or about America. I want a diagnosis of the dispute. Is the stalemate concealing an empirical question — whether the Norwegian regime is causally responsible for any of its outcomes, or whether comparable results would follow from any small, well-funded, comparatively homogeneous system — such that some study could settle it? Is it a conceptual disagreement about what punishment is, in which the two sides do not agree on which features of a penal system count as severity in the first place? Is it two groups answering different questions under one heading — one explaining the quantity of punishment in a society, the other explaining its form? Or is it at bottom a clash of starting intuitions about whether the state may legitimately concern itself with the character of the people it confines, which no argument of the kind either side offers could reach?

I have a hypothesis of my own, and I would like you to test it rather than accept it. I suspect the participants are working with two different measures of severity without noticing. One measures the depth of the state's interference — how long you are held, how much of your life is taken. The other measures the state's claim to authority over you — whether it asserts the right to reshape what you are while you are held. On the first measure the two systems diverge sharply; on the second, the Norwegian regime may be asserting a larger claim than the American one, since a prison that offers you treatment, work, and a plan for your release is also a prison that has decided what you ought to become. I am not confident this is right, and here is a case I think tells against the easy version of it: two regimes with identical sentence lengths, identical material conditions, and identical restrictions, where one explicitly declines to take any interest in the prisoner's character and the other runs programmes aimed at changing it. Which is the more punitive regime, and on what understanding of punishment is that the answer?

Diagnose the disagreement using the positions as I have described them. Say what kind of disagreement you take it to be and why the alternatives are worse. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an empirical result, a reformulation of the question — or argue that none should be expected and explain why. Where my hypothesis is wrong, say so and say what the right description is instead.
