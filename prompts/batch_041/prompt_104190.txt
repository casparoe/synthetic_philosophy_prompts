I retired about five years ago after 35 years in condensed matter physics — neutron scattering, mostly — and since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group at the local university, largely to annoy the grad students. Right now we're stuck on Gödel's rotating universe. You know the one: a solution of Einstein's field equations where the whole cosmos spins, and if you travel far enough along certain worldlines you loop back into your own past — closed timelike curves, and no global way to slice the thing into simultaneity surfaces, so no universe-wide "now." What's got us arguing isn't the physics, it's the philosophical moral Gödel drew from it. Roughly, he splits "time" into two things: first, just a one-dimensional ordering of events by earlier and later, which even the rotating solution still hands you locally; second, the thing we actually feel, the objective lapse or flow, the sense that only the present is real and keeps sliding into the past. His claim, as I understand it (and I've probably mangled it), is that taking the flow seriously forces you to posit a global present shared by the whole universe, and a rotating universe can't provide one, so the flow can't be a feature of the world at all — time in that second sense is "ideal," something more like a fact about us than about the world. He ties it back to Parmenides and Kant and to McTaggart, if that helps orient you.

Here's what I'd like you to make for the group. A decision tree — a flowchart written out in prose — that starts from the commitment "I'm willing to grant the rotating solution is a legitamate solution of the field equations, not just a mathematical curiosity" and leads the reader to a named position about whether time is real. Every branch node should be one of the actual premises in the argument, phrased as a question a person could answer honestly without already knowing which label it steers them toward: whether the flow of time is something over and above the mere ordering of events; whether an objective flow requires a global present; whether the rotating universe is a genuine physical possibility or only a formal one; whether physics needs a "now" at all; and whether calling time "ideal" means unreal, or mind-dependent, or merely tenseless. Don't organize it as a list of -isms with definitions bolted on — build it out of the questions first and let the positions fall wherever the answers put them. I do want these leaves to show up somewhere: Gödel's own ideal theory; the block-universe eternalist who thinks time is perfectly real but tenseless; the presentist who simply won't grant the rotating solution is a real possibility; the moving-spotlight view; and the relationist who says all that exists is the order of events. If two of those collapse into the same leaf once the branches are fixed, tell me so instead of papering it over — my hunch is a couple of them come out indistinguishable on this tree and I'd rather know which. Also flag any combination of answers that nobody actually seems to hold, with a one-line diagnosis of why (incoherent, just unattractive, or simply unexplored). Keep it to about six branch nodes and eight leaves, and write it as a one-pager I can hand out — no citations or reading list, please, half the group can't get to the library anyway.
