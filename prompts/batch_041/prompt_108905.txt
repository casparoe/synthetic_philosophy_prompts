The question:

A further education college trialled two tutoring programmes, New and Old. The scheduling office assigned students to programmes, and it's well known that the office steers students with strong prior attainment towards Old, because Old runs in the slots that suit the students who are already ahead. Pass rates were:

High prior attainment: New 9 passed out of 10; Old 440 out of 500.
Low prior attainment: New 40 out of 100; Old 19 out of 100.

(a) [3 marks] Report the overall pass rate for each programme, and the pass rate for each programme within each attainment stratum. Does the pattern reverse?

(b) [4 marks] The college asks which programme to keep. Which comparison answers that question, and why? Say what has to be true of the allocation for your answer to go through.

(c) [3 marks] A student in the same class writes: "Whenever you find a reversal like this, the disaggregated comparison is the causally correct one. That's what Simpson's paradox teaches." Assess that claim.

The script:

(a) Overall, New = 49/110 = 44.5% and Old = 459/600 = 76.5%. Within the high attainers, New = 9/10 = 90% and Old = 440/500 = 88%. Within the low attainers, New = 40/100 = 40% and Old = 19/100 = 19%. So yes, it reverses: Old is far better overall, but New is ahead in both strata.

(b) The stratified comparison answers the college's question. Prior attainment is a confounder: it affects which programme you get, because the office sends the strong students to Old, and it also affects pass rates, so the overall figures are mixing two different groups in different proportions and the comparison between them is picking up the allocation rather than the programmes. And since the reversal itself shows there is confounding here, we should read the effect off the strata. The effect of switching to New is the average of the two within-stratum differences, i.e. (90 − 88) and (40 − 19), averaged over the two strata, which comes to about 11.5 percentage points, so New raises the pass rate by roughly 11.5 points. Assuming no unmeasured confounding, the college should keep New.

(c) Yes, that's right, and it's the standard moral of the paradox. The aggregate table is an artefact of how the two groups happen to be weighted, so the honest comparison is always the one that holds the confounder fixed. It isn't a quirk of the pass-rate statistic either: it comes out the same whether you look at pass rates, risk ratios or odds ratios. And you couldn't get a reversal at all if the allocation had been randomised, since randomisation removes the confounding — which is why randomised trials don't have to have this discussion.
