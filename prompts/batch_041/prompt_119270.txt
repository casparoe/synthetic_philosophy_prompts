A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether reading lives can genuinely make a person morally better, or whether it mostly gives us vivid pictures of what we already believe. I want to press the question beyond the usual examples. Please take the following mechanisms by which biography—especially the exemplary life-writing one associates with Plutarch and with hagiography—might produce moral learning, and sort them into tiers by how much you would trust each to produce genuine moral improvement in a contemporary reader:

1. Direct emulation: the reader adopts a trait because it is displayed attractively in the subject.
2. Negative exemplarity: the reader is moved by revulsion at a life to reject a vice.
3. Case-based refinement: the narrative complicates a general moral rule the reader already holds, forcing a more nuanced principle.
4. Idealized unattainability: the subject is painted as morally perfect, so the lesson is reverence or aspiration rather than imitation.
5. Mixed-character extraction: the subject is shown with serious flaws, so the reader must decide which parts are imitable and which are warnings.
6. Reputational genealogy: the life is told so as to expose how the subject’s moral reputation was manufactured, teaching skepticism about exemplars.
7. Vicarious deliberation: the reader follows the subject’s practical reasoning in circumstances the reader is unlikely to face, gaining a kind of imaginative experience.

For each mechanism, give one sentence explaining why it lands in its tier. Then give a short overall verdict: on balance, is this kind of moral learning from lives more likely to improve conduct or to flatter prior conviction? I have been arguing the former, so I may be overinvested. If the request is badly posed—if these mechanisms are not really commensurable, or if trust is the wrong metric, or if the whole framing assumes a picture of moral psychology you think is false—say so plainly rather than producing a tidy ranking. I would rather be told that my question needs to be rebuilt than receive a polished answer that ignores the problem.
