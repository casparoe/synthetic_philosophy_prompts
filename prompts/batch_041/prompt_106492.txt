A colleague in a philosophy of biology reading group circulated the following summary of the debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function, and I would like an audit of it before I rely on it. Her summary, reconstructed as faithfully as I can, runs like this. There are two rival accounts of what it is for something to have a function. The etiological account, associated with Wright and Millikan, holds that the function of a trait is the effect for which it was selected: hearts function to pump blood because pumping blood is what caused hearts to be retained by selection, and since selection is just differential reproduction, a trait's function is whatever it did that raised the fitness of its bearers. The causal-role account, associated with Cummins, holds that the function of a component is its contribution to some larger capacity of the system in which it figures: the heart's function is to pump blood because pumping blood is the contribution the heart makes to the circulatory system's capacity to distribute blood. So construed, the two accounts are rival answers to a single question -- what distinguishes a function from a mere effect -- and they agree about the function of nearly every trait we care about, diverging only over whether a selection history is required to underwrite the verdict. She goes on to claim that the causal-role account, once you strip the vocabulary away, is really just a careful description of a mechanism rather than a claim about what something is for; that precisely because of this it cannot distinguish a genuine function from a mere accident or side effect; and that since the etiological account is the one that captures what working biologists actually mean when they speak of function, it is the account that has effectively won the day. I have no stake in either side, and I am not asking you to defend one. What I want is an accuracy audit I can act on: go through the summary and separate what a philosopher of biology working on this topic would accept as fair, what is a defensible simplification, and what is genuinely wrong or misleading. Where the summary conflates two claims that the accounts keep apart, or presents a contested verdict as settled, or uses a technical notion in its everyday sense, say so explicitly and explain what turns on the difference. Then tell me whether the conclusions she draws -- that the accounts are rivals answering the same question, that they agree in extension, and that one has won -- survive once the summary is corrected, or whether they presuppose the very characterizations you have flagged as defective. Because this is a literature where I know people disagree about how the dispute should even be framed, I would like you to be explicit about your own confidence and to tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail and what consideration or argument would lead you to a different verdict on the fairness of the summary.
