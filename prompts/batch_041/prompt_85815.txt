I'm in a small reading group and we're stuck on the two-envelope thing in a way that's starting to grate. We all know the setup: one envelope has twice the other, you open yours and see X, and the slick argument says the other holds 2X or X/2 with equal probability, so it's worth 1.25X and you should switch. Half of us think that's the end of it — the 50/50 step is illegitimate because it needs a uniform prior over the possible amounts, and there's no such thing on the positive reals, so the "paradox" is just a mistake and nothing more. The other half says that only shows why the *calculation* is bad; it doesn't tell you what a chooser should actually do once they've opened and seen $10, and something is still owed there. To stir it up I threw in a version where we're told up front the amounts are whole dollars between 1 and 100, so at $1 the halving branch is impossible and at $100 the doubling branch is, and yet people still report the pull toward switching at $10. So: is this a real disagreement or are we talking past each other? Is the leftover bit a decision-theoretic question, a question about what a paradox is even supposed to deliver, or a psychological itch no math will scratch? My hypothesis — test it, don't assume it — is that we're really disagreeing about what a resolution has to *do*, not about the probability. Diagnose the dispute, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis, and please don't just hand me the standard resolution again.
