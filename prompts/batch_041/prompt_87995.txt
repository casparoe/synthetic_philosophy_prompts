I teach a 90-minute session in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on social choice — eighteen students, mostly philosophy and political science majors — and I have taught the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem often enough to know exactly what goes wrong. My students can all state it: with at least three possible outcomes, any voting rule that is not dictatorial is manipulable, in the sense that some voter facing some configuration of the other voters' preferences does strictly better by misreporting her ranking than by reporting it honestly. What they cannot do is feel it. They hear it as a theorem about a filing cabinet of exotic voting rules rather than as a claim about the room they are sitting in, and the standard illustrations — plurality voters deserting a hopeless favorite, Borda voters burying a rival below their true least favorite — get filed away as familiar folk wisdom about tactical voting rather than as instances of a general result. I want an activity that reverses that order: something that puts them inside a rule of their own choosing, with real if trivial stakes, so that they run into the incentive to misreport before anyone has named manipulation, and the theorem then arrives as the explanation of what has just happened to them. Please design it in full — setting and timeline for the ninety minutes, materials, payoff scheme (no real money; points or tokens are fine, and I am happy for the prize to be something silly like control of the end-of-term session), roles, rules, and a debrief that maps particular moments of the game onto particular parts of the theorem.

A few constraints, since I have run versions of this badly before. Run the elections at tables of four or five rather than in the full room of eighteen: with eighteen voters almost no one is ever pivotal, the lesson evaporates, and the exercise degenerates into a lecture with ballots, whereas in small electorates the same student can be decisive twice in ten minutes. Give each participant a private card assigning point values to each of four outcomes, forbid showing the cards, and make the outcomes genuinely differentiated — I want at least one table where a sincere plurality vote elects the outcome that the table's median voter ranks last. Let them agree on a rule they consider fair before any of this begins, and then break it: in the later rounds let one or two secretly briefed participants vote insincerely, or give everyone a private bonus for landing their own top outcome, and watch the rule they designed come apart. When they patch it — and they will patch it, moving from plurality to Borda to a runoff, each time confident that the previous rule was the problem — offer them the one rule that cannot be gamed and let them reject it themselves: a dictatorship, in which a single named participant's ranking settles everything and no one else's vote can do anything, honest or otherwise. What I want them to reach by their own route is that fairness and strategy-proofness were never two names for the same thing, and that the only exit from the trade-off on offer is to give up the fairness. If time allows, the debrief can add the extension to rules that randomize over outcomes, where the only strategy-proof rules turn out to be lotteries over dictators.

The debrief should reconstruct the pivotality mechanism in their own case: with the other ballots held fixed, a voter's misreport can matter only when her vote is decisive, so any rule immune to manipulation must in effect give some voter her way whenever she is decisive — and iterating that thought is how the impossibility is actually proved, which is precisely the part the activity cannot stage. Two further things the game cannot reproduce, and which I will have to supply from the front of the room: the universality of the theorem, since students who have broken four rules will reasonably conclude that they merely chose badly; and the fact that the hypotheses do real work — on a restricted domain of preferences, such as single-peaked orderings, a strategy-proof rule exists and is in everyday use. I also want the debrief to separate vulnerability from exploitation: the theorem says the incentive exists, not that voters know enough, or are decisive often enough, to act on it. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I am especially worried about a round in which the planted manipulator's lie fails to change the outcome, leaving the whole design inert, and about the student who insists that the point cards are not her real preferences and that the exercise is therefore rigged from the start.
