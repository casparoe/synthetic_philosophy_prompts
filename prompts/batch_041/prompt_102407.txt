My reading group is finishing a term on the Vienna Circle and we're ending on Neurath — specifically the picture-language stuff, the pictogram charts he and his team made in Vienna in the twenties and thirties, the ones where you never draw a bigger man to mean more people, you just draw more little men, each identical, each standing for the same fixed number. I'd like to close the last session by reading something out loud, and I'd rather it be a poem than one more seminar summary, so I'm asking you to write it.

Here's the shape I want. Write it in the voice of the little human pictogram himself — the identical black figure who gets stamped out over and over in every chart, ten thousand of him in a row meaning ten thousand people. He's asked to say what he's for: about standing in for facts, about being copied until the copy is the meaning, about being a helping language and not a real one. Make it 14 to 16 lines, with one line that repeats word for word, a refrain that comes back at least three times and means something different each time it returns, the way the same icon means different things depending on which chart its standing in. And here's the constraint that does the real work: the poem may only use words for things you could actually draw as one of these pictograms — concrete, countable, drawable things (a person, a house, a loaf, a ship, a wheel, a bed, a hand, a star). No abstract nouns, and no words whose whole job is to relate or deny: no because, no if, no not, no all, no means. The poem should be trying to say something it can't draw, and the place where it runs out — where it needs a word like same or cause or nothing and there isn't one — should be the turn. I want that failure to be the point and not a joke at Neurath's expense: he knew the picture language was a helper and never a full language, and I want the poem to land on that honestly rather than just waving at it. It should be good enough that someone who's never heard of Isotype still gets a real poem out of it, and specific enough that someone who's read Neurath recognizes the exact worry he had. Please dont let it turn into a Wikipedia summary in verse, and don't cite or quote anything — just the poem, plus maybe a one-line note on the refrain if you think it needs one.
