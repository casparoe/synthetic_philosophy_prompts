So my AP US History class just finished the 1998 Clinton impeachment, and in my philosophy elective we'd just done a unit on when you're actually allowed to blame somebody -- the idea that even if what you're saying is true, you might not have the standing to say it, because you're just as bad. Which is obviously the whole Clinton thing: the House impeached him in December of '98 over lying under oath to the grand jury and obstruction of justice, not the affair itself, and then the Senate acquitted him in February. But what made it a circus was that alot of the loudest people pushing it turned out to have affairs of their own, and the guy who was about to be the next Speaker had to back out of it over his. My friend said case closed, total hypocrites, they had no right to say anything. I said it's definately not that simple and then I couldn't actually explain why, so now I'm asking you.

I want a real analysis of the episode through the standing-to-blame ideas, not a hot take. So: does the hypocrisy actually take away their standing, or does it just make them jerks who happen to be right? I know there's a version where hypocrisy doesn't matter at all -- blame can be fitting no matter who's giving it, and the charge doesn't get truer or falser based on the person making it -- and then versions where it does matter, either because a hypocrite is being insincere (he doesn't really accept the rule for himself) or because he's setting himself above the standard, and blame is supposed to be a kind of address between people who hold each other to the same rules. Tell me which of those actually handles this case and why the others don't. Then deal with the thing I think breaks the whole comparison: the actual charges were perjury and obstruction, not adultery, so the accusers weren't guilty of the specific thing they were accusing him of. Does that kill the hypocrisy objection, or does it not matter because the real story underneath was that they were punishing him for the affair and using the lying as the excuse?

Then flip it and tell me what the episode does to the theory. In actual politics it seems like everybody accuses everybody of having no standing, and it mostly works as a way to change the subject rather than as a serious moral point -- so is standing doing a different job in public and political blame than it does between two friends? And there's stuff the theory seems to have no story for: can a whole body like a House have standing, or only individual people? Can you lose it for a while and get it back, like if you admit it and apologize, do you get to blame again? Does it matter that these guys were lawmakers with a job that includes enforcing the law, or does the job not fix the standing problem at all? Where the theory is genuinely silent, please just say so instead of making it answer, and be fair to them -- I don't want hypocrites are hypocrites and nothing else.
