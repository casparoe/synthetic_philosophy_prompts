I am a postdoctoral fellow in philosophy and have been asked to lead next week's session of an interdisciplinary reading group on pain, whose members are philosophy graduate students and psychiatry residents with little formal background in pain science. The session is fifty minutes, with a whiteboard and no slides, and I want to spend it on pain asymbolia and what it might show about the badness of pain. My plan is to use a composite case, not a real named patient: after a stroke, a man can localize a pinprick and describes it as a sharp burning sensation, but he does not withdraw, does not grimace, and says the sensation does not bother him; when asked whether he wants it to stop, he says he does not care either way. I want that case to carry the session, but I am worried it will be treated as a mere curiosity unless the conceptual sequence is right. Please plan the explanation for me rather than giving me the explanation itself: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and why each depends on the previous one; what the composite case must include and what it must leave out if it is to do the work; the misconceptions the group will predictably bring or form, in the order they will arise, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. I want the pivot to be a distinction that is not the usual sensory-versus-affective contrast: the idea that pain's badness consists in its issuing a demand or command to stop, rather than merely in its felt unpleasantness, and that asymbolia might be read as a case where the sensation survives but the command is missing. If that pivot is philosophically shaky, say so and propose a better one, but do not simply replace it with a standard taxonomy.

At the end I need one question I can ask the group that would show whether they have understood the distinction between the presence of pain and its badness, rather than merely learned a new label. I also want to know what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say so out loud: I do not want to get pulled into lesion localization, the neuroscience of the insular cortex, or the details of analgesic pharmacology, and I do not want to assign readings. Please give me a single numbered sequence of teaching moves, under 700 words, with no separate introduction or conclusion, and make clear at each step what the group is supposed to be able to do or say before we move on.
