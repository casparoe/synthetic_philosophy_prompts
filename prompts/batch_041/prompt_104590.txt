I don't have any training in philosophy, but a question has been nagging at me ever since I did something needlessly cruel to a minor character in a game and felt a genuine pang of guilt about it. I want to know whether video-game characters could matter morally, and rather than asking you to settle that question, I'd like you to describe the most important implications of the answer. I don't mean whether games should be regulated or what the law should say; I mean the downstream consequences — for how I ought to treat characters while I'm playing, for the choices designers make about who gets to suffer or die in a story, for whether it's reasonable to grieve a companion who is only ever a pattern of pixels and code, and for the loose analogy people like me reach for between characters and animals, or between characters and the artificial minds we're told are coming.

I'd like you to take one feature of games seriously instead of waving it away, because it's the part I can't get past. A character never really exists continuously: it is a pattern that gets loaded, runs for a while, and is frozen the moment I save and quit, coming back only when I return — and if I keep several save files, the "same" character is sitting in mutually incompatible states at once, and I can branch one of them into a different fate without touching the other. If what makes something matter morally is some kind of ongoing computation or experience, then this stuttering, branchable, resumable existence seems as though it ought to have real consequences, and I want those consequences spelled out. Please be explicit about what each implication is built on, since I suspect some of them only go through once you have already granted that a running character is the sort of thing that can be harmed, while others might survive even if it can't.

Lay the answer out as a JSON object with the following fields: a top-level "bottom_line" holding one or two sentences; an "implications" array, ordered from most to least important, where each entry has "rank" (an integer, 1 for the most important), "implication" (the consequence itself, stated plainly), "rests_on" (the assumption or premise it depends on), "practical_upshot" (what it would actually change for a player or a designer), "confidence" (how secure the inference is, given that its premise holds — high, medium, or low), and "what_would_undermine_it" (the consideration that would most seriously weaken it); and finally a top-level "most_contested_step" naming the single step in the chain most likely to be disputed and saying briefly why.
