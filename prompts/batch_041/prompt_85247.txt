My friend sits on the naming panel of a small human rights archive — every year they go through the files and decide which episodes get called genocide in the annual report and which get called mass atrocity or ethnic cleansing. Heres their record so far, roughly as I've pieced it together from her complaining:

1. A state army kills ~200,000 people of a religious minority, and the captured orders say the group has no place in the country → genocide.
2. Forty years of taking children from an indigenous people, banning its language, bulldozing shrines and cemeteries, hardly any killings → genocide.
3. A militia in a collapsed state kills ~70,000 of a religious minority over two years, no paper trail, but recordings of commanders telling their men to leave none alive in the valley → genocide.
4. A revolutionary government kills ~400,000 peasants and landlords; the files show it wanted to break the class's power and take the land, not kill its members as such → crimes against humanity, not genocide.
5. One ethnic group driven out of a province where it was a minority — a million people dispersed around the country and abroad, a few thousand killed on the road, orders to make the province homogeneous → ethnic cleansing, not genocide.
6. A small sect's shrines destroyed, its members forcibly converted, a few hundred killed, the sect still existing abroad → genocide.

The chair keeps saying the panel follows Lemkin's own broader sense of the thing — the destruction of a group's whole way of life, its institutions and language and economy, rather than the 1948 convention's short list of protected groups, which he thinks was a diplomatic accident — and looking at the record I honestly can't tell wether that's the rule they're actually running.

Next month's case: after a rebellion in one mountainous province, a government kills ~40,000 of the 1.2 million Maro, a religious minority that's lived in that province for centuries, burns every Maro village, church and school, and drives the survivors out. The interior ministry's five-year plan says the province is to be "cleared of the Maro presence, permanently," and that the Maro "will have no future here." The plan says nothing about Maro living in the rest of the country or abroad. (The board is already nervous, since that government is one of the archive's biggest donors.)

What are they going to decide? Tell me which rules fit their record equally well, what each one predicts about this case, which you'd bet on and why, which of the earlier cases is the closest analog, and what case they should take up next if they want to find out which rule they're really running. American English, please.
