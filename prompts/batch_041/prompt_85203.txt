I got voluntold onto the bylaws committee of our housing co-op and it has turned into a six-week argument that I did not sign up for. We need a draft by Thursday and the two loudest people on the committee have stopped speaking to each other, so I'm hoping you can write the document they should have written in week one.

Here's the fight. Person A — she's the one with the highlighters — says no set of rules can put itself in charge of changing itself. Sure, a rule can say how it gets amended, and then amending it that way is just following it. But the second you amend the rule that governs amendments, she says you've done something no rule authorized: the old version licensed changes made under it, not changes to it, and the new version can't help because it wasn't in force when you used it. So every change to the amendment procedure is either outside the rules altogether — a room full of people taking power, i.e. a coup with minutes taken — or it's circular. Which means a clause saying it can never be repealed isn't really binding, it's just holding because enough of us happen to go along with it, and the day we stop, it's gone. She also thinks self-reference in rules breeds outright contradictions: a rule that says no rule binds unless it says so, a rule that says it's void, that kind of thing.

Person B — he plays alot of board games, and keeps bringing up some game where the entire point is that you can rewrite the rules, including the rule about rewriting rules — says this is all a category mistake. Rules aren't claims that can be true or false, they're instructions we collectively follow, so there's no contradiction to be had, only a question of what we're willing to do. The regress stops in us, not in a rule. He says A's "coup" is just what every rule change looks like if you squint: every amendment is people deciding to do things differently, and the ones that count as inside the rules are only inside because we keep treating them that way. On his view a clause can absolutely entrench itself — it's just a rule that makes repealing it cost more, and costs can always be paid, by a big enough majority or by everyone walking out and starting over.

They keep seperate scoreboards and they've been talking past each other for a month. I want the joint statement. Specifically:

- A numbered list of claims they can both actually sign — about what our rules are, what it is for a rule to be in force, what has to be true for an amendment to have happened, whether any of the paradoxes are real contradictions or just rules nobody can successfully use, and what's at stake practically. Include anything they can jointly say about cases 1 through 6 below, even if they disagree about everything else.
- Then a table of the real disagreements. Columns: the question at issue in neutral words; A's position in wording she'd accept as fair; B's position in wording he'd accept as fair; the crux — the sub-question such that if it went one way, one of them would have to give ground; and a case or test they both agree would bear on that crux, with each of their predictions written down in advance.
- If part of it turns out to be a fight about words — e.g. one of them means by "rule" something that has to be validated by another rule, and the other means anything we actually follow — say which part is verbal and what's left over once the words are fixed.

Cases I need them to take a stand on, because these are the ones on the table:

1. Clause E: "This clause may not be repealed, amended, or suspended by any means whatsoever." Next month someone moves to repeal Clause E and gets the required majority. Did it work? Was it ever binding?
2. We currently need two-thirds to amend anything. A group gets exactly two-thirds to change the amendment threshold to ninety percent, then uses the new threshold to block everything for a year. Was the first vote valid? Is the second? What if the change had gone the other way — lowering the threshold — and then a bare majority had used it?
3. A clause that says: no rule of this co-op is binding unless it appears in Appendix A, and this clause does not appear in Appendix A.
4. A clause that says: this clause is void.
5. Ninety-five percent of the membership meets outside every procedure we have, throws out the bylaws, and adopts new ones. Did they amend, or is that a different kind of event altogether? If it's different, what exactly is different about it from case 2?
6. A single clause that says the amendment procedure is whatever this clause says it is, and that this clause may be changed by a simple majority. Does it work? Does it make literally everything changeable by half of us?
7. Give me at least one case where the two of them would actually give me different advice about what a member should do on Monday — comply, refuse, appeal to something, quit — so I can see whether the disagreement has any practical cash value at all.

And please don't tell me which one is right — if you think one of them is obviously right, that's definately a sign the joint statement isn't fair yet, so say what would have to be true for the other one to be right, and build the crux around that.
