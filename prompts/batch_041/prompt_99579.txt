A friend and I have been going back and forth about sphexishness — the idea, named after the solitary digger wasps whose nest-provisioning sequence is said to restart from the top if you nudge their prey a few inches, that behavior which looks intelligent, deliberate and goal-directed can be the output of a wholly rigid routine, so that the appearance of flexibility is an illusion. I'm after the idea itself, not anyone's particular published treatment of it, so don't worry about citing a source. Our disagreement is whether flexibility contributes anything at all to freedom of the will: my friend insists that once you hold behavior fixed, whether the machinery behind it is rigid is simply irrelevant to whether the agent acts freely, and I want a case that tests this cleanly. Please build the thought experiment to the specification below rather than handing me the standard discussion.

The case is to involve two agents whose complete observable behavior is identical: for every situation either one ever meets — including the ones where we would ordinarily say the agent deliberates, weighs reasons, changes its mind or acts creatively — the two produce the same response, and we may stipulate a shared environment so that the inputs match as well. What differs is only the causal machinery behind the behavior. The first agent is rigid to the core: its entire behavioral trajectory, from birth to death, is generated by a single fixed finite program, every step of which is fixed in advance — it may rewrite its own sub-routines and present itself as inventive, but each such rewrite is itself produced by that one fixed program. The second agent's behavior is not generated by any fixed routine; it is open-ended, and it is up to you to specify the machinery in whatever way makes the case work. No one, including the agents themselves, can tell the two apart from the outside, and each can give the same first-person report of having decided.

Now the analysis. I want you to engineer the case so that a reasons-responsiveness compatibilist and a sourcehood (agent-causal) libertarian agree about the second agent but part ways over the first, and then, in the same piece, to tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work of producing that split; what becomes of the split if that feature is removed — say, if the second agent's open-endedness turned out after all to be a fixed finite routine, or if the first agent's routine were infinite but still fully fixed; and what the losing side's best counterattack would be, i.e. what a defender of the position that comes out worse would claim is being smuggled into the case. Two ground rules: the second agent's freedom must not be grounded in quantum indeterminacy, and the first agent's routine must not be a mere reflex arc — it has to be rich enough that its outputs, taken on their own, would be described as thoughtful. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold or tables, the way you would set it out in a letter to a colleague; a page or two is fine.
