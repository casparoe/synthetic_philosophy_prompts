I have spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and forum threads to have acquired two habits of mind that pull against each other: a strong prior against any position that nothing could count against, and a recognition that empirically equivalent metaphysical views are common enough that the tie has to be broken on theoretical virtue rather than observation. A position of the second kind has been slowly winning me over, and I want you to tell me honestly what would count against it.

The position is Berkeleyan idealism. I will state it as four commitments that can fail independently, because I suspect the case against it is usually aimed at the wrong one. First, the objects of perception are ideas and nothing besides: a table is a collection of sensible qualities, its existence consists in its being perceived, and the notion of something existing with no relation to any mind is at best obscure and at worst empty. Second, the argument commentators call the master argument: whatever we conceive is conceived by a mind, so we cannot conceive of anything existing unconceived, and what cannot be conceived cannot be possible. I regard this as suggestive rather than conclusive, and I know that most readers think it slides from the impossibility of doing two things at once to the impossibility of the thing conceived; tell me whether that diagnosis is fatal and whether any version of the argument survives it. Third, ideas are inert and so cannot cause themselves or one another, which means their regular order must be produced by a mind — a vastly greater one, which is also why unperceived objects do not wink out of existence. Fourth, and this is the claim I actually care about, the view asserts a parsimony advantage: it accommodates every observation the materialist accommodates, minus an inert and unobservable substratum that we are asked to infer and can describe only by saying what it is not.

Given that, what would count against it?

I want the defeaters ranked by how decisive they genuinely are rather than by how often they are recited. For each one, tell me which of the four commitments it hits; whether it is internal (the view cannot do something it promised) or external (it collides with something else I have reason to believe); what it would concretely look like — what we would have to observe, what case would have to be described, what argument would have to go through; whether a committed idealist would concede it or dismiss it as question-begging; and, where the view makes a prediction its rivals do not, what that prediction is. I am less interested in the standard objections than in their aftermath.

Four things I would like addressed specifically.

Whether the view has any empirical exposure at all. If it is empirically equivalent to realism in both directions, neither view can be refuted by observation, and I want to know whether that makes the dispute empty, makes it an ordinary case of underdetermination to be settled on other grounds, or makes it a defect peculiar to idealism. Relatedly, tell me what to make of the frequent suggestion that certain results in physics, in which observation seems to play an ineliminable role, bear on the question in one direction or the other. My suspicion is that they do not; I would like that suspicion either confirmed with reasons or shown to be lazy.

Whether the view can draw the distinctions it needs with its own resources. Can it distinguish a veridical perception from a vivid hallucination, or a dream, without covertly appealing to an order that is nobody's experience? Can it say what makes one table, perceived by you and by me, one object rather than two exactly similar ones, given that ideas are particulars? For each case, what is the defender's best reply, and does the reply cost him the parsimony advantage that was the view's selling point?

Whether the idealist's method is applied consistently. He refuses inference to the best explanation when the materialist uses it to posit matter, and then, as far as I can tell, relies on exactly that inference to reach other minds and God. Is that a real inconsistency, how much does it cost, and could the view be reformulated to avoid it without surrendering either other minds or the divine cause?

Whether the whole dispute turns verbal. If the idealist retreats to the claim that physical theory only ever describes the order and structure of our experience, he sounds very close to a structuralist about physics who calls himself a realist. If both sides end up agreeing about everything except a label, say so, and say which part of the disagreement survives as substantive.

Finally, an honest verdict on insulation. If nothing could count against the view, tell me whether that is because it is empty, because it is a genuine but underdetermined option, or because the question is at bottom verbal — and whether its insulation should worry me or is simply what empirical equivalence buys any metaphysical position.

What would move me most is not a knockdown argument but a demonstration that the idealist's own resources cannot supply a needed distinction without importing the mind-independent order he set out to eliminate. A close second is a clean case in which the very inference he forbids his opponent is one he cannot do without himself.
