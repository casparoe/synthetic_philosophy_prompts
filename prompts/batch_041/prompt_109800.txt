I've been keeping a running list of cases where I have a gut verdict about whether someone's choice actually tells us what they prefer, and the list is starting to look like it can't all be right. Third-year PhD student, decision theory / philosophy of econ (interdisciplinary program, whatever), so I care about this more than a normal person should. Here's the list.

My buddy buys a Powerball ticket every week. He knows the odds. I think that choice does reveal something he prefers — he's paying two bucks for a week of imagining being rich, and he genuinely prefers that to two bucks. Reveals a preference.

Same buddy, if he bought the ticket because he's sure he's due and would take it back the second he did the math — I don't think that reveals a preference over outcomes at all. That's a mistake, not a taste.

A guy I knew in undergrad, dry for a year, drank again. I don't think that choice revealed that he preferred drinking to being dry. It's a compulsion; preference talk doesn't get a grip. (He said so himself after, which I know isn't decisive.)

Another friend quit a decent job to move to a city with worse everything because her partner got a job there. That reveals a preference, I think — she prefers being with him to the job and the city. Fine.

But my cousin, who sells plasma twice a week and took a roofing job with a scary injury rate, I also think that reveals a preference — he prefers the money to the risk. He's not compelled, he's just broke.

Now the ones that I think break the pattern:

Mug case. I won't sell you my mug for six dollars, and I wouldn't buy the same mug for six dollars either. I don't think that shows I have no preference about mugs — I think it shows I prefer not to lose what I have over gaining what I don't. Loss aversion, sure, but it's a real preference, a preference over gains and losses rather than over final states.

Framing case. If you tell me a surgery has a 90% survival rate I say yes; if you tell me it has a 10% mortality rate I say no. Same surgery. There I want to say no preference over the outcomes was revealed at all — the choice was tracking the words, not the surgery.

Those two look like the same move to me. Both are cases where what the person "chooses" depends on how the options are described rather than on the options. But in the mug case I let the description-relative thing count as a preference and in the surgery case I don't. Can't tell if that's a principled distinction (final states vs changes from a reference point maybe doing real work) or if I'm just inconsistent.

Two more:

Someone picks A over B, B over C, and C over A, asked on three seperate days. I want to say there's no preference ordering here, but I also want to say each of the three choices was a real expression of something. Is "preference" just the wrong word and I should be saying "choice disposition"?

The 11pm case. Same person picks the apple at the store and the cake at the party. I think each choice reveals what she wanted then, and there's no further fact about what she "really" prefers. But then I don't know why I get to say the alcoholic's drink isn't a preference and this is — both are just what the person did at a time.

The tentative thing I've been telling myself: a choice reveals a preference when (i) it's stable under redescription of the options, (ii) it's not something the chooser would disavow from a cool informed standpoint, and (iii) it isn't just the path of least resistance. But (i) kills the mug case, which I want to keep; (ii) looks like it could cut either way on the addict, since he might endorse sobriety and the drink both; and (iii) is doing all the work in the addict case while being definately the vaguest thing on the list.

So what I want from you: a principle, or two if you think the domain splits, that gets these, that says precisely which of my verdicts I have to give up, and that tells me what to say about a case I haven't listed — someone who votes against his own stated interest every election because that's how his dad voted. Preference, habit, or neither?
