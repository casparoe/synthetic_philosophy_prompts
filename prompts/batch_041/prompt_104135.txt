I am preparing to open a graduate seminar session on self-deception, and the more I work through the material the more the topic looks like a trap: to deceive someone you must get them to believe something false, and to do it deliberately you must know the truth, so a self-deceiver would apparently have to know and not know the same thing at the same time. My classmates have floated four ways out, and I would like you to treat all four as live options rather than dismissing any of them. First, that self-deception is a species of imaginative pretense: the person never really loses sight of the truth but keeps rehearsing a convenient fiction — a flattering version of a failing marriage, say — until the fiction quietly takes over as belief. Second, that it is deflationary: nothing in the process is aimed at deception, and the label names only a motivated false belief produced by ordinary belief-forming habits pushed off course by desire. Third, that the mind is divided, so that one part of a person deceives another and the apparent contradiction is really a question about the relations among the parts. Fourth, that self-deception is purely a matter of time: the person believes one thing at one point and its opposite at another, and the contradiction appears only if one insists that both are held simultaneously.

What I need is not another definition but the most important implications of taking the phenomenon seriously in each of these forms — the consequences I could actually defend in seminar. I am particularly concerned with what follows for three things: whether a person can be held responsible for a belief arrived at in this way, including when that belief causes serious harm to someone else; whether telling someone that they are in denial can ever be a legitimate move rather than a piece of theory dressed up as an observation; and what all of this does to the ordinary picture on which each of us has privileged first-person access to our own minds. I don't need names, schools, or reading lists — the seminar has plenty of those. Please answer in no more than 600 words and arrange the response as a short list of implications rather than a single continuous essay.
