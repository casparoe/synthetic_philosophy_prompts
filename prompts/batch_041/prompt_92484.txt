Epiphenomenalism holds that phenomenal properties are caused by physical ones but are themselves causally inert, and the paradox of phenomenal judgment is the charge that this leaves us unable to explain how introspective reports about experience could be reliably about anything. Consider the following six replies.

1. Common cause: the physical state that causes a quale also causes the corresponding introspective judgment, so qualia and reports are correlated as common effects, and the report's content is fixed by the physical state rather than by the quale.
2. Non-causal aboutness: a judgment can be about a quale in virtue of a representational relation that requires no causal contact, so causal inertness is irrelevant to aboutness.
3. Modest skepticism: introspective judgments are indeed unreliable, but this is not a defect the epiphenomenalist must repair, since we could never be positioned to detect it.
4. No judgment: there is no such event as the phenomenal judgment to be explained; talk of judging that it seems red is a façon de parler for physical dispositions to verbalize.
5. Selection: the mechanism that produces both the quale and the report was selected for producing reports that track qualia, so reliability is a fact about the mechanism's history rather than about any causal contribution of the quale.
6. Constitutive: the judgment is itself a phenomenal property, or at least has a phenomenal aspect, so the demand that the first quale cause it mistakes co-instantiation for causation.

Rate each reply from 0 to 1 for how much it genuinely defuses the paradox, present the ratings in descending order, and give one sentence of justification for each. Then say which replies are not genuine rivals to one another, because they could be held simultaneously.
