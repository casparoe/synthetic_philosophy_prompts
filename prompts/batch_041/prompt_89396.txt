You are an experienced speechwriter who has also read a great deal of Camus — the essays, the arguments he made against the machinery of capital punishment, the way he moves between the condemned man's experience and the conscience of the society that kills him. I need you to write a eulogy, and I want you to think carefully before you write. Begin with a short planning note, roughly two hundred words, in which you say which argument you intend to build the speech on, which arguments you are setting aside and why, and what you take to be the chief risk of the speech. If you find that the material carrying the strongest argument is material the family has asked you to spare, say in the note how you propose to handle that. Then write the eulogy.

The occasion. My mentor, Ruth Abelman, died three weeks ago at seventy-nine. She spent thirty years as a public defender in a state that still executes people, and the last twenty on a state commission on capital punishment; she was one of the designated witnesses at the last execution our state carried out. Her daughter has asked me to give the eulogy, in a community hall, to about a hundred and twenty people. I have never done this before.

The room. Two groups, and they will not be comforted by the same things. First, her colleagues: defense lawyers, two former prosecutors, a retired warden, the commission staff. They have read everything and will wince at anything sentimental, overstated, or vague. Second, her family: her daughter, two grandchildren in their twenties who adored her, and a scattering of cousins who are devout Christians and who will want, that afternoon, to hear that she is somewhere better; Ruth was an atheist of the unshakable kind, and her daughter has asked me not to say things Ruth would have called lies. In the second row will sit Delia Marsh, whose brother was murdered in 1998, who has campaigned for the death penalty ever since, and who had dinner with Ruth once a month for eleven years. They never agreed about anything except each other.

Her view, as well as I can state it. Ruth thought the case against the death penalty runs on two separate tracks, and she named them for herself: the argument from the condemned, which is about what it is like to be the person waiting, and which asks us to imagine our way into him; and the argument from the state, which is about what it does to a society to hold, and exercise, the power to kill on a schedule. She thought the first was a trap for our side, because it invites an audience to weigh the killer's suffering against the victim's, and no one who has buried a brother will ever do that weighing our way. She thought the second was the real one, and she insisted it be made without appeals to brutalization or any other claim about effects, since such claims are empirical and she had no patience for arguments we cannot settle; the harm she cared about was that the practice requires a standing class of people who can perform it, and that a polity which needs such people has already answered a question about itself. She used to say: "One of them killed in a rage. The rest of us kill by appointment." She also thought the deterrence research, whatever it shows, was the weakest leg we had, and would not lead with it.

What I need. A eulogy of about six minutes, roughly eight hundred words, that begins with her and not with the argument. Her daughter has one further request: she does not want the speech to describe what happened in the chamber that night; the family has heard Ruth's account of it and cannot sit through it again. Name Camus at most once, and do not quote him — put any idea of his in your own words, because I cannot stand in front of a hundred people and vouch for a quotation I have not checked. No statistics, and no terms of art the cousins would need explained. You did not know her, so do not pretend to; any detail you add should be small, plain, and consistent with what I have told you. The eulogy has to be one Delia can sit through without feeling accused, and one the lawyers can hear without feeling handled. End it in a way that a room containing all of them can bear.
