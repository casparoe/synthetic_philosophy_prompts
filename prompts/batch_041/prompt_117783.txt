Three years ago I would've bet money that panpsychism was crank material. I came out of a neuro undergrad assuming consciousness was just a matter of finding the right neural correlates, then spent two years in a lab doing roughly that and came out the other side thinking some version of panpsychism might be the least bad option, which is a sentence I never expected to write about myself. The annoying part is that every time I get genuinely interested in it, the conversation collapses into the combination problem, and I leave the seminar more confused then when I went in — I can never tell which piece of it people actually think is fatal versus which piece is just the one that's fun to argue about. I've got about a year of fairly free time before I start a PhD (in a lab, not in philosophy, so this is realistically my one shot at doing this seriously), and I'd rather spend it on the part of the combination problem that actually matters than on whatever happens to be easiest to turn into a seminar paper.

So here's what I want: rank the open questions inside the combination problem by how much they matter for whether panpsychism is viable at all — not for whether it's elegant or fun — and defend the ordering. A friend of mine (physics postdoc, annoyingly confident) slices it into three and thinks the third is the whole game: the glue problem, which is how micro-experiences get bound into one experience at a time; the variety problem, which is how a short list of micro-qualities could yield the enormous range of macro-qualities we actually have — colors, sounds, moods, the whole palette; and the subject problem, which is where the "I" comes from, given that a pile of micro-subjects doesn't obviously add up to a single subject. He says the first two are either downstream of the third or artifacts of doing bad physics, and I honestly can't tell whether that's a real insight or just him being a physicist about it. For each of the three, tell me how upstream it is of the others, how tractable it is for someone with a year and no physics past a bio BA, and how much of the field's effort it currently gets relative to how much it deserves; then tell me if there's a fourth thing we're both missing, and what evidence or argument over the next couple of years would actually move something up or down the ranking. I want a defensible agenda for my year, not a survey — and don't bother pointing me at papers, I'll find those once I know what I'm looking for. Please write it in American English.
