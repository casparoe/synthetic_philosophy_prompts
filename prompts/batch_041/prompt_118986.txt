I'm a science-and-ideas features writer (I do the long stuff — the pieces with a philosopher on a hilltop in them), and I've been circling the surprise exam paradox for a piece that's been kicking my ass for about four months now. You know the setup: teacher says there'll be one test next week, and it'll be a genuine surprise — nobody will see it coming. The students grind through the backwards induction, kill off Friday (because by Thursday night they'd know), then Thursday, then all the way back to Monday, and conclude there can't be an exam at all — and then it lands on Wednesday and everyone's flat-footed. Here's my problem. Every time I explain this to someone they nod and say some version of "oh, that one," and I can tell nothing has moved — they can recite the resolution (something about common knowledge, or the announcement being self-undermining) and they walk away exactly as smug as they came in. So I've stopped trying to explain it. I've been asked to run a live session as part of the reporting — a room of about 20 adults at an ideas festival, mixed backgrounds, maybe three of them have read any philosophy since college, 90 minutes, I can rearrange the furniture and I've got a projector I probably won't use. I want to actually stage the thing so they feel the induction collapse in their own bones before they see the way out.

My rough idea, which I'd like you to make actually runnable: I play the teacher, and at the top I make the announcement — one exam this week, Monday to Friday, and you will not know it's coming on the morning it comes. Then I let them do the backwards reasoning themselves, in small groups, and get them to commit publicly — sealed envelopes, or everyone writes down a prediction for each day — so that by the end of the reasoning they're on record saying there can't possibly be an exam. Then, obviously, it comes, and I want the payoff scheme (points, tokens, a ridiculous trophy, whatever, no real money) arranged so that the people who trusted the induction actually loose something and the one or two who hedged look like geniuses. But I don't want to stop there. What I'm really after is a second round that turns the whole thing into a coordination problem: once they know the game, can the group defeat the announcement by making the exam expected — if everyone is certain it's coming every single day, then by the announcement's own logic it can't be a surprise, so either I break the announcement or there's no exam. I want them to definately feel that shift from "the announcement is a logical puzzle" to "the announcement is a prediction about us, and we can falsify it." So: rules, materials, how to seed who-knows-what-when, the payoff structure for both rounds, and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the moving parts — the backwards induction, the role of trust and credibility, and the gap between everyone knowing the announcement is true and everyone knowing that everyone knows it.

And be straight with me about two things. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with a room of adults, some of whom have had a drink — I'm already worried about the one guy who refuses to play along and poisons it, and about the guessing degenerating into pure chaos where the paradox just evaporates. Second, tell me what the game can't reproduce: my instinct is that there's something about the a priori argument — that it's supposed to go through no matter what anyone actually does — that no live game can capture, and I'd rather hear that from you than from some logician in the audience with his hand up.
