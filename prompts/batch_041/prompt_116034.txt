I am finishing a novel about the last decades of Newton's life, and I need a poem to close the chapter in which he decides, once again, not to publish what he believes. What I want is a villanelle in his voice — nineteen lines, two refrains — with the form doing the argument rather than ornamenting it. The two refrains should be the two sentences he cannot stop producing: the received formula he can say aloud in chapel without lying, and the conclusion he reaches every time he reasons from his own premises. Each of them will have to survive four returns without hardening into a slogan. In the closing quatrain they meet, and I want them to meet in that order — the recited line first, his own conclusion second — with nothing in the rhyme or the syntax allowed to reconcile them. The form is not of his century; I don't care. The chapter is about a man who keeps one sentence in his mouth and the other in his desk, and a villanelle is the only shape I know that will hold both without pretending they agree.

The theology has to be exact, because some of my readers will know the manuscripts. The argument I want the poem to rest on is the one he gives in the General Scholium: that God is, in his phrase, "a relative word, and has a respect to servants"; that deity consists in dominion — over servants, not over one's own body, as those imagine who take God to be the soul of the world — so that a being, however perfect, without dominion could not be called Lord God. From this the conclusion he cannot shake is that a second person sharing the dominion would be a second God, and a second person not sharing it would not be God at all: the title is constituted by rule, and rule cannot be divided without being multiplied. Let one of the middle tercets carry the text-critical habit that feeds all of it — the two verses he took to be the work of scribes, and the way a single pronoun, altered into the name of God, is enough to turn a man into the Creator. I would rather the poem never use the words Trinity or heresy; let the reader assemble what he is declining to say.

End it where his life ended, in silence: the formula said, the paper kept back, the treatise sent to a friend with a request to hold it. Plain diction of the period, no archaism for its own sake, no winking at the reader. Someone who has read the Scholium and the account of the two corruptions should find nothing to wince at; someone who has read neither should find a poem.
