I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years in condensed-matter research — who has, over the past three years, drifted into the philosophy of probability and rational choice, and I have been asked to conduct the interview segment of a small interdisciplinary seminar with a philosopher who has just published a book-length defense of a new resolution of the two-envelope paradox. I would like you to act as an expert philosopher of probability and decision theory and draft the questions I will put to her, since I understand the mathematics well enough but do not yet have the instincts for where a philosophical position of this kind is actually vulnerable.

The resolution she defends, as I understand it from the book's introduction, runs roughly as follows. She argues that the paradox is misdiagnosed when it is treated merely as an artifact of an improper, uniform prior over the sums of money. On her account the real error in the switching argument is a failure of reference-class invariance: the argument silently conditions on the observed amount, a choice of reference class that is not stable under the symmetry of the problem, and the residual "paradox" is a symptom of a deeper non-uniqueness in how we assign a measure to an open-ended gamble. She then claims that the genuinely interesting version of the problem is a two-agent variant she constructs, in which two people each hold one envelope of the same pair and are each offered the opportunity to swap before either opens; each reasons symmetrically and each, she says, has a prima facie reason to accept, and she uses this to argue that the real work is done by the distinction between ex ante and ex post rationality — which she connects to the disagreement between evidential and causal decision theory. I want the interview to press on exactly these commitments rather than letting her walk through the standard setup again.

Please begin by brainstorming, for yourself, the places where a position of this shape is most likely to come under strain, and then produce eight to ten interview questions, ordered as I would actually ask them, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I need at least one question on the role she assigns to the prior and why she thinks the uniform-prior diagnosis is insufficient; one on the open-envelope versus closed-envelope cases and whether her reference-class diagnosis applies equally to both; one on whether her resolution is meant as a claim about what is rational to do or a claim about what is correct to believe, and whether that distinction matters for the two-agent variant; one that tests whether the two-agent swap really restores a paradox or quietly assumes the conclusion; and a closing question about what evidence or argument would count as a decisive refutation of her position. Take a definite stance throughout: where there is a strongest line of attack, commit to it and tell me why it is the strongest, rather than laying out several possibilities and leaving the choice to me. Write the questions in American English, and keep the tone suitable for a public seminar.
