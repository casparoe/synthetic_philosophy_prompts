I teach a graduate seminar on explanation in the social sciences and I am setting the take-home final. Please write one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. The subject is the claim that a culture — a body of values, beliefs, and practices — can be a cause of economic or social change, in the tradition of the thesis that an ascetic religious ethic helped bring about a new economic mentality. You may name Max Weber as the source of that thesis, but do not build the question around any particular text, and do not require students to cite anything.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the thesis or by listing the standard objections to it. To force students past recitation, ground the question in the following invented case, which they are to treat as the object of analysis:

Over roughly two generations, a devotional movement — call it the Ashen Path — spreads through a set of highland villages. Its teachings are otherworldly: they concern ritual purity, the fate of the soul, and the authority of a lineage of teachers. They say nothing about commerce, contracts, or profit. Shortly after the movement takes hold, a distinctive institution appears in the same villages: long-distance trading partnerships in which unrelated households pool capital and hold it for years, on terms enforced by reputation rather than by courts. The geography and the timing of the two developments coincide almost exactly. The traders themselves almost never invoke the Path to justify what they do; asked to explain their partnerships, they speak in flatly practical terms. And the villages that adopted the Path were, before its arrival, already somewhat marginal and mobile, and they faced the same new trade routes as neighboring villages that did not adopt it.

The question should require students to do three things: first, to say what it would take for the Path to count as a cause of the trading institution rather than a mere accompaniment of it, or an ex post narrative frame laid over it; second, to distinguish the several senses in which values might be said to explain a practice — the agent's own reasons, a functional fit between doctrine and institution, a selection effect on which practices survive, and a constitutive rather than causal relation between the two; and third, to state what evidence could discriminate among these readings when only this single historical case is available. Aim the question at a candidate who can answer it in ninety minutes.

Then write a model answer of about 800 to 1000 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks that names five moves a strong answer must make, the marks each carries, and, for each move, a one-line note on how candidates characteristically miss it. Design the hardest move — the one that separates an A from a B — so that it turns on whether the student can say what would have to be true for the causal claim to be false, and can acknowledge the respects in which the case underdetermines the answer.
