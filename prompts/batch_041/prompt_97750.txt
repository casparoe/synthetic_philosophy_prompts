I retired from bench chemistry a few years back and have been trying to teach myself philosophy, which mostly means reading in the evening and annoying my old labmates alot with questions. I keep coming back to the symbol grounding problem, but not the version everyone trots out with the Chinese room. The part that nags me is the difference between a system having the right inferential role for a symbol and the symbol actually latching onto anything. You can have a perfectly coherent web where every term is defined by its relations to every other term, and the whole thing can run like a clock, and still none of it touches the world. A model can answer every question about a rose and never smell one. I've been reading a bit about teleosemantics, the idea that a representation is grounded when it has the right kind of function to track something, and it seems to me a text-only system can have all the inferential relations and none of that tracking function. What I want is a poem that makes that gap feel real, not just says it. Can you write a pantoum (the repeating-line form where lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the last stanza reuses the first stanza) in the voice of a dictionary or a language model that has learned all the relations but has no contact with anything outside the text. The repetition should do the work: the same line should mean something different each time it returns, because the context has shifted, which is exactly the problem -- meaning looks like it comes from relations, but the relations never cash out. Maybe use a refrain line that starts as a boast and ends as a confession, something like I know every word for water but I have never been wet. I definately don't want a lecture; I want a poem. And if pantoum is too rigid, a poem with a returning line is fine, but the return has to be structural, not decorative. Also, a philosopher of mind should be able to read it and say yes, that is the distinction between inferential role and grounding, not just a vague poem about computers.
