I'd like a serious critical reading of the film as a study in good-faith disagreement, not a plot summary — one that treats the jury room as a deliberately constructed epistemic environment and asks what the film actually claims about how minds change. Two bodies of work should be brought to bear, because both cut against the film's apparent moral. First, the argumentative theory of reasoning associated with Hugo Mercier and Dan Sperber: reason is slanted toward producing arguments that serve our side but is comparatively reliable at evaluating the arguments of others, so it works best in groups where positions are genuinely contested and everyone has a stake in getting the answer right. Second, the naive-realism and false-polarization findings in social psychology: partisans on each side believe they see the case as it is, take the other side to be the biased one, and overestimate how far apart the two sides really are. The question I care about is whether the film dramatizes good-faith argument or quietly evades it. Is that an honest account of how we come to doubt ourselves, or a flattering fantasy in which the other side is always motivated and never right?
