I spent about 35 years as an ecologist, mostly on social insects, and since I retired I've been reading alot of philosophy, so forgive me if I mangle the vocabulary. I've ended up in a place I'd like you to try to knock me out of.

The view: an ant colony can genuinely have a mind. Not "as if" it has one, not a useful fiction, not a shorthand for the behaviour of the ants — the colony itself is the cognitive system, and the individual ants are more like cells, or transistors. I'll call the colony's mind Aunt Hillary, borrowing Hofstadter's thought experiment (I know that's a borrowing, not an original observation).

Please start by brainstorming: spend a couple of paragraphs just getting all the candidate defeaters onto the table, including the ones you think are weak or already answered, before you evaluate any of them. Then put on the hat of a professional philosopher of mind with a serious background in levels of description, supervenience and emergence, and work through them carefully and honestly rather than reassuringly. Don't just point me at the literature — I want the arguments themselves; a reading list is no use to me.

Here's the wrinkle that actually makes me unsure, and it's the one I'd most like you to take seriously. In the colonies I studied, the colony-level patterns — the ones you'd want to call thoughts, moods, decisions — unfold over years to decades. A worker lives a few months at most. So no single ant ever lives through a whole colony-level mental episode; the episode is stitched together across generations of ants that never overlap. Does that matter? Is a mind that is never present to any of its own parts, and that only becomes visible to an observer using a much longer time-window, still a mind? Or does it quietly stop being one mind and become a sequence of overlapping, partly disjoint ones? If you think it survives, tell me what it survives on, because the same argument seems to threaten any long-lived organism whose parts turn over.

Second wrinkle, and this one is my own doing. Suppose my old department builds a colony where we drive the pheromone landscape from outside — a puppet colony. Its colony-level behaviour looks for all the world like deliberation, hesitation and something like regret. We know exactly how we're producing it, and we're producing it. Is that colony a mind? If yes, my view survives but gets strange, and I want to know how strange. If no, I want to know what the puppet colony lacks that the free one has, and whether that difference is a difference in kind or merely in who is holding the strings.

So: I hold that ant colonies have minds. What would count against it? Sort the defeaters from the ones that should genuinely move me down to the ones that are merely awkward, and say plainly which of them a defender of colony minds would have to concede rather than dismiss. Where I'd most like honesty is on whether my view has been quietly protected from refutation — whether anything could ever happen that would make me give it up, and if not, whether that is a problem or just what you'd expect from a claim pitched at this level of description. And please tell me if there's a version of the view that is much more defensable than the one I've stated, because I suspect I've stated it too strongly.

Write your answer in British English, please — behaviour, realise, colour, and so on.
