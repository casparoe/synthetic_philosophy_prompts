I am a fifth-year PhD student in philosophy and I run a monthly interview series for graduate students in the department. Next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a distinctive answer to Glaucon's challenge, and I need to prepare questions that will push her beyond the version of the view she has already published. Her position, which she calls the accountability-first answer, is that Glaucon's challenge gets its force from a mistaken picture of practical reason as a private cost-benefit calculation. On her account, moral reasons are not external constraints that a rational agent might opt out of when the ring of Gyges removes the sanctions; they are reasons that exist only within a practice of mutual accountability. The ring of Gyges does not reveal what we would do without morality; it reveals what happens to the normative situation when the possibility of answerability is removed. She argues that the challenge therefore cannot be answered by showing that justice is instrumentally rational or by appealing to the agent's own interest in integrity. Instead, the answer is that the question why be moral is malformed when asked by an agent who has stepped entirely outside the second-personal standpoint. She also claims that this view avoids the standard objections to social-accountability theories because it does not reduce moral reasons to actual social approval; the relevant practice is idealized, but not in the way that ideal-observer theories are.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond having her restate the view. I want at least one question each on: (1) whether the view makes moral reasons contingent on the existence of actual or possible accountability relations, and what happens in cases where the agent is the last person on earth or is otherwise unanswerable to anyone; (2) what exactly does the idealizing in her idealization, and how her view differs from ideal-observer and social-contract theories; (3) how the view handles secret wrongdoing that no one will ever know about, including the case of the prudent wrongdoer who is convinced that secret injustice is rational, and whether it can explain the wrongness of such acts without appealing to a hypothetical observer; (4) whether the view excludes agents who cannot be held accountable, such as young children or people with severe cognitive disabilities, from the scope of moral reasons, and if so, what fills the gap; and (5) how she understands the residual force of Glaucon's challenge if she thinks the question is malformed, why the challenge has seemed compelling to so many philosophers, and what she would say to someone who finds it compelling. For each question, include a likely answer and a follow-up that presses on the weakest point of that answer. Arrange the questions in the order they should be asked, so that the sequence makes the position progressively harder to maintain. End with one question about what evidence or argument could count against her view. Skip biographical and warm-up questions, and do not ask for reading recommendations or citations.
