Ok so I retired from a biology department about two years ago and I've been spending my mornings reading philosophy, mostly ethics, and I've gotten stuck on something I'd like help with. I don't want a verdict on utilitarianism, I want you to audit an analogy for me, because I suspect it's doing more work than anyone admits.

The analogy is the one that gets wheeled out whenever somebody raises the objection that utilitarianism doesn't take seriously the seperateness of persons -- the complaint that it treats a society the way you'd treat one person, letting some parts lose so the whole comes out ahead, when really the loser never gets paid back. The reply goes: but I do exactly this to myself. I go to the dentist, I let my Tuesday self suffer so my Friday self is better off, and nobody thinks I've wronged Tuesday. So a person is really a series of stages, and we routinely let some stages lose so others can win. If that's fine inside one life, why isn't it fine across lives? A community is just a bigger version of the same thing, only the boundaries between the units are drawn a bit differently.

Here's where my old training kicks in. My field spent about a century arguing over whether an ant colony or a termite mound is one organism or a lot of organisms, and the answer always came down to what you wanted the word organism to do for you -- a shared metabolism, a shared fate, a single control centre, a common evolutionary interest. So my suspicion is that the ethics argument is pulling the same trick with the word person.

What I'd like from you, laid out as a correspondence table if that's easiest:

1. What maps onto what. Does person-to-time-slice really stand in for society-to-person, or does the first relation have something in it with no counterpart in the second -- compensation, memory, the later stage remembering it was the earlier one, the earlier stage caring about the later one, the fact that Tuesday and Friday are the same legal and moral subject?
2. Which of the mismatches actually matter for what the analogy is being used to do, and which are just decoration. And what does the analogy quietly assume before it gets going -- that a society has a welfare of its own the way a person does, for instance, or that summing across time-slices and summing across people are the same kind of operation?
3. The part that really interests me. I'm told some philosophers try to rescue the analogy by arguing that personal identity isn't what matters anyway -- what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness -- so that the gap between my Tuesday self and my neighbour shrinks. That looks to me like it cuts both ways: if it repairs the analogy it also guts the objection, and if the objection is serious then maybe so is the analogy. Can you sort out whether that's right, and where it leaves the original arguement?
4. Whether there's a better analogy lying around for the same job, or whether arguing this whole thing by analogy was a mistake from the start.
