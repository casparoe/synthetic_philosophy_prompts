My father died in February. Two days before he did, he asked me to promise that I would never sell the family cabin, and I promised. I am now the one paying its taxes, the roof needs replacing, and there is an offer I would otherwise accept; and I find that I cannot say what it is about that promise that holds me, or how much it holds me for.

To get my bearings I have been going through my other promises and writing down what I actually think, and the results do not fit together.

(a) A colleague and I agree to meet for coffee; nothing turns on it; a better offer arrives. I think I still have to go, or at least to reschedule and explain.

(b) My flatmate assumed I would drive her to the airport, because I always have; I never said I would. I do not think I owe her anything beyond an apology for a reasonable assumption.

(c) My father's promise.

(d) I told a stranger on a forum that I would send him a file; he never wrote again. I think I am still bound, though faintly, and only until he clearly no longer wants it.

(e) A man holds a knife to my ribs and I promise to hand over my wallet. Nothing at all.

(f) I promised to help a friend move, and my daughter was hospitalized the night before. I do not think the promise was void — I owed him an explanation, and I owe him the equivalent help another time.

(g) I promised to keep a secret, then learned the secret was a plan to injure someone. The promise does not hold.

(h) I promised to give a talk, and the organizers wrote to say they had found someone else. I was released, and the promise simply ended.

(i) I resolved privately to run every morning; breaking it does not feel like the same kind of failure at all.

The trouble is that the cases that hold me most tightly seem to have the least of what I would have said makes promises binding. My father expected nothing further, relied on nothing, and cannot be disappointed. Case (b) has all the reliance in the world and no promise. Case (d) is barely a promise and still binds. I have been assuming that what matters is the expectation I create and the disappointment I would cause, and that assumption cannot survive case (c).

Is there a principle — or a small set of them — that gets my verdicts, or nearly all of them? I would like it stated precisely enough that I can apply it, with the case that forces each clause, a verdict on a case I have not settled (I promised my four-year-old nephew I would take him to the zoo on Saturday; a work emergency intervenes; he will be disappointed for a week and then forget), and an honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and must overturn. If one principle will not do the work, give me the two or three that come closest, say exactly where each one breaks, and say which I should prefer.

Please open with the principle itself, in the first sentence, and then argue for it; no preamble, and do not restate my cases — refer to them by letter.
