For eleven years I ran four locations of a regional restaurant franchise and spent two of those years sitting on the franchisee advisory council; I have no academic training and never studied politics formally, but I read a great deal and argue about this with friends. Lately I have been reading about how the European Union is supposed to leave to its member states anything they can handle for themselves, including a procedure where national parliaments can jointly object to a proposal as overreach; about American fights over what Washington may impose on the states; and about Swiss cantons and German Länder running their own affairs. The arguments sound uncannily like the ones I used to have with our franchisor, and I want to know how far the resemblance really goes. Please take the main positions on that side of the debate — the case for deciding at the lowest level because that is where the knowledge is; the Catholic version of the idea that a larger body does wrong when it takes over what a smaller one can do for itself; the claim that states and cantons are laboratories whose experiments the others may copy or refuse; the argument that people and businesses can move to whichever jurisdiction suits them; and the standard reasons for pulling a question up to the higher level anyway — and restate each one in the vocabulary I actually use: brand standards, territory, local operators, compliance, corporate mandates, the advisory council, renewals. Be as faithful as you can, and do not quietly improve the political arguments on the way over. Then give me the residue: which parts came across cleanly, which had to be bent to fit, and which have no counterpart in a franchise system at all. My suspicion is that the whole thing reduces to the ordinary business point that the person closest to the problem should make the call, and that whatever is left over is just sentiment about local identity; tell me whether that is right, and where it is not, say whether the leftovers are things my vocabulary is blind to or signs that the political version was less clear than it sounds.
