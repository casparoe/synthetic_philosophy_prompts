I write about science and ideas for a British weekly, and I am putting together a piece on how philosophers of science handle disputes that are still live in the laboratory rather than comfortably settled in the seminar room. To that end I spent two days at a workshop on methodology in the physical sciences, where I filled several pages of my notebook with an argument between Nadia, a philosopher of science, and Tobias, a cosmologist, over whether Lakatos's notion of a research programme can settle anything in real time. I stayed out of it and I honestly could not tell who had the better of it; I found each of them convincing in turn, which is rather the difficulty. I would like your help adjudicating the exchange.

Here it is, as close to verbatim as my notebook allows.

1. Tobias: Lakatos is more useful than people admit. Apply him to cosmology and you get a verdict. The dark-matter programmes have repeatedly produced predictions that later came out right; they told us to expect structure of a certain kind before we could see it. The modified-gravity programmes have mostly absorbed bad news after the fact: every galaxy-scale anomaly that turns up is met with another field, another coupling, another free parameter, until the original idea is buried under its own repairs. A programme that only ever accommodates is degenerating. That is the whole of the criterion, and it does not need a philosopher's gloss.

2. Nadia: That verdict is bought with hindsight. The test cannot be run in the present, because you never know at the time whether a patch is a temporary expedient or the beginning of a successful line of work; that is simply what research is like. The same evidence you cite for dark matter looked like post-hoc repair when it was new: the rotation curves came first, the particle came later, and for a while it was one anomaly after another with nothing novel on offer. Had we applied your test then, we would have called the winning programme degenerating. Lakatos knew this. His methodology was deliberately more forgiving than Popper's, and he treated his reconstructions as something one does after the fact.

3. Tobias: Then the criterion has no application to a live dispute, which is the only kind that matters to anyone. If the verdict is always available only in retrospect, you are not supplying a method, you are writing history with a verdict-shaped flourish. And forbearance cannot be unlimited: at some point waiting longer becomes never saying anything, and then Lakatos has no more normative bite than a shrug.

4. Nadia: There is an asymmetry, though. A progressive programme has to succeed at something positive: it has to anticipate a result that would otherwise have come as a surprise. A degenerating one only has to survive. So even in the present you can ask whether anything novel has actually been delivered lately, and the burden of proof sits on one side only. That is not hindsight; that is a live question.

5. Tobias: Which is exactly why I would operationalise it. Take the ratio of belt additions to novel successes. Every time a programme adds an auxiliary assumption, a new particle, a new field, a new parameter, you tally it; every time it delivers a successful novel prediction, you tally that. A programme whose ratio is rising is degenerating; one whose ratio is falling is progressive. That is checkable in the present, and it gives you a number rather than a mood.

6. Nadia: The count presupposes that you already know how to individuate the additions and what counts as novel, and those are precisely the matters in dispute. Is a second species of dark particle one addition or a family? Does a successful retrodiction of something already half-known count as novel? Any historian who wants a particular verdict can gerrymander the tally to get it. Your number is a mood with arithmetic on top.

7. Tobias: That objection proves too much. We individuate and count things throughout the sciences, species and particles and events, without waiting for a finished theory of individuation, and vagueness at the margins does not stop the counts being robust in clear cases. Cosmology is a clear case.

8. Nadia: Cosmology is the case you already had a verdict about. That is not a criterion, it is a taste.

Could you take the exchange move by move: for each of the eight, say how strong it is and why, where the speaker overreaches or concedes more than they need to, and whether it is answered by a later move. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who has the better of the argument overall. I do not need a verdict on cosmology itself, only on whether either of them has made Lakatos do the work they want him to do. Our style guide is British English, so please write your answer in British English.
