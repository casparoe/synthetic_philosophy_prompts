For a while now I have been trying to settle, for myself, whether a famine is best understood as a natural disaster or as a political crime, and I have ended up with a test I would like you to take seriously and then attack. Call a famine political if there existed a feasible course of action, open to people who had the power to take it, which they knew or could reasonably have been expected to know would have prevented most of the deaths; call it natural if no such course of action existed. By that test Bengal in 1943, Ireland in the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward all come out political, which is more or less where Sen's analysis of Bengal, with its emphasis on entitlement rather than on food supply, points. But I distrust the test, because it seems to have only two settings. Read "feasible" strictly and every famine is political, since with hindsight some alternative policy can always be described; read it loosely and every famine is natural, since no one is to blame for failing to foresee what no one foresaw. Ireland is the case that pulls me hardest in both directions at once: the blight was as natural as anything gets, and yet food was still being shipped out of the country while people starved. Please write an essay that takes the test seriously and then decides the question. Weigh the case for treating famine as a political crime against the case for treating it as a natural disaster, and reach a verdict: either the distinction is real and can be drawn in a principled place, in which case say where, and defend that place against my worry that the setting of the test is arbitrary; or the distinction cannot survive, in which case say what it was doing for us and what we should put in its place. Do not end on a non-committal note.
