Quick version of my read, so you can see where I'm coming from. Ordinary expected utility says: multiply each outcome's utility by its probability, add up, pick the biggest. Buchak's complaint (as I understand it) is that this bakes your attitude to risk straight into the utility numbers, which seems wrong — your taste for risk and your ranking of outcomes feel like two separate things. So she adds a "risk function" that reweights the probabilities: a risk-averse person, roughly, gets the probabilities of the bad outcomes bumped up and the good ones bumped down. Fine, sure.

But two things bug me and I'd like you to really dig in.

First: if the risk function is just transforming probabilities, then isn't that exactly what prospect theory does? So is risk-weighted expected utility really anything more than prospect theory with the loss-aversion filed off and a normative label slapped on it?

Second, and this is the one I actually care about: if all you're doing is swapping the objective probabilities for the person's own reweighted ones, then haven't you just relabeled her subjective probabilities? In which case REU isn't a rival to subjective expected utility at all — it's SEU wearing a costume, and the whole "third way between expected utility and prospect theory" pitch collapses. The only difference would be bookkeeping: whether we file those numbers under beliefs or under risk attitudes. If that's right, the debate is basically terminological and I should say so in the piece.

So my question: am I right that it's SEU in disguise? And if I'm wrong, what's the actual structural feature that stops it collapsing — what is it about the machinery that I'm missing?
