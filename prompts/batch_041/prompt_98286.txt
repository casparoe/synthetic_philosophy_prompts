I'm a third-year PhD student in legal theory, and next Thursday I'm presenting a paper to our criminal-law theory reading group — it's the Hart–Devlin debate that's the backdrop, but I'm trying to push it somewhere new — and I'd like you to build me a Q&A prep sheet. The thesis I'm defending is a deliberately narrow version of legal moralism, which I've been calling the institutional-meaning account. Roughly: the criminal law is not in the business of enforcing morality as such, and it isn't in the business of preventing harm to others in the ordinary Millian sense either. Its distinctive and legitimate job is to settle, authoritatively, the public meaning of certain shared institutions — elections, the professions, the degree, the marriage contract, the criminal trial itself — and it may criminalise conduct only where that conduct, if widely practised, would change what one of those institutions means to the people who take part in it. So, for instance, I argue that buying and selling authorship on academic papers is properly criminalisable, not because any individual case involves fraud, and not because the practice harms anyone in a measurable way, but because a general market in authorship would change what a published paper *is* — it would stop being a claim about who did the work and become a claim about who paid for the credit. That's my running test case, and I contrast it with something like ticket touting, which I claim the account doesn't reach.

The audience is the part I really need you to take seriously. It's a mixed group: two retributivists who are basically desert theorists and think the criminal law's business is giving people what they deserve; two liberals who are Millians through and through and will hear the words 'legal moralism' and reach for their copy of On Liberty; one Foucauldian who thinks the whole of the criminal law is a technology of power and will be deeply unimpressed by all my talk of public meaning; and one empirically-minded criminologist who keeps asking what the evidence is that any of this changes behaviour. Draft me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these people, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how much damage each would do to me — I'd rather know which one is fatal than get a flat list. For each, give me a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is thin, and what I should concede up front so I don't come across as evasive. And tell me the one question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks.
