Ok so I'm setting this as an essay question for my third-years and I want you to have a go at it before I inflict it on them. You're a philosopher of epistemology with a good grasp of the Meno and the contemporary value-of-knowledge litrature — think carefully, and maybe spend a few lines at the start brainstorming the moves available before you commit to one.

Here's the setup. Socrates ends the Meno with the suggestion that true belief is no less useful than knowledge for getting things done — the man who has merely been told the right road to Larissa will get there just as well as the man who has walked it. Then there's the Daedalus bit: true beliefs are like the statues of Daedalus, they run away unless you tie them down, and what ties them down is working out the reason why. And then there's the modern complaint, usually called the swamping problem: if true belief is the fundamental epistemic good, then adding reliability or justification to a true belief looks like adding a good quality to something already good, the way a good cup is made better by being red — the redness gets swamped. So the extra bit doesn't seem to add any value.

Your job: argue for a particular answer to the Meno problem. Don't just survey the options. I want you to defend the view that the extra value of knowledge over true belief is not a matter of the belief being more stable, more likely to be true, or more useful, but of the believer being able to run the route to the belief again for themselves and to hand the warrant on to someone else. Put a name on it if you like — call it re-runnable warrant, or whatever you prefer, just be clear about what you mean.

Two things you have to deal with along the way:

1) The oracle case. Suppose a perfectly reliable source tells you something true, and you believe it on their say-so with complete confidence, but you have no idea why it's true and couldn't begin to reconstruct it. You have, arguably, a true belief with excellent warrant of a sort. Say whether your account counts this as knowledge, and if it does, whether it has the same kind of value as the slave's geometry belief after Socrates has got him to work through the reasoning.

2) The swamping objection, aimed at your own view. If the value you're pointing to is the value of being able to do something — reconstruct, transfer, defend — hasn't the value just become instrumental, in which case it's still the true belief doing all the work? Answer this properly rather than waving at it.

Keep it in British English. Aim for something you could read aloud in a seminar without embarrassment, so no bullet-point sprawl — proper prose, though you can use numbered sections if that helps. And finish with a single line, starting Verdict:, giving me your position in about ten words so I can see at a glance what you went for.
