A colleague of mine in comparative cognition has spent the past year issuing off-the-cuff verdicts on animal consciousness in our reading group, and I would like to use that track record to predict how she will respond to several cases we have not yet discussed. Her verdicts to date are these: humans, monkeys, dogs, and rats are conscious; crows and parrots are conscious; the octopus is conscious; fish, including trout and zebrafish, are conscious; and she denies consciousness to the nematode C. elegans, the sea slug Aplysia, the jellyfish, plants, and bacteria. She has never stated a general criterion, only these case-by-case calls, and she treats each as settled. What I want from you is less a verdict on consciousness itself than an account of the rule she appears to be running.

First, tell me what she would probably say about three animals we have not discussed: the honeybee; the jumping spider Portia, which takes long detours and appears to plan routes to its prey; and the nautilus, a cephalopod whose nervous system is far less centralized than the octopus's. Second, set out the candidate rules that fit her existing verdicts equally well — I suspect one rule that turns on a threshold of neural centralization or size and another that turns on flexible, model-based behavior rather than fixed reflexes — and state what each predicts for the three new cases. Third, give me your best guess at the rule she is actually running, with your reasons, and identify any pair of her verdicts that quietly commits her to more than she may realize. Finally, name the single further case that would most sharply separate the rules you identify, and explain what each rule would say about it. I am more interested in the discriminating logic than in which side of the consciousness question you personally endorse. Please write your answer in American English.
