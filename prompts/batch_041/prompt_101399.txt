Act as an expert in algorithmic fairness, causal inference, and the philosophy of discrimination. I have just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether a machine-learning system can be fair in more than one sense at once, and I want to know whether my own commitments hang together. Please think carefully before answering. Begin by privately brainstorming the logical relations among the items below, then give me a structured audit rather than a general essay. Do not give me citations, reading lists, or bibliographies; I want the consistency check itself.

Here is where I have landed.

1. A risk score is fair only if it is calibrated within groups: for any score value, the probability of the outcome is the same for members of different protected groups who receive that score.

2. A decision rule is fair only if it satisfies equalized odds: across protected groups, the true-positive rate and the false-positive rate are equal.

3. The morally fundamental notion of discrimination is causal: a decision is discriminatory just in case it would have been different had the person’s protected attribute been different, holding fixed all features that are not causally downstream of that attribute.

4. A model that does not take the protected attribute as an input cannot be discriminatory in the causal sense, because its output does not depend on the attribute. Proxies that correlate with the attribute are regrettable, but they are not themselves discriminatory.

5. The impossibility results showing that calibration and equalized odds cannot both hold when base rates differ are artifacts of demanding exact equality. In practice, a model can be close enough on both, so no genuine trade-off is forced.

6. Individual fairness, understood as treating similar individuals similarly, is the ultimate criterion, and group metrics are only useful as proxies for it.

7. When base rates differ across groups, that is almost always the downstream effect of past injustice, so the right remedy is social policy that changes the base rates, not algorithmic adjustment.

8. Auditing for disparate impact requires using the protected attribute, but that is entirely different from using it as a model input, so commitment (4) is unaffected by the need to audit.

9. Race and gender are socially constructed rather than natural properties, so it is not clear that there is a fact of the matter about what would have happened had a person’s protected attribute been different.

My friend says I am trying to have it all: I want race-blind models, a causal definition of discrimination, calibration, equal odds, and individual fairness, and I refuse to accept that any of them must be sacrificed. I suspect he is right about at least one, but I do not know which. Please tell me which of these commitments are in genuine tension, which only look like they are, and which single revision would clean up the most. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity, say what would have to give, and say what the cheapest repair would cost me in the other things I care about. Do not simply list every pair as tense; a good audit has to discriminate.
