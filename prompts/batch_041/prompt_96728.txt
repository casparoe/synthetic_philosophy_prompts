Ok, so in civics we did the Condorcet jury theorem — the thing where if everyone is even slightly more likely than not to get a yes/no question right, and they all make up their minds seperately, then a big enough majority is basically guaranteed to be right, and the bigger the group gets the closer to certain it is. My teacher presented it like the mathematical proof that democracy works and I mostly bought it, but two things bug me. First, if everyone's getting their info from the same place — same group chat, same viral video — they'll all be wrong in the same direction, so it definately feels like the independence assumption is doing all the work and the "better than a coin flip" part is almost irrelevant. Second, I have this feeling that a big group can land on the right answer even when most of the individuals in it are worse than a coin flip, just because their mistakes cancel out, which would mean the main assumption isn't even needed. I want to keep believing it, so tell me straight: what would actually count against the theorem? Which of its assumptions could realistically fail, what would the world have to look like for each one to fail, which failures would be fatal and which just annoying, and what would its defenders admit counts against it? And am I right that a crowd can be right when nobody in it is, or am I mixing up two different things?
