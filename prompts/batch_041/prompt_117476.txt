I am a postdoctoral fellow in the philosophy of economics, and I need you to draft a one-page proposal (single-spaced, roughly 800 words) for a nine-month internal research grant. The subject is Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, but I want it approached from an angle I have not seen done properly, so I would much rather you commit to one sharp framing of the problem than survey the field. Please do not include a bibliography, a literature review by citation, or any references at all; refer to positions and arguments by description, and I will supply the citations myself.

The half-formed idea is this. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, as I understand it, tells us that if individual and social preferences both admit an expected-utility representation, and the social ordering satisfies a Pareto-type condition with respect to the individuals' preferences, then the social ordering can be represented as a weighted sum of individual utilities — with the weights left undetermined by the theorem itself. The further step that is supposed to pin the weights down, and thereby convert the aggregation theorem into an argument for utilitarianism, is the veil-of-ignorance argument: a chooser who does not know which member of society they will turn out to be, and who treats each possibility as equally likely, will select the arrangement that maximises average utility. What strikes me is that this step is an argument about identity — the chooser's uncertainty is uncertainty about whom to be. That is exactly what a Harsanyi-style aggregation across moral theories cannot supply, since one can be uncertain which theory is correct but cannot be any theory. My suspicion — and I want the proposal to test it rather than assume it — is that the transplant survives at the level of the theorem's formal structure and fails at the level of the argument that fixes the weights, and that the failure is not a technicality but carries the entire normative payload. A second, related hunch: the equiprobability assumption is doing work that only makes sense for identity, and any attempt to replace it with something available under theory-uncertainty — a prior over theories, a risk attitude toward being wrong, a maximin rule — alters the aggregation rather than merely reinterpreting it.

What I want from the proposal:

- a question stated precisely enough to have a determinate answer, and narrow enough to be settled in nine months;
- an account of why the existing positions leave the question open: where the aggregation theorem's defenders, its critics, and those who use it as a template for aggregating across theories each stop short;
- the approach: what I would actually do, month by month, and why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — in particular, why the project should be conceptual and axiomatic rather than a survey of applications;
- what a positive result would look like, what a negative result would look like, and what would count as the project failing;
- a realistic scope for nine months, including which parts I should cut first if I fall behind.

Take a definite position throughout: choose one framing of the question and defend it, make the methodological choices concrete, and give your reasons. If you think my suspicion is wrong, or that the framing is a dead end, say so and propose a better one rather than hedging and presenting every option as equally live. Finally, tell me the single strongest objection to the proposal as you have framed it, and what you would say in reply.
