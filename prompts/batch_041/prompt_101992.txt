A survey chapter I am reading on the theory of causation contains the following summary of the difference between counterfactual and interventionist accounts, and I would like a careful audit of it before I take it on board.

The chapter says: "The counterfactual theory holds that an event C is a cause of an event E just in case, had C not occurred, E would not have occurred. The interventionist theory is at bottom the same idea, only better equipped: it says that C causes E when manipulating C makes a difference to E. Its real contribution is to replace the counterfactual theorist's vague talk of what would have happened with the precise notion of an intervention, which is simply any deliberate change to a single variable. The two theories therefore differ mostly in emphasis: the counterfactual theory is about what would have happened under a hypothetical change, whereas the interventionist theory is about what we can actually do, which makes the latter a practical, action-oriented restatement of the former. And because an intervention is itself defined in causal terms -- an intervention on C must not be produced by other causes of E -- the interventionist theory is circular, and it is this circularity that ultimately sinks it."

Go through this paragraph clause by clause. For each claim, tell me what someone working on causation would accept as accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a genuine misrepresentation -- paying particular attention to any point at which the chapter blurs the very distinction it sets out to explain. Then address the closing verdict directly: even granting that the interventionist theory is circular in the sense described, does that circularity refute it, or is it the sort of feature its proponents would claim the theory was constructed to accommodate? I am inclined to think the account cannot be as deflationary as the chapter makes it sound, but I do not know the subject well enough to locate where the deflation goes wrong.
