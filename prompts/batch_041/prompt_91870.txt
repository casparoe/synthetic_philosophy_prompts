Write me a short story that's actually doing some work on the difference between freedom as non-interference and freedom as non-domination -- you know the republican idea, that you're unfree if somebody has the power to mess with you arbitrarily even if they never actually do. I want it to earn the distinction instead of announcing it, so no mouthpiece character who stops the plot to explain it. Also no named philosophers, I don't want it reading like a lecture with dialogue.

Set it somewhere small and closed where exit is expensive and everyone knows everyone -- a company town, a religious commune, a ship, an Antarctic station, whatever fits. The thing I actually want to see tested: a character who is, by the plain non-interference measure, completely free. Nobody bosses her, nobody blocks her, she goes where she likes. And yet she's arranged her entire life around what one particular person might do. Around the mere possibility. I also want a formal channel for complaint or appeal that exists on paper, that everybody in the place treats as real and meaningful, and that cannot actually change any decision. Does having the channel make her free? I'm honestly not sure, and I'd like the story to be not sure either.

One more constraint, because I don't want the easy version: there's no villain and no threat. The powerful person is decent, never leans on anyone, maybe even kind. If your story needs a cruelty or an ultimatum to make the point land, you've written the wrong story. And at least one character should think she *is* free, and should be able to say why, and shouldn't be stupid or a strawman. I'd rather the story ends on the disagreement than on a verdict.

Oh and if you can work in the thing where the test is whether you can look the other person in the eye without having to check yourself first, do it, but only if it falls out of a scene naturally.

After the story, give me a short note, under 150 words: which reading of the non-domination idea the story ends up betting on, where it takes liberties with the concept or leans on a contested version of it, and what a rival reading would have forced you to write differently -- basically, what would make you tell this story another way. Keep the story itself under 900 words.
