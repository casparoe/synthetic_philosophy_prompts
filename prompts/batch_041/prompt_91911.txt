I teach a grad seminar on translation and I'm putting together the take-home final. Most of the term is Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot and the whole headache of what survives when you translate a text whose *form* is the point. I want one essay question that's genuinely hard, and I've got a half-formed idea I need you to turn into a real question with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The idea: lipograms. Give the students a short source text — say a little prose paragraph in English that never once uses the letter "e" — and ask them to translate it into a language where dodging that same letter is close to impossible (French is the obvious one, "e" is everywhere). The twist that makes it interesting is that the constraint *is* the text. A translation that nails the meaning but lets "e" back in has failed, full stop; but a translation that keeps "e" out will almost certainly have to say something different from the original. So the student is stuck: what exactly are they supposed to be faithful to?

Please write me:

1. The essay question itself, worded so a student can't get away with reciting the usual "form vs. content" cliché — it has to make them commit to a position on whether preserving a constraint can ever justify changing what the text *says*, not just how it says it. Include a short sample lipogram paragraph in the question so it's concrete (you can just write one).

2. A model answer, about 700 words.

3. A marking scheme: five must-hit points, 20 marks total (assign each a number), and for each point one line naming the specific mistake students make that loses them the marks.

If it helps you sharpen the question, feel free to coin a term for the thing the translator is actually obligated to carry over — I don't need a name that already exists, I'd rather you make one up and defend it. And dont worry about my spelling, I know I'm sloppy.
