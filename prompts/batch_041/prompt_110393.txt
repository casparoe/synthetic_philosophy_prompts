I'm in a reading group that's working through the idea of reasons without reasoners, and I'm stuck on a passage from Darwin's Origin of Species — the chapter on instinct (my copy has it as chapter VIII, if that matters). Here it is:

"Thus, as I believe, the most wonderful of all known instincts, that of the hive-bee, can be explained by natural selection having taken advantage of numerous, successive, slight modifications of simpler instincts; natural selection having, by slow degrees, more and more perfectly led the bees to sweep equal spheres at a given distance from each other in a double layer, and to build up and excavate the wax along the planes of intersection. The bees, of course, no more knowing that they swept their spheres at one particular distance from each other, than they know what are the several angles of the hexagonal prisms and of the basal rhombic plates; the motive power of the process of natural selection having been the construction of cells of due strength and of the proper size and shape for the larvae, this being effected with the greatest possible economy of labour and wax; that individual swarm which thus made the best cells with least labour, and least waste of honey in the secretion of wax, having succeeded best, and having transmitted their newly-acquired economical instincts to new swarms, which in their turn will have had the best chance of succeeding in the struggle for existence."

What I can't get my head round is the bit after the semicolon in the second sentence, the "motive power" clause. Darwin has just told us the bees know nothing — not the angles, not the distances — so there's no mental representation of the geometry anywhere. Fine. But then he says the motive power of the whole process was the building of cells of the right strength, size and shape, done with the greatest possible economy of labour and wax. What sort of thing is that economy meant to be, in the explanation? It isn't a reason the bees have; he's ruled that out. Is it a reason natural selection has? But selection doesn't represent anything either. Or is it just a fact about which swarms did best — in which case calling it a "motive power" looks like leftover language from talking about purposes, and the real story is just differential survival?

Our handout for the week draws a three-way distinction: a rationale that is available to the agent doing the thing, one that is available only to the process that produced the agent, and one that is available to nobody at all except the person giving the explanation — a reason that's genuinely there but that nothing and no one grasps. Which of those three is Darwin committed to here, and does the third one even make sense, or is it just a way of smuggling in a purpose by the back door? Related: what work is the "no more knowing" clause actually doing? If we struck it out, would the explanation be any worse, or is it load-bearing in a way I'm missing?

The other thing that nags at me is why he goes through the whole sphere-sweeping rigmarole at all — the equal spheres in a double layer, the letter to Professor Miller of Cambridge about the angles coming out right. If the economy of labour and wax is the motive power, doesn't that on its own explain why the good cells get built, geometry or no geometry? It feels like the geometry is doing something in the argument that I can't name, and I'd like to know what.

Please don't give me a potted history of Darwin on teleology — I want a careful step-by-step reading of this passage, with a clear verdict on the "motive power" sentence. Tell me if my reading above is wrong; I'd rather be corrected than reassured. British English, please.
