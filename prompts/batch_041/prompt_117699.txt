I am a philosophy professor whose teaching is split between a graduate seminar in the epistemology of testimony and a methods course for students who arrive from statistics and machine learning. Every year I watch the same translation failure: my students treat the reductionism/anti-reductionism debate as though it were a disagreement about how much weight to assign to a noisy channel, and while that gets them part of the way, it also makes the remaining disagreement look either trivial or confused. I would like you to perform the translation systematically and then give me the residue report. The target vocabulary is the one my methods students actually use: priors and likelihoods, conditionally independent sources, hierarchical models with speaker-level random effects, calibration, provenance and provenance laundering, sensor fusion, adversarial and correlated error, robustness to model misspecification, and the distinction between a default setting and an update rule. Assume I am fluent in that vocabulary and do not need it explained to me; assume also that I know the philosophical literature well enough that you need not rehearse the standard positions from scratch. Restate the reductionist position, the anti-reductionist position, and at least one intermediate position—for example, a local reductionism, a default-entitlement view, or a view that distinguishes the transmission of knowledge from the transmission of mere justification—as faithfully as you can in those terms. For each, say what maps cleanly, what maps only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. For instance: is the anti-reductionist appeal to a default entitlement captured by a prior, by a policy for setting priors, or by neither? Is the reductionist demand for independent corroboration the same as a demand for conditional-independence assumptions? And does the distinction between testimonial justification and testimonial knowledge survive at all once testimony is modeled as an observation?

Then give me the residue report proper. Tell me which parts of the debate are artifacts of the probabilistic vocabulary, which are genuine features of the epistemology that the vocabulary cannot express, and which are places where the philosophical dispute was less substantive than its participants thought. I am especially interested in whether the target vocabulary has any way to represent the second-personal or normative dimension of testimony—the idea that a speaker offers me her word and that I may owe her a certain kind of uptake or trust—or whether that necessarily drops out as an unmodeled residual. Conclude with a judgment: is the residue a defect of the target framework, a defect of the original debate, or a bit of both? Please write in American English, and do not give me a reading list or citations; I want the translation and the residue analysis, not a bibliography.
