I've been drafted into providing the after-dinner entertainment at a reading group that is spending a term on welfare economics, and the one thing nobody else will touch is a joke about Harsanyi's aggregation theorem. Roughly, the theorem runs like this: take a society, and ask what a single rational chooser would settle on if he had to live the life of one of its members without knowing which — the veil of ignorance — and if his choice over social arrangements obeyed the ordinary Pareto and independence conditions and the usual machinery for decisions under uncertainty, then the social ranking he lands on is a weighted sum of the members' utilities, and the weights are just the probabilities he assigns to turning out to be each of them. Utilitarianism, with equal weights, follows once you add the assumption that those probabilities are equal, i.e. that you are as likely to be anyone as anyone else. What I want the joke to fasten on is precisely that seam in the construction: the equality of persons enters as an equiprobability assumption about identities rather than as something derived, and the whole derivation needs the members' utilities to be cardinal and comparable, measured on one common scale, which is exactly what the Arrow-style social-choice setting it grew out of declares you cannot have — the theorem is bought with the very assumption that makes the puzzle tractable. So the punchline should land on that machinery: the veil, the lottery over who you turn out to be, the weights that are really probabilities, the exchange rate between one person's utils and another's, or the difference between a chooser who is risk-neutral behind the veil and one who, like Rawls, refuses to gamble and takes the worst position as the standard.

What I don't want is a generic joke about economists, about utilitarians being bad at dinner parties, or about "the greatest good for the greatest number" in the abstract — anyone who has actually worked through the derivation should be able to hear the joke and recognise that its logic survives being explained, even if the scenario is as homely as a household splitting a thermostat, a department choosing a candidate, or a family picking a restaurant. Please avoid hanging anything on a particular paper, year, or quotation; the audience knows the shape of the argument, not the literature. Give me the joke first, then a sentence or two saying exactly what it is playing on, and finish with a last line beginning "Mechanism:" followed by one sentence stating what the joke turns on.
