I'm a philosophy professor, and through a series of decisions I won't defend I've ended up on the advisory board of a small grantmaker that funds insect-welfare work. The board is three economists, one commited utilitarian, and me. Last month they got me to build the scoring sheet that decides which proposals get funded, and now I can't stop seeing the philosophy leaking out of it.

Here's how it works. Every proposal gets a single number: N, the number of insects affected, times P, a sentience probability we read off a rubric we made up ourselves (honeybee 0.6, black soldier fly 0.2, ant 0.05, springtail 0.002, and anything not on the list gets whatever the person doing the rubric feels like that morning), times D, duration in insect-hours, times I, a suffering-intensity weight from 1 to 5, times S, the probability the intervention actually does what the proposal claims. We sort by the product and fund down the list until the money runs out. When we compare across the whole portfolio we multiply anything vertebrate by 100, which nobody has ever justified out loud. Two more features of the process: we have a standing rule that no grant may itself involve deliberately harming insects, no matter how big the expected number comes out, and most of the interventions we like work by changing how many insects there are (fewer larvae hatching, more overwintering habitat) rather than by making a fixed set of insects feel better — and we've never worked out what the N cell is even supposed to mean in those cases.

So: what does this practice presuppose? I don't want a survey of insect cognition, I want the implicit theory. Does the product formula commit us to sentience being a graded magnitude that can carry a probability and be compared cardinally across taxa — or could you hold that and still find a spreadsheet like ours absurd? What does the multiplication quietly rule out (threshold views, precautionary views, moral status as all-or-nothing, the idea that you can't do arithmetic on moral status at all)? Where is the practice underdetermined — which incompatible theories would license exactly the same spreadsheet? And is the no-deliberate-harm rule consistent with ranking everything by expected value, or is the board just holding two things that can't both be true?

Then the counterfactuals: for each main rival, what would have to change — new columns, a different aggregation, or no aggregation at all?

Skip the citations and reading lists, just give me the analysis, and finish with a JSON object with exactly these keys:

- "presuppositions": array, at most six. Each element an object with "claim" (the commitment, one sentence), "strength" (one of "strict", "loose", "merely compatible"), "why" (the argument that the practice actually needs it), and "rules_out" (array of rival positions it excludes; empty array if none).
- "compatible_rivals": array of strings — theories that would license the practice exactly as it stands.
- "underdetermined": array of objects with "question" (a question the practice can't answer) and "all_fit" (array of the competing answers that the practice can't distinguish between).
- "tensions": array of objects with "tension" (one sentence), "poles" (array of the two things pulling apart), and "severity" (one of "fatal", "serious", "cosmetic").
- "counterfactuals": array of objects with "alternative" (the rival view) and "change" (what the spreadsheet or process would have to become).
- "bottom_line": string, three sentences max, on how much philosophy is actually baked into the thing.
