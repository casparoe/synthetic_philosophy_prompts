Hey, I'm a philosophy PhD student (social ontology / phil of social science) and I've gotten into a running argument in our game-theory reading group about where conventions stop and norms begin, and I want to nail down what I actually think before I put it in a chapter. So here's what I'd like from you: act like a philosopher who works on convention and social norms -- Lewis, Sugden, Bicchieri sort of territory -- and who has no patience for hand-waving, and work through my gut verdicts below. Start by brainstorming the two or three candidate principles that might be in play, then test each against the cases, then give me the best principle (or small set of candidates) you can state precisely, with which of my verdicts it has to throw overboard. Please don't give me a reading list or citations, I can't get online where I'm working anyway, I just want the argument.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. Driving on the left in Britain: convention, obviously. Two hikers meeting on a narrow trail who both step to their right: convention, even though nobody taught them and it's arbitrary which side. Calling a dog a dog: convention. Everyone in my department using the back stairwell to go down and the front one to go up, which just sort of happened over the years: convention, I think. Turning off the lights when you're the last one out of the lab: not a convention, just a sensible rule. Wearing black to funerals: convention, even though it's also expressive. Tipping 20% at a US restaurant: convention -- but here's where I get uneasy, because I also feel the amount is supposed to track how good the service was, and that stiffing a genuinely bad waiter is wrong in a way that driving on the wrong side of the road definately isn't. QWERTY: not a convention, even though it's arbitrary and everyone conforms and there are alternatives -- it's just lock-in. Hemlines rising and falling: not a convention. Everyone in town taking the same shortcut because it really is the shortest route: not a convention, since it isn't arbitrary.

The one I keep going back and forth on: a friend group where, without anyone ever deciding, the person who picked the restaurant is never the one who pays, and if a newcomer grabs the bill for their own suggestion the whole evening feels slightly off. Is that a convention? My tentative principle is roughly this -- a regularity is a convention when it's arbitrary, everyone conforms, everyone expects everyone else to conform, and each person prefers to conform conditional on the others conforming -- and I'm assuming the 'arbitrary' clause is doing all the work, since the light-switch and shortcut cases fail on it while the funeral and tipping cases pass. But tipping makes me think the 'prefers to conform' clause is wrong, since people tip out of something closer to fear or duty than preference, and the stairwell case makes me think the common-knowledge clause is too strong (I'm honestly not sure anyone else knows the others do it). So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? What does it say about the friend-group paying case, and about one I haven't listed -- a village where everyone uses the same well even though there's a closer one, purely because the first family to settle there used it and everyone followed along? And be honest about which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop, and whether dropping them is a cost or a relief.
