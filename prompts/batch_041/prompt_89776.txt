I am a retired natural scientist — forty years in population biology, mostly quantitative genetics and, in the last decade of my career, some behavioural ecology — and since retiring I have been reading my way into philosophy with considerably more appetite than method. A friend has lent me a short book by a science journalist whose work I generally respect, on the idea of national character, and it has left me with the pleasant but uncomfortable feeling of having been persuaded by an argument I cannot yet inspect. I would like you to act as a referee for it: not to hunt for a reason to reject it, but to tell me precisely which parts I can carry away, which I should hold at arm's length, and which of its steps are doing work that the evidence does not license.

The book's central argument runs, more or less, as follows (this is my own paraphrase of its gist, not its wording). The idea that nations have characters — that the French are one way and the Japanese another — is a relic of eighteenth-century theories about climate and mores and of a mid-twentieth-century research programme that tried to explain political life by way of national personality. That programme collapsed for a simple reason: whenever anyone actually looked for the trait, the variation within a nation turned out to swamp the variation between nations, which means there is no such thing as a national character. A much-cited cross-national survey makes the point neatly: when people are asked to describe the typical member of their own country, those descriptions bear little relation to how the people of that country actually score on personality measures, exactly as one would expect if the whole notion were an artefact of stereotype. The concept is therefore not merely unfashionable but incoherent: a national character would have to be a property that all or most members of a nation share, and no such property exists. And since the claim is empty, generalizing about peoples is not merely bad science but a moral failing — it treats individuals as mere instances of a group — so there is no honest use for it, in diplomacy, in journalism, or anywhere else. What I want from you is a claim-by-claim audit. Separate what is well established and would be accepted by people who work on this material, what is a defensible but genuinely contested position, what is a simplification or a conflation of two claims that ought to be kept apart, and what is simply mistaken. Tell me in particular what a careful defender of a modest version of the notion would say in reply, and whether the book's concluding moral claim survives correction of the empirical claims it is drawn from — or whether it needs an argument of its own that the book never supplies.

Before you answer, take a moment to lay out for yourself the distinct claims the passage is running together, since I suspect several of them are travelling under one banner. Think of yourself as a philosopher of social science with a working knowledge of the empirical literature on group differences — someone who is neither a debunker by temperament nor a defender of lost causes, and who is scrupulous about the difference between a distributional claim and a claim about essences. I would much rather have a map of the terrain with the live disagreements marked on it than a confident verdict, so please mark your own uncertainty where it exists, and do not let anything that specialists still dispute be presented to me as though it were settled.
