I sit on the mosquito-control subcommittee of my county board of health — county of about 180,000, river runs through it, plenty of floodplain, a handful of farms and one commercial apiary out on the river road. Next Thursday we vote on whether to authorize aerial adulticide spraying in the river corridor. The trigger: two confirmed human cases of mosquito-borne encephalitis (one still in the ICU, a nine-year-old), plus a dozen or so positive mosquito pools. The state vector-control office is recommending we spray. Our own vector-control officer is lukewarm — she says the local Culex population has partial pyrethroid resistance, and that the field evidence that ULV fogging at our scale actually moves human case counts is "weaker than people assume." She'd rather run larviciding plus door-to-door source reduction: ten to fourteen days slower, and in her words it "might cost us a case or two." On the other side of the room: a beekeeper with forty hives who can't fully shield them from a 4 a.m. fogging, an organic vegetable grower, and about three hundred residents who signed a petition against spraying, some of them asthmatic, some of them just objecting on principle to being sprayed without being asked. We also have a rule on the books — passed after a bad experience years ago, never actually defined — that any emergency order has to pass an "irreversibility test." Nobody can tell me what that means and I definately need to say something about it, because whatever we decide, I'm the one who has to draft the opinion we vote on.

So here's the ask. Write me that written opinion, addressed to the subcommittee, one to two pages, with an actionable bottom line — spray, don't spray, or spray with specific conditions and a timeline — plus the reasoning that gets you there. Take the role of someone who works seriously on environmental ethics and the ethics of pest control, and think this through carefully rather than reaching for the standard moves; I don't want a memo that just chants "precautionary principle" or "follow the science," and please no citations, reading lists, or case references (a couple of us have no library access and it'd just be padding). Two things I'd like you to actually dig into, since half the subcommittee insists they're non-questions. First: whether "pest" is doing any real work here or is just a label we apply to animals whose interests we've already decided not to count — and what the decision looks like if insects turn out to have some capacity for suffering. I'm not asking you to settle that, I'm asking what changes under each assumption. Second, the asymmetry that keeps bothering me: the harms of spraying are fairly certain and fall on particular, identifiable parties (the hives, the asthmatics, whatever's in the river), while the benefits are statistical and counterfactual — and "might cost us a case or two" cuts both ways depending on how you feel about the cases we never see. Also do something honest with the strongest argument on the side you end up rejecting; I have to defend this in front of people who disagree with me, not just win on paper. Maybe start by laying out the decision in your own words and listing the considerations on each side before you commit to a recommendation.
