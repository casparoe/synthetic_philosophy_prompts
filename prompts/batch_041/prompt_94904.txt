Responsibility Without Alternatives: A Case Against the Principle of Alternative Possibilities

The principle of alternative possibilities, which I will call PAP, says that a person is morally responsible for what they have done only if they could have done otherwise. Harry Frankfurt's well-known cases are supposed to refute it. In the usual version, a neuroscientist has secretly arranged someone's brain so that, if that person were about to decide against some course of action, the neuroscientist would intervene and produce the decision to act; as it happens, the person decides to act on their own, and the intervention is never needed. The person seems responsible, and yet, the argument goes, they could not have done otherwise.

Two features of these cases invite resistance. The first is the science fiction: a reader who does not believe in that sort of brain surgery can tell themselves the case is impossible and move on. The second is that the backup is a person with an unexplained power over someone else's mind, which makes it hard to say what the agent's own contribution was. I want a case that is ordinary and institutional, where the backup is nothing more sinister than a rule and a plan.

The case I want to offer is this. Ravi is a high-school debater who learns, the night before the regional final, that he is technically ineligible. He once attended a practice tournament that the rules count as a qualifying event, and two qualifying events disqualify a team. Nobody else knows about this. The rules also provide that if an ineligible debater competes and the ineligibility is discovered later, the team forfeits all of its wins. Ravi's coach, Ms. Adeyemi, has also learned of the ineligibility and has decided what to do about it: if Ravi has not withdrawn by the start of the final, she will withdraw the team herself. Either way, the team does not compete. Ravi withdraws the team himself, on the morning of the final, because he thinks it would be dishonest to compete while ineligible. His own judgment, and nothing else, produces his decision.

I claim this is a Frankfurt case. Ravi could not have competed, because Ms. Adeyemi's plan guaranteed that the team would be withdrawn. Yet Ravi is responsible for the withdrawal, and it seems right to praise him for it. The backup made no difference to what actually happened.

I want to distinguish two ways a backup can work. A choice-closing backup closes off the agent's alternatives at the moment of decision, the way the neuroscientist's device does: had the agent been about to decide otherwise, the decision itself would have been replaced. An outcome-closing backup only guarantees the result: the agent might have decided otherwise, but the world would have been arranged so that the result came out the same anyway. I take it that only choice-closing backups produce the Frankfurt result, and I take my case to be choice-closing, because Ms. Adeyemi's plan makes the team's withdrawal unavoidable no matter what Ravi decides.

Someone might object that the trigger of Ms. Adeyemi's plan is not Ravi's decision but a public event, the start of the final. If that is right, then Ravi could have decided to keep silent and only been thwarted afterwards, so his decision to come forward was not itself unavoidable. I think this is a technicality rather than a real difference: if he had kept silent, the team would still have been withdrawn, so the alternative of competing was never genuinely open to him.

I also propose a probabilistic version, to show that the case does not depend on the backup being certain to work. Suppose Ms. Adeyemi checks her email only about half the time, so that there is only a fifty per cent chance she would have learned of Ravi's silence in time to act. I claim this changes nothing. What makes Ravi responsible is that in the actual world he acted for his own reasons, and a lower probability of intervention does not give him back the alternative of competing.

Finally, an objection from the other side. A defender of PAP may concede that Ravi could not have competed, but insist that he could have done otherwise in some smaller respect: he could have come forward a minute later, or angrily, or for a worse reason. Some philosophers call this a flicker of freedom. I reply that a flicker is not enough to satisfy PAP. PAP concerns the action the agent is responsible for, which here is the withdrawal of the team, and Ravi could not have failed to withdraw the team. So PAP, as it is usually stated, is false.

My conclusion is that moral responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. What matters is why the agent acted, and whether the action came from the agent's own judgment, rather than how many alternatives were open to them.

I would be grateful for comments on the following, roughly in order of importance.

First, does my case actually work as a Frankfurt case? I am fairly confident about the general shape of the argument, but I am less confident that the coach's plan closes Ravi's alternatives in the same way the neuroscientist's device does. If there is a gap, I would like to know exactly where it is and whether it can be repaired without turning the case back into science fiction.

Second, is my distinction between choice-closing and outcome-closing backups a real distinction, and have I applied it correctly to my own example? It is my own coinage and I am not sure it survives contact with someone who has read more of this literature than I have.

Third, does the probabilistic version help or hurt me? I suspect I am not clear about what the fifty per cent figure is supposed to be a probability of, and that a careful reader will press me on this.

Fourth, does my reply to the flicker-of-freedom objection work, or have I simply restated my conclusion in a different vocabulary?

Fifth, what is the strongest thing a defender of PAP would say to me that I have not anticipated? I would rather hear the objection I have missed than be told the essay is promising.

Sixth, is this interesting, or does it read as a summary of something my reader will already know? If it is worth keeping, tell me what to cut and what is currently underdeveloped. I do not need citations or a reading list; I would rather have the objections and suggestions in your own words.
