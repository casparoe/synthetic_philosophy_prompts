I'm in a grad seminar on Williams and his critics and I have to hand in a short response paper on Thursday, and I've been going back and forth about my central move for a week now without settling it. What I want is comments on the draft below, not polish — argue with me.

Here's the draft, roughly:

I want to defend a reading of the integrity objection, and I think the one-thought-too-many case is the weakest part of it. Take the two familiar cases. In the drowning case, a man can save his wife or save two strangers, and Williams's target is the idea that if he saves her, the right thing for him to think, or at least to be able to offer if pressed, is that this is a situation in which saving one's own wife is permitted. Williams thinks that is a thought too many: the thought that she is his wife is the appropriate one, and anything further is surplus. In the Jim case, Jim is offered the choice of shooting one Indian himself, after which the rest go free, or refusing, after which all twenty die. Williams says utilitarianism tells Jim to shoot, and that this is an attack on Jim's integrity: it turns his own projects and commitments into one more input into a calculation he doesn't own.

Here's my worry. The wife case, taken on its own, is an argument about what occurs in the agent's head, and that argument is cheap to answer. A consequentialist can say: fine, don't have that thought; the theory doesn't require you to rehearse permissions in the moment, it requires you to be the sort of person whose dispositions produce the best outcomes, and a man who loves his wife as he should is exactly that sort of person. The justification then lives entirely off-stage and the occurrent thought never has to happen. As usually stated, the case seems to me to show at most that a certain kind of clumsy utilitarian is clumsy.

But I don't think the objection dies there; I think it changes shape. The point isn't whether the permission-thought occurs, it's that it's available. The agent knows that his partiality stands in need of a licence from a standpoint that treats his wife as one person among others, and that knowledge doesn't evaporate because he doesn't articulate it. And I think that's what the Jim case is really about. Jim's problem isn't an extra thought; it's that the theory asks him to do something he cannot identify as his own action, for reasons that are his only in the sense that the calculation came out that way. The theory gets a result out of him and calls the result his deed.

So I want to distinguish deliberative integrity, which is about what goes on in the agent's deliberation, from what I'm calling narrative integrity, which is about whether the agent can own his life as a course of action that is his. My claim is that Williams's argument only works on the second reading, that the wife case is evidence for the first and therefore useless to him, and that the standard disposition reply defeats the first reading while being irrelevant to the second. I also suspect the second reading generalises past utilitarianism to any view that requires the agent to be answerable to a standpoint he doesn't occupy, which might be a problem for me or might be the whole point.

What I want from you, in this order: (1) is my reconstruction of the two cases faithful, especially the role I give the wife case — am I importing my own view when I say Jim's case is about identification rather than deliberation? (2) Does the deliberative/narrative distinction earn its keep or is it a restatement of something already in the objection, and if it's a restatement, where exactly does it collapse? (3) My availability-rather-than-occurrence move — is that a real reply to the disposition line or a rhetorical dodge, and can you give me the strongest version of the disposition line against it? (4) Does the narrative reading really generalise to non-consequentialist views, and if so does it prove too much? (5) Which single paragraph is the weakest and why. If there's an obvious reply I've ignored, give me the argument rather than the label. If I've misdescribed Williams's own position anywhere, say so bluntly.
