I retired about four years ago after 35 years as an ecologist — freshwater community stuff, a lot of it on priority effects, which is just the fact that the order species happen to arrive in can decide what community you end up with. Since then I've been reading philosophy, badly but happily, and I've gotten stuck on the path-dependence debate, mainly the biologist's version of it: is history 'locked in', or would the same outcomes have arrived anyway if we ran the whole thing again?

I'd like you to turn my half-formed idea into a short research proposal — a semester's worth, 2-3 pages, for a visiting-scholar slot at an interdisciplinary institute, so the audience is half philosophers and half scientists and I need to be readable to both.

The idea: I think the debate goes in circles because 'would the same outcome have arrived anyway' is at least three different counterfactuals being asked at once, and each side is quietly answering a different one. (a) Re-run from the same starting point with a tiny nudge — my priority effects live here, and so does a lot of what gets called chance. (b) Re-run with the outcome un-done but the pathway constraints left in place — the outcome holds because everyone is now coordinated around it, like the keyboard story the economists are so fond of. (c) Re-run where the outcome was never really on the table, because of how the system is built — developmental constraint, physics, whatever, the stuff the convergence crowd points at.

My technical hunch, and this is definately the part I'd want you to press on: (a) and (c) are usually claims about the shape of the landscape — how big the basins of attraction are, how steep the walls — while (b) is a claim about whether there's more than one basin at all. If that's right, a lot of the contingency-vs-convergence shouting is people answering different questions and thinking they're disagreeing. If it's wrong, its just fancy relabeling and I've wasted a semester.

So please write the proposal: state the question so it has a determinate answer; explain why the standard positions don't settle it; say what I'd actually do month by month (I'm imagining working through a handful of cases — my pond bugs, the keyboard, camera eyes, whatever survives scrutiny — and testing whether the taxonomy genuinely sorts them or collapses into one thing); what a real result looks like and what would count as failure; and whether this is honestly a semester or a decade. Assume I know the biology and not the philosophy, so tell me where I'm likely to be reinventing a wheel someone already built. No bibliography — I'll fill that in myself, I have library access and I'd rather you spend the words on the argument.
