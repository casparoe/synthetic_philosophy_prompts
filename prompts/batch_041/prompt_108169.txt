I am assembling materials for a seminar on the ethics of pain and pain relief, and I want to open with a specification rather than a survey. Write me the desiderata for a theory of the badness of pain — the requirements any account of why pain is bad for the one who has it must meet in order to be adequate for our purposes: the phenomena it has to accommodate, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it may not collapse, the explanatory debts it owes. No literature review, no citations, no names; describe each position by what it claims rather than by who holds it. Treat the material below as given, and do not spend words doubting that things are reported as I report them.

The material. Pain asymbolia: after damage in and around the insula, and in a related presentation after cingulotomy or other frontal surgery for intractable pain and in some patients on morphine, the sensory and the aversive sides of pain come apart. Patients localize the noxious stimulus, distinguish sharp from burning, rate its intensity much as anyone would, and call it painful — and then deny that it troubles them. They do not guard the limb, do not ask for relief, and may seem amused while being tested; meanwhile there is nothing in the aversion to keep them out of further injury. Patients who have had the surgery for intractable pain describe it roughly like this: the pain has not moved or weakened, it simply no longer matters to them. Nothing in the sensory-discriminative profile seems to be missing; what is missing is the aversion. Congenital insensitivity to pain is the mirror case: the sensory side is gone, and the person is harmed by that absence — burns, unnoticed fractures, joints destroyed — so it is not a pain that fails to be bad but a life missing something the theory must be able to call worth having. Then there is pain that is wanted (the masochist, the athlete who welcomes the burn of the last mile, the ascetic), pain that cannot be escaped (the dying patient for whom the pain is not the worst of it), and pain in creatures whose attitudes we cannot check (a fish, a newborn, a patient with advanced dementia). Also in play: the difference between pain and suffering; the difference between the sensory-discriminative and the affective-motivational aspects of a single pain; and the choice between treating unpleasantness as a felt quality of the sensation itself and treating it as an attitude — dislike, aversion, a command to stop — taken toward a sensation that is not in itself unpleasant.

Give me the response in four sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths.

"The list" (700–900 words). Numbered requirements, each with the case or consideration that generates it, one line on whether it is a genuine requirement or a prejudice of one camp, and a mark for whether it is negotiable. Where a requirement can be met only by denying that the asymbolic patient is in pain at all, or only by denying that a fish or a newborn is badly off, say so plainly and price the denial.

"Load-bearing requirements" (200–300 words). Which single requirement does the most excluding work, and what it excludes.

"Collisions" (300–400 words). The pairs of requirements that cannot be met together, and which one has to give in each case. I suspect at least one collision between what asymbolia demands and what the badness of pain in creatures without the relevant attitudes demands; confirm, refute, or replace that with a better one. If some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, that is the result I most want.

"The contenders against the list" (200–300 words). How the main families fare — sensory-hedonic, desire-based, imperative or motivational, evaluative or perceptual-representational, and any hybrid you think the list forces. A few sentences each, and flag the ones the list kills outright rather than rating them.
