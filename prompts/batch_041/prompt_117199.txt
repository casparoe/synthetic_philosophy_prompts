A student in my metaethics seminar has an arguement she thinks turns Williams's integrity objection into a proof that no moral theory with a general criterion can be true, and I can't decide whether it's a real result or a trick of phrasing. It goes:

(1) A moral theory's job is to say what an agent may and must do.
(2) If the theory has a general criterion of right action, then what justifies the agent's act is, at bottom, that the criterion selects it.
(3) So when the man saves his wife rather than the stranger, if his reason is just that she's his wife, the theory will ask what makes that enough; and the answer has to come from the criterion (partiality is permitted, or the act maximizes the good). His justifying thought has to be, at least implicitly, a thought about the criterion.
(4) So the theory treats his attachment to her as not justifying anything on its own — it needs the criterion to back it.
(5) That's Williams's complaint about the wife case: the theory wants one thought too many.
(6) But nothing in (1)-(5) is about consequentialism in particular. Any theory that sorts acts by a general criterion does this, agent-relative theories included.
(7) So the objection, if it lands, refutes every general moral theory; and a charge that convicts every theory isn't an objection to any of them. Which leaves either that Williams is just describing what it is to have a theory at all, or that there can be no general theory.

She also says the wife case is a distraction — swap in a man whose deepest project is famine relief and she claims the argument runs identically — which I think is either the sharpest thing she's said or a sign something broke upstream. Where's the break? I'd like to keep (5), since I think Williams is onto something real, but (7) is absurd. And if you tell me the whole thing never gets off the ground, tell me what the agent's justification is supposed to be instead, because it can't just be that she's his wife with nothing behind it.
