Two people in my reading group have been going at each other for months over Kant and the murderer at the door, and I'd like them to produce a document together instead of another forty-minute shouting match. The two sides, roughly:

Side A, the rigorist: Kant got it right. You may not lie to the murderer, period. What you may do is refuse to answer, or say something you're not obliged to volunteer. If the victim dies because the murderer then goes looking, that's the murderer's deed and his guilt, not yours. The duty of veracity doesn't get suspended because the consequences look bad — if it did, it was never a duty.

Side B, the contextualist: Kant's own machinery pushes the other way. The murderer isn't owed the truth; he's using your answer as a weapon, so either this isn't the kind of exchange the duty of veracity governs in the first place, or the duty not to be a tool of murder and the duty to aid the innocent outweigh it. On this view you may deceive him, and arguably must.

Write the joint statement the two of them could actually both sign. I want the claims they agree on (including any claims about the case itself that both can accept without giving up their position), then the precise remaining disagreements, each phrased the way that side would want its own view phrased, then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move somebody — and for each crux a case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance.

To keep it from going vacuous, make them take a stand on these, and say for each whether it actually moves anyone:

(a) The leaky-silence case: the murderer asks "back bedroom or attic?" — and the mere fact that you don't answer already tells him. Refusing to answer isn't a clean out here, because the silence is itself a signal. Does the rigorist have to concede that in this case you may lie, or does he bite the bullet?

(b) The true-but-misleading case: you say something literally true that you know will send him the wrong way, or you answer a slightly different question than the one asked. Lie in the relevant sense, or legitimate non-cooperation? Is what's being assessed the truth of what you asserted, or your intention to produce a false belief?

(c) The third-party case: he makes clear that a non-answer means he starts killing everyone else in the house. Now it's several lives against your veracity rather than one. Does that shift either side, and can the rigorist move at all here without giving up the unconditional duty?

(d) The wrong-guess case: your friend isn't actually in the house, your lie sends the murderer out into the street, and he runs into your friend there. Whose deed is that? This seems like the rigorist's strongest move — you took on the consequences the moment you chose to deceive — so I want to see how side B answers it.

And get the underlying cruxes straight, I think there are at least two: is the duty of veracity grounded in something owed to the person you're talking to (so the murderer's standing is doing work), or in a duty to yourself, or to humanity in your own person, which nobody's wrongdoing can waive? And what counts as a lie at all — Kant's own narrow notion (an untruthful declaration made to another person) versus the ordinary notion of intentional deception. My suspicion is that a good chunk of their fight is verbal and lives in the second one, but make them say which chunk and why rather than just asserting it.

Format: numbered agreed claims first, then a table of disagreements with each side's own preferred wording, then the cruxes with the relevant case and both predictions, then a short closing note on what's merely verbal and what both sides agree would be decisive. No citations, no reading lists, no appeals to what Kant "obviously" says somewhere — argue it or drop it. Also flag anywhere they can't agree on what the other side's view even is.
