I keep running into a puzzle when I try to judge whether someone made a mistake, and I cant tell if it has a name. Here's the shape of it. A doctor at a tiny rural clinic gives a patient a drug that later turns out to be dangerous -- the study that would have flagged it hadn't reached her yet. Everyone says she did the best she could with what she had. Fine. But it's not just that she had a wrong belief about the drug. The two situations, drug-is-safe and drug-is-harmful, were completely indistinguishable to her; from where she stood there was one situation, not two. So when I grade her choice, am I grading it against my world, where those are two separate states, or hers, where there's just one? And the verdicts come out differently: against my world she was reckless, against hers she was fine.

Same shape with an old video game where a guard reacts to you identically whether or not you're carrying a hidden weapon, because the guard's code never tracks the weapon. Did the guard face my decision problem with worse information, or a genuinely simpler problem?

What I'm after is a name, if one exists, for this specific thing: the disagreement isn't about what someone believes but about what counts as a distinct possibility in the first place, so the evaluator and the agent aren't even carving the world up the same way. I know the usual objective-vs-subjective-ought distinction, and the idea that you can only be blamed for what you could have known, but both of those seem to keep the set of possibilities fixed and just change what the person knows about them. Here the set of possibilities itself shrinks. Does that have a name, or a cluster of near names? And if nothing fits cleanly, can you name it and say how it differs from the objective/subjective thing and from plain ignorance? I care less about the label than about where the line falls -- when is it a false belief, and when is it a different world?
