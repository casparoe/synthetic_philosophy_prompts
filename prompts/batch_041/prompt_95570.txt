I am a software engineer on a team of about fifteen people that maintains a widely used open-source library along with the service that hosts it. We have a long-standing policy that any engineer with production access may merge and deploy a change on their own judgment, with no second reviewer, so long as they believe the change is a net improvement. We adopted that policy because mandatory review had become slow and, in practice, a formality.

I recently came across a pattern that some writers call the unilateralist's curse, and I suspect it describes us. The pattern is this: when each member of a group is individually able to take some action, when the action is worth taking if it looks beneficial on balance, and when each member decides independently whether to take it, then the action will be taken whenever the most optimistic member of the group judges it worth taking. The group as a whole therefore acts more boldly than any of its members would choose to act if the decision had to be made jointly. I do not want to repeat the catchphrase; I want to know what it actually implies for how we run things.

Please describe the most important implications of this pattern for our team. I would rather have a short list of points that genuinely change what we do than an exhaustive taxonomy. In particular:

- Some of our changes can be rolled back within minutes, while others are one-way: a schema migration, a published release, a change to a public API we have promised not to break, a rotated signing key. Does the reasoning apply equally to both kinds, or does reversibility change what the group ends up doing?
- Our judgments are not independent. We read the same incident reports, the same internal analyses, and the same vendor announcements, so when one of us is optimistic about a change, the rest of us often are too. Does shared evidence make the pattern worse, or does it blunt it?
- Our changes interact. Ten individually defensible deploys in a week can produce an outage that none of them would have produced alone. Is the pattern about single actions or about the cumulative stream, and does that distinction matter for us?
- Holding back is also something any of us can do unilaterally: anyone can halt a rollout or roll back. Does the same reasoning predict that we will be too cautious about as often as we are too bold, or is there a real asymmetry between acting and refraining?
- We are hiring, and production access is spreading from five engineers to more. Does the effect get worse as the group grows, even if each individual's judgment stays the same? What would change if everyone had to act through a single person?
- Is this a bias in the group that we could correct, or just arithmetic — the union of a set of independent judgments, which is precisely what we asked for when we removed the review requirement? If it is arithmetic, does anything follow for how we should feel about it?

Finally, what would you actually change? Consider a norm of acting only when you believe the group would approve, a designated decider for each area, requiring one other person to sign off on certain classes of change, and simply leaving things as they are. Say what each option would cost us, including the risk that we reintroduce the slow, formalistic review we deliberately got rid of, or that decisions migrate to whoever happens to answer a message first. And tell me which parts of your answer are specific to our situation and which would hold for any group with this structure — an open-source project, a research lab, a company.
