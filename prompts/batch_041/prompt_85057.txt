In most common-law jurisdictions a witness can give answers that are literally true but deliberately engineered to create a false impression and commit no crime, because perjury requires a statement the witness believes to be false. Yet the same legal systems are aggressive about literally true statements that mislead in other settings: securities fraud reaches half-truths that omit what is needed to keep a statement from misleading, and consumer-protection law bars advertising that, without being false, is likely to deceive. What does the perjury rule presuppose about the moral difference between lying and merely misleading? Does it commit the law to the view that misleading someone is not wrong in itself, or only that it is not the criminal law's business, or that the adversarial system shifts the risk of deception onto the questioner who failed to ask precisely? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say which rival accounts of the lying/misleading distinction it rules out and which it merely fits, and judge whether the perjury rule is consistent with the same legal system's treatment of misleading-but-true speech in securities and advertising. If the literal-truth defense were abolished, what would have to change in how witnesses are questioned and charged, and what would be lost?
