I'm in a graduate seminar on the experience machine and theories of well-being, and every paper anyone writes makes the same two moves: the machine shows that hedonism is false, because we would refuse to plug in, and then somebody replies that the intuition only shows we care about contact with reality, which is just one more item on a list of goods. I want to write the paper that does not do either of those things. My thesis is that the interesting disagreement is not between hedonism and its rivals at all but between additive and holistic conceptions of well-being, and a friend in the seminar keeps insisting that any case I invent to support this will quietly turn out to be about something else, so I want the case built carefully and then attacked. What I need is a thought experiment that does, for the temporal shape of a life, what the experience machine does for its contact with reality.

Build me two lives that satisfy all of the following at once. An additive hedonist must rank them equally good, and that verdict has to fall out of additivity itself rather than out of any contestable auxiliary assumption about which pleasures are worth having. An additive informed-desire theorist must also rank them equally good. A theory that treats the shape of a life as itself a component of well-being, meaning the view that a life which builds toward something, or hangs together as a whole, can be better for the person than a life with the same moments that is a shambles, must rank them unequally, and must do so without the case giving one life any good the other lacks. And finally, the point of the whole exercise: a hedonist who is not additive, someone who thinks the arrangement of pleasures across a life can matter in itself, so that a life of steady contentment and a life of ecstasy punctuated by despair need not be compared by totaling them up, must be able to side with the shape view on this case while remaining a hedonist. If the case cannot do that, it does not test what I want it to test. Constraints: these have to be two lives an ordinary person could actually have lived, so no machines, no neuroscience, no possible worlds, no time travel, and no stipulating that the two lives are identical in ways real lives cannot be. Neither person may be deceived or ignorant about anything that matters to them, neither may have been manipulated into their desires, and the difference between the two lives must not be describable as one of them containing more of anything on any ordinary list of goods, whether that is pleasure, desire-satisfaction, knowledge, achievement, friendship, or autonomy.

Once you have the case, give me the verdict and the reasoning for each of the four positions named above, tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and tell me what happens to the disagreement if I remove that feature. Then give me the strongest version of the attack on the case from someone who defends the additive theories and thinks shape is an aesthetic preference smuggled into ethics, and say honestly whether the attack succeeds. Tell me whether the shape view is really just an objective-list theory that puts narrative structure on the list, and if it is, what that does to my thesis that the real fight is over additivity. If I have to stipulate that the two people are strangely indifferent to how their lives hang together in order to get the additive desire theorist to rank the lives equal, say so plainly, because that would be the weakest joint in the case. And if some of these requirements cannot be met together, do not paper over it: tell me which one has to give, why, and what the closest case that does work looks like. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold, no tables, the way you would write a long email to someone who asked you a favor. American English, please.
