I have been trying to work out, for myself, a consistent set of judgments about the liberties a dramatist may take with the real dead, and I would like to know whether my verdicts add up to a principle, and if so what it says. My intuitions, going case by case, are these.

A screenwriter invents a private conversation between two long-dead statesmen, in a room where nothing was recorded, but the exchange is consistent with everything that is on the record. I think that is permissible. A novelist puts a speech into the mouth of a well-documented nineteenth-century general at a moment when historians are confident he said nothing of the kind, but the speech expresses what he plainly believed; I think that is permissible too, and I would even defend it as truer than silence. A biopic invents a sustained affair for a writer whose surviving letters show a devoted and faithful marriage: not permissible. A film folds three real campaigners into a single composite figure, unnamed but transparently a stand-in: permissible. A film invents that a recently dead musician, who leaves a widow and young children, died of a deliberate overdose when the coroner found the death accidental: not permissible, and I suspect the living survivors are a large part of why. A novel gives a real slave-owner a late conversion of conscience that the surviving record suggests never happened, in order to make the moral texture of the period vivid: here I keep going back and forth, and my gut says there is something wrong that is not wrong in the invented-conversation case, though I cannot say what. A play invents that a real but obscure woman was illiterate when she in fact signed her name to surviving documents: not permissible. A film has a real person deliver lines drawn in substance from his own diaries and letters: permissible, obviously.

The tentative principle I would offer, and want tested and repaired, is this: an invention is allowable when it could have been true given the surviving evidence and does not purport to report what the record contains; it is not allowable when the evidence positively rules it out, or when the invention would leave an audience with a false belief about what the record shows. I already suspect this principle is too crude, because the slave-owner case seems to satisfy it and still troubles me, and because the dying-musician case seems to violate nothing about the record so much as something about the survivors.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of competing principles, best captures these verdicts, stating each clause precisely and naming the case that motivates it. Then say what your preferred principle predicts for cases I have not put to you: inventing a sexual relationship between two real, long-dead adults where the evidence is silent but they lived in a time when such a relationship would have been hidden; and the same invention transposed to figures dead for six centuries with no identifiable descendants, so that the only thing left to wrong is the dead themselves. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should give one of them up.
