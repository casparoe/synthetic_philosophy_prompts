I run the search-and-rescue operation for a large alpine park, and I have just been handed the job of rewriting our deployment protocol for the situation the handbook has always dodged: one aircraft, one weather window, and people we could reach on two different routes but not both. On paper our rule is "maximize expected lives saved," which in practice means the larger party gets the flight. Since taking over I have been reading the philosophical literature on whether the numbers count — in particular the argument associated with John Taurek, that when I can save one stranger or save five, the five do not have a weightier claim merely because they are more numerous, and that what fairness requires instead is something like a lottery among the individuals at risk. It is more persuasive to me than I expected, and it has made me distrust our protocol. But I cannot hand a duty officer a rule that amounts to "toss a coin" without working out what that actually means at three in the morning in a storm. These calls come perhaps two or three times a season, but each one is somebody's life and the decision is irreversible.

I would like you to act as a philosopher working in normative ethics and the theory of rational choice, and also as someone used to advising operational agencies, and to turn this principle into a procedure my duty officers can actually run. Please think carefully before you answer, and begin by brainstorming in writing: set out what the principle is really committed to, separating the claim about the separateness of persons from the claim about how ties are to be broken, and say which parts are load-bearing and which are rhetoric. Then give me the procedure itself — the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a definite answer, and where it hands the decision back to a human because the question is one of value rather than fact.

It has to survive the cases that actually come up: (a) one badly injured climber on one route against five uninjured but stranded climbers on another; (b) one person reachable before the window closes against five who could also be reached, more slowly, by a ground team that is already committed and would otherwise be free; (c) a certain rescue of one against a low-probability rescue of five; (d) one death against five serious injuries rather than five deaths; (e) a solo climber against a rope team, where only one member of the rope team can be lifted at that moment. One of my team leads has proposed a "weighted lottery" — everyone gets a ticket, but a party of five gets five tickets — and I want the procedure to say plainly whether that is a fix or our old rule in disguise.

Then run the procedure on two of these cases, one of which it decides in a way I will not like, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which judgments it is quietly smuggling in, and what would have to be added by someone with the authority to make policy rather than by a duty officer on shift.

Constraints: the duty officer has minutes, not hours; the person running the procedure may have no background in philosophy; the decision has to be defensible afterwards, in writing, to a review board and to the families involved; and the procedure cannot depend on information we will not have, such as life-years gained, age, or dependents.
