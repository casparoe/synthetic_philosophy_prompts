Bit of an odd request, but here's where I'm at. I did a physics undergrad, jumped to a philosophy of science masters, and I've just started a two-year fellowship where nobody is going to tell me what to work on, which is terrifying. It also means I get the same two reactions from my old lab friends on repeat: either "oh so you do ethics now," or one of them quoting Feynman at me about how philosophy of science is useless to actual physicists. I dont want to relitigate whether philosophy helps science in the abstract. I need to pick a line of work and I want it to be one that isn't a waste of two years, so I want to know which open questions in this area actually deserve the time.

Here's the frame my old supervisor gave me, which I think is the interesting version of the question. Stop asking whether philosophy helps science; ask where in the pipeline it helps and whether anyone can tell. She splits it three ways. (1) Philosophy that runs ahead: it clears up a conceptual mess before the science has to build on it, so someone gets straight on what counts as a measurement or a cause and a decade of confused work never happens. (2) Philosophy that runs behind: it rationalizes what scientists already do, and the physicists say this is worthless because the science got there first. (3) Philosophy as error-detection: it hands a field vocabulary for noticing that it has been using one word for two things. My favorite example is "information," which seems to mean four different things depending on whether I'm in a physics, bio, or comp-sci seminar, and nobody seems bothered. She thinks (3) is the underexplored one, (2) is mostly our department flattering itself, and that she might be wrong about both.

So: inside that map, what are the open questions most worth two years of one person's life, ordered? I care more about the ordering and its criteria than the list. For each, why is it upstream of the others, is it tractable for a philosopher rather than someone who'd need to run experiments, and how neglected is it? And be straight with me about which of those three roles the historical record actually supports, because if (2) is right I should probably go do something else. Also tell me what would move a question up or down your list over the next couple of years. Please dont hand me a reading list, I can't get to a library this month and I'd rather have your reasoning than titles.
