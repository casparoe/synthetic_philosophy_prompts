A hospital's clinical ethics committee has, over the past several years, issued a series of rulings on responsibility for harms involving autonomous systems. The committee's record is as follows.

In the first case, a factory worker disabled a robot's safety guard and was injured. The committee ruled that the worker was responsible and the robot's manufacturer was not.

In the second case, a surgical robot made an unexpected movement during an operation. The surgeon had been monitoring the robot but could not have overridden the movement in time and had no reason to expect it. The committee ruled that the surgeon was not responsible, but that the hospital was responsible for inadequate training on the robot's failure modes.

In the third case, a manufacturer deployed an autonomous vehicle with a failure mode it knew about but judged acceptably rare. The vehicle caused a fatal accident. The committee ruled that the manufacturer was responsible.

In the fourth case, a trading algorithm caused a flash crash. The developers had not foreseen the specific interaction that triggered the crash and could not have prevented it. The committee ruled that the developers were not responsible, but that the firm was responsible for inadequate risk management.

The committee now faces a new case. A hospital has deployed an AI diagnostic tool that is generally reliable but has a known bias against a particular demographic group. The hospital's procurement committee knew about the bias but decided that the tool's overall accuracy justified deployment. The radiologist using the tool does not know about the bias. The radiologist has the authority to override the tool's recommendation and, in retrospect, could have overridden it in this case, but had no reason to suspect bias. The tool misdiagnosed a patient from the affected demographic group, and the misdiagnosis caused serious harm.

I want to know what the committee would decide about the radiologist and about the hospital's procurement committee. More importantly, I want to understand the rule the committee seems to be running. Several rules fit its past rulings equally well: for example, that responsibility requires both control over the relevant action and knowledge of the relevant risk; that responsibility requires either control or knowledge, provided the harm is traceable to the agent's choices; and that responsibility attaches to whichever party was best positioned to prevent the harm. State what each of these rules predicts about the radiologist and the procurement committee in the new case. Give me your best guess about what the committee would actually decide, with your reasons. Then tell me which further case would best distinguish these rules, and what verdict in that further case would count as evidence for each rule. If instead you think the committee should decide the new case in the way most consistent with its own precedents, say what that decision is, which past case is the closest analog, and what principle the committee would be adopting by deciding either way.
