A tutor of mine once summarised Friedman's F-twist roughly like this: 'A theory is just a tool for turning inputs into predictions, so the realism of its assumptions is neither here nor there — if anything the more unrealistic the better, because that's what buys you generality. Perfect competition is the obvious case: it doesn't need any actual firms to be doing any actual competing, the only question is whether the predictions come out right. Which means that when someone objects that people obviously don't maximise utility, that isn't an objection at all. Strip the labels off and it's just instrumentalism.'

Most of that sounds right to me, but I suspect the second half is smuggling something in. A mate of mine recons the whole thing hinges on a distinction I dont properly get — that 'unrealistic' covers at least three different cases: an assumption that's true as far as it goes but leaves out things whose effect doesn't matter much; an assumption that's false and deliberately so, a first step you're meant to relax later; and an assumption that's really just marking out which cases the theory is meant for. Can you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on this would nod at, what they'd wince at, and what's flat wrong — and then whether the conclusion about critics of utility maximisation survives once the errors are fixed? I'd also like to know whether the as-if bit commits him to saying agents literally do the sums.
