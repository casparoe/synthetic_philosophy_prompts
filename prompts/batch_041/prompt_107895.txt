I have been trying to work out what, if anything, survives of the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, and I think I have found a design that settles the matter decisively. Imagine a language whose grammar obligatorily attaches a shape classifier to any object a speaker refers to — long and thin, flat, round, and the like — so that one cannot mention an object without committing to its shape class; and contrast it with a language that has no such obligatory marking, though it can of course describe shapes with adjectives whenever it likes. Now suppose we show speakers of both languages a set of unfamiliar objects and give them a brief memory test. I would expect the first group to do markedly better, precisely because their grammar compels them to attend to shape every time they speak. And that, as I understand it, is exactly what the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis claims: that grammar shapes perception, so that speakers of different languages genuinely inhabit different cognitive worlds.

What I cannot understand, then, is why the hypothesis is so widely dismissed. If it can be demonstrated as cleanly as this, why do linguists and psychologists keep treating it as at most a weak and marginal effect? And does it not also follow that certain things a speaker of the shape-marking language perceives are strictly inexpressible in the other, so that translation between the two is impossible in principle?
