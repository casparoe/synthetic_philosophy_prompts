I spent forty years doing probabilistic hazard assesment — volcanic and seismic, mostly — which is a polite way of saying I spent a career putting numbers on things that wipe out a valley once every few thousand years and then watching policy people ignore them. Since I retired I've been reading philosophy, and I've gotten sucked into the AI existential risk argument. I've read maybe two hundred blog posts and comment threads on it by now, and I've noticed something: the two camps never move. Not a inch.

Roughly: one side says that if there's even a small chance — a few percent, a tenth of a percent — that building powerful AI ends badly for everyone forever, then that risk swamps everything else we might do, so it's the obvious priority, and anyone who disagrees is either innumerate or in denial. The other side says the probabilities are unfalsifiable hand-waving built on loose analogies to evolution and to nuclear weapons, that nobody can estimate these things, and that treating a made-up 1% as a real 1% is itself the error. Both sides seem to agree on a lot of the intermediate facts — compute is growing, alignment is hard, we don't know how to specify goals robustly — and yet they disagree about what follows, and have for years.

My suspicion, and I'd like you to tell me if it's wrong: the real disagreement isn't about the facts at all, it's about the decision rule. In my old job we never just multiplied probability by body count — we used risk-weighting, thresholds, explicit precaution, because a 1-in-10,000 event that kills a city does not get treated as 0.0001 times a city. I gather there's a formal version of this in decision theory, something called risk-weighted expected utility, where you weight outcomes by where they sit in the probability distribution rather than just multiplying them. If that's the crux, then the whole debate is downstream of a question about rationality that has nothing specifically to do with AI, and people are re-fighting an old decision theory dispute under a tech-news heading.

So: diagnose it. Is this a verbal dispute, a factual one in disguise, a values dispute about how much the far future counts, or a decision-theoretic one? Where exactly does the stalemate live? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a formal result, a distinction, an agreed way to price these probabilities, or nothing, because it's bedrock? Don't survey the literature at me; just tell me what kind of disagreement this is and why, using what each side actually says.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question.
