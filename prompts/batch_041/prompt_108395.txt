I am a junior in high school and this year I am the student representative on my city's Commemoration Review Committee, a nine-member body of historians, clergy, neighborhood association officers, a veterans' group delegate, and two at-large residents. We meet once a month and recommend to the city council what should be done about monuments, plaques, and the names of streets, parks, and schools in a city of about ninety thousand people. The committee was created after a petition to rename a street turned into a two-year fight, and in the year since, we have handled eleven requests: eight to remove a name, two to add one, and one to keep a monument where it stands but add an interpretive sign. At present every case is argued from scratch by whoever shows up to the hearing, and the outcome seems to depend more on who speaks last than on anything about the case itself. Our chair has asked me to write the operating procedure we will use from now on, and I would rather it rest on something more solid than my own instincts, so I am asking you to write it: a reusable decision procedure, in the form of questions asked in order, that nine people with no philosophical training can actually run inside a sixty-day window on a small budget.

For each question, the procedure should say what counts as a sufficient answer, when the question ends the case with a no, and when it hands the decision to a vote because the issue is one of values rather than of fact. Certain distinctions have to survive the simplification, because they are the ones our arguments actually turn on, and I would rather you tell me which can be dropped than quietly drop them: the difference between a thing that honors a person, a thing that marks an event or a loss, and a name that has become a bare address with no commemorative work left in it; the difference between objecting to what a person did, objecting to what the monument asserts, and objecting to the fact that the city is the one doing the asserting; the difference between a harm the city is still asserting today and one that lies simply in the past; the trap in the thought that everyone held those views back then, given how contested those views were at the time; the options that are neither keeping nor removing, such as relocating, reinterpreting, adding a counter-marker, or retiring a name to the historical record, along with whether adding context is a genuine resolution or a way of not deciding; who has standing to petition and to object, including descendants, alumni, property owners, and tribal nations; and whether the committee should presume in favor of the status quo or presume against continued honor, which I suspect is the single biggest fork in the whole procedure. It must also work for requests to add a commemoration, not only to remove one. Please do not send me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations, since I would have no way to check them; I want the procedure itself, written so it can be read aloud at the start of a hearing, with a one-page version for that purpose and a fuller version, including the reasoning behind each step, for the record. Mark clearly which steps are real tests and which are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and be specific about where the procedure will misfire.

Then run it from start to finish on three cases. First, Pemberton Avenue: named in 1911 for a physician who donated the land for our first public library and founded the hospital's free clinic, and who, as a state legislator in the 1890s, sponsored the bill that let the state sterilize people in its institutions without their consent. Second, the soldier on the courthouse lawn: a bronze figure of an anonymous infantryman, erected in 1908 by a women's memorial association, that lists the local war dead by name and whose pedestal carries an inscription praising the cause those soldiers fought for. Third, Fenn Street: named in 1889 by a developer after his own daughter, a name no one has ever taken as an honor, which a neighborhood group now wants renamed for a civil rights organizer who lived on it until her death two years ago. For each, give the recommendation the procedure produces and the reasoning, including any step where it dead-ends. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like, and I would rather you name that one and explain why the procedure forces it than soften the procedure to avoid it. Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: the questions it will never settle, the cases where the honest answer is that nine people must vote and live with the result, and the ways a committee could run every step correctly and still reach a recommendation the community will experience as an injustice. Please write the whole thing in American English, in plain enough language that a committee member who has never read any philosophy can follow it.
