A recurring move in discussions of turnout is to compare casting a vote to buying a lottery ticket: both cost the voter something small, both are undertaken for the sake of a prize, and in both cases the chance that the purchase is what makes the difference between winning and losing is vanishingly small. The comparison is then used to license a further claim: that the expected instrumental return on a single vote — the probability of being pivotal multiplied by the value of the difference between the candidates — is negligible, so that the fact of mass turnout cannot be explained by instrumental reasoning and must be explained instead by expressive or civic motives. Some go further and treat the comparison as showing that any instrumental account of turnout is a post hoc rationalisation. (I have in mind the puzzle about turnout, not the Condorcet paradox of cyclical majorities.)

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on it. Please do the following.

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly, a table is fine: say what plays the role of the ticket, the price, the prize, the draw, the odds, the other ticket-holders, and the seller.

2. Identify where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support from those that are merely decorative, and say why each matters or does not. As part of this, be explicit about which features of lotteries the analogy quietly imports into voting — features the target may not have — and about which features of voting the lottery frame cannot represent at all.

3. Two structural differences strike me as potentially important, and I want your assessment of whether they are. First, in a lottery the odds are fixed by the number of tickets sold, whereas the probability of being pivotal in an election depends on how many other people turn out, which depends in turn on how close they expect the contest to be; the quantity the analogy treats as a datum is thus partly a product of the very decision being explained. Second, a lottery prize is excludable, going only to a ticket-holder, whereas the prize of an election — the policy difference between the candidates — is delivered to everyone regardless of whether they voted, so the analogy makes voting look like a private gamble when it may be a contribution to a public outcome. Are these differences fatal, repairable, or irrelevant to the intended conclusion?

4. If they are repairable, describe the repaired analogy and say whether the repair still supports the conclusion about instrumental versus expressive motives. A colleague has proposed repairing it by making the prize a public good shared by ticket-holders and non-holders alike; tell me whether that saves the analogy or dissolves it.

5. If a better analogy exists for the situation of the individual voter, offer one, and say what it captures that the lottery comparison misses.

Work this out from the structure of the case rather than from the literature; I am interested in the argument, not in who said what. At the end, be explicit about how much of the original conclusion survives. I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, but I would rather be told that I am wrong than be humoured.
