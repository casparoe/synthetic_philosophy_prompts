I'm in a small decision-theory reading group and we've spent weeks going around in circles on Omega-style cases, so I want to try something different: instead of arguing theory-first, here are my gut verdicts on seven cases, and I want you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to, and which of them I'd have to give up. (1) Counterfactual mugging: Omega flips a fair coin; heads and he gives me $10,000, tails and he asks me for $100 and tells me it's tails; he's a perfect predictor, so in the heads branch he only pays me if he predicted I'd pay in the tails branch. I pay the $100. (2) Same thing, but he lets me sign a binding precommitment before the flip; I sign, and this one doesn't even feel like a decision. (3) An ordinary blackmailer, no coin, no crystal ball: 'pay me $1,000 or I burn your house down,' and he really would, and there's no second round, no one watching, and he'll never come back. I don't pay. I know that's not maximizing; I'd rather lose the house than be buyable. (4) The termites one a friend calls XOR blackmail: a perfectly honest source sends me a note saying it sent the note iff my house is infested with termites xor I pay it $1,000, and a termite infestation would cost me $1,000,000. I pay the $1,000. (5) The smoking one: there's a brain lesion that causes both lung cancer and a taste for cigarettes, and smoking itself does nothing to your lungs. I smoke. (6) Newcomb's boxes: I take only the opaque box. (7) A variant I keep chewing on: same as (1), except that in the heads branch Omega gives the $10,000 to a stranger in another country instead of to me. I refuse.

The trouble is these feel like they come apart into two or three incompatible theories. (4) seems like it can only be justified evidentially -- I'm paying because my paying would be evidence that the note was triggered by the payment clause and not by termites -- but that exact same evidential move says don't smoke in (5), and I'm certain I should smoke. (1) and (2) seem to need something more like 'pick the policy with the best expected value across all the branches you might be in,' which I can't tell apart from the evidential thing in a principled way, and which also makes me queasy in (3), where it seems to want me to pay the blackmailer. And (7) is the one that nags me most: if I'll pay $100 for the sake of my counterfactual heads-self, why not for a stranger's counterfactual heads-self? I half suspect (7) is just me being selfish and there's no principled difference, and half suspect there is one and I'm missing it.

So what I want: the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that actually covers these, with each clause tied to the case that forces it; the verdicts it gives on cases I havent listed -- in particular a version of (4) where the source is honest but you have no earthly idea how it knew, and a version of (1) where the coin is never revealed to you and you're asked to pay blind; and an honest statement of which of my seven verdicts can't be kept. If the truth is that I need one theory for (1)-(2), another for (4), and a third for (5), just say so and tell me which verdict is the weakest link. I'd rather drop a verdict then keep a theory that's really three theories in a trenchcoat.
