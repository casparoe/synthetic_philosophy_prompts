I retired eighteen months ago, after a career of some thirty years in molecular genetics, mostly yeast, and I have spent the time since reading philosophy of biology with the growing suspicion that I spent that entire career using a word I could not define. Next month a colleague has asked me to run a two-hour session in a graduate concepts-and-methods seminar in her department; the students are formidable at the bench and have never once been asked what they mean when they say that a gene encodes something. I want the session to turn on a contrast that I have not seen drawn cleanly in one place, and which I believe accounts for most of the noise in the argument over whether DNA is a code: the difference between a code in the engineering or cipher sense — an arbitrary, systematic, invertible mapping from symbols to symbols, such that any competent decoder in possession of the mapping recovers the original message and nothing else — and a code in the legal or statutory sense — a body of rules that an interpreter applies to a case, which constrains the outcome without determining it, which admits of paraphrase and gloss, and behind which no single message waits to be recovered. My working suspicion, which I want you to test rather than take over from me, is that the textbook genetic code is cipher-like while the rhetoric of the genome as a code borrows all its force from the statutory sense, and that the dispute is largely an equivocation. A crude test I keep returning to: a cipher can only be decrypted, whereas a statute can be paraphrased. Do not explain the distinction to me — I know what I take it to be. Plan how I should teach it.

What I need is the architecture of the explanation, not the explanation itself. Give me the order in which the pieces must come and why each depends on the one before it; in particular, tell me whether the students need the arbitrariness of the codon assignments settled before the statutory analogy can do any work, or whether that order is backwards. Tell me the single example that should carry the whole session, what it has to have and what it must lack, and whether it can be the codon table itself. If the usual candidates — the codon table, the blueprint, the Morse-code analogy, which I once tried and which I believe made matters worse, though I cannot tell whether the analogy was at fault or only my handling of it — ought to be avoided, say what replaces them and why.

Then map the misconceptions for me. These are biologists, so I expect them to arrive believing that a gene is a code because it is written in letters; that the genetic code is the code in which the genome is written; that arbitrariness implies convention, convention implies a language, and a language implies a speaker; and that a mere causal correlation between a sequence and a product is sufficient for information. Tell me where in the sequence each of these has to be headed off, and what it costs me if I let one stand too long. Finish with a single question I can put to them in the last ten minutes that would show whether the distinction has landed rather than been parroted back, and tell me what I should openly refuse to settle in two hours — I would rather name the open questions than pretend to close them. I do not want a reading list; the session has to stand on its own.
