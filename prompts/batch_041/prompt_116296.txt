My flatmate's doing a philosophy PhD and he's decided I'm "money-pumpable" — he reckons he can set up a series of bets against me that he wins no matter what happens, without knowing anything about the thing we're betting on that I don't already know, and without me ever accepting a bet I think is a bad deal. I've been keeping a spreadsheet of my credences for a month now, so I'm personally invested in whether he's right. He says the magic ingredient is that I know I'll change my mind about things later. I don't buy it, but I can't argue the point, so I want you to build his case for him, to spec, and then tell me honestly what it does and doesn't show.

What I want: a diachronic Dutch book — a money pump for opinions rather than for tastes — that works on an agent as unimpeachable as you can make them. Specs, all of them please:

- They update by conditionalisation, never forget anything, and their credences are coherent at every moment.
- Every bet they accept is one they'd endorse at the moment they accept it. No accepting bets they think are bad deals, and no "well, they'll regret it afterwards" hand-waving.
- They know the whole plan in advance. The bookie tells them the full sequence of bets, up front. Nothing about their own future is a surprise to them.
- The bookie knows nothing about the matter being bet on that the agent doesn't know. The profit has to come out of timing, not out of better information.
- The profit has to be guaranteed, not merely positive in expectation.
- Boring and realistic, please: two ordinary moments in the same day, small amounts of money, no brainwashing machines, no drugs, no demons, no amnesia.
- And the agent shouldn't be wrong about anything either. If they predict what they'll think later, they should turn out to be right.

Then do the work: give me the sequence of bets, why the agent takes each one at the time they take it, and a payout table showing the bookie is up in every outcome. Then tell me the one feature of the agent that's carrying the whole thing, what happens if you take that feature away (does the case die, or does it survive in a weaker form?), and the strongest line for someone defending the agent — someone who says the agent is doing nothing wrong at any moment and it's the bookie who's exploiting something the agent isn't actually obliged to care about.

Two things I'd rather have than not: if it can't be built to all those specs, say so plainly and tell me which spec has to give, rather than handing me something that technically ticks the boxes. And if the claim that a guaranteed-loss book shows the agent is irrational is itself contested, tell me that too — I don't want to stand up on Thursday and repeat my flatmate's argument only to find out it's the textbook version of a disputed move.

(sorry, I know the spec list is fussy, but the fussiness is the point — otherwise it's just the example everyone's already seen.)
