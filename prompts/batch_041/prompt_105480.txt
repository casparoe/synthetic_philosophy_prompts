I teach an upper-level seminar that mixes philosophy of science with causal inference, and I'm drafting the take-home final. I want one question on Simpson's paradox that cannot be answered by reciting the Berkeley admissions example, so I've built a fresh case to anchor it. Here is the table, with entries as (patients recovered / patients treated):

- Treatment A — mild cases: 9/10; severe cases: 30/90
- Treatment B — mild cases: 89/100; severe cases: 1/10

Write the exam question around this table, with a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme. The question must make students do three things: (1) state the two defensible answers to "which treatment has the higher recovery rate" and show where each comes from; (2) say what role the severity variable would have to play — confounder, mediator, or collider — for the pooled comparison to answer the causal question "which treatment is more effective," for the stratified comparison to do so, and what changes if severity is something the treatment itself affects; and (3) evaluate a classmate's claim that the reversal by itself proves that severity is a confounder. Part (3) is the crux: I want students to see that reversal is neither necessary nor sufficient for confounding, and in particular that with a non-collapsible measure like the odds ratio the pooled and stratum-specific estimates can disagree even when there is no confounding at all, so the mere fact of a reversal does not diagnose a causal structure. Students should also be pushed to say what the table alone does and does not settle — the numbers are consistent with several causal stories, and choosing among them requires assumptions the data cannot supply.

For the model answer and the marking scheme, keep the causal-inference points distinct from the interpretive ones, and have the scheme name the specific moves an A answer must contain versus a B answer, with the marks for each and a one-line note on how students typically get that point wrong. Out of 20 marks, across five must-hit points.

Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested — for instance, whether drawing the causal graph simply dissolves the paradox, whether it exposes something deeper about statistical evidence, and whether non-collapsibility-induced reversals should even be counted as instances of the paradox at all. Do not present a contested position as the consensus.
