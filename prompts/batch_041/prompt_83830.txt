I'm a novelist, three books in, and I've got a decent shot at a nine-month fellowship at a university humanities center — the kind that mixes artists and academics and makes you write a research proposal even though what I actually do all day is make things up. I need you to write that proposal for me. Treat yourself as a philosopher who works on personal identity and has read a ton of narrative theory, but also as someone who can write for a committee where half the readers aren't philosophers. Think carefully before you start, and brainstorm the framing out loud to yourself first — the question is the hard part, the rest is furniture. Two pages, 1000 words or so.

The topic is the self as a center of narrative gravity, Dennett's old move. Here's my half-formed idea, and it comes out of the novel I'm writing. Dennett's metaphor computes the center from one trajectory, one body moving through the world. But characters in novels — and I think people — are made at least as much out of the stories other people tell about them. I've got a character in the new book who barely speaks and who we only know through what her neighbors say about her, and she's as solid to me as anyone else in it. Then there's the real-world version I keep circling: someone who is publicly known for a thing they didn't do. Everyone's got a story about them. Its false. Is the protagonist of that story a self? Dennett's view seems to say accuracy isn't the point, the model just has to fit the pattern, which I like — but then it starts looking like it can't tell the difference between a real person and a character I made up. And if the answer is that real selves are anchored in an actual body and its causal history, then my plural-stories version has pulled the anchor loose. That's the crack I want to work in.

Two things I want the proposal to actually do: say what changes if the narrative field is plural and partly external (individuation — can two people share a center of gravity, or one person have two?), and say whether the view survives the truth question or has to be given up. Also worth a seperate paragraph: the people who insist their lives don't feel like stories at all. My version seems to handle them easily, maybe too easily, and if it handles every case then it's stopped being a claim about anything, which I'd rather own up front than have a reviewer say it for me.

So give me: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer; why the existing positions leave it open (positions and arguments, not a lit review — no citations, no titles, no dates, I'll fill all that in myself, so don't invent any); the method, month by month across nine months, keeping in mind I'll be drafting the novel at the same time; what the deliverable is; what would count as the project failing or ballooning; and a straight answer on whether a novelist is the right person to do this or whether I'm just dressing up the novel as research.
