Hey, I'm TAing a grad seminar on famine and political economy and I need a second pair of eyes on one of the exam answers. The question was:

"Famines are sometimes called natural disasters and sometimes called political crimes. Drawing on Sen's entitlement approach and on at least two of the following — the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish Great Famine, and the Chinese famine of the Great Leap Forward — assess the claim that famines are better understood as political failures than as natural disasters."

The student wrote:

Sen's entitlement approach says that famines happen when people lose the ability to command food through legal channels, not when food as such runs out. Your entitlement depends on your endowment (land, labour, etc.) and the exchange entitlements you can get from it. So a famine can strike even if total food availability hasn't fallen, because the starving group has lost its purchasing power or access. The Bengal famine of 1943 is his central case: food availability per head wasn't much lower than in previous years, and the deaths were driven by wartime inflation, hoarding and speculation, the collapse of rural labourers' real wages, and the decision to keep Calcutta fed. That's why it's called a "boom famine." So Sen's approach shows that famines are never really about food shortages — they're about distribution.

Sen also argues that famines don't happen in democracies, because elected governments with a free press will be punished if they let people starve. India after independence is his example. But the Irish Great Famine looks like a counterexample: Britain had a parliament and a free press, and yet around a million people died in Ireland in the 1840s while food was still being exported. So the democracy thesis seems too strong.

The Great Leap Forward famine is the clearest case of a political crime. Mao's collectivization, the backyard steel furnaces, the exaggerated harvest reports, and the refusal to allow a free press all caused mass starvation, and the government's denial made it worse. And since China's grain output actually went up during those years, this confirms Sen's point that famines are about entitlement failure, not food shortage. Taken together, Bengal, Ireland, and China show that the decisive factor in famines is political power, not nature.
