I am in my second year of a philosophy degree, currently taking a course in contemporary ethical theory, and our final assessment permits one piece of satirical writing in place of a standard essay. I have chosen to write mine on the utility monster, and I would like you to draft it.

The piece should take the form of an annual fundraising appeal from a fictional grant-making foundation — the sort of letter that thanks past donors, reports on the year, and asks for a modest monthly gift — of roughly 700 words, including a short section headed something on the order of "Questions donors have put to us." The foundation has just concluded a review of its own grant-making criteria and has decided, sincerely and without a trace of cynicism, that its entire programme budget should be directed to a single beneficiary: a being that converts resources into wellbeing far more efficiently than anyone else, because it experiences more, faster, or more intensely than we do. Write it deadpan. The comedy should come from the arithmetic and from the administrative consequences — the letters to discontinued partners, the staff retraining, the delicate conversation with the legacy donors — and never from jokes at the beneficiary's expense.

Some constraints, because I want this recognisable to someone who knows the literature, not merely funny.

1. The letter must never use the phrase "utility monster," and it should avoid the vocabulary of utilitarianism and consequentialism altogether. In the fiction the foundation would not describe itself in those terms; it would speak of impact per pound and of not wasting donors' money. The reasoning must be unmistakable without the labels.

2. The beneficiary must not be a sadist, a fraud, or a manipulator. Its welfare should be genuinely enormous, its preferences unremarkable, and its appetite for resources finite rather than insatiable. The piece is only interesting if the pull towards the conclusion is real.

3. The foundation's position should rest, in passing, on two commitments it never quite notices it is making: that wellbeing can be measured on a single scale and compared between persons, and that no one's claim may be discounted merely because it arrives in one body rather than in many. A reader who has never encountered the thought experiment should be able to reconstruct the argument from the letter alone.

4. The "Questions donors have put to us" section is where I want the standard escape routes fielded: that such a being is empirically absurd; that welfare gains diminish, so the transfer ought to stop somewhere; that what matters is the position of the worst off rather than the total; and that the whole case tells against hedonism rather than against adding welfare up. Give each its strongest form before the foundation answers it, and leave at least one visibly unanswered — I do not want the letter to win. A specialist should be able to see that I know these objections are live rather than settled.

5. Include one dissenting voice inside the document — a trustee who resigned, or a partner organisation that lost its funding — who argues, among other things, that the difficulty is not peculiar to the foundation's own rule, since a being can be tailored to defeat any rule, including the one she favours. State her objection fairly, and have the letter accommodate it rather than refute it.

6. Close with a piece of small print, in the register of a footnote, in which the foundation concedes in passing that on some views the relevant comparison is not between the size of two beings' welfare but between the size of the change a donation makes to each. I want it to read as a technicality that has been dealt with, so that a careful reader can see that it has not.

7. No citations, no names of real organisations or living people, and no quotations from any source. Everything in the document should be invented.

Finally: the reader should finish feeling the force of the argument rather than merely laughing at it, and the letter should offer no reassurance that the foundation has made a mistake. Our tutor has warned us that she will mark down any piece which treats the position being satirised as obviously stupid.
