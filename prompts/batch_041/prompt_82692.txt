I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, and I am the grader for a 300-level course on moral worth and the right reasons. The midterm has been anonymized, so I cannot ask the student to clarify anything, and the course rubric obliges me to write comments that tie each judgment to a numbered part of the question — the student will see those comments, and the department expects that a deduction can be defended on appeal. I would like a second opinion on one answer before I finalize its grade.

The cases in the question are ones I constructed for this exam, so there is nothing written on them; the exam was worth thirty points, ten for each part. The question read as follows.

Case A — Priya. Priya is a pharmacist at a hospital. While reconciling invoices she discovers that a supplier has been shipping a drug whose potency is far below what its label states, and that patients have been under-dosed as a result. Reporting the discrepancy will end the supplier's contract and, she knows, damage the career of the sales representative who handles the account — a man who publicly mocked her at a conference the previous month, and toward whom she feels a settled resentment that she freely acknowledges. She reports the discrepancy to the regulator and to her hospital's purchasing office. She also believes, correctly, that reporting it is what her position obliges her to do. It is a fact about her, and not merely her opinion of herself, that she would have reported the discrepancy even if the representative had never crossed her path.

Case B — Lena. Lena was raised in a community that holds that injuries within the community are to be settled internally, and that involving outside authorities in them is a betrayal of one's family that brings lasting shame on it. Her neighbour is being beaten by her partner. Lena drives the neighbour, at night, to a shelter in the next town and lies to the community about where they went. She is moved by the neighbour's terror and by an unwillingness to leave her to it. She also believes that she has done something shameful, that she has betrayed her family, and that she acted from a weakness she is ashamed of. Assume for the purposes of this question that helping the neighbour is what morality in fact required, and that Lena's community's code is mistaken.

(1) For each case, state whether the action has moral worth on the Kantian account, and defend your answer. (2) For each case, state whether the action has moral worth on the reasons-responsiveness account — the account on which an action has moral worth when the agent's motive is a response to the considerations that in fact count in favour of the action, whether or not the agent represents those considerations to herself as duties. (3) Does either account require the agent to know the motive from which she acts? Does either account have the resources to distinguish the moral worth of an action from the praiseworthiness of the person who performs it? Answer in no more than 600 words.

The answer I am asking about is reproduced below, unaltered.

The distinction the question turns on is Kant's between acting from duty and acting merely in accordance with duty. In Case A, Priya's report is in accordance with duty — it is what her position obliges her to do, and she knows it — but the motive that moved her was her resentment of the sales representative. Kant's shopkeeper, who deals honestly because cheating would ruin his trade, does what honesty requires, but his honesty has no moral worth; it is a reliable policy, not a good will. Priya's report is the same kind of thing. Her resentment was doing the real work, and so the Kantian account must deny her report moral worth. Nor is she praiseworthy for it: an action without moral worth is, for Kant, no better than an accident, and we do not praise people for accidents.

Lena is the harder case, and the more interesting one. She does what morality requires, but she does not do it because she takes it to be required; she takes it to be forbidden. Since acting from duty requires acting from a recognition of the action's obligatoriness, the Kantian account must deny her action moral worth as well — and this looks like a reductio, because she is the only agent in the two cases who incurs a real cost for the sake of someone else.

This is why the reasons-responsiveness account is worth having. It asks whether the agent's motive is a response to a reason she has, rather than to the action's obligatoriness. But the response has to be to a reason as the agent sees it: an agent is only responsive to the considerations that figure in her deliberation. Since Lena's deliberation contains no consideration that counts in favour of driving her neighbour to the shelter — she thinks the whole business is a betrayal and acts only because she cannot bear the alternative — her action is not a response to the reason that there is, and the reasons-responsiveness account must deny it moral worth too. Her sympathy is an inclination on any account, and an inclination cannot do the work of a reason.

So the two accounts agree about both cases, and the difference between them is smaller than it is usually made out to be. The real disagreement is about whether a good will requires a certain kind of self-understanding.
