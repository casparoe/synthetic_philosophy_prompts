I'm writing a novel in which a painter spends a chapter making very fine colour judgments — deciding, over and over, whether two patches of paint match — and I want the chapter to turn on a distinction I've only half understood, so I'm hoping you can explain it to me properly. At the climax of the scene she is standing at the studio window at dusk, looking at the display of a digital thermometer on the roof of the building across the street: a small panel of glowing digits reading twenty point three, with no unit marked and nothing to indicate whether the reading is Celsius or Fahrenheit, or whether the instrument is accurate at all. What interests me is the exact shape of what she can and cannot know from where she stands. She can see, perfectly clearly, that the display reads twenty point three. So she knows that the temperature is at least twenty degrees Celsius — that much seems plain, since the display says so and displays of that kind are normally accurate enough for a claim as coarse as that. And yet it also seems plain to me that she cannot know that she knows it: from the inside, she has no way to establish that the display is working, that she is reading it correctly, or that it hasn't been reset. She knows the fact, or so I want to say, but she is in no position to know that she knows it. What I can't see is whether those two claims can both be true, and if so why.

I gather this general shape has a name — the question whether knowing entails knowing that one knows, the so-called KK principle, and something about knowledge being "luminous" or not — but I've never understood the mechanism. So please explain the conceptual issue to me: how can it be that she knows something and yet cannot know that she knows it?
