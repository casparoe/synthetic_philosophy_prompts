Consider the Piltdown Man affair: the fragments found in Sussex in the early twentieth century, reconstructed as a missing link between ape and human, accepted by much of the scientific community for decades, and eventually exposed as a composite forgery. Analyze this episode through the Nyāya theory of pramāṇas. Nyāya recognizes four pramāṇas: pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (knowledge of something through its similarity to a previously perceived thing), and śabda (testimony); unlike Mīmāṃsā, it does not treat arthāpatti (postulation) or anupalabdhi (non-apprehension) as independent pramāṇas. In your analysis, use the distinctions between nirvikalpaka (indeterminate) and savikalpaka (determinate) perception, saṃśaya (doubt) and nirṇaya (ascertainment), vyāpti (pervasion) in inference, and the five-membered Nyāya inference scheme (thesis, reason, example, application, conclusion) where they do real work. Map the episode onto the framework: what was actually perceived, what was inferred and from what reason, what role upamāna played in classifying the fragments, and how śabda—the testimony of the finder, the anatomists, and the broader community—secured assent. Then say what a Nyāya-trained inquirer would have been warranted in believing at each stage, and what the community should have done differently. Finally, assess the framework itself: where does it illuminate the affair, and where is it silent or misleading? In particular, can Nyāya's account of śabda, which centers on the trustworthiness of the speaker and the veridicality of the utterance, handle prolonged, coordinated forgery in a distributed scientific community? Does the episode reveal a need for a social epistemology of testimony that Nyāya lacks, or can its resources for doubt (saṃśaya), hypothetical reasoning (tarka), and ascertainment (nirṇaya) be stretched to cover it? Be specific about which pramāṇa failed and at what point, and avoid treating the hoax as simply a failure of perception or simply a failure of testimony.
