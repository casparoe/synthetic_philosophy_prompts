I'm a novelist, no philosophy training, so bear with me. I'm writing a book where two characters spend the entire middle section fighting about when it's ok to believe something you can't prove. One of them is a hardass: if the evidence doesn't add up you have no business believing it, full stop, and you're a coward or a liar if you do. The other thinks that's inhuman — some things you can only get by going in on them first, and a person who waits for proof before trusting their spouse or their own chances is missing the whole point of being alive. I dont want either of them winning the argument by name-dropping some philosopher, so I want the actual spec sheet underneath the fight: what does any decent account of when believing is permitted, admirable, or blameworthy have to be able to deliver?

Give me the requirements list. For each, what it demands and the concrete case that forces it — cases, not abstractions. Some things I already know I need it to handle and have no idea how: the shipowner who talks himself into believing his vessel is seaworthy and sends it out (is he a murderer or just unlucky?); believing you'll make the jump, or pass the exam, when believing it genuinely makes it more likely, so the evidence can't be in yet; a wife who believes her husband is faithful on mixed evidence, and whether that's loyalty or self-deception; a doctor or teacher who has to believe in someone in order to help them; a hiring manager whose belief about a group is statistically well-founded but poison to hold; and the plain fact that I can't decide to believe something on command, so what is even the point of telling me I should.

Then the part I really want: which of these requirements are geniune and which are just the house rules of one camp dressed up as requirements? Is there a pair in there that can't both be met — where any theory has to give something up, and if so, what does it give up and who loses? And which single requirement does the most to kill off the popular answers?

Format: a table. Columns: requirement; what it demands, one sentence; the case that forces it; verdict on whether it's non-negotiable or one camp's preference (name the camp); and which kind of theory it rules out. Then a second, smaller table for the clashes: requirement A; requirement B; why they pull apart; whether the conflict is real or just sloppy framing; and what a theory has to sacrifice to keep both. Skip the bibliography, obviously — I just want the arguments.
