I am a high school student writing an essay for a philosophy seminar, and I have become stuck on a conceptual question that seems to be less about which choice is right and more about what it means for numbers to count at all.

The case I am working with is this. I can save either five people or six people; both groups are strangers to me, each life is worth the same, there is no time to save both groups, and there is no other difference between them. The position I have encountered, which I have seen attributed to Taurek, is that it is not obvious that I am required to save the six, and that I might even be permitted to settle the matter by tossing a coin. The reasoning behind it, as I understand it, is that each person has only their own life to lose, that there is no single subject who loses six lives when the six die, and that separate losses therefore do not accumulate the way quantities of a single good do.

What I keep running into is that the phrase the numbers count seems to be doing at least three different jobs, and I cannot tell whether they can be pulled apart:

1. The numbers count in the outcome: saving six produces more of something good than saving five does.
2. The numbers count in the procedure: the claims of six people, or six equal chances, receive more weight than the claims of five in how I arrive at my decision.
3. The numbers count in each person's prospects: whether I am one of six or one of five changes the probability that I in particular am rescued, so each individual has a stake in the size of their group.

Please explain, as precisely and intuitively as you can, what distinguishes these three claims from one another. Then tell me whether there is a coherent position that accepts the third, and perhaps the second, while rejecting the first — that is, a view on which the numbers genuinely count for something even though there is no value that grows with the number of lives saved. If such a position exists, I would like to know what it says the numbers count as, and how it avoids quietly smuggling aggregation back in through another door. If no such position exists, I would like to understand what forces the choice between straight aggregation and the conclusion that the numbers do not count at all.

Finally, tell me whether the underlying disagreement here is really about the value of outcomes, about what one person may do to another, or about what it means to give each person equal consideration. My own suspicion is that it is the last of these, but I would like the argument for whichever answer you give, rather than a survey of who has said what.
