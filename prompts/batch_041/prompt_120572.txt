I teach a second-year MA seminar on practical rationality and the philosophy of action, and I am drafting the final exam. I would like a single essay question on Buridan's ass, worth 25 marks and answerable in about ninety minutes, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme.

The question must not be answerable by reciting the three dissolutions that students reliably produce. The first is that no real agent ever faces two options that are exactly equal, so the case is an idle fiction. The second is that any real agent has some psychological tie-breaker — a habit, a twinge, a slight lean — that tips the scale. The third is that rationality demands only a reason to choose and not a reason to prefer, so the ass may simply take one pile and be done. These three moves should remain available to the student as materials, but the question must make each of them cost something.

The crux I want the question to isolate is the one the third dissolution creates. If the ass may simply take one pile, then nothing about the agent favors A over B and nothing about A over B favors A; yet her taking A is supposed to be an action rather than a twitch. The question should oblige the student to ask what makes the pick an intentional action when no reason distinguishes it, and whether the possibility of rational choice among equal options can be reconciled with the requirement that intentional action be performed for a reason. To give the question a concrete grip, I want it anchored in a case the student must work through: a hiring committee with two finalists who are exactly equal on every dimension the committee's rubric recognizes, which must nevertheless decide and must afterwards be able to account for its decision. The student should be made to notice that a coin flip appears to answer the committee's practical problem while leaving its action unaccounted for, and to connect that observation back to the ass.

The question should also require the student to assess at least two attempts at reconciliation rather than merely declaring the puzzle dissolved. One is the thesis that an agent can act only under some appearance of the good, so that she must find the chosen option good in some respect even when it is no better than its rival — which raises the question whether the requisite goodness is discovered in the option or conferred on it by the choice. The other is the decision-theoretic thought that the agent's real action is the adoption of a policy, such as randomizing whenever indifferent, a policy that is itself chosen for a reason — which raises the question whether the policy merely relocates the problem to the level of policy selection. The student should see that each such move either reintroduces a reason that does the choosing or concedes that some actions are performed for no reason at all.

Please provide the essay question, the model answer, and the marking scheme. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a first-class answer must make and the specific errors that should cost marks, and it should above all separate answers that merely restate the three dissolutions from answers that recognize the action-theoretic problem those dissolutions leave in their wake. The question should be answerable from the seminar alone, without outside reading and without citations.
