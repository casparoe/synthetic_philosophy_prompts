I am preparing a seminar on social choice theory and film, and I want to use Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men as a case study in the paradox of voting. I am not interested in a plot summary or a textbook recitation. I want a sustained critical reading of the film through the lens of the paradox of voting, understood as the tension between individual votes and collective outcomes. The paradox of voting shows that majority rule can be unstable when preferences are cyclical; the discursive dilemma shows that a similar instability can arise when a group aggregates judgments on interconnected propositions. I want you to use both ideas to ask whether the film vindicates or undermines the aggregative model of collective decision-making, and I want a reading that takes both the film and the theory seriously, without reducing the film to an illustration or the theory to a metaphor.

The film is a jury deliberation, so it is not simply a preference-aggregation problem: the jurors are trying to converge on a judgment of fact, not to register pre-existing rankings. But their deliberation is structured by votes, unanimity rules, and the possibility of deadlock. The jurors make separate judgments about the reliability of the old witness's testimony, the woman's eyesight, the knife, the defendant's alibi, and the meaning of reasonable doubt. Does the film dramatize the discursive dilemma?
