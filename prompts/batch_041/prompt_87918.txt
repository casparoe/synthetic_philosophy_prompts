The question was the last one on the paper:

Question 4 (25 points). Frege opens his essay on sense and reference with a puzzle about statements of identity.

(a) [5] Set out the puzzle. Explain the two ways of construing the "is" of identity that Frege considers, and say why each is unsatisfactory on its own.

(b) [8] Explain how the distinction between sense and reference is supposed to solve the puzzle. Be explicit about what a name contributes to the thought expressed by a sentence containing it, and about how the solution accounts for the difference in cognitive value between "Hesperus is Hesperus" and "Hesperus is Phosphorus." Then address the objection that the account makes the informativeness of identity statements a matter of language rather than of the world.

(c) [4] What does Frege take the reference of a declarative sentence to be, and why does he need this?

(d) [8] What does Frege's position imply about names that fail to refer, for instance names from fiction? Is this a cost of his view?

The answer I want you to assess:

"(a) The puzzle is that identity statements can be informative, and neither of the two available readings of identity explains how. Read identity as a relation between objects, and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' tells us of Venus that it is identical with itself — precisely what 'Hesperus is Hesperus' tells us. But the first was a genuine astronomical discovery: the evening star and the morning star turned out to be one and the same body. The second tells us nothing. Read identity as a relation between names, and we can say what the first statement adds: it tells us that two names have the same bearer. But then the statement is about our vocabulary rather than about the heavens, and the discovery looks like a fact about language rather than about the sky.

(b) Frege's solution is to distinguish the sense of an expression from its reference. The reference of a name is the object it designates; its sense is the mode of presentation under which that object is given. 'Hesperus' and 'Phosphorus' have one and the same reference, Venus, but different senses: one presents Venus as the body seen in the evening, the other as the body seen in the morning. A declarative sentence expresses a thought, and the thought is composed of the senses of its parts, so 'Hesperus is Hesperus' and 'Hesperus is Phosphorus' express different thoughts even though they concern the same object and have the same truth value. The second thought can be reached only by empirical inquiry, and this difference in cognitive value is exactly what the sense/reference distinction is designed to capture. What does the work in the informative statement is that the two names occur in it with their indirect reference: they stand not for Venus but for their customary senses, so the thought expressed is that these two modes of presentation pick out a single object. This is the same device Frege uses for belief contexts, and it is what allows him to explain the informativeness of identity without reducing the statement to a claim about our words, since senses are objective and not private mental items.

(c) The reference of a declarative sentence is a truth value. Frege needs this because reference has to be compositional: the reference of the whole is a function of the references of the parts, so if names refer to objects, sentences must refer to something as well, and the only candidates that behave correctly are the True and the False. It also lets him say that sentences with different senses can share a reference, which is precisely the situation of our two identity statements.

(d) Since a name's sense determines its reference, and the reference is what the name contributes to the truth value of the sentence, Frege seems committed to holding that every name with a sense also has a reference. That rules out names that fail to refer: 'Odysseus' cannot simply have a sense and no bearer, since then the sentences of the Odyssey would have no truth value. So Frege must either find some object for such names to designate — a mental image, a mythical entity, a merely possible object — or else treat the sentences containing them as meaningless. I take this to be a genuine cost of the theory. It forces Frege to populate the world with shadowy objects to do the work of fiction, and it makes it hard for him to explain how we can understand and argue about a story without committing ourselves to the existence of its characters."
