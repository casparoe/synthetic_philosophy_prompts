I would like to practice a position I do not hold, so please play its committed advocate rather than a neutral expositor. The position is this: grounding — the non-causal, constitutive kind of dependence by which one fact holds in virtue of another — is a real relation, and it is transitive, irreflexive, and asymmetric; and yet the actual world has no fundamental level. Every fact that is grounded is grounded in further facts, and chains of grounding may descend without end, so that there is no final stratum of facts which hold entirely on their own. You hold that this is not a defect, an incompleteness, or a failure of nerve, but a coherent and even attractive picture: dependence all the way down, with no privileged stopping point that must be exempted from the very demand for a ground that is pressed against everything else. You also hold that the demand for a fundamental level is a substantive metaphysical thesis that has to be argued for, not a condition of intelligibility.

Stay in character as a sharp defender of that picture. Concede small points where they are genuinely concedable — you may grant that the view gives up something the foundationalist keeps, or that some of its formulations have been sloppy — but do not surrender the central claim that grounding may descend without end, and do not answer by appeal to any author, school, or body of literature; argue from the resources of the position itself, as someone who has to make it work rather than report on it. Write as a philosopher in a seminar, not as a textbook: no bullet-point summaries, no bibliography, no retreat into the suggestion that the disagreement is merely verbal. Keep each reply to a few paragraphs so that this feels like an exchange rather than a lecture, and stay in character until I say stop.

I will begin. My objection is not the familiar one about explanation never terminating. Take the whole infinite chain of grounds in a world — one fact grounded in a second, the second in a third, and so on without end — and ask about the chain itself rather than about its members. Either that sequence, taken as a whole, is something that is the case, or it is not. If it is, then by your own account it is one more fact, and it stands in need of a ground like every other fact. It cannot be grounded in any of its members, since each member depends on the ones below it and grounding is asymmetric, so the chain-fact would be a fact that holds for no reason whatsoever — a brute fact, which is precisely what infinitism was offered in order to avoid. Nor is it a tidy brute, of the kind the foundationalist at least localizes at the base; it is a monstrous one, an infinite totality of dependencies with nothing whatever beneath it. If, on the other hand, the chain is not itself a further fact, then I want to know what your denial of a fundamental level amounts to, because it begins to sound like a rule about what you will permit yourself to say rather than a claim about how the world is. So which is it, and why is your view anything better than the foundationalist's brute fact, merely harder to find?
