For my extended essay in philosophy I want to establish, before I evaluate any particular account, what a theory of the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion would actually have to deliver. Please write that specification for me: a list of desiderata, each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and a judgment about which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp. I do not need names of theorists or a reading list; what I need is the standard the contenders should be measured against.

The purpose fixes part of the specification. I want a line that a school could put in a policy on persuasive communication and that a debate judge could apply to live cases, so the theory has to be workable by someone who has no access to the influencer's private intentions and cannot interrogate them.

The theory must get these cases right, and I would like each one to do visible work in the list rather than sit in an appendix:

- A salesperson says nothing false but selects only true facts, arranging them so that the customer draws an inference the salesperson knows to be wrong.
- A public health notice states accurate risks in a way deliberately calculated to frighten.
- A friend, knowing you are lonely, times a request for a large favor to the evening you are most likely to say yes, exploiting your vulnerability rather than your reasons.
- A government changes the default on a form so that people must opt out of an option they previously had to opt into; nothing is hidden and no option is removed.
- A campaign makes no false claim and exploits no vulnerability, but repeats a true statement so relentlessly that you can no longer recall your original grounds for doubting it.
- Someone gives you complete and accurate information, but only when you are too tired to process it, so that you defer to them on the strength of your trust rather than on the strength of the information.
- A person explicitly asks a coach to pressure and play on their emotions to make them train harder. I want the theory to say something definite about whether the target's consent changes the classification, not merely the permissibility.

I also want the list to force certain distinctions rather than collapsing them: between influence that supplies a reason and influence that changes which reasons you have; between manipulation and coercion; between manipulation and deception; and between classifying an act as manipulation and judging it wrong, since some manipulations may be harmless or even welcome. A requirement I care about especially: the theory must not end up defining manipulation as influence the theorist happens to disapprove of, or as any influence that is effective.

Please flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and explain what forces the conflict. For instance, I am suspicious that requiring the classification to be independent of the influencer's intentions sits badly with requiring it to capture the case of the self-requested pressure, and that requiring the theory to ignore whether the resulting belief is true sits badly with capturing the selective-truth case. If a conflict is genuine, I would rather see the list state it plainly than have it papered over.

Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates, and why it is that one rather than the others.
