I'm trying to get the function debate straight before I commit to a side, and I keep jumping in at the level of arguments when what I actually want first is the spec. So: what does any adequate account of biological function have to deliver? Give me the desiderata -- the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the background results it has to stay consistent with -- each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and a note on which of them are non-negotiable and which are really just a preference of one camp.

The ones I keep bumping into, roughly: malfunction (a heart that can't pump is still "for" pumping, so the account has to make room for a trait failing at its job without smuggling in a designer); novelty (a brand-new trait in the very first generation it shows up, or something that's never been under selection at all -- does it have a function yet, and does your account say so for the right reason rather than by accident?); co-option, where something selected for one role gets repurposed for another; and the byproduct problem -- a trait that's just along for the ride because it's hitched to something beneficial. Plus the grain question: whose function is it anyway -- the gene's, the organ's, the whole organism's -- and can a single account do all three without quietly equivocating.

Two things I want you to add to the list that I haven't seen people bother with. First: whatever the account says has to survive being pointed at things that aren't products of selection at all -- a trait held in place purely by drift, or by genetic hitchhiking, or a structure in an organism we built in a lab and never let evolve. Second: it has to be usable downstream -- if a conservation biologist or a clinician leans on "this structure is dysfunctional," the account should underwrite that without collapsing into "dysfunctional just means we don't happen to like it." Tell me why each of those is a genuine requirement and not just a prejudice dressed up as one.

Then flag the pairs you suspect can't be had together. I have a feeling that making room for malfunction without a designer and letting novel traits count as functional right away pull against each other, but tell me if I'm wrong about that. And finish with which single requirement does the most to rule out the etiological picture on one side and the causal-role picture on the other, since those are the two I'm actualy choosing between. Don't bother scoring every contender, I'll do that myself once I have the list in hand.
