I'm 300 pages into a novel and stuck in a way that has nothing to do with plot. The book is set in a small island country that abolished its parliament and replaced it with a lottocracy: 300 citizens drawn by lot, two-year terms, a real salary, real legislative power, no elections at all. I've got the world built — the induction week, the briefing binders, the lobbyists learning to work a room full of random people, a scene where my protagonist, a retired schoolteacher, gets the letter saying her name came out of the drum — and what I can't decide is whether I'm writing a fragile, genuinely better form of government or a slow-motion disaster with better manners. Its not that I need a happy ending, I don't. It's that every time I go looking for the argument that would tell me which of those two books I'm writing, I find the same two camps shouting past each other, and it's been that way since I first got interested in this stuff years ago.

Camp one says sortition is simply more democratic than elections: no donors, no safe seats, no career incentives, an assembly that actually looks like the country, and — the bit they lean on hardest — a big random sample of ordinary people who deliberate for a couple of weeks will land closer to the common good than a self-selected political class ever does, by the old Condorcet jury-theorem logic that a large enough crowd of mediocre but better-than-coin-flip judges beats any one of them. Camp two says that's a category error in a nice suit. A sample can be representative, fine, but it was never authorized by anybody, nobody can vote it out, and it's consent rather than good decisions that made the old system democratic in the first place. Then they throw the same examples at each other with opposite morals: the Irish experiments with randomly picked citizens' bodies, the ones that fed into the marriage and abortion referendums, get cited by camp one as proof it works and by camp two as proof it doesn't, since those bodies were only ever advisory and the actual parliament took the heat. And underneath the big fight there's alot of smaller unresolved ones I keep tripping over — who writes the briefing materials the assembly reads (somebody has to, and that somebody has an agenda); whether the people who say yes when the lot falls on them are a different sort of person from the ones who say no; whether deliberation sharpens a group's judgment or just makes it more confident and more correlated, which if true would quietly gut the jury-theorem argument that got us here; and whether an assembly that passes law is better or worse than one that only recommends.

What I want from you isn't a verdict on sortition, it's a diagnosis of the fight. Is this a verbal dispute about the word 'democratic' — one side meaning authorized-by-the-governed, the other meaning likely-to-produce-good-decisions — or is it two camps answering two different questions (how do we get better policy? versus how do we get decisions people have reason to accept even when they lose?) under a single heading? Is it a hidden empirical bet that better data could settle, or a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether a decision is legitimated by who made it or by what it says, which no evidence will ever touch? My own hunch, and tell me if it's lazy, is that it's mostly the input/output equivocation plus an empirical bet about deliberation that nobody has run the right experiment for, and that the reason nobody moves is that each side's argument is only fatal to a version of the other side that its holder doesn't endorse. So diagnose it, say what would count as progress (a distinction, an experiment, a formal result about when the jury theorem survives deliberation, a change of question), and be honest about whether this one is ill-posed or just hard — because I can write a novel about a question that has no answer, but I can't write one about a question where nobody can say what an answer would even look like.
