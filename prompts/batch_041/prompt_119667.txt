I'm writing a parody for a zine and I could use a hand with it. The form is an advice column — the kind with short reader letters and a columnist replying under each one — and the columnist is a retired actuary who now sizes everything, from his portfolio to his holiday plans, by the Kelly criterion. Call it 'Ask the Growth-Optimalist'. Around 700 words, four letters with four replies, deadpan the whole way, no winking at the reader. It's partly a reaction to a group chat that's gone all-in on 'geometric rationality' lately, so the whole thing should feel like it's written by someone who actually lives this way.

The comedy has to come from him being right rather than from him being a crank — someone who knows the criterion should laugh rather than wince. So each letter should poke at a real feature of it:

- A guy whose brother doubled his money at roulette and now calls him a coward. His reply should turn on the difference between the average over many parallel copies of you and the average over one life lived forward, in that order, and he should be genuinely, boringly unimpressed by the brother.
- Someone who's been offered an even-money bet that wins 50% of the stake or loses 40% of it, and has been told it's obviously good because the expected value is positive. He should say what actually happens to a person who takes that bet over and over at full stake, then say what fraction he'd risk instead — and make him show the working, out loud, on the page.
- A reader who's an economist, saying the whole thing is just risk-aversion with extra steps and that maximising the geometric mean was never forced on anyone by the axioms of rational choice. He should concede the technical point (it's true) and then explain why he doesn't care — that's the interesting bit, so don't rush it.
- His own husband asking whether they should have a second kid. He stalls. The criterion is a theorem about long sequences of repeated multiplicative bets, and a life is one run, not a sequence, and he knows it. Put this one last and don't let the piece resolve it — no warm final paragraph.

He can be smug but he can't be wrong, and he can't quote anybody or cite papers, and definately no invented citations. No jargon for its own sake: someone who's never heard of Kelly should still follow it, and someone who has shouldn't catch a single mistake. He's also not allowed to drift into being a normal financial adviser halfway through, unless that's the joke, which it isn't.

Then finish on a seperate line beginning 'Verdict:' that says in one sentence what the piece is really claiming — that bit out of character, just for me.
