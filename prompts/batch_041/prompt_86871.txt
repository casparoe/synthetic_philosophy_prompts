I am the policy-integrity officer at a state benefits agency. Our manual runs to roughly 240 rules and is being encoded into an automated decision engine, and we have a recurring problem that neither the analysts nor the engine's authors can handle: rules that refer to themselves, or to one another in a loop. A rule may refer to facts settled outside the manual (filing dates, amounts, whether a form was submitted), to whether another rule is in force, and to whether another rule applies to a given case — and sometimes, directly or through a chain, to its own applicability. Twice in the past year we have adopted a rule whose applicability no one could settle by reading it, and both times it surfaced at a hearing. Amendments are adopted quarterly, and each cycle turns up a few more.

Write the procedure our analysts should follow. It will be run at two points: when a rule is drafted or amended, and when the engine flags an existing rule as unsettled. The person running it is a policy analyst with no training in logic, working under a deadline of about an hour per rule, whose output goes into a worksheet that the general counsel's office reviews and that the engine consumes directly. The manual itself must never cite a philosopher; the checklist has to stand on its own.

Specifically:

1. Give a numbered procedure, in order, saying at each step what counts as a sufficient answer and what to do when no sufficient answer is available. The first step should say how the analyst converts the as-adopted English of a rule into the dependency structure the rest of the procedure needs, and what to do with the drafting ambiguities that will not resolve.

2. Fix a closed vocabulary of at most six status labels, each with a one-line definition and a prescribed downstream action (enforce; enforce with a stated default; suspend; return to the drafting committee; escalate). No free text in the label field.

3. Ground the procedure. State plainly which machinery you are using — for instance, building up from the settled facts until nothing further changes; treating no-stable-answer as a third status alongside applies and does-not-apply; permitting some rules to be both; or holding that a rule's status is fixed by an ongoing process of revision rather than by any single stable assignment — and say why that choice is defensible for an administrative manual rather than a logic seminar. Where a verdict would come out differently under the main alternative, say so and tag it [contested].

4. Address whether these difficulties arise for rules at all, given that rules prescribe rather than describe. If using any of this material requires translating "rule R applies to case C" into something truth-apt, say how you do the translation and what it costs.

5. Say how the procedure behaves when the manual is amended: whether a label can flip, what happens to actions already taken under the old label, and whether an ungrounded rule can be made grounded by later drafting.

Then run the procedure on the following as-adopted rules, giving the label, the action, and your reasoning:

- R3: "A claim is denied if it is filed more than thirty days after the notice is mailed." (Assume the facts of a specific case are settled.)
- R14: "This rule governs every rule in this manual that does not govern itself." (This was the centerpiece of the last amendment; the drafting committee regards it as the clause that keeps the manual tidy.)
- R22: "This rule is in force."
- R30: "This rule governs every rule whose applicability this manual does not determine."
- R5: "Rule 5 governs every matter governed by Rule 6." Together with R6: "Rule 6 governs every matter governed by Rule 5."

Present the worked cases as a table with columns for the rule, the label, the action, whether the verdict rests on the text of the rule or on your theoretical choice, and whether the verdict is contested. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the drafting committee will not want, and say plainly that it does.

Close with a section on where the procedure cannot decide and a person must: the trigger, who that person is, what they are being asked to decide, and whether the question before them is one of fact or one of policy. Include the points where the procedure will misfire and why.

Throughout, keep separate what is settled among people who work on truth and self-reference from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.

End with a single sentence beginning "Summary:" that states what the procedure is for and the single largest limitation on it.
