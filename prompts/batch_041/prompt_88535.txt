I'm taking a seminar on free will and moral responsibility, and a friend of mine in the department — a graduate student who is usually reliable on these things — gave me his one-paragraph account of Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment." He puts it like this: the essay's whole point is that determinism is simply irrelevant to moral responsibility. Whether someone is responsible is not a metaphysical fact about whether he could have done otherwise; it is a question about whether it is appropriate to hold him responsible, and that is settled entirely by our practices of blame and praise. He labels the hard determinist the "pessimist" and the defender of these practices the "optimist," and says Strawson comes down on the optimist's side. He concedes that if determinism were true, no one would be ultimately responsible in the deep sense the pessimist wants, but says the concession costs nothing, because the reactive attitudes are so deeply woven into our nature that we simply cannot give them up — we are psychologically incapable of it — and what we cannot abandon we may as well keep. So, on his reading, the essay is really a defense of compatibilism: it shows that determinism and moral responsibility are compatible, just not for the usual reasons.

I suspect this is roughly the shape of the essay but that at least one thing in it is off, and I cannot put my finger on what. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and sort it into what Strawson actually holds, what is a defensible simplification, and what he would flatly reject? I am especially unsure about the last move — the idea that we keep the reactive attitudes because we cannot help having them — and about whether it is fair to file the essay under "compatibilism" at all, given how Strawson treats the optimist. And if part of the summary is wrong, tell me whether the friend's concluding claim still stands on the corrected version, or whether it collapses along with the errors.
