I run a reading group on liberalism and empire and I need a handout piece for it, so take this one slowly rather than going with the first framing that comes to mind. You know Mill's corpus and the East India Company material inside out — use that.

What I want is a dialogue. Late 1850s, Mill's office at East India House. The India correspondence is his actual day job; the salary from it is what pays for everything else he writes. By now he's also worked out the liberty argument — the only justification for interfering with someone's freedom is to stop them harming others, and the doctrine is meant for people old enough to run their own lives, so it doesn't cover children, and in his view it doesn't yet cover whole societies he thinks aren't there yet either. For those, despotism can be legitimate as long as it's aimed at their improvement, and he's convinced British rule in India is that kind of government: a trust, meant to carry them along until they can govern themselves.

Across from him sits a younger utilitarian, just back from India, who doesnt work for the Company and has nothing riding on the answer either way. He's read the liberty argument and thinks Mill is either fooling himself or cheating, and he wants to make that case using Mill's own tools — the harm principle, the utilitarian arithmetic, the idea that a trust can be betrayed — rather than denouncing the empire from outside in terms Mill wouldn't accept. Hard rule for him: no anachronisms. He's a man of 1858, he argues in 1858 vocabulary, he can't reach for concepts that don't exist yet.

Neither of them is a dolt. Mill gets his best real answers, including the one about what happens if the British simply pack up and leave. The critic lands at least one blow Mill can't fully absorb, and Mill lands at least one the critic can't. No tidy synthesis at the end, and nobody wins by fiat.

Before you start, work out quietly — don't show me — the three strongest moves each of them has and the exact point where the two of them actually collide, so they're answering each other instead of talking past each other.

Format: exactly five exchanges, i.e. five speeches each, alternating, and not one more. Each speech a short paragraph at most. No scene-setting at the top, no narrator, no wrap-up at the end — just the exchanges, with the barest speech tags. And don't have Mill recite lines from his own books; I want the arguments in their own words, especially the part thats hardest for him.
