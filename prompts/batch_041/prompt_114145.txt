I'm putting together some seminar material for a masters epistemology module and I want a dialogue my students can really get thier teeth into. Two speakers, both sharp, neither one a strawman. Here's the setup.

A is the enthusiast. They think Aumann's agreement theorem is a deep result about the limits of rational disagreement: if two agents share a common prior, are rational, and have common knowledge of each other's credences, then they simply cannot agree to disagree — any persistent disagreement has to be traceable to one of those assumptions failing. B is the sceptic. They think the theorem is a triviality dressed up as a discovery — a consistency constraint internal to one particular formal framework, which tells us next to nothing about how actual people actually disagree, because the assumptions (the common prior especially) are basically never met, so the whole thing reads more like a reductio of the idealisation than a fact about rationality.

I definately want them to actually scrap. A few threads I'd like them to pull on, if you can work them in without it feeling like a checklist:

- whether the theorem is a real constraint on rationality or basically a tautology of the framework — B should push that if you build a common prior plus common knowledge of rationality into the model then of course agreement falls out, and A should have a decent comeback (maybe something about how that's like saying Bayes' theorem is uninteresting because nobody's a perfect Bayesian)
- a case where the thing they disagree about is itself about the other person's credence — some sort of self-referential proposition — and whether the proof even goes through there
- whether the result has anything to say about disagreements over values or over what to do, as opposed to straight credences
- whether stubborn disagreement between people who seem to share all the evidence is best explained by hidden private information, by different priors, or by something the framework just can't capture

Please don't have them hug it out at the end — I want the disagreement still live when the dialogue stops, and I don't want it to read like a lecture. They should respond to each other's actual points, pick up on specifics, concede where they have to. British spelling throughout. Keep it to around 900 words.
