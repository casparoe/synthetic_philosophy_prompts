I am a graduate student in philosophy of computation, and a dispute from a recent seminar has left me unsure how to adjudicate the matter. One classmate maintains that the Church–Turing thesis is best understood as an explication of the pre-theoretic notion of an effective procedure, so that it is not the sort of claim that any physical discovery could overturn. Another maintains that it is a substantive, defeasible empirical hypothesis about what can be computed in the physical world, and thus that a single counterexample would refute it. To make the disagreement concrete, imagine that physicists present us with a device—call it a Dedekind dial—that is stipulated to be physically constructible in a universe whose spacetime structure allows an infinite sequence of operations to be completed in a finite time. The dial returns a real number whose binary expansion encodes the halting status of every Turing machine; no Turing machine can compute that number. The two of them disagree about what, if anything, this would show about the Church–Turing thesis.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers of computation, one defending the thesis as an explication of effective calculability and therefore as immune to the dial, the other defending it as a substantive empirical claim about physical computation and therefore as vulnerable to the dial. Both characters should be equally competent, and neither should be a strawman; they should engage with each other's actual arguments rather than talk past each other. In the course of the dialogue, have them address whether the distinction between the thesis as a definitional claim and as an empirical claim is itself stable, and whether the Dedekind dial would count as a computation or merely as a physical process that yields a result. The dialogue should be in prose, not a list of talking points.
