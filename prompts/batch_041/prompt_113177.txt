I teach a course pairing science fiction with the philosophy of social science, and I want a reading of Asimov's Foundation through the lens of cliodynamics — the research program, associated with Peter Turchin, that treats history as a quantitative science of long-run social dynamics. For this reading, take the theory's core to be the structural-demographic account of "secular cycles": societies oscillate over a few centuries between expansion and breakdown, driven by popular immiseration (population growth outrunning resources), elite overproduction (more elite aspirants than positions, generating counter-elites and intra-elite conflict), and the fiscal strain of the state, with the theory claiming at most phase-level, probabilistic prediction — it can say a society is ripe for crisis, not which spark ignites it.

Read the novel against that theory. Where is psychohistory simply structural-demographic theory in fictional dress, and where does Asimov want something the theory cannot supply? Is the Mule the theory's own nightmare — the singular, exogenous event that voids statistical prediction — or is he exactly the kind of counter-elite that elite overproduction anticipates in a crisis phase? Does the Plan's indifference to individuals, together with its assumption that psychohistory works only on a population ignorant of its own predictions, express a real consequence of a science of history (reflexivity included), or a technocratic fantasy about a hidden elite — the Second Foundation — that the theory's own elite dynamics should teach us to distrust? And where does the novel resist the reading entirely? I want a reading faithful in both directions, written in the register of philosophy of social science — covering laws, holism versus individualism, prediction versus postdiction — ending with what the fiction supplies that the journal articles do not.
