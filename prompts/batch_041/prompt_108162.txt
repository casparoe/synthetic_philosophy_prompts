I teach a graduate seminar in decision theory and formal epistemology — sixteen students, a single ninety-minute session — and I want to introduce the unit on growth-optimal betting with a runnable activity rather than a proof. The idea I want them to feel is the one that motivates what is now often called geometric rationality: that when a quantity compounds multiplicatively, the rational objective is the expected logarithm of that quantity rather than its expected value, and that this is a claim about rationality itself and not merely a piece of risk management.

Design me an in-class game that produces this as a felt result. I want the students, by the end, to have run into the following in their own outcomes, before I name any of it:

- that a bet with positive expected arithmetic value can nonetheless be a losing proposition once it is repeated;
- that the growth-optimal fraction of one's bankroll lies strictly between zero and one, and that the penalty for over-betting is far more severe than the penalty for under-betting — an asymmetry they should feel rather than be told;
- the gap between the average outcome across many players and the typical trajectory of a single player who keeps playing (the ensemble-average versus time-average distinction, i.e. the non-ergodicity of the multiplicative process);
- that the arithmetic mean of a round's multiplier exceeds its geometric mean by a term that grows with the variance (volatility drag).

Constraints and requests. The setting is a seminar room with a whiteboard and a projector; no real money, only chips or tallies, and nothing requiring software beyond a spreadsheet I can drive from the front. I want the students themselves to choose how much of their bankroll to stake each round, privately at first, so that the choices diverge and the divergence does the teaching. Please specify the coin or payoff structure (a favourable but genuinely uncertain event with a substantial multiplicative swing), the number of rounds, the starting bankroll, how individual and group totals are displayed, and a timeline that fits ninety minutes including the debrief. They should not hear the standard name for the rule until the debrief.

I want at least one inserted complication that a student who has merely memorised the slogan will mishandle: for instance, a single mid-game contract or side-offer that is favourable on the arithmetic reckoning but unfavourable on the geometric one (or the reverse, keyed to the player's current bankroll), so that the criterion has to be applied rather than recited. Tell me how to price it so that the two verdicts genuinely come apart, and what the correct move is.

Then give me the debrief: a mapping from specific moments in the game onto the four points above, together with an honest account of the further inference the students will be tempted to draw — that this is only about gamblers, or only about caution — and how I should connect it to the wider claim about rationality in multiplicative environments. Please also say what to do about the students who will want to know the true probability of the favourable event and how confident they should be in it, since in practice the growth-optimal stake is over-bet when the edge is over-estimated; the debrief should gesture at fractional staking without turning into a lecture on estimation.

Finally: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — including the failure modes where the randomness does not cooperate in the time available, where the group converges on a single copied stake size, and where students treat the whole exercise as gambling rather than as a decision problem. And be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.
