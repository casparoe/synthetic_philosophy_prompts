I am trying to get clear on middle knowledge, and I would like you to work through a single constructed example rather than survey the literature.

By middle knowledge I mean the knowledge God is said to have of what each possible free creature would freely do in any circumstances in which it might be placed — knowledge supposedly prior to any decision of God's about which creatures and circumstances to actualize (the position usually labelled Molinism). The propositions in question are standardly called counterfactuals of creaturely freedom: for a given creature and a given set of circumstances, the proposition that the creature, if placed in those circumstances, would freely do such-and-such.

Construct one case of this kind and develop it.

First, the example. Choose a single creature and two circumstances that differ only in a feature that is morally and motivationally inert — a difference no one could take to supply a motive. Describe the creature's character, beliefs, and reasons closely enough that we can see the situation from the inside, then stipulate the two counterfactuals: in the first circumstance the creature would freely do A, in the second it would freely refrain from A. The case must be psychologically credible; it should not be a coin flip, and the difference between the circumstances must not be doing any explanatory work. You may take one of the two circumstances to be one that never obtains.

Second, what the example forces. If the truth of such a counterfactual must be grounded in something, take the candidates in turn — the creature's nature or character, the circumstances, God's will or decree, and the counterfactual as an ungrounded brute fact — and say what each does to your case. For each, say what becomes of the creature's freedom and what becomes of God's responsibility for what the creature does.

Third, the issue itself. State in a sentence or two the conceptual problem your example exposes. I am not asking about the problem of evil, nor about whether middle knowledge is a useful device for reconciling providence with freedom, but about what makes these counterfactuals true at all, and about why the example makes it difficult to say that the creature's character settles them.

Fourth, a verdict. Say whether the example shows the doctrine to be incoherent or merely incomplete, and if incomplete, what a defender of it would still have to supply.
