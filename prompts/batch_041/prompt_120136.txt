I chair the education committee of a national association of history teachers, and at next month's meeting we are to adopt written guidance for members on how to handle, in the classroom, the question of who was responsible for the outbreak of the First World War. The question has been reopened by the argument Fritz Fischer advanced in 1961 — that Germany's leaders deliberately sought a war in order to make Germany a world power — and by the reply of his critics that no single government controlled the crisis of July 1914 and that responsibility was broadly shared. Our members are split three ways. Some hold that a verdict is owed, and that refusing to give one is a failure of nerve. Some hold that no verdict is defensible, both because we cannot know what would have happened otherwise and because a nation is not the kind of thing that can intend, decide or be blamed. Others want whatever responsibility there was apportioned among the powers in proportion to their causal contribution, though one member objects that responsibility for a collective outcome cannot be cut into shares in that way at all. Please draft the decision memo I shall circulate before the vote. It should come down on one side rather than split the difference, recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and treat the strongest of the positions above as it deserves rather than as a straw man. Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with the recommendation first and no preamble or closing.
