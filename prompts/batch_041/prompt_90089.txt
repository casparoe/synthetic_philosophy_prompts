In the United States, a single federal program regulates drinking-water contaminants in two steps, and I have come to suspect the two steps presuppose incompatible things about vagueness. First the agency fixes a health-based goal: the concentration at which no adverse health effect is anticipated, allowing an added margin of safety. That goal is expressly non-enforceable; it is set without regard to whether the substance can be detected at that level or removed by any available treatment; for microbial contaminants it is set at zero. Only then does the agency fix the enforceable standard, which is to be as close to the goal as is feasible — feasibility being a matter of detection limits, treatment technology, and cost. Sometimes, instead of any number, the rule mandates a treatment process. Sometimes it fixes a number that is not a violation limit at all but an "action level" that triggers monitoring and corrosion-control duties. The enforceable figure has moved repeatedly over the decades as instruments and treatment improved, while the health-based goal stayed where it was.

Everything downstream is a cliff. A reading a hair above the standard triggers mandatory treatment, public notice, and legally binding obligations on a water system; a reading a hair below triggers nothing. The program's own monitoring rules concede that measurements carry error bars wide enough that the two readings may be the same water. And courts will not second-guess the choice of number: whether the standard is 4 or 5 parts per billion is treated as a technical judgment within the agency's discretion, not as a claim that can be assessed for truth.

The same legal system treats vagueness as a defect elsewhere: a criminal statute that fails to give fair notice and invites arbitrary enforcement is struck down. In other areas — reasonable care, the best interests of the child — the law deliberately declines to stipulate any line and decides case by case. So we stipulate hard boundaries where the science cannot find one, refuse to stipulate them where it easily could, and treat vagueness as fatal in one setting and tolerable or even virtuous in another.

What does this cluster presuppose about vagueness? Does the enforceable standard presuppose that there is a sharp fact about the highest safe concentration, which the number approximates — and if so, how can facts about our instruments and our budgets enter into the approximation? Or does it presuppose that there is no boundary and that the law manufactures one, in which case why does the practice speak in the language of health and evidence rather than of decision? Does the pairing of a zero health goal with a nonzero enforceable figure presuppose a distinction between a vague natural predicate and a sharp stipulated one, with the sorites applying only to the former? What does the margin of safety commit the agency to — that the boundary is unknown, or that it is nonexistent?

Reconstruct the implicit theory, then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits, and say where it is underdetermined: would an epistemicist, a supervaluationist, and a degree theorist all be able to endorse it, or does it quietly rule some of them out? Finally, say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. If we took seriously that the number is a stipulated decision rule rather than a discovery, would judicial deference to the agency's expertise survive? Would the practice's "evidence-based" rhetoric have to go? Should we expect margins, sliding scales, and tolerance ranges in enforcement instead of cliffs — and does the program's own resort to treatment techniques and action levels already betray that it half-knows the line is a decision rather than a finding?
