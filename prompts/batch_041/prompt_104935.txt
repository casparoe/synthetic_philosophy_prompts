I am a retired microbial ecologist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy of history and philosophy of biology, and I keep returning to H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds. I want a philosophical reading of the novel, not a summary. Adopt the role of a philosopher of history and of the natural sciences who is also a careful literary critic, and begin by brainstorming the possible ways one might describe the Martians' defeat by terrestrial microorganisms: as a contingent accident, as a case of environmental determinism, as an instance of non-human historical agency, or as something else. Then give me your considered reading.

The novel, as I understand it, sets up a human drama of empire, technology, and invasion, and then resolves it through a cause that has no intentions, no plans, and no interest in the outcome. I have been calling this 'the bacteriological coup' and using it as a test case for a distinction I am trying to draw: between a factor that is merely causally efficacious in history and a factor that is a historical actor. I suspect the distinction matters, but I cannot say precisely where the line falls. Does the novel dramatize a genuine form of non-human agency, or does it expose the very idea of non-human agency as a confusion? Does it vindicate a deterministic picture in which human purposes are epiphenomenal, or does it instead show that history is settled by contingency, and that our narratives of agency are retrospective impositions?

Please address the following, in whatever order makes for the strongest argument:

1. Where in the novel does the lens of non-human causation illuminate a scene or a character's choice, and where does the novel resist that lens? Be concrete about plot and scenes, but do not quote the text.

2. What does the novel get right or wrong about the difference between causal efficacy, historical significance, and agency? Is a germ that kills an invader an 'actor' in any philosophically respectable sense, or only a cause? What would be lost, and what would be gained, by extending the term 'actor' to germs, climates, and volcanoes? I am especially interested in what I call the 'agency inflation' problem: if a virus is an actor, why not a drought, a falling asteroid, or a shift in the jet stream? What principled stopping point, if any, is available?

3. The perils of environmental determinism: does the novel's ending invite us to explain historical outcomes by biological or environmental factors in a way that flattens human responsibility, or does it use non-human causation precisely to dramatize the limits of human control? How should we read the ending so as not to slide from 'non-human causes mattered' to 'human intentions did not'?

4. Finally, what does the novel add to the philosophical debate that a straightforward argument about non-human agency and environmental determinism could not? If the novel is making a philosophical point, state it as clearly as you can; if it is evading one, say so.

Think carefully and take a position. I am not asking for a survey of what critics have said, and I do not want citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; I want your own reasoning. Be explicit about your assumptions, define any terms you introduce, and where you are uncertain, say what would resolve the uncertainty. Aim for a polished, argumentative essay of the sort a philosopher would write for a scientifically literate but non-specialist audience.
