Illusionists deny that there is any such thing as phenomenal consciousness — no inner show, no qualia, nothing that it is like to be you — and they hold that the brain merely represents itself as having these things. Since neuroscience has found no place for experience in the physical world, the honest conclusion is that the felt quality of our mental lives is a kind of conjuring trick: the audience believes that something has happened on the stage, but nothing has. Illusionism is therefore a form of eliminativism, and like other eliminativisms it recommends that we eventually stop using the vocabulary of experience altogether: once we see that pain picks out nothing over and above certain functional and behavioral facts, we can drop the word. The hard problem of consciousness, on this account, is simply the problem of explaining why human beings are so attached to the idea that they are conscious — what illusionists call the meta-problem — and once we have a good empirical story about why people say and believe these things, the belief that there is something it is like to be us is shown to be false, and the last obstacle to a fully physical account of the mind is removed.
