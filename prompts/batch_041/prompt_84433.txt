I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years now, and one thing that has started to bother me is how small the joke canon in that orbit is — Bayes, paperclips, the least convenient possible world — repeated until they stopped being funny to anyone who actually knows the material. So I'm putting together a small collection of philosophy jokes that land specifically with people who know the underlying problem, and I need one about the St. Petersburg paradox.

Here's the shape I want. The setup should be recognizably the paradox: a game whose payout doubles with each round, an expected value that diverges, an entry fee that no sane person would pay. But the punchline has to turn on something real about the structure rather than on math being hard or economists being strange. In particular, I want it to exploit the fact that the divergence is carried entirely by outcomes of vanishingly small probability, so that the expected value and the typical result sit on completely different scales and never actually meet. I'd also be pleased if it brushed up against the Pascal's-mugging-style worry about unbounded payoffs — the sense that a lottery with infinite expected value and a threat with unbounded disvalue ought to receive symmetric treatment and conspicuously don't. Pick a setting and commit to it: a casino with finite reserves, a secondary market in tickets, a meetup where someone tries to sell a seat at the table, whatever strikes you as funniest. No character should explain the paradox to the reader; if the joke needs a footnote to land, it has already failed.

Keep the joke itself to 100–150 words, then add two or three sentences telling me exactly which piece of the paradox the punchline is leaning on, so I can tell whether the thing is load-bearing or merely decorative. One constraint: don't let the diminishing-marginal-utility resolution be the punchline. It's the first thing everyone reaches for, and at this point it is not funny.
