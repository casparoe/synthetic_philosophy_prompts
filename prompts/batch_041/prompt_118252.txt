I'm covering for a colleague next week at a physics department journal club — audience is eight or nine condensed-matter and astro grad students, sharp, none of them has read any philosophy of science. Topic: Humeanism vs anti-Humeanism about laws of nature. Don't explain the debate to me, plan how I teach it in fifty minutes with a whiteboard.

What order do the pieces have to go in so the Humean move feels like a live option rather than obviously silly? They'll walk in assuming 'the laws of nature' just means the Standard Model plus GR, and that anyone who says laws are mere summaries of what happens is saying it's all a coincidence. I want them to feel the pull of both sides, so the sequence has to get them to the problem the regularity view has with true-but-accidental universals, then the best-system reply and its awkward question of who decides what counts as simple, then the anti-Humean comeback that laws have to be prior to the facts they govern or they explain nothing (do the facts make the laws, or the laws the facts?), plus the older worry that if lawhood is an extra ingredient in a regularity we could never tell which regularities have it. And if someone raises entanglement as a problem for Humean supervenience, is that a real objection or a red herring — decide and tell me where it goes.

What single example should carry the whole thing? I don't want a philosophers toy — I want something from their own physics that they already feel is a law and can't say why it is one and that all the coins in my pocket are silver isn't. Tell me why your pick works and what a tempting-but-wrong pick would smuggle in.

Where exactly do I head off each misconception, what's one closing question that shows they got the distinction and not just the labels, and what do I openly skip?
