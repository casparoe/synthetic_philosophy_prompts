Ok so I'm 74, I did 38 years as an analytical chemist, and since I retired I've been working through philosphy of science on my own, so bear with me if I use the terms a bit loosely. What I'd like is a poem about the three main stories people tell about what a probability actually *is* -- the long-run frequency one, the propensity/disposition one, and the personal-credence one. Here's my constraint, and it's the whole point: I want a refrain line that comes back in every stanza, but by the end it has to be doing a different job each time, because what keeps bothering me is that the numbers and the axioms look exactly the same whichever story you tell about what they're tracking. Give the frequency stanza the reference-class headache (fine, 1 in 6 -- but 1 in 6 of *what* population, and who gets to pick it). Give the propensity stanza the single-case problem (a 70% chance of rain, and then it rains or it doesn't -- what was the 70% a property *of*). Let the credence stanza start out sounding reasonable and a bit smug and then get quietly undermined, don't just declare it wrong. Then a last stanza that holds all three at once without picking a winner. No more than 25 lines, don't name-drop philosophers to do the work the images should be doing, and just give me the poem -- no preamble, and seperate notes after are not needed. If a couple of the lines can make a scientist wince a little less at the poetry, even better.
