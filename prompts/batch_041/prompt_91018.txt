I've been rereading Fear and Trembling and I want to try it out on a real case instead of just Abraham. Take Edward Snowden — swore an oath, broke it, dumped a pile of classified NSA documents, then went public saying he did it for a higher cause. Run him through Kierkegaard's idea that the ethical can be teleologically suspended: the single individual, as higher than the universal, sets aside the moral law for the sake of something above it. I already get the standard contrast — the tragic hero (Agamemnon killing Iphigenia so the fleet can sail, Brutus condemning his own sons) sacrifices inside the ethical, for a public and universal cause, and we can understand and even honor him; the knight of faith is private, incommensurable, silent, and can't be mediated or explained to anyone. So which one is Snowden, and be careful with the mapping — what's the actual telos, is it public or private, is what he did comprensible or absurd, and does his going public with his reasons already disqualify him from being a knight of faith? Then turn it around: what does the case do to Kierkegaard? In a world where every Abraham has to post a manifesto and sit for interviews, is the knight of faith even possible anymore, or does the demand to justify yourself in public collapse the whole suspension back into ordinary civil disobedience? And if Kierkegaard admits we can't tell the knight of faith apart from a madman — the difference being faith and not reason — does that make the teleological suspension useless (any fanatic can claim one) or does it actually capture something true about Snowden that the law and the ethics can't reach? I want the analysis running in both directions, not just the analogy.
