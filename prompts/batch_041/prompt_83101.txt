My friend and I spent most of a long drive arguing about how far consciousness reaches into the animal kingdom, and by the end the disagreement had shifted from verdicts to evidence: we both think a crab and a bee are live possibilities and a sea sponge is not, but we cannot agree on what a good marker of phenomenal consciousness in a non-human animal even looks like. He holds what he calls the cortical-integration line. On his view, a creature counts only if it has something like a single re-entrant, globally integrating system of the sort vertebrates use, and he treats everything short of that — nociceptors, withdrawal reflexes, opioid signalling, learned avoidance — as machinery that could in principle run with nobody home. I think that standard is doing covert work: it converts the question of whether consciousness is widespread into the question of whether the vertebrate solution is the only possible solution, which is precisely what is at issue. We ended the drive without settling anything, and I would like a more disciplined version of the dispute than either of us managed.

Here is what I want from you. Take the candidate markers below and sort them into tiers — S, A, B, C, D, as many as you need — by how much evidential weight each should carry for the claim that a given animal is phenomenally conscious. Weight here means how far a confirmed instance of the marker should move a reasonable prior, not how easy the marker is to test, how well it plays in policy debates, or how much we would like it to be decisive: (1) nociceptors together with endogenous opioid signalling; (2) withdrawal responses that survive decerebration and are modulated by noxious input as such; (3) conditioned place avoidance acquired from a single noxious episode; (4) motivational trade-offs, where an animal accepts a noxious cost to secure something it wants or pays a price to escape a noxious state; (5) self-administration of an analgesic; (6) open-ended associative learning, in which arbitrary novel cues become linked to aversive outcomes in ways that cannot be read off innate releasing mechanisms; (7) metacognitive sensitivity, such as declining a difficult discrimination while accepting an easy one; (8) sleep architecture with alternating activated and quiet phases; (9) signs of affective state that generalise across otherwise unrelated kinds of reward and punishment. For each marker, give the tier, a one-sentence justification, and — the part I care about most — whether its evidential value stays constant across a vertebrate, an arthropod and a cephalopod, or whether it should be discounted once we leave the vertebrates.

Then close the loop on the original fight. Tell me which of these markers my friend's cortical-integration line would have to concede as evidentially relevant in order to avoid begging the question, which ones it can legitimately discount, and which single marker, if it turned out to be present in insects but absent in fish, would most destabilise my side of the argument. If you can attach a rough number to each tier expressing its weight relative to the top tier, that would help; I want something I can use to actually revise credences, not a survey of the literature or a reading list.
