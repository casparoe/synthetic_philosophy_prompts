I am a backend and distributed-systems engineer with roughly a decade of professional experience and no formal training in philosophy. My working vocabulary is the vocabulary of version control and API lifecycle management: commits and history rewriting, release tags and semantic versioning, deprecation windows, breaking changes, forks and vendored dependencies, interface contracts, technical debt, and dependency graphs. I have absorbed, secondhand and at a lay level, a cluster of claims about nations that I take to run roughly as follows: that the nation as a political form is a modern construction rather than an ancient inheritance; that a great many customs, ceremonies, and symbols presented as immemorial were in fact manufactured or drastically remodelled within the last two centuries; that a nation is imagined in a specific technical sense, namely that its members conceive of themselves as a community of mutual obligation despite never meeting most of their fellows; and that cheap print and standardized vernaculars supplied the substrate on which that conception became available at scale. I cannot reliably attribute these claims, and I do not want a reading list or citations; I care only about the content, so correct my summary if it distorts the position before you begin translating it.

Restate that position in my vocabulary, claim by claim, as faithfully as the vocabulary permits. Map each central claim onto the nearest thing in my world: a repository and its history; a released artifact and its version string; an interface contract and its backwards-compatibility guarantees; a fork; a rewrite shipped under a minor version bump; documentation written after the fact and thereafter treated as specification; a widely depended-on library whose thousands of consuming teams never meet and never coordinate. For each mapping, tell me whether it is tight, whether it requires distorting the original claim to fit, and what precisely gets distorted.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually want. For each claim, state what survives translation, what survives only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I am suspicious of four places in particular, and I want your honest verdict on each: (i) the emotional and motivational dimension, in which people are moved to sacrifice for a community they will never see; (ii) participant belief and sincerity, since my framework has no natural slot for whether the people maintaining the artifact actually believe the changelog they are maintaining; (iii) the normative claim that members owe one another something, which in my world would be at most a service-level agreement and at worst a convention; and (iv) temporality, since my framework treats history as instrumentally relevant to the present artifact, whereas the position I am describing seems to treat the past as itself a present-day reason.

Finally, tell me whether the engineering frame forces an answer to the question of how old nations are. Does it push the question toward a versioning and archaeology dispute — an empirical matter of when the artifact was actually built — or does it reveal the question as definitional, a dispute about what counts as the thing, analogous to asking when a codebase became a product? Say which answer my vocabulary smuggles in and whether that is legitimate. And tell me where my frame has already decided something before I noticed: I assume that artifacts have authors and intentions, that the commit log is the real history, and that an entity's identity is its interface. Which of those assumptions would the position I summarized reject, and which parts of it become unrecognizable once I stop assuming them? Do not soften the report to make the mapping look better than it is.
