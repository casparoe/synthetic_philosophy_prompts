I am an undergraduate in a seminar on the moral status of non-human minds, and for our final session we have been asked to bring in a poem rather than a paper. The idea we keep circling is that sentience is not a threshold but a ramp: that an insect, a fish, a rodent, and a person sit at very different points along it, and that somewhere near the bottom it stops being clear whether there is anything it is like to be the creature at all. I would like the form of the poem, rather than any statement in it, to carry that thought, and I would like it written accurately enough that someone who works on this would recognize the problem rather than merely hear it named.

The form I want is a shrinking one: five stanzas of five, four, three, two, and one lines. Each stanza closes on the same single-line question, word for word identical throughout. That question is yours to write, and it should be put, by context alone, to a different occupant at each rung — the wording stays fixed while the creature it is addressed to becomes simpler, until in the final stanza, which is the question and nothing else, the addressee has quietly gone missing and the poem is asking something of no one. That last line should not read as a verdict in either direction: it should not imply that everything down to a thermostat feels, nor that nothing below a mammal matters, and it should not resolve where the line falls. It should, though, leave the reader able to feel how arbitrary any line drawn across the ramp would be.

Two further constraints. First, keep apart two gradients that are easy to fuse: how confident we are that a creature has experience, and how much its experience would matter if it did. Somewhere in the middle of the poem, let one rung carry confidence with little at stake and another carry a great deal at stake with the question of experience wide open; this should be visible in how much the poem is willing to claim at each rung, not announced in a line. Second, the poem may not use the words sentience, consciousness, experience, or moral status — the scale has to be made felt through what the poem is and is not willing to describe.

Someone who has done the reading should be able to say what shape the problem takes here and where the poem declines to solve it; someone who has not should still find it a good poem.
