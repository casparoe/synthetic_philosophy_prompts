I am in my late sixties and have just received a diagnosis that will in all likelihood kill me within a few years. What has surprised me is that my dread has almost nothing to do with dying. It attaches instead to a thought I cannot shake: that human beings might one day simply cease to exist. When I try to say what disturbs me about that, I keep returning to the same inarticulate image — a world with no one left to read a book, argue about an idea, or care about anything. And I notice the fear is not really about how well or badly those future people would fare. Even if I knew that whoever came after us would be happy, I believe I would feel the same vertigo at the thought that there might be no one at all. It is the absence of anyone, rather than anyone's suffering, that gets to me.

My brother, who has no patience for metaphysics, thinks I have tied myself in knots. His argument runs roughly as follows. The badness of extinction must consist either (a) in the fact that some people will suffer and die, or (b) in the fact that valuable things which exist now will cease to exist. Case (a) is just the ordinary badness of death, multiplied; but since I claim to care about something over and above that, it cannot be what I mean. Case (b) proves too much, because a thing's value plainly does not depend on its continuing to exist — a great painting is not diminished by being destroyed after everyone who saw it is gone. So my anxiety, he says, is either a confusion or a dressed-up fear of my own insignificance.

Here is the part I cannot answer him on. When I imagine humanity ending, I do not only feel sad about the future; I feel as though the things I do now — my work, the things I care about — would be somehow drained of their point. But that seems absurd, since by hypothesis I would be dead and would not be around to find anything pointless.

Please help me think this through, and structure your answer as follows: (1) "What I am really asking" — a brief restatement of what is at issue in my situation, in your own words (about 100 words); (2) "The strongest version of my brother's case" — state his argument as forcefully as it can be stated (about 250 words); (3) "Where that case goes wrong" — your own assessment, with the reasoning made explicit (about 350 words); (4) "Extinction and the meaning of the present" — address directly whether the prospect of human extinction could make things in the present lose their point, and if so how (about 300 words); (5) "What I would advise you to do" — what you would actually tell me to do with these thoughts (about 150 words).
