Ok so I'm running the logic/philosophy-of-maths reading group this term and I'm sick of doing blackboard proofs every week, so I want to do a fiction session for once. The book is Flatland — Abbott, 1884, the one written as "A Square". You know the shape of it: two-dimensional world, women are line segments, men are polygons ranked by how many sides they have, and the narrator is a square; on New Year's Eve he dreams he's visiting Lineland and can't get the King to see a second dimension, then a Sphere turns up and hauls him out into Spaceland, and later he's shown Pointland, a single point who is his own entire universe and hears any speech as his own thought; then he comes back and tries to tell everyone about the third dimension, can't make anyone see it, and ends up imprisoned, writing the whole book as a memoir seven years in.

What I want from you is a reading of it through Skolem's paradox. My handle on the paradox, in case its rusty: by Löwenheim–Skolem, if ZF (or any first-order theory of sets) has a model at all, it has a countable one. But ZF proves that uncountable sets exist — it proves there's a power set of the naturals and that it can't be put into one-to-one correspondence with them. So there's a countable model M in which some set S is, by M's own lights, uncountable; and yet from outside, since M is countable, S has only countably many members. The standard line is that this isn't a contradiction, because "uncountable" is relative to M: the bijection that would witness S's real countability isn't an element of M, so M simply can't see it. Skolem himself took this to show that set-theoretic notions like countable and uncountable have no absolute meaning; plenty of people think that's an overreaction, since M's notion of uncountable is perfectly determinate and M just isn't the universe.

So read Flatland through that. Where does the analogy actually earn its keep? Is the Square in Lineland (or the Point, or the Square himself as he was before the Sphere) a countable model that "believes in" uncountables — a thing whose expressive resources can't reach the fact that makes it small, so that from the inside its everything really is everything? Is the Sphere the outside vantage point, or does the story's ending cut against that — the Square can't state his own situation to anyone inside, and the Sphere turns out to be just as closed about a fourth dimension as the Square was about the third? And I want the critic's half of it too: where does the analogy break? Flatland dangles an absolute Spaceland, a final dimension from which the lower worlds are at last seen for what they are, and I think Skolem's paradox denies exactly that, since there's no dimension-free standpoint, only another model with its own blind spots. Finish by saying whether the story gives you anything the technical literature can't — e.g. about why the relativity of countability feels like a loss even when it isn't a contradiction.
