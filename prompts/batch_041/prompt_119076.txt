I'm writing a paper for a seminar on explanation in history, and I've been arguing about it for weeks with a friend who works in enterprise IT, so I want to think this through properly rather than produce the usual essay. Take the Year 2000 problem as a historical episode and analyze it through hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy. The shape of the episode is common knowledge, so I'll only fix the barest facts: for years the public was told that the millennium bug -- two-digit year fields in decades of legacy code -- might bring down infrastructure; something on the order of several hundred billion dollars was spent worldwide on remediation; and then almost nothing happened. What I don't want is the standard moral that we now find the panic laughable only because we know how it turned out. I want a disciplined mapping that runs in both directions, theory onto case and case back onto theory, and I want you to say plainly where the theory has nothing to say instead of forcing it to speak.

Start with the case from the inside. Reconstruct what was actually knowable in, say, 1997 to a competent actor: the underlying defect was a design shortcut buried in code nobody had inventoried, the volume of genuinely date-dependent systems was unknown to anyone, and the warnings arrived mixed in with ordinary vendor alarmism. Then take the two retrospective narratives everyone now tells -- that the risk was always overblown hype, and that the risk was real and the remediation is what saved us -- and say precisely how the narrative fallacy smooths the messy episode into one or the other, and why the surviving evidence underdetermines both. Note the awkward datum that the second story has to absorb: critics at the time and since observed that sectors and countries that did comparatively little remediation also came through largely intact, which neither narrative handles comfortably. Bring in two things I've been trying to formulate myself and tell me whether they are already standard or whether I'm inventing vocabulary. The first is what I've been calling the no-trace problem: prevented harm leaves no evidence of the harm it prevented, so the observation that nothing happened is evidentially inert with respect to whether the risk was ever real. The second is a warning-list point: with enough retrospective search one can always assemble a list of warnings that were issued, so the existence of such a list does not show that the outcome was foreseeable from the actor's position. Does the received account of hindsight bias -- creeping determinism, the knew-it-all-along effect -- even apply to a non-event, or does it silently presuppose an outcome to creep toward, in which case some distinct error is doing the work here and I'd like it named and characterized? And do hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy come apart in this case, or is one of them carrying the whole explanation while the other is decorative?

Finally, turn it around. Is Y2K a confirming case for these ideas, a limiting case they never anticipated, or an anomaly that should make us trim the theory's ambitions? Then draw the practical thread: if successful prevention is systematically invisible in retrospect, what follows for how we should now assess large-scale preparedness efforts -- pandemic readiness, climate adaptation, and the like -- and can you state any evidence that would actually count against the verdict that such a risk was always overblown? Argue this out in continuous prose; I don't want a literature review or a reading list, and I don't need you to attribute any of it.
