I convene a final-year option on conditionals, and I am drafting the summer paper. My students can reproduce the standard apparatus in their sleep — the truth-functional material conditional and the Gricean rescue, Adams's thesis and the Ramsey test, the Lewis–Stalnaker possible-worlds semantics with its ordering of worlds, the indicative/counterfactual contrast as it surfaces in the familiar Oswald pair, the uniqueness assumption and conditional excluded middle, and the triviality results that make a probabilistic reading of indicatives so awkward. What they cannot do is think with the apparatus. So the essay question I want must turn on material that is in none of the readings, and that no one can lift from the secondary literature. I have therefore invented the following case, and I want it reproduced in the question as I give it here: at the Ashcroft Lane depot, a fire destroyed the loading bay last March; the inquiry established that the fire was started deliberately, by a person, and that Merrick, the night porter, was the only person on the site. Candidates are to consider (i) 'If Merrick did not start the fire, somebody else did'; (ii) 'If Merrick had not started the fire, somebody else would have'; (iii) 'If Merrick had not started the fire, somebody else might have'; (iv) 'If the sprinkler system had been armed, the pressure drop would have been logged'; (v) 'If the sprinkler system was armed, the pressure drop will have been logged'. The verdicts I have in mind are true, false, true, true, true respectively, but I want candidates made to defend or reject those verdicts rather than take them for granted, so the question should require them to say which they accept and why.

The question should have two parts, with the marks split stated on the paper (20 in total; split them as you judge best). Part (a) should require candidates to say, for each of the five conditionals, what the standard two-semantics package predicts and whether that matches the verdict, and then to locate precisely where the package breaks: in particular, why the 'would' counterfactual and the 'might' counterfactual with the same antecedent come apart, what the 'would' side would have to concede to keep them apart (an ordering with a limit assumption, uniqueness, conditional excluded middle), and why the sprinkler pair shows no such contrast at all — what distinguishes the pairs where the contrast appears from the pair where it does not. Part (b) should ask what the cheapest repair is, and here the question must not hand candidates a menu: they should be left to propose and assess the options themselves — an epistemic reading of 'might' counterfactuals, a wider domain for the modal than for the 'would', or something else entirely — and to say whether the repair leaves the indicative/counterfactual distinction doing any work, given that the counterfactuals then turn out to be sensitive to what is epistemically open after all. The two parts are meant to hang together: (a) is the diagnosis, (b) is the cost.

Please write the exam question as it would appear on the paper, with the case set out and the marks for each part shown; then a model answer of roughly 800 words; then a marking scheme of five must-hit points worth four marks each, each point accompanied by a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it; and finally a two-sentence note on what separates a first-class answer from an upper second. Candidates sit a three-hour paper, answer two questions from six, and may bring nothing in with them. They are not expected to cite anything, so the model answer should refer to positions by their ordinary labels rather than by author and date, and I want no quotations from the literature anywhere — the invented case is the only material the question needs. Everything in British English, please: my students are used to 'analyse', 'judgement' and 'defence'.
