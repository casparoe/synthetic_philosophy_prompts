Write a dialogue between two equally competent theorists of life. The first holds that to be alive is to be a self-maintaining, far-from-equilibrium chemical system: metabolism and self-repair are the essence of the matter, and reproduction is at most a common consequence. The second holds that to be alive is to belong to a lineage subject to evolution by natural selection: heredity, variation, and selection are the essence, and metabolism is at most a common accompaniment. Neither should be a strawman. Each should press the case that the other's criterion misclassifies something, and each should answer the other's actual objection rather than restate a position.

Have them adjudicate three cases, which I stipulate and which they should treat as fixed:

1. A sealed apparatus in which a self-sustaining chemical network draws in feedstock, maintains its own boundary, repairs its own components, and has persisted for years — but has no machinery for reproduction and no capacity to vary.
2. A population of replicating molecules that mutates and undergoes selection under controlled conditions, but only because an external machine feeds it monomers, removes its waste, and holds its temperature; nothing in the population maintains itself.
3. An organelle descended from a free-living bacterium that metabolizes and replicates, but only inside a host cell, on whose products it now depends at every step.

Each theorist should give a verdict on all three and defend it against the other's counterverdict. They should then confront the suspicion that they are answering different questions — which things are alive, versus what explains the origin and maintenance of living organization — and say whether their disagreement is substantive or merely terminological, and whether "life" is better treated as a graded property, a cluster concept, or a term of art fixed by stipulation. Close with each saying what single finding would make them abandon their criterion.
