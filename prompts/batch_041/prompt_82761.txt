I teach a 90-minute session of an upper-level political philosophy seminar — eighteen students, a room with a whiteboard and four movable tables, and no calculators, so anything needing long division is out. We have just finished a unit on representation, and I want to close it with a simulation rather than one more discussion. My problem is that the students can all recite, fluently and without conviction, that first-past-the-post wastes votes and manufactures majorities; they have never had to feel why anyone would defend it, and they have never noticed that a proportional chamber can leave them feeling unrepresented in a different way. So I want you to design a runnable classroom activity — rules, materials, roles, timeline, and a payoff scheme that needs no real money — in which one fixed set of voter preferences is counted two or three different ways and the results diverge in front of them. The first thing I want them to notice on their own, before I name anything, is that a majority is made by a counting rule rather than found in a population. The second, in the back half, is that proportional seats do not buy proportional power: that a small party sitting in the middle can auction its support and walk away with more of what it wants than a large party that won twice as many votes. Keep the polity fictional — invented parties, invented regions, no real countries or real elections — and make the participants themselves the geography, so that each table is a district with a population and the votes are cast by the people sitting at it. The arithmetic has to be checkable by eye: small electorates, a chamber of fifteen to twenty-five seats, everything countable on the whiteboard while they watch. I would rather have two clean rounds than four ragged ones.

Two specific things I would like built in, if they can be made to work. First, a moment where the chamber is enlarged partway through — I add two or three seats, in the spirit of making the assembly more representative — and one party comes out of the recount with fewer seats than it had before. I have read that some apportionment rules have exactly this property and I have never seen it staged in a classroom; if you can construct a small hand-checkable table that does it, I want that table, and if it cannot be done cleanly at this scale with a rule the students can actually follow, say so plainly and put something else in its place rather than pretending. Second, a redistricting minute late in the session in which the side that just lost gets to redraw the district boundaries and then has to defend the map it drew; I want them to feel the discomfort of realising how much of the previous result was the map. Do not let the debrief become a lecture, and do not let the names of the systems into the room until after they have felt them — no aside about what political scientists call this or that before the final ten minutes. And please do not stack the deck: these students arrive assuming proportionality is simply the more democratic option, and I want the accountability argument for single-member districts lodged in them as a genuine cost, not a slogan.

Write the whole thing as continuous prose — no headings, no numbered lists, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way you would explain it to a colleague in a long email, because I will be reading it on my phone between classes. Work as someone who has both taught this material and actually run rooms like this: an experienced designer of classroom simulations who also knows the theory well enough not to fudge it, and who thinks carefully before committing, since a design that reads well and does not run is worse than useless to me. Start with a short stretch of thinking aloud about two or three candidate mechanics and why you are discarding all but one. Then give me the activity itself in order, with the minute-marks embedded in the sentences rather than listed: the setup, the ballots and what is printed on them, the first count, the second count, the enlargement, the bargaining round and the payoff scheme that makes the bargaining actually hurt, and the redistricting minute. Then the debrief, mapped moment by moment onto whatever you think they have just felt. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the specific failures, including the ones my own students' instincts will cause, not generic advice about watching the clock. Finish with an honest account of what this activity cannot reproduce, what I will therefore have to supply myself in the last three minutes, and how to do that without turning it into a lecture. Assume you have no internet access: do not cite anything, and do not give me reading suggestions.
