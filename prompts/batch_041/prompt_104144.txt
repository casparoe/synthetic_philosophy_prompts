In my political philosophy seminar we have just worked through the paradox of voting—the result that, since the chance any single ballot decides an election is vanishingly small, casting one looks instrumentally irrational—and I keep noticing that real institutions behave as though they had never heard of it. Several democracies, Australia most prominently, impose a fine on citizens who fail to turn out, small but real. I would like you to reconstruct what such a policy presupposes about the value of voting, rather than to evaluate compulsory voting on its merits. What theory of why voting matters does the fine only make sense under? Does it presuppose that the paradox is simply false—that a vote does matter instrumentally in some way I have missed—or does it grant the paradox and locate the value of voting somewhere other than its causal effect on the outcome, and if so, where? Which rival accounts of democratic legitimacy does the practice quietly rule out, for instance any view on which an election's authority does not depend on turnout? And is the practice internally consistent, given that compulsion also drags uninformed and indifferent citizens to the polls? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say where it is underdetermined—which alternative theories of voting would license the same fine. Close by telling me what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the presupposed view with a purely instrumental one, or with a purely expressive one. Write this as a single continuous essay of no more than six hundred words, addressed to me; I do not want a survey of the literature, only the reconstruction and your own reasoning.
