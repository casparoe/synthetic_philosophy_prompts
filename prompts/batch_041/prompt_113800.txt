ok so I'm in a third-year seminar on Victorian philosophy of religion right now (we just spent like three weeks on Sidgwick and I've become insufferable about him), and I have to give the after-dinner speech at our student society's annual dinner in a fortnight. I'd like your help. Take the role of someone who knows Sidgwick's ethics and the whole psychical-research business inside out -- not so you can lecture me, but because I need the speech to be actually right.

Context, in case it helps: Sidgwick was one of the founding members of the Society for Psychical Research (maybe even its first president, don't quote me), and he was obsessed with the question of wether we survive death -- but he was also the most scrupulous person imaginable about it. He thought wanting something to be true was no evidence that it is, and he refused to let his own longing count in the scales, so after decades of sittings and experiments he never really came down on either side. That's the guy.

The dinner: about sixty people. Half are my society -- students, a few of them genuine believers who've been going to mediums since they were fifteen, plus two of the founder's old friends in their eighties. The other half are philosophy grad students and a couple of faculty who will definately pounce if I say anything I can't back up. The tradition is that the after-dinner speaker toasts the founder, who died a few years ago and who spent her whole life on this stuff and firmly believed.

What I want: a five-minute speech that honours her without me claiming anything I don't believe -- and honestly that's the hard part, because all the obvious warm things to say are claims about where she is now. I want the Sidgwick thing to do real structural work here, shaping what can and can't be said, not just be a name-drop. I also want to end with a small ritual: everyone at the table writes one sealed prediction about next year, and we open them at next year's dinner. It should feel like a real test, in his spirit, rather than a cute game.

Before you write, think it through: brainstorm what a toast can and can't do under that constraint, decide what the single carrying idea is, then write the speech. Keep it sayable out loud -- no footnotes, no citations, no quotes from books, nothing I'd have to go and look up. And after the speech give me a short note on the two or three places where I'm most likely to slip and start sounding like I believe more than I do.
