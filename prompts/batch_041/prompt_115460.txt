I am a novelist, and this spring I am to appear in public conversation with a philosopher of probability at a literary festival. My guest defends the thesis — usually called the Principal Principle — that an agent who knows the objective chance of an event to be p should hold credence p in that event, on the ground that credence is answerable to chance as a subordinate's judgment is answerable to a superior's. What I want to press are two qualifications of her own. First, she treats the principle as derived rather than fundamental: what we are ultimately answerable to is the evidence that fixes the chance, and deference to the chance is a shortcut that is reliable when, and only when, we know nothing about the event beyond its chance. Second, she holds that the principle lapses in what she calls self-cancelling cases — where the chance hypothesis itself assigns a nonzero chance to a future in which that hypothesis is false, so that learning it would commit you to something that might unlearn itself — and that the rational response there is to abandon deference rather than patch it. Treat the position, not the person, as the object of the questions; the reconstruction is mine, and I would rather you engaged it than her.

Draft eight to ten questions for me to put to her, ordered so that each is a difficulty rather than an invitation to summarise. I want at least one on the metaphor itself — what it is to defer to something that is not an agent, and whether the authority of chance is really the authority of an expert; at least one on what survives of the principle once it is derived from evidence-responsiveness, including whether the two can come apart in some case, and whether the position can be read as making the principle true by definition; at least one on how the evidence that fixes a chance is to be identified without circularity, given that evidence which already settles the event would make the chance beside the point; and at least one on the self-cancelling cases — what governs credence there, and whether the replacement rule quietly reintroduces the very principle it was meant to replace. For each question, give me the follow-up I should have ready, keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Nothing biographical, and no literature review: I want the arguments.

Say, for each question, how confident you are that it is a genuine difficulty for the position rather than a confusion of mine, and how confident you are in the reply you expect her to make; then single out the one point you are least sure of.
