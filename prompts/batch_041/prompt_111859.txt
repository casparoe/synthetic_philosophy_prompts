I read history as an undergraduate and now run the corpus team at a company that sells research tools to historians: we digitise newspaper runs, tag every item against a fixed event schema, and have annotators write a short narrative summary for each item. An argument started in standup last week that I have been unable to settle in engineering language, and I want a memo I can circulate rather than another seminar. The first position is the old Rankean ideal, that the historian should get out of his own way and let the sources show the past as it really was, adding nothing of himself. One of my engineers holds this without knowing it has a name; he wants us to stop annotating and simply surface the documents. The second is Carr's: that selection is not a regrettable concession to our limitations but the thing that makes history possible at all, since a fact of history exists as a fact only once a historian has singled it out and put it to use, and what gets singled out is decided by the questions the present happens to be asking. My head annotator holds this without the theory; we digitise the runs our search logs say customers want, and our schema has a category for protest and none for slow demographic change, because nobody queries the second one. The third is Hayden White's: that the shape of a historical account — romance, tragedy, comedy, satire — is not something the historian chooses once the facts are in, but a commitment made before the research starts, so that emplotment is one of the ways a history explains rather than a decoration applied afterwards. Our own summary template has four fields — setting, principal agent, complication, resolution — which a colleague points out is a story shape and not a neutral container.

What I want is not an essay about objectivity but a translation followed by a residue report. Take each of the three positions and restate the strongest version of it in the vocabulary my team actually uses: schema and ontology, sampling frame and coverage, provenance, label noise, inter-annotator agreement, templates, held-out evaluation sets, versioning, ground truth. The test is that an engineer who has never read a page of philosophy should be able to read your restatement and find himself disagreeing with the same claim, not a watered-down cousin of it. Where you had to cheat to make something fit, say so and say what the cheat cost. Where a claim has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, say that as well — I expect White's four tropes, metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche and irony, to drop out entirely, and I want your judgment on whether that is a hole in my vocabulary or a sign that White was making a claim about rhetoric which my vocabulary has no reason to represent.

Then the residue report proper. Go through each position claim by claim and give each claim a verdict — translates cleanly, translates only with distortion (say what is lost), or no counterpart — and finish with two questions. The first is the one I care about most: is there anything in the historians' argument that is simply invisible from where I sit, so that I would never have known to ask about it? The second runs the other way: is there anything in my working vocabulary that the historians' debate has no room for? Two candidates occur to me and I want your view on both. We treat high inter-annotator agreement as evidence that our summaries are objective, and it may be evidence of nothing but shared conventions. And our summaries exist to be read by customers who want to feel a particular way about the past; we file that under product requirements, whereas a historian might say it is the whole question. I do not need references — I have the books and I am not asking you to prove anything out of them. Aim for something in the region of fifteen hundred words, in labelled sections, ending with three sentences I could actually say in standup without embarrassing myself.
