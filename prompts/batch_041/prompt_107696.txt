My flatmate and I have been going around in circles on this for weeks and I'd like an outside read, because at this point we're just restating our positions louder. It started with the hitchhiker thing: you're stranded in the desert, a driver pulls up and says he'll only give you a lift if you agree to pay him when you reach town, and you both know that once you're in town you'll have no reason to pay. My gut verdicts, for what there worth:

1. Before the ride: if I can actually get myself to mean it, I should. Refusing to form the intention because it wouldn't survive scrutiny feels like refusing a lottery ticket because you can't win twice.
2. After the ride, wallet out, driver standing there: I think I should pay. I know paying buys me nothing at that point, but I keep coming back to the thought that the lift I got was paid for by the kind of person who pays, and I don't want to be a free rider on that.
3. Same shape, opposite verdict. The toxin puzzle: a billionaire puts a million in my account today if I now form the intention to drink his foul toxin tomorrow. Tomorrow the money's mine either way, the toxin will only make me ill, and he can't do anything about it. There I'm pretty confident I should keep the intention and not drink the stuff. Drinking would be idiotic.
4. The missile case. A country can only deter an attack if it's known to retaliate even when retaliating is suicide, so it should make itself the kind of state that would. But if the attack actually comes and retaliation is genuinely pointless, I think it shouldn't retaliate, and I don't feel anything like the pull I feel in (2).
5. Domestic version: I promised my friend before she died that I'd keep paying her mum's care home fees. Nobody is auditing this. The money is mine, the payments are a drag, and if I stopped nobody would know and the mum would probably be fine on the state. I think I should keep paying, and that it would be shabby not to, and if you pushed me for a reason I'd end up saying something vague about who I'd be.
6. About belief rather than action: my brother sits a borderline exam next week and will do better if he believes he'll pass. I told him not to look at his practice scores the night before, and I stand by that. But I don't think his confidence is rational afterwards — useful, sure, not warranted — and that mismatch bothers me.

My flatmate says the principle is just be the person you'd want to be, which sounds either empty or false to me. My own guess was something like: rationality evaluates the process by which you came to have a state, not the state or the act itself. But that seems to make (2) and (5) come out wrong, since by that standard I should have arranged things in advance and then stiffed the driver, and I can't get myself to accept that.

So: what principle, or small set of them, actually captures these? State it precisely, tell me which of the six cases each clause is being paid for, and be honest about which verdicts it can't save. Two cases I haven't decided that I'd like it applied to. First, suppose I can reach the paying outcome either by handing the driver my passport as security at the start or by becoming the sort of person who pays — does it matter which, and if the second is better, is that because of results or because of something about integrity? Second, I once wrote a promise to myself in a notebook nobody will ever read, and I've since moved on and no longer want the thing I promised. Does the principle still bind me, and does it bind me for the same reason as (5), where there's at least a real person at the other end? And if you can name the single case that would do the most to separate the surviving candidate principles, I'd like that too.
