I'm putting together a set of cases where I can act unilaterally — where my doing the thing is good if I'm one of a few who do it, but bad if enough others do it too — and I keep getting verdicts I can't square, so I want the principle behind them. Here's the pattern of what I actually think. Geoengineering: if I (as some actor with the capacity) judged that a one-time solar-aerosol release at the optimal level would be net good, I still think I shouldn't do it, and the reason is precisely that lots of other actors are in my position and could each reach the same judgment; the fact that I've concluded it's worth doing is evidence they'll conclude it too, and the sum overshoots. Antibiotics: I think I should take the full prescribed course even though I know overuse collectively breeds resistance — my individual contribution is negligible and the personal benefit is large, and it would be absurd to abstain on curse grounds. A gift to a grieving friend: I should send it, even knowing a dozen others may send something too and it'll be a bit much; the multiplication doesn't ruin it. A marginally useful paper: publishing is fine even though the field is flooded with near-identical work. Signing one more petition, donating to a cause where I'm a drop in the bucket, using the leaf-blower at 7 a.m. when everyone else's noise would make the block unbearable: in each case I think it's permissible to just do it, curse be damned. But a researcher who could publish synthesis instructions for a dangerous pathogen: I think they shouldn't, on exactly the geoengineering-style reasoning, even though the marginal informational value is small and others may publish it anyway. And here's the one I go back and forth on: a person deciding whether to have a child, or to take a scarce ICU bed they're not sure they need, where the "good if few, bad if many" structure is real but where I can't tell whether to reason like the leaf-blower or like the pathogen.

What I want is a principle, or a short list of competing candidates, that gets these verdicts or tells me which to abandon. Please be precise about the clause in the principle that each verdict is doing work for, and be explicit that the leaf-blower verdict and the pathogen verdict look structurally identical to me — good if few act, bad if many, and I can't observe how many others will — so any principle that saves both has to point to a difference I haven't named, and if no honest difference exists, tell me which verdict to give up rather than inventing a distinction. Then apply your principle(s) to cases I haven't raised: a neighborhood where each household could install a sump pump that helps its own basement but, at scale, drains a shared aquifer; a company that could add one more antibiotic to livestock feed; a voter deciding whether to vote when the outcome hinges on a coin-flip margin; and a scientist deciding whether to run one more replication of a study already replicated a hundred times. Say which verdicts your principle delivers on those and where it strains. In the end I want the answer to separate what is actually settled among people who've thought hard about this from what is genuinely contested, and not to present a contested line as the consensus — if the whole "curse" framing is itself disputed, tell me that rather than smoothing it over.
