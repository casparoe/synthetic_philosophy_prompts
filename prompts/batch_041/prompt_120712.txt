I'm a novelist and im stuck on a scene, so I'm hoping you can just write it for me.

Two philosophers share an office. The older one, Frances, is 66, spent her whole career on theories of reference, and has watched her subfield get quietly absorbed into linguistics and cognitive science. The younger one, Dev, is 29, a couple years out of the PhD, does formal stuff, and has just read the entire literature on one problem — I don't much care which, pick one that fits the scene — and come away stunned that after all that nobody agrees about anything. They're arguing about whether their own discipline makes progress. I want the argument to be real, not a summary of a famous debate, and neither of them should be a dummy or win by being smug.

Dev's line is the record. Ask a roomful of philosophers in 1900 what makes an action right and a roomful in 2024 and you get the same spread of answers. His best card is what I think of as the textbook test: a physics textbook teaches you what's true, a philosophy textbook teaches you the problems. And when the field does seem to settle something, he says, it's usually by quietly changing what the words mean, which he takes to be sleight of hand rather than discovery.

Frances's line is that this is the wrong model of progress altogether. For her the track record isn't a list of theorems, it's a body of case law — we accumulate distinctions, not results, and a distinction can be a real advance even when the underlying question stays open. Her best cards: the export model, where philosophy progresses by giving away whatever it solves, logic to mathematics and computing, decision theory to economics, big chunks of the mind to cognitive science, so of course the residue looks like it never moves, that's what's left rather than what's been done; the straight-face test, where there are arguments that convinced nearly everyone a century ago — the argument from illusion on the road to sense-data is the one I want her to use — that today no competent person can state without wincing, and that shift didn't come from any single decisive refutation; and the idea that the unit of progress is the objection, that we can date objections and know who had which, and that having a better objection counts as progress even with no answer in sight.

I want them actually going at each other, responding to the point the other just made rather than talking past each other. Dev needs a real reply to the export model (something like: an export is just a name for abandoning the hard part) and Frances needs a real reply to the textbook test. Let the fight turn on whether the straight-face test is evidence of anything and on whether changing what a word means can ever count as finding something out. Dont let them drift into a polite both-sides ending. End on the exchange where the actual disagreement is finally visible, whatever you decide it is.

Write it as continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just the scene, dialogue with the minimum of business around it, the way you'd put it in a novel.
