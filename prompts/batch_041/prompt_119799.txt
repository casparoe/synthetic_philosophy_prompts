I've got a favour to ask, and it's a bit of an odd one. I run a small seminar on rationality and decision theory for final-year students, and for the last session I want to hand them a short story instead of a problem set, something they'll argue about on the walk home. So what I'd like from you is a piece of fiction built on superrationality, Hofstadter's idea that in a symmetric game you shouldn't ask what's best for me given what the others will probably do, you should ask what rule you'd want every player to follow, on the assumption that anyone reasoning as well as you will land on the same answer you land on. The shape I have in mind is the luring-lottery kind of thing: the pot gets divided among everyone who enters, so the more people play the smaller each share gets, and there's an enormous pull toward being the one clever person who enters while everyone else nobly refrains, which is exactly the thought the whole idea forbids you from having, since the others are running the same calculation you are. Please invent your own version of the game rather than restaging the one everybody has heard, though. Change the payoffs, change who's playing, change what's at stake, put a live tally up on a wall somewhere so the characters can watch the number climb while they dither. The reasoning should be the plot, not a lecture with people stapled to it.

A few things I care about. Keep the cast small enough that a reader can track who is thinking what, but make the size of the group actually matter, so that the argument which works for eight people in a room doesn't obviously survive being scaled up to a whole city. I want at least one character who works out the symmetry argument properly and pays a real price for it, one who sees it clearly and defects anyway and isn't punished by the story for that, and one who tries the probabilistic move, the claim that the only defensible thing is to enter with some chance rather than decide yes or no at all, and I want that move genuinely tested on the page rather than voiced and then dropped. Nobody should get to explain the theory. If the theory has to show up it should come out of two people half-winning an argument, both of them a bit wrong.

And I'd like the person running the game to have their own skin in it, ideally to be running it in order to find something out, so that the reader ends up having to decide whether the organiser's move is itself the superrational one or just a con with extra steps. The story can end however it wants, on a defection, on a stalemate, on somebody quietly changing their mind, but please don't tack on a closing paragraph telling me what it all meant. If I want the moral I'll find it myself.

Format: just the story, continuous prose from start to finish, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, nothing that looks like an outline. I'll be reading it aloud, so it needs to run like one person talking. Sorry for the length of this, I've definately over-specified it, but the last thing I want is another story where the game is just set dressing and the characters are basically footnotes.
