I am a high school senior and the student coordinator for a small interview series run by my school's philosophy club. Our next guest is a philosopher who defends a version of the hinge of history hypothesis: roughly, the view that we may be living in a uniquely pivotal window in which a relatively small number of decisions, made by a relatively small number of people, will determine the long-run trajectory of civilization, and that this possibility should dominate our practical reasoning even if the probability is modest. She is careful to say that the hypothesis is not a prediction about the future but a claim about expected value, and she tends to deflect objections by saying that even a small chance of a hinge justifies extraordinary measures. I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that press her on the actual content of that position rather than letting her restate it. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give.

The questions should be intelligible to an audience of high school students and teachers, but they should not be watered down. I want at least one question on how she distinguishes a genuine hinge from mere historical importance and how she avoids the objection that every generation believes it is living at the decisive moment. I want at least one question on what I have been calling the quiet hinge problem: if a hinge is defined by its downstream consequences, then by the time we can confirm that we were in one, the chance to act may have passed, so the hypothesis risks being unfalsifiable in real time. I want at least one question on the relation between the hinge hypothesis and self-locating belief, including what we should expect to observe if we are in a hinge and whether the absence of obvious signs counts against the view. I want at least one question on whether the hypothesis can distinguish a hinge from a near-miss hinge without relying on hindsight, and at least one on whether the existence of many possible hinges dilutes the expected-value argument or instead strengthens it. Please make the sequence such that her standard deflection—that the hinge is a claim about expected value, not a prediction—becomes progressively harder to use, and end with a question about what evidence or argument could count against the view. Do not ask for citations, reading lists, or bibliographies, and do not ask generic questions about how she became interested in philosophy. If a question depends on a technical term, define it briefly in the question itself.
