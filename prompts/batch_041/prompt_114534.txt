I'm in my third year and taking a module on divine foreknowledge and human freedom — we've just finished the Molina block (middle knowledge, counterfactuals of freedom, the grounding objection, the whole Jesuit/Dominican scrap over grace) — and the final essay lets us pick any film or novel that dramatises the problem and read it through one of the frameworks. I've gone for Minority Report, partly because I rewatched it and it seems to be doing something wierder than the standard "is the future fixed?" argument, and partly because nobody else in my seminar will have touched it.

What I want is a genuine Molinist reading, and I want it honest about where the film won't cooperate. A few threads I've been chewing on, take them or leave them:

Precrime's entire warrant is a counterfactual of freedom — you get locked up because you would have done it — so the institution needs those counterfactuals to be both true in advance and genuinely free. That's exactly the Molinist's squeeze. Then Dr Hineman hands the precogs a causal explanation (their abilities come from their mothers' neuroin addiction in utero), which is either the grounding objection dramatised or a complete misfire, depending on how you read it: a counterfactual grounded in a mechanism isn't a free choice, and one with no ground at all is a brute fact. Does the film know it's staging that dilemma, or does it just want the precogs to be infallible?

The minority reports and the purge are definately the sharpest bit, I think. The system only holds up if the counterfactuals are impeccable — no dissent, no alternative — and the purge is the film's version of stipulating the alternatives away. And the murder Anderton is predicted to commit turns out to have been manufactured: Crow is paid to goad him into it. So it isn't foresight at all, it's providence-by-arrangement — the Molinist God arranging circumstances so that a free creature freely does his will, except here the arrangement *is* the crime. Does the film think middle-knowledge providence is just manipulation with better PR, or does it let the difference between God and a murderer do the work? There's also the preventive-punishment angle: the Molinist idea that some people wouldn't come round in any world God could have put them in sits oddly close to precrime's justification for the coma.

Where I suspect it breaks down: the film's future looks genuinely open — Anderton can change things once he knows them, and the vision gets fulfilled by a different route rather than the same one — and Molinists aren't open futurists about counterfactuals of freedom. So the honest reading might end up being foreknowledge-plus-open-future with a Molinist veneer, and I'd rather you said so than forced it.
