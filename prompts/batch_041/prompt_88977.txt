I'm a TA for a second-year course on space, time and motion -- we do the Leibniz-Clarke correspondence, a chunk of the Principia, and then some modern philosophy of physics -- and the essay I have to grade next week asks students, for each of four theories, to say whether it's substantivalist or relationist about spacetime. The trouble is I keep disagreeing with my own grading. Two students both write 'substantivalist' about Newton, one because the theory's inventory includes absolute places and one because the bucket needs absolute motion to come out right, and I have no consistent way of saying which of them actually did what the question is asking for. I've also marked an answer wrong for calling something relationist when its own formulation only makes sense if the geometry is there independently of the bodies, and I could feel the verdict was wrong but couldn't say which step of my reasoning licensed it. So before the next batch I want a decision procedure I can actually run on paper, maybe ten minutes an essay, that goes from a stated theory to one of: substantivalist / relationist / neither / not determinable. The course convener has to sign off on anything I add to the rubric, and students can appeal a mark, so it needs to be something I can walk someone through out loud.

Here's the half-baked thing I've been telling students, which I suspect is doing some of the damage: ask (1) does the theory's inventory of things include points, regions, or a metric as items in their own right, over and above bodies and their relations? (2) does the theory treat the same bodies uniformly shifted, or the same bodies spun up, as a different physical situation or the same one? (3) can the laws be stated without presupposing the geometry as an independent thing? I want you to either fix that into something that works or tell me it's the wrong three questions. The procedure has to say what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it terminates in a verdict, where it should stop and say the question isn't well-posed for this theory, and which steps are really interpretation calls dressed up as tests -- I'd much rather flag 'this one is a judgment call' than pretend a test decided it. Also: a colleague tells me I should keep separate what a theory is committed to existing from what it needs in order to be stated at all, and I'm not sure how to build that distinction into a checklist rather than just nodding at it.

Then run the procedure on three cases and show your work: Newton's absolute space with the bucket (including what exactly the bucket is supposed to prove, and whether it proves that), a Leibniz-style relationalist theory, and one where I expect the procedure to hand me an answer I won't like -- I've been assuming any theory whose laws mention a fixed geometry is automatically substantivalist, and I have a feeling that's going to bite me with a theory where the geometry is itself dynamical, or with some reformulation that's supposed to be empirically equivalent to a relationist one. One student last term argued the whole dispute is verbal; I need the procedure to say whether that's a cop-out or sometimes the right call. Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do -- the cases it'll always get wrong, and whether 'substantivalist' is even one property or a family of them that I've been treating as one. No bibliography please, I just want the procedure.
