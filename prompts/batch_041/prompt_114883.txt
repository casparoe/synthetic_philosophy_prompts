I retired a couple years back after thirty-odd years as an analytical chemist, and the one lesson that career beat into me is that when two labs rank the same batch of samples differently, it's almost never because one lab is wrong. It's because they quietly picked different measurands and then printed their numbers side by side as though purity were one thing. So imagine my delight when my niece, who's about three years into a poli-sci PhD, got me arguing with her over Thanksgiving about whether Sweden or Norway is the more liberal country. She pulls up one index on her phone that counts low taxes and light regulation as liberty; I pull up another that counts a generous welfare state and a strong safety net as liberty; and wouldn't you know it, the answer flips depending on whose phone we're looking at. Then her officemate chimes in that we're both stuck in the seventeenth century, because the real question isn't interference but domination — whether you're subject to somebody else's arbitrary power — and by that measure a sovereign wealth fund might actually buy you freedom, which sent me home confused. I'd like to take a swing at this in writing.

So could you write me a parody press release — from the Nordic Institute for Comparative Liberty, headquarters in Uppsala, completely made up, all the figures invented, so don't worry about whether they're the real ones — announcing that after four years of study the Institute has definately settled the Sweden-versus-Norway question and handed each country a letter grade. Six hundred to seven hundred words, deadpan the whole way through, no winking at the reader, formatted like the real thing: a headline, a dateline, a paragraph of triumphant summary, a seperate Methodology section that gives the game away a little at a time, a small table of the grades with a one-line justification under each that quietly undercuts one of the others, a Frequently Raised Objections section in which the Institute is made to answer its own question about what would actually settle it, and one quote from a dissenting commissioner who thinks the Institute is measuring something else entirely and dressing it up as liberty. The jokes should come out of the actual conceptual problem here — that you can't rank two countries on one liberty scale until somebody stipulates which liberty they mean, and that the weighting choice, not the data, does the work — and not out of lazy country stereotypes. It should be funny to somebody who knows the difference between negative and positive liberty and has watched the freedom-index crowd fight about construct validity, but a neighbor who just wants to know which country is freer should still be able to follow it and get the joke. Aim it at the whole practice of grading countries on a single liberty scale rather than at either country, and never come right out and say the thing is undecidable — I want the deflation to be visible in the methodology itself. American English please, and thanks; my niece says real press releases in this genre always bury the weighting choices in a footnote, which sounds about right to me.
