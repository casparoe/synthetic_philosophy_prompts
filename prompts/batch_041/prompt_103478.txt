Please take on the role of a philosopher of causation and historical explanation -- someone at home with counterfactuals, with the explanation of particular events, and with the long-running disputes over contingency and inevitability in history -- and who also knows the relevant literature well enough to tell me when a distinction has already been drawn, and under what name. Here is the pattern I cannot find a name for.

Writers use "contingent" and "inevitable" as though each were one property. But I think there are two separate questions tangled up in them. The first is whether the outcome could have gone otherwise at all -- whether there was genuinely more than one live possibility. The second is how much would have had to change for it to go otherwise -- whether the outcome sat on a knife-edge that any small nudge would have tipped, or whether it was so overdetermined that almost any small disturbance would have left it standing. These look independent to me, and the words slide between them constantly.

Two places where I see them come apart.

First: a process that is fixed from the start, yet runs through a channel so narrow that its starting conditions had to be a coincidence for it to happen at all. Once under way, nothing in the middle could have gone differently; but that it was under way when it was looks like an accident. Here the fragility has migrated upstream into the antecedents instead of sitting in the outcome. A version of this is the deterministic system in which a hair's difference at the beginning produces an entirely different result -- fully determined, and yet nothing about the outcome is stable.

Second: an outcome that was genuinely open -- it really could have gone the other way -- but was not within reach of any small intervention. A margin wide enough that only a different economy, or a different generation of decisions, would have closed it. Here "it was contingent" and "no fluke decided it" are both true, and people treat them as contradicting each other. The ordinary example is a battle that turns on a message arriving an hour late, against a war of attrition settled by industrial output: in one, a messenger's sprained ankle is decisive; in the other, no plausible accident changes anything, though the loser could have won with a different decade of preparation.

The disputes I care about are the ones where two people agree on every fact and still disagree about whether something was inevitable. My suspicion is that they are answering different questions, and that the phrase "could have gone otherwise" hides an unstated range of alternatives that is doing all the work. I also suspect a third thing gets folded in -- possibility confused with probability, so that "it could have gone otherwise" is heard as "it was reasonably likely to go otherwise."

So: is there an established name for either of these two things, or for the pair, or for the fact that "contingent" and "inevitable" each do double duty? If so, tell me what it is and how it differs from the nearest concepts -- I expect a good answer will have to distinguish this from path dependence, from sensitive dependence on initial conditions, from the robustness or fragility of a causal claim, and from the ordinary modal contrast of necessity with contingency. If nothing established fits, say so plainly rather than forcing a match, and coin a term with a definition. Either way, I want the carving rather than the label: what exactly the distinction is, what it is not, and where its edges are. Be explicit about boundary cases -- what would have to be true for the two questions to collapse into one, and whether there are cases outside the combinations I have sketched that any adequate account would have to handle. Where you are unsure whether a term is standard or whether you are reconstructing someone's view rather than reporting it, flag that rather than smoothing it over.

Close by telling me which single question I should have asked, if I have mis-framed the whole thing.
