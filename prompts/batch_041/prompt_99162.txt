I'm a senior engineer on a platform team, and much of my job is running postmortems after production incidents. The data-gathering part usually goes fine; what stalls is the argument about what actually caused the incident. We'll have the timeline, the logs, and the dashboards on the table, and four people will be defending four different stories, each of which fits the evidence we have, and the meeting ends with whoever is most senior or most fluent getting their story into the writeup. I've been reading about inference to the best explanation as an account of how scientists choose between theories, and I'd like to turn it into something my team can actually run in a ninety-minute meeting, with no statistician in the room and no ability to rerun the incident on demand. Please write me that procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the order in which to weigh the standard virtues of an explanation (how much it explains, how well it fits what we already know, whether it is simple, whether it makes predictions we could check), where the procedure stops and reports that the current evidence cannot separate two candidates, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining question is a judgment call rather than a fact. Two things I'd like it to take seriously, because they keep coming up and I don't have names for them: first, an explanation can be attractive because of how much it would explain if it were true, even when it is quite unlikely given everything else we know, and these two considerations pull in opposite directions; second, choosing the best of the stories currently on the table tells us nothing if all of them are bad, so the procedure should include a step that asks whether we have even considered the boring explanation. I'd also like it to handle the case where two candidates are both adequate and we must choose anyway, and the case where the honest answer is that several factors combined, none sufficient on its own, since our postmortems always seem to want a single root cause and I am no longer sure that shape is always right.

Run the procedure on three incidents. First: a payments reconciliation job that fails on roughly one Tuesday night in six, where the candidates are an index rebuild that overlaps the job window, a slow memory leak that crosses a threshold only after several days of uptime, intermittent rate limiting from the bank's API, and clock drift on two hosts that breaks the job's idempotency check. Second: a checkout service returning a slightly wrong total for a small share of orders in one country, where the candidates are a rounding rule that changed in a library upgrade, a misconfigured tax table, a cache serving a stale price, and an unusual sequence in which users apply coupons. Third, and this is the one I expect to be uncomfortable: an intermittent 500 error that I have been arguing comes from a subtle race condition in our new connection pool, when the least interesting candidate on the list is a known bug in a library version we pin; I suspect the procedure will tell me to drop my explanation, and if it does I want the reasoning spelled out rather than softened. For each incident, show what the procedure rules out, what it cannot separate, and what single piece of evidence or small experiment would move it. Finally, be explicit about what this procedure cannot do — which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as method, where it will misfire, and what a team should not use it for.
