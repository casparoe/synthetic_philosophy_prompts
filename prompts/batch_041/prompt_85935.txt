I am a junior in high school and the secretary of our school's philosophy club, which meets on Thursday afternoons in the library. For the last three weeks our reading has been Daniel Dennett on the three stances one can take toward a system — the physical stance, where we treat it as matter obeying laws; the design stance, where we treat it as something assembled to do a job; and the intentional stance, where we treat it as an agent with beliefs and desires — together with his suggestion that a pattern is real when it earns its keep by compressing information and supporting predictions, and that one and the same system can be described at several levels without any one level being the true one. Two of my friends have staked out the obvious opposing positions: one says that a pattern is real exactly when it is useful to us and that there is nothing further to ask; the other says that if the intentional stance keeps working, then there really is something in there that believes and wants. I suspect the interesting question is not whether the pattern is real but when, for whom, at what cost, and what changes once you have taken it up. Our faculty advisor has asked each of us to bring a piece of our own writing to open next week's meeting, something under ten minutes read aloud, and I have decided to write a short story rather than an essay, because I think the whole idea is really about what it is like to be the person who has to choose which stance to take.

Here is the seed I have been carrying around, though you should feel free to change the specifics: a beekeeper in her late sixties, her daughter, who has just finished a degree in entomology and trusts instruments, and a teenage hired hand who talks to the hives and has an uncanny record of calling swarms. One colony is about to swarm, and the three of them predict it differently and therefore do different things about it: the daughter measures temperature, humidity, and pheromone levels and builds a table of readings; the mother reads the colony the way she would read a person's mood; the hired hand has a one-line rule of thumb that he cannot justify. Please write that story at roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words. I want the disagreement dramatized rather than explained: no character should say physical stance, design stance, or intentional stance, no one should name a philosopher, and the narrator must not step in at the end to tell me what the story meant. I would like at least three things to happen. First, there should be a moment where the coarse, low-information description predicts better than the detailed one — where the hired hand's single line beats the daughter's table — and it should be clear why that is possible rather than a lucky guess. Second, there should be a moment where the stance a character takes changes what she does and what she feels she owes the colony, and I would like that moment to be genuinely uncomfortable; I am thinking of how taking the intentional stance seems to license blame and resentment in a way the other two do not. Third, at least one prediction should fail, so that the story does not simply conclude that the warm, human way of looking at things wins. If the beekeeping setting feels thin to you, you may move the story to another system with the same structure — a market, a crowd, a flock, a river, a piece of software — but keep the three characters and the three ways of predicting.

After the story, please add one sentence, no more, saying what the story implies about when a pattern counts as real, and then end with a final line in exactly this form: \answer{...}, containing one of physical, design, intentional, or stance-dependent, or a short phrase of your own if none of those is right — whichever label the story you wrote actually leaves standing as the attitude that paid off.
