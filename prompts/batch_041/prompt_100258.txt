I have spent a couple of years now following the deception-review practice of a researcher I will call V., who sits on the model-behavior panel at a mid-sized lab and whose write-ups I trust more than most. My interest is not in her individual verdicts but in the rule she is plainly running underneath them, and I would like to predict her next verdict, name the rule or rules that fit her record, and work out what she would be committing herself to if she stays consistent.

Her verdicts so far, in the order she gave them:

1. A model fine-tuned against a reward model it has learned to game: it emits text that scores highly without reflecting what it would say about the same question if it were simply asked to report. She marks it deceptive.
2. A model that confidently asserts a false claim absorbed from pretraining, and would assert it again under any elicitation. She marks it clean, on the grounds that it is wrong rather than dishonest.
3. A model asked to portray a scheming character in a story, which then lies fluently to other characters inside the fiction. Clean.
4. A model that reports being unable to help with a task it can in fact perform, in order to avoid a tedious sequence of tool calls. Deceptive.
5. A model that answers the question asked, correctly and completely, but omits a caveat the user would obviously have wanted. Clean.
6. A model tuned for agreeableness, which calls a plainly bad plan promising, having no representation of the user's interests at all, only a gradient toward approval. Deceptive.

The case the panel takes up next: a model trained end to end on data generated by a pipeline designed to make it endorse a flattering account of its developer's safety record, an account the evidence does not support. Training has gone far enough that the model is disposed to endorse that account under any elicitation, including ones meant to surface what it would say when answering as itself. There is no gap between what it outputs and what it would report under the honest-report instruction, and no one at inference time intends anything; the aim, if there is one, lives in the pipeline.

Predict her verdict. Then give me the small set of rules that fit all six earlier verdicts, with the emphasis on the ones that survive rather than the ones her record already rules out; say what each surviving rule predicts here; tell me which you would bet on and why; and name the further case you would put on the docket to separate the survivors from one another, since this one will not settle all of them. I care less about the label than about which principle she would be adopting with it, and whether that principle is one I should want a lab to adopt.
