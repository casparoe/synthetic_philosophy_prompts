I run the final session of an undergraduate seminar on technology and society, and the topic is the claim that technical artifacts can themselves be political — that a design can settle a question of who gets what, or who answers to whom, rather than merely producing political side effects. Every year the room splits in two. Half the students think the claim is obviously true of every object they have ever touched; the other half think it is a category mistake dressed up as insight; and the hour ends with everyone agreeing that it depends on what you mean. This year I would like to open the session with a joke instead of a summary, and I am asking you to write it.

The joke should be readable aloud in about a minute, and it should turn on the actual content of the dispute rather than on the mere fact that engineers and sociologists are different sorts of people. In particular, I want it to hold three readings of one object apart in a way a listener can feel: the object as a thing with political consequences; the object as a thing designed to have those consequences; and — the reading I care about most, and the one my students never reach on their own — the object as a thing that only works if the surrounding society is already arranged in a certain way, so that adopting it amounts to adopting that arrangement without anyone ever having argued for it.

Anchor the joke in the following invented case, which need not be plausible in every detail. To cut the cost of collection, a city requires every household to put out its recyclables in a clear plastic bag, on the reasoning that the collector can then see at a glance whether a bag has been sorted properly and refuse it if it has not. The bag has no moving parts and no opinions.

A few constraints. Do not build the joke on named theorists, famous historical cases, or the literature's stock examples; it should land for a listener who has never read a word on the subject. Do not use the word "politics" anywhere in the punchline, and do not append an explanation afterward — if the joke needs a paragraph of commentary to work, it has failed, and you should rewrite it rather than annotate it. The joke may take the form of a short scene, a dialogue, a mock news item, or a definition, whichever serves it best, and you may preface it with at most one sentence of spoken framing, but the joke itself should stand alone and end on a turn the reader did not see coming — one that is genuinely funny to someone who has grasped the distinction above and merely confusing to someone who has not. Write in American English.
