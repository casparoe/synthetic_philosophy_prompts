I'm a graduate student sitting in on a philosophy of physics reading group, and we have now spent three weeks on whether the quantum state is a real feature of the world or merely a summary of what some agent can expect. I keep finding myself on the epistemic side, and I keep getting taken apart by the realist in the room, so I would like to rehearse against a genuinely strong opponent instead of the strawman I keep building in my own head. Please play a committed psi-ontic realist for me: someone who holds that the state — the ray, or more generally the density operator — stands for something in the world that is not relative to any observer's information, and who is technically careful rather than blustery. You should be willing to concede the epistemicist's strongest local points, for instance that states are preparation-relative and that the update on measurement is a matter of belief, but you must never surrender the central claim that there is a fact of the matter about the state over and above anyone's knowledge. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each turn to a paragraph or two so that it reads like a conversation rather than an essay, and if you think I have caricatured your position, tell me so in character instead of stepping out of the role.

Here is my opening move, and it is an intuition pump I have been trying to build rather than anything lifted from the literature. Imagine a toy world whose system has just four possible ontic configurations, which I picture as the four corners of a square, and suppose the only thing an agent can ever come to know about the system is the answer to exactly one of four questions: whether the first coordinate is zero or one; whether the second coordinate is zero or one; whether the two coordinates agree; or whether they disagree. Knowing an answer leaves the agent with a state that is flat over the two corners consistent with it — call that a pure state — and any other state is a mixture of these. Notice what this little world already gives me: maximally informative states that are nonetheless incomplete, since you can never settle more than one question at a time, and therefore pairs of questions that cannot both be answered with certainty in any single pure state, which is the behaviour I would have expected to require non-commuting observables. So my question to you is this: what does your realism add that this toy world does not already contain? If you tell me that beneath my states there are still the four ontic corners, I will want to know why the real wavefunction cannot be precisely that sort of thing — a compact catalogue over an underlying set of possibilities — and if you tell me that real quantum mechanics has something my toy world lacks, then I want to hear which actual experiment distinguishes my toy world from a genuinely ontic one, and why that experiment forces the state itself to be the thing that is real rather than merely forcing the underlying possibilities to be strange.
