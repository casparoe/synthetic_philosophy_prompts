For about a year I have been running a reading group on cultural evolution, and we keep returning to a question that ought to have been settled long ago: whether the transmission of culture is, in any interesting sense, a Darwinian process. The disagreement is not about the phenomena. Everyone at the table accepts the same stock of findings — that cultural variants are transmitted with uneven fidelity, that some spread and others vanish, that much copying is reconstructive rather than mechanical, and that recognisably similar forms recur across unrelated populations. What divides us is what all of that shows. One faction holds that once you have heritable variation plus differential retention you have selection, and that the cultural case is therefore Darwinian in the thin sense that matters; the interesting work is then measuring transmission biases, migration and drift. Another faction insists that the real contrast is between transmission that is selectional, in which variants are preserved and propagated because they are copied, and transmission that is transformational, in which variants are drawn toward a small set of stable forms by shared cognitive dispositions, so that the population-level distribution is a summary of individual reconstruction rather than a mechanism in its own right; on that view selection talk is at best bookkeeping. A third position, which I suspect is where the trouble actually lies, grants the selectionist its formal conditions but denies that they explain anything unless transmission is faithful enough to sustain cumulative adaptation — and this is met with the reply that the objector is confusing the fidelity of the representation with the fidelity of the result. One colleague in the group, a historian, thinks both sides are simply imposing a biological template on something that is not a transmission process at all. The word meme has largely been retired in favour of cultural variant, but the dispute has survived the rebranding intact.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that this is not one disagreement but three wearing the same coat: a partly verbal dispute about how thin a notion of the Darwinian we are willing to countenance, a genuinely empirical dispute about the fidelity and structure of transmission, and a methodological dispute about what an evolutionary explanation of culture is supposed to deliver — the distribution of variants, or cumulative adaptive complexity, or something else again. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey, and I want it to engage the positions as I have stated them. Tell me which parts of the impasse would dissolve under a stipulation and which are merely being concealed by one; which parts are empirical and what measurement or observation would actually settle them; and which parts rest on explanatory standards that neither side can argue for from within the dispute. Where the two camps are answering different questions under a single heading, say which question each is really answering. Then tell me what progress would look like here: a formal result about how much fidelity a selectionist explanation requires, a decisive empirical measure of transmission, a distinction that would let the two camps stop contradicting each other, a replacement question — or an honest argument that no progress should be expected, and why.
