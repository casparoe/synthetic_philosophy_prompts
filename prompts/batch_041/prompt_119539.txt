I am a high school student preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club, and I would like you to explain a conceptual issue in philosophy of mind. The issue is the paradox of phenomenal judgment as it arises for epiphenomenalism. Epiphenomenalism says that conscious experiences are caused by physical brain events but do not themselves cause anything. The paradox is that if experiences are causally inert, then our judgments and reports about them cannot be caused by the experiences they are supposedly about, which makes it mysterious how those judgments could be about the experiences at all. I would like you to explain the paradox clearly, then assess the standard reply that a single neural event causes both the experience and the judgment, so the judgment can be about the experience even though the experience does not cause the judgment. Does that reply actually resolve the paradox, or does it merely relocate the problem by making the judgment about a neural state rather than about the experience? I am especially interested in a variant of the reply: suppose a neural event N causes a nonphysical experience E, and N separately causes a verbal report R; E and R are reliably correlated, but E does no causal work. What would have to be true for R to count as being about E rather than merely about N? If aboutness can be inherited from N, what is the relation that does the inheriting, and does it do any explanatory work? Please also consider the strongest version of the objection and the strongest response, and explain whether epiphenomenalism can survive the paradox or whether it must give up something important. I do not need citations, a reading list, or references to particular authors; I want the conceptual explanation itself. Please write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
