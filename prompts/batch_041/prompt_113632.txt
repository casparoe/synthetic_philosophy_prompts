Bit of an odd one, sorry in advance. I'm doing a philosophy MA part time and I pay the rent writing marketing copy for a small insurance outfit, so I want to kill two birds for a seminar paper: a satirical piece about pragmatic encroachment that also works as a parody of the insurance genre. The premise is simple and I want whoever writes this to commit to it all the way: if how much is at stake for you can change whether you know something, then stakes are a resource, resources get priced, and somebody will sell insurance against them. So give me the marketing and policy documents of a fictional insurer, Fortify Knowledge Assurance Ltd., which has built its entire product line on taking encroachment literally. Corporate deadpan throughout, no winking, no "of course this is absurd" asides -- a reader should be able to tell what's being ironized from the documents alone. Aim for 700-800 words, formatted as a numbered policy with clauses plus a short FAQ, and maybe a sidebar on the rival firm.

Things I'd like in there, because the humor has to come from the epistemology being right: a tiered schedule where the same belief -- say, that the branch is open on Saturday, which is where all of this started for me -- costs alot more to insure when more hangs on it, with a cheap teller-window tier and a pricey claims-desk tier, the copy insisting all the while that nothing about the customer's evidence has changed, only the stakes. A stake-reduction rider, which is the joke I care most about: for a fee Fortify will lower your stakes (buy the spare tire, move the wedding, hire someone else to do the caring) so that the evidence you already had becomes sufficient, and the FAQ should present this as the thrifty alternative to gathering more evidence, which is expensive. An actionability clause: no payout if you failed to act on the belief, since you can't have known the bridge was out if you drove onto it, and also no payout if you acted and it worked, since that's evidence you knew, and knowledge is not an insurable loss. A stakes questionnaire customers have to fill in, including a question about how much is at stake in their answers to the questionnaire. And Context Mutual, the rival, who keep telling customers that nothing changed except the meaning of the word "know" -- Fortify's copy should be politely contemptuous about this, without ever admitting that a fair number of philosophers would say Context Mutual is right.

All the standard objections should be present but never stated: knowledge turning into something you can buy, knowledge becoming a matter of prudence rather than truth, whose stakes actually count (the bystander's? the insurer's?), where the threshold sits and who sets it, and the looming regress about the stakes involved in settling the stakes. If someone who's read the encroachment literature laughs and finds it fair rather than a strawman, I've won; if a reader who's never heard any of this can still follow the bit, better still. No names of living philosophers, invented colleagues are fine, and please don't let the company ever notice the reductio.
