I write novels, and for the past few years I have kept a second notebook in which I try to say what I take myself to be doing when I make a person out of sentences. The notebook has grown long enough that I can no longer tell whether it records one position or four, so I am asking you for an audit rather than an essay.

There is a fact of the matter about what a character would do, and it is a fact I find rather than invent. When a draft goes wrong it usually goes wrong because I have made her do something she would not do, and the correction feels like noticing that a sum is off. I mean this even about situations the book never shows: what she would have done if the letter had arrived on Tuesday is not up to me. The hardest and best case is the unreliable narrator, who lies about herself and is wrong about herself, and whose being wrong is something the reader is meant to be able to see.

A character becomes real to the extent that a short description of her generates the right behavior in situations I never wrote. Realism, in the sense I care about, is compressibility: if you can say enough about her in few enough words that her behavior falls out, she exists; if you need a separate clause for every scene, she does not. And I do not think there is anything further that has to be there — no inner person, no extra ingredient that the compression keeps failing to capture.

The instrument I use is the intentional stance, one of the three Dennett distinguishes alongside the physical and the design stance. I treat her as an agent with beliefs and desires, and the ascription is normative: to say that she believes it is raining is to say what she ought to do, given everything else she wants, if she is at all reasonable. Belief and desire talk presupposes a floor of rationality; below that floor it is no longer belief talk.

And yet the characters I care most about are the ones the instrument fails on: the woman who acts against what her own desires recommend, the man who cannot stop, the person who destroys the thing she has arranged her whole life to keep. Akrasia and compulsion are not decorations in my work; they are the work. The stance is least useful precisely where I find the book.

I am a physicalist. The physical stance tells you what there really is; the design and intentional stances are ways of getting a purchase on it, and the world does not gain an extra storey when a pattern becomes available.

Finally, I do not believe in a self over and above the pattern. There is no inner author, no small novelist in the skull, and I do not experience this as a loss; it seems to me the plain truth about what people are.

What I want from you is this: which of these pull against each other, which only appear to, and how bad each genuine conflict is. Rank them rather than handing me a list of equal grievances. For each real conflict, tell me what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs, and tell me which single change would resolve the most. I would rather be told that one item has to go than that everything can be reconciled with sufficient care. Since I can check none of this against anything but my own ear, also tell me where your reasoning is thinnest, and what you would have to see — some case, some distinction — to reach a different verdict about which conflict matters most. Write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter; no headings, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables.
