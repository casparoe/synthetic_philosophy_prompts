I'm a second-year PhD student in philosphy, working on dreams and phenomenal consciousness, and next month I'm giving a work-in-progress paper to our mind and cognition group. My thesis is a version of Malcolm's old line -- that dreams aren't experiences -- but I've tried to give it a new spine. I call it the waking receipt account: sleep mentation happens, sure, but there is no dream experience that gets stored and later remembered. What we call a dream is a memory-like state constructed at or after waking, a kind of receipt for a transaction that never had a phenomenal side. I know that sounds like Dennett's cassette theory, and in some moods I think it is, but I want to push it further: the dream report isn't a report of an experience; it's the waking construction of the very thing it seems to report. My supervisor is an ordinary-language person and thinks I'm being too concessive to the sleep lab; the sleep lab thinks I'm doing armchair nonsense. I'm kinda dreading the Q&A.

The audience is a specific mix, not a generic philosophy-of-mind crowd: two ordinary-language philosophers who cut their teeth on Malcolm and Ryle and think experience is a term with a grammar rather than a natural kind; a predictive-processing/active-inference person who thinks waking perception is controlled hallucination and will therefore find my dream/waking distinction arbitrary; a phenomenologist who works on imagination and will ask what separates dreaming from vivid imagining or from the as-if mode; and a sleep scientist who runs a lucid-dreaming EEG study and will say that lucid dreamers can signal in real time, so there is a subject in there having experiences. Please draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this room -- not the ones I'd get from a generic audience -- ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that person would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front so I don't look evasive, and what to hold even if it costs me the room. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and be honest about the weakest point in my own position -- the one I should prepare to defend rather than pray they miss. I'd especially like the predictive-processing person's objection in their own vocabulary (priors, generative model, etc.) and the sleep scientist's in theirs (signal, report, awakening, etc.), because I keep answering the philosopher's version of their objection and getting killed on the empirical version. Write it in British English, please. Don't give me a reading list or citations -- I know the literature; I need the Q&A.
