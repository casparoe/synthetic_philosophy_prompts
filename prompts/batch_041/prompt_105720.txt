OK so I've talked myself into a fairly strong version of the meme view: that culture is a genuinely Darwinian process and not just a useful analogy, that memes are real replicators, that cultural change is largely natural selection acting on them, and — this is the part I actually care about — that you don't get to assume high-fidelity copying up front. Fidelity is itself something selection can build, an adaptation of the meme rather than a precondition for the whole story.

What I want from you is the case against that. Not the lazy objection that memes aren't real and it's all just a metaphor — I mean the real defeaters. Which empirical findings, which arguments, which formal results would actually count against the strong claim, and how decisive is each? Rank them, roughly fatal down to merely awkward. I also want to know which of these a defender of my view would themselves concede count against them, versus which they'd wave away, and whether the waving-away is legitimate or just saving the hypothesis.

Two things I'm specifically worried about. First, the idea that cultural transmission is reconstructive rather than copying — that what looks like selection is really attraction to certain forms, so the variation isn't blind and selection isn't doing the work. Second, whether anyone has measured copying fidelity in an actual transmission chain (lab or field) and gotten a number low enough that selection couldn't outrun drift and mutation anyway. If you think either of those sinks the view, say so rather than being diplomatic.

And be honest about the bootstrap problem: if fidelity is defined as an output of selection, and selection needs fidelity to get going, does the view risk becoming unfalsifiable? If it does, is that fatal, or just the price any theory pays when it has to account for its own preconditions? Tell me what would have to be observed for me to have to give this up.
