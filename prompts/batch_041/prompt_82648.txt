I collect odd bits of writing about art crime, and I've got a pattern I cant find a word for -- or not one that fits.

Here's the thing. When a fake gets exposed, something strange happens to its price. Not always, but often: the object that was worth a fortune as an original is worth nothing as a fake for about five minutes, and then it comes back up, sometimes higher than it would have been if it had been genuine, because now it's *the* object that fooled the experts. The deception itself has become the asset. I've seen this with old forgeries that sat in museums for decades, and with more recent ones where the entire appeal at auction was that this was the one that got past the lab for eleven years.

What's odd is that it isn't the skill. Some of these things are mediocre paintings. It isn't the age or the period either -- there are a million objects from the same year that nobody wants. And it definitely isn't the aura of the original, since the whole point is that its not one.

My first thought was that it's just the guitar thing: people pay more for a guitar because a famous person smashed it on stage, value by association with a notable event. But that doesn't quite do it, because here the notable event is a mistake, and specifically a mistake made by people whose job was to know better. There also seems to be a sweet spot. If the forgery is too good it just gets filed as a good painting of the period and loses the magic; if it was obviously bad, nobody was ever fooled and there's no magic either. The value peaks when it fooled the right people for the right amount of time.

And there's a cousin that makes me doubt it's all one thing: a painting downgraded from the master to studio of, whose price falls, and which then a certain kind of collector wants *because* it's the one that used to hang as the master. No deception there, just an error that everyone now agrees on.

So is there a name for this? I know the general neighbourhood -- authenticity, aura, whether a perfect forgery can be as good as the original -- but all of that is about the original, or about the fake before it's exposed. I'm after the specific thing where the exposure is what makes it valuable. If there's no name, coin one, but give me the edges: is it a species of the celebrity-guitar thing or is there something left over that that doesn't cover? How is it different from the teaching-collection case, where a museum keeps a fake to show students how fakes fool people? Does the cousin belong with it, or does deliberate deception have to be in there somewhere? And what do you call the sweet spot itself?

A bare name won't help me, I need the carving.
