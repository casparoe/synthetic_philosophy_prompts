Ok so this is a slightly weird request, but bear with me. I'm a novelist — two books out, neither of them bestsellers, currently behind on a deadline I keep pretending I'm not behind on — and I keep getting into the same stupid argument at family dinners, and it's driving me a little nuts. The other week at my aunt's place out in the county, my cousin (an engineer, comfortable, likes his job fine) heard me mention a friend who left a stable union gig to "follow her passion" and is now broke and doing people's taxes for cash, and he told me I was missing the point, because — his words — do what you love and you'll never work a day in your life. He said it like it settled everything, like my friend had obviously made the right call and the broke part was just a bad attitude. And I sort of grunted, because I couldn't say what I thought was wrong with it. I'm writing a novel right now with a home health aide in it, a woman who does a job she doesn't love and does it well, and I keep circling this exact thing, so I'd definately like to get it straight, not just win the argument.

What I want from you is a menu. Take that one sentence — do what you love and you'll never work a day in your life — and lay out the different precise things it could actually be claiming. Not the nice fuzzy version. The whole spread, from the reading that's basically harmless advice about picking a job you don't actively hate, through the reading where "work" just gets quietly redefined so that anything you enjoy can't count as work anymore, up to the reading where it's a moral demand on people, or a claim about how a society should be arranged, or something that only even makes sense to say if your work is already the kind that pays well and doesn't wreck your body. For each one, tell me whether it's true, or defensible, or just a platitude, or flat false. And then the part I actually care about: which of these readings is my cousin using? Which reading is doing the work when someone says this to shut down a conversation about a person who chose badly, or about people who never got to choose at all? Does the slogan have to be a claim about what's true of a person, or about what's true of work itself, or about what we owe each other? And is it even a real disagreement with the other thing my grandma says, work to live, don't live to work — or are those just two slogans people pick up depending on which one is convenient that day?

One more thing I keep tripping over. I've noticed that the people in my life who say do what you love all have jobs where the loving and the earning happen to line up, and the people who say work to live mostly don't. So I half suspect the whole slogan is really about class — a thing the professional-class gets to say about itself and hand down as wisdom — but I can't tell if that's a real philosophical point or just me being resentful. If there's a precise version of that suspicion, I want it; if there isn't, say so plainly.

Please write it to me like an email — flowing prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs I can read over coffee. I know a menu of readings is sort of naturally a list, but walk me through it in sentences anyway, because I'm going to steal the shape of this for a chapter and lists read like a lecture.
