I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar on free will, and I'm writing the final exam. One of the essays has to be on Frankfurt cases and the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP), and I am tired of reading answers that recite the dialectic without doing any work: students learn the shape of a Frankfurt case — an agent who acts on her own reasons while a counterfactual intervener stands ready to make her act that way should she waver — and then simply assert that responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. So over the winter break a colleague and I built a new case, and I intend to put it on the exam. Please treat every detail of it as stipulated; there is no published discussion of it and I do not want students appealing to anything outside the case. Here it is, the Auditor case. Nadia supervises the night shift at a water-treatment plant and has to decide whether to file a truthful contamination log or a falsified one that conceals a discharge. Unknown to her, an engineer has installed a monitoring system that runs a fine-grained model of her deliberation: whenever it predicts that she is about to decide to file the truthful log, it stimulates her a fraction of a second before the decision completes, so that she files the falsified log instead. The system is not perfect — its predictions come out right about nine times in ten; in the remaining cases it misses the onset of her decision or mispredicts it, and then it does nothing. On the night in question it correctly predicts that she is about to falsify the log, does nothing, and she falsifies it on her own, for her own reasons (she wants to avoid a fine and a row with her manager). Two variants may be worth mentioning in the question or in the model answer: (B) the system is infallible and the state it reads off is a deterministic one that already fixes her decision; (C) the system is infallible, but what it reads is an indeterministic neural event that does not itself settle what she will do.

Acting as an expert in the philosophy of action and free will, please write me three things. First, the exam question itself, phrased for students who know PAP and the standard Frankfurt set-up but who have never seen this case: it should ask them whether the Auditor case refutes PAP as it is usually stated, what if anything it does refute, and what the comparison with variants B and C teaches about what a Frankfurt case can show; it should be answerable in about 75 minutes and should not be answerable by listing familiar objections. Second, a model answer of roughly 750 words that a strong student could plausibly produce; it must not depend on remembering what any particular philosopher said, and it should contain no quotations, citations, or reading lists. Third, a marking scheme worth 20 marks, built on five or six must-hit points, each with the marks it carries and a one-line note on how candidates typically miss it; make clear which points separate an A from a B. Before you write, think the case through carefully — in particular, get clear on what it takes for the ability to do otherwise to make PAP a substantive principle, and on whether the probabilistic structure of the Auditor case is a defect in it or the whole point of it — and feel free to jot a short outline before drafting. Keep everything self-contained: no appeals to what the literature says, and nothing in quotation marks.
