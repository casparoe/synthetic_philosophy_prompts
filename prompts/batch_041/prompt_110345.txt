I'm about 200 pages into a novel set in Brussels. My narrator is a mid-level translator at the Commission who slowly works out that her whole job is keeping one sentence alive. And I keep hitting the same image — everyone there talks about the EU as a bicycle: integration is a bicycle, you have to keep peddaling or you fall off. It gets deployed to shut down any suggestion of a pause, a consolidation, even just a slow year. No such thing, you're either moving or you're on the ground. Someone said it at a dinner and three people nodded like it was physics. The word they use for the other thing is spillover, which I notice is a hydraulic image, and now I can't stop seeing the whole discourse as a plumbing diagram.

I want to audit it properly, because I think the book is secretly about this and I don't want to be the novelist who just repeats the dinner party. So: what maps onto what — is the rider the member states, the Commission, or history? Where does the bicycle break down as a picture of a political order? Bicycles coast downhill. Bicycles stop at red lights and nobody says they've fallen over. A bicycle has a destination someone chose, and one set of handlebars, not 27 people arguing about the direction. What does the image quietly import that a political order doesn't have — a single will, a gradient, a road already there?

And the bit that actually matters to me: the conclusion it licenses. That stasis is catastrophic, so ever closer union isn't a choice anybody makes, it's a physical necessity of the vehicle. Does that survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? I've definately heard the same bicycle used about trade rounds — if so, do the two uses stand or fall together?

My wife is a marine biologist, hates bicycles, and says it's obviously a coral reef: it grows only at the edges, the middle is dead coral, and there's no rider at all. Is that a better analogy or just a nicer one? Give it the same treatment.

Last thing, for the other thread in the book, the democratic deficit stuff. My translator keeps getting told the EU is a club of states, not a country of citizens, so the deficit isn't a failure of democracy, it's a category error to expect one. How far does that hold, and where's the line between it being a different kind of thing and it being an excuse?

I'm not writing a thesis here, so no reading lists — just the audit.
