I'm in a grad seminar on consequentialism and we spent a whole session last week on cluelessness — the idea that because the causal ripples of what we do spread out forever (I hold a door for someone, that changes when they catch their bus, who they end up sitting beside, who they marry, what their grandchildren do...), the consequences that really determine whether an act was good aren't just unknown to us but mostly unknowable, and not because we lack computing power, but because the relevant facts are dispersed and chaotically sensitive to small diffrences. I want to write a dialogue between two consequentialists who both take that description seriously but draw opposite morals from it. M thinks it's close to fatal: once you see how much of an act's value is hidden, maximizing expected value starts to look like a slogan, and M wants to save consequentialism by moving the unit of evaluation up — rules, dispositions, institutions, whole ways of life — or else by some satisficing or robust-choice story. N thinks that's a panic response and that cluelessness is at worst an engineering problem, with one central move: you never need to know the absolute value of an option, only the sign of the difference between the options you're choosing between, and if the unknowable downstream effects of the two options are diffuse they'll largely cancel; so all you need is a defensible account of the tractable, local differences.

M should push hard on that rather than let N have it: why assume the unknown effects are symmetric instead of a skewed tail, and if changing your model of how the causal chain runs can flip the sign of the difference, isn't the difference just as clueless as the total? M might also add that if you take expected value literally over an open-ended outcome space, the ranking gets dominated by gigantic remote possibilities, which is another way of saying it can't guide anything. N should have real replies — robustness across models, or shifting to a level of description where the difference is stable, or biting the bullet that guidance just is hard — and neither of them should win by being a strawman. Don't let them just trade abstractions either: have them thread two cases through the whole exchange, one small and one big — whether to stop and give a stranger directions on the street, and whether a donor should fund deworming in a poor country or research into reducing the suffering of wild animals — and make each of them say what they'd actually do differently, if anything, from a cluelessness-aware standpoint.

End with them trying to say precisely where they part company: is this really a disagreement about decision theory under deep uncertainty, or about what makes acts right in the first place, or about which unit of evaluation is even in question? And please don't bother with who said what or with citations, I just want the argument.
