A colleague in our statistics reading group has produced an argument that I cannot break, and I would like to know whether to accept its conclusion or to identify the step that fails.

She puts two hospital audits side by side. In the first, patients given a new drug died at a higher rate than those who did not (60 deaths among 200 treated, 40 among 200 untreated). Split by the severity score recorded on admission, before any treatment was assigned, the drug looks better in both bands (mild: 2 of 50 treated against 12 of 150 untreated; severe: 58 of 150 against 28 of 50). In the second audit the drug looks better overall (50 deaths among 200 treated, 74 among 200 untreated), yet among patients still alive on day three it looks worse (30 of 180 against 14 of 140), and in the band that did not survive to day three both arms stand at 100% mortality.

Her argument: (1) In the first audit the pooled table and the split disagree; in the second they disagree in the opposite direction. (2) Both audits present the same kind of object, a set of counts plus a choice of partition. (3) Nothing in the counts marks one partition as the correct one; the numbers do not arrive stamped 'confounder' or 'collider'. (4) So in both audits the partition is chosen by the analyst's prior story rather than by the data. (5) A claim whose truth depends on a story the analyst brings is a convention, not a claim about the world. (6) Hence neither 'the drug lowers mortality' nor 'the drug raises mortality' is true or false, and the same holds for any claim of the form 'X raises the probability of Y'. (7) Causal inference is therefore the imposition of a convention rather than the discovery of a fact.

She adds that she has heard the standard reply — condition on the confounder — and that it begs the question, since nothing in the counts says which variable the confounder is. I want to keep (1) and (2); the arithmetic is not in dispute and the reversals are real. But (7) makes it a matter of taste whether the drug kills people, and it does the same to the claim that smoking causes cancer, which I take to be a reductio. Which step should I give up? Say why it looked acceptable, and tell me what giving it up costs — what else I must then concede, and where the argument still has force after the repair.
