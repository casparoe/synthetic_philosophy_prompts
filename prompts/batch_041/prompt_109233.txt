ok, I need help with a speech. My best friend's wedding is in three weeks and I'm the best man. He and his fiancée met at a LessWrong meetup years back, so half that room is people from the local rationalist scene and the other half is her family from rural Wisconsin who have never heard any of this and don't especially want to. She pulled me aside and said: no jargon. Which, fair.

Here's what I want to build the speech around, and I need you to actually get it structurally, not just name-drop it. There's this puzzle I've been chewing on since forever — the absent-minded driver. You're driving home along a road with two intersections, and they look identical from the driver's seat; you can't tell which one you're at, or whether you've already passed the first. Getting off at the first is bad (call it 0), getting off at the second is what you want (4), and sailing past both is mildly bad (1). The answer everyone converges on is that the best you can do is continue with probability two-thirds at each intersection — which means that when you're sitting at the second one, where exiting is plainly better, the right thing to do is usually keep going. That's the bit I find genuinely weird: the optimal policy requires doing the worse thing at one of the two nodes, and from the inside you can never tell which node you're at.

Why this is a wedding speech: these two spent something like four years parked at the first intersection. Should we, should we not, is now the moment, what if it isn't — endless re-derivation. What actually got them out wasn't a better answer at any particular moment. It was giving up on the moment-by-moment question and picking a rule they couldn't re-litigate from inside it. I want to say something like: the rule that works is one you'd flinch at if you stared at it from the wrong node. I'm definately not a philosopher, I just read a lot of blog posts, so I also want to be honest that this is my reading, and that "commitment is the answer" might be the boring reading of the puzzle — I don't want to stand up there and say the boring thing, or the false thing.

So: write the speech. Four minutes or so, warm, funny, followable by someone who has never thought about any of this, and not embarrassing for the people in the room who have. The puzzle should be the spine — it should shape what I can and can't say, not decorate it. Don't say decision theory, expected value, or priors out loud. Don't let it become a lecture, and please don't end on so, kids, precommitment. The two-thirds thing can appear if you can make it land as a joke; I keep trying and I can't tell whether it's funny or just baffling, so use your judgement.

After the speech, add a short note addressed to me only, not for the mic. I want to know what I'd be getting wrong if I said all that: which parts are actually settled among people who know this puzzle and which are genuinely contested. Is continue with probability two-thirds the standard answer, or one camp's answer? Is my reading — that the point is you have to adopt a policy you'd disavow at one of the nodes — a normal thing to say about it, or a misreading that someone in the audience would wince at? And is it fair to use this as a metaphor for a relationship at all, or am I over-reading a toy problem with fixed numbers? Separate the settled from the contested, and don't just tell me it's all up for debate — I'd rather know which parts I can say with a straight face.
