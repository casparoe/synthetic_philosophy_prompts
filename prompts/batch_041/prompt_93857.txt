My two housemates Nadia and Oscar got into a genuinely nasty argument in our group chat last week about the large worlds / evidential cooperation thing, and since I'm the one who got both of them into the rationalist blogs in the first place I've been drafted as referee. Which I'm not qualified for, so I'm outsourcing to you.

Quick recap of what they were actually fighting about, in case it isn't obvious: if the world is big enough — many copies of you, simulators running your source code, lots of agents instantiated from decision procedures like yours — then you can get "cooperation" with people you'll never meet and can't causally affect, because your action is evidence about what they'll do rather than a cause of it. Standard test case: one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger, no repeat, no reputation, no causal channel. Causal decision theory says defect. The evidential / large-world view says that in a big enough world, if there are enough other agents whose choice is tied to yours by a shared decision procedure, cooperating can be right, because your defection is a sample of the whole class of you.

Oscar thinks the whole thing is wishful thinking in a lab coat. Nadia thinks Oscar is refusing to follow the logic where it goes. Here's how it went, near as I can reconstruct it:

1) Nadia: in a big enough world the thing that gets instantiated isn't you, it's your policy. If a bunch of agents out there are running something like my procedure, then my defecting isn't one defection, it's a sample from a policy that shows up many times over, and summed across that class, cooperating beats defecting. So cooperating in a one-shot PD isn't saintliness, it's prudence for the class I'm a member of.

2) Oscar: that only works if the correlation is strong, and a large world makes it weaker, not stronger — for every agent running my procedure there are astronomically many who aren't, and the stranger across the table is probably not a cousin of my code. And if the world is that big, there's probably some agent out there whose payoffs are anti-correlated with mine and who profits when I cooperate. Worse, any agent who can predict or simulate me can now count on my cooperation, which turns my policy into a public resource that I pay for. "I cooperate because somewhere a copy of me is being nice to someone" is a great way to get farmed.

3) Nadia: you're running together evidence and control. Simulating me doesn't let anyone make me cooperate — it just means there's another instance of me, and it'll do what I do. And I'm not cooperating because of what my copies get up to; I'm cooperating because defecting makes the world contain more defection by me, and the payoffs of those instances are the payoffs at stake. If your fix is to be unpredictable so nobody can farm you, you're choosing to be a worse cooperator in every actual coordination problem in order to dodge a hypothetical farmer.

4) Oscar: fine, here's the strong version, and I think it kills it. First, sharing a procedure doesn't mean sharing an action — if the counterpart faces even a slightly different game or has slightly different information, the link to my choice evaporates, and in a messy big world most near-copies are near-copies facing different games. Second, your principle has no stopping rule. Suppose a hostile agent arranges for copies of me to face a game where my cooperating pays them. Do I now owe them cooperation? You'll say only genuine procedure-sharing counts — but that's exactly what they manufactured. Third, your view makes my cooperation depend on facts about the multiverse I can't check, so a well-crafted threat ("we have your code and our payoff tracks your choice") extracts cooperation from me under your principle, whereas under mine I just don't cooperate.

5) Nadia: the stopping rule is the counterfactual, not the resemblance. What binds me is that my action and the counterpart's come out of the same computation, so whatever I do is what happens in that slot — defecting buys nothing there. Someone announcing they're running me doesn't change my computation, it just adds an agent betting on it. And the near-copy point doesn't refute the principle, it just means I weigh how much of the link survives, which is what any decision under uncertainty looks like.
