An eccentric donor offers a research institute a large endowment on the condition that every member of its ethics committee sincerely believes, for a full year, that his amateur treatise on distributive justice is correct; the members can induce the belief by hypnotic suggestion, and they know it will lapse once the year is out. The case is a variant of the wrong kind of reasons problem, and five responses to that problem are set out below without their usual labels. Rank the five from most to least adequate, where adequacy requires both doing justice to the intuition that the donor's offer is no reason for the belief and giving a principled account of what a reason for an attitude is rather than stipulating one. Justify each placement in a sentence, and close with a sentence naming the one response you would defend if you had to defend one.

1. Only considerations that bear on the correctness of an attitude are reasons for it. The offer bears on the costs and benefits of holding the belief, not on its correctness, so it is at most a reason to want the belief, not a reason for it.

2. Every attitude has a constitutive aim — truth for belief, the object's merit for admiration — and the aim fixes which considerations can be reasons for the attitude. An offer cannot be registered by an attitude whose aim is truth, so it is not a reason for the belief.

3. There are no reasons for attitudes at all. Talk of a reason to believe or to admire is an ellipsis for a reason to bring it about that one believes or admires, and the case shows only that the committee may have such a reason.

4. The offer is a reason for the act of cultivating the belief, not a reason for the belief itself. Since the members can cultivate it at will, the offer gives them a reason to do something while leaving the epistemic standing of the belief untouched.

5. The offer is a genuine reason for the belief, and the intuition that it cannot be is an artefact of conflating the fittingness of an attitude with the reasons there are for holding it. Once the two are separated, it is the intuition, not the offer, that has to go.
