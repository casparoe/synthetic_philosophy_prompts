I am a novelist, and in a fortnight I am to interview on stage, at a literary festival, a philosopher who defends a teleosemantic account of mental content — roughly, the thesis that a state represents whatever it is the biological function of that state to indicate. I want eight to ten questions that press the position rather than let her rehearse it. Include at least one on the indeterminacy of content: what fixes a state as being about one condition rather than a more proximal or disjunctive condition that equally reliably produces it. Include one on misrepresentation: how a state can be false when its content is set by what it is supposed to indicate. Include one on whether the explanatory work is done by the history of selection or by the way the state is currently put to use by the systems that consume it. Anchor the last third of the questions in the central case of the novel I am writing — a fictional moth species whose ancestors steered by moonlight and whose descendants now circle streetlamps, so that what the state was selected to track and what now triggers it have come apart. I want to know what her theory says the moth's state represents, and whether that verdict is a success or an embarrassment for the theory. For each question give a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Take a definite view: tell me which questions you think actually bite and where the standard teleosemantic reply is weakest, rather than handing me a neutral menu. Open with the single question you judge most likely to expose a weakness, then give the full set with your reasons for each. Please write in British English.
