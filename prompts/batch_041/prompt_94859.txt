I am a sixth-form student taking an A-level in Government and Politics, and for the past few weeks our class has been reading Tocqueville's Democracy in America; ever since, I have been arguing about it with my friends in the debating society, and we have reached an impasse that I would like your help in resolving. I would like you to act as a historian of political thought who specialises in nineteenth-century liberalism, and to reason carefully rather than reaching for the obvious answer. Before you write anything, please brainstorm: set out what you take to be the core of Tocqueville's diagnosis of modern democratic society, and in particular his fear that democratic peoples might slide into a mild, tutelary, administrative despotism — a central power that manages their affairs for them, soothes and directs them, and leaves them free in form but unfree in substance — and then use that diagnosis as the lens for the question below.

Here is the question. Tocqueville died long before any of the arrangements I am about to describe existed, so he had no view of them; what I want from you is a prediction, argued as faithfully as you can manage, of what he would have thought. The question is how much citizens should be permitted to hide from the state, and there are three contemporary answers to it that I would like you to place side by side. In Germany there is a strong constitutional tradition of data protection, in which control over one's own personal data is treated as part of one's personality and as a precondition of democratic self-government, so that the state's power to gather information about its citizens is tightly constrained. In the United States there is a much more open tradition, in which citizens are expected to be transparent to one another and to their government, and in which privacy protections are comparatively thin — information that a person voluntarily hands to a bank, a telephone company or a shop, for instance, has traditionally enjoyed little shelter from the state's gaze. In China there is a system of social credit, in which the state aggregates data about its citizens in order to assess their trustworthiness, so that it is the citizen who is highly visible to the state rather than the state being visible to the citizen.

Which of these, if any, would Tocqueville have recognised as continuous with his own worries, and which would have surprised or baffled him? Would he read the German emphasis on privacy as a bulwark against the administrative despotism he feared, or as a withdrawal into private life that starves the public spiritedness he believed democracy required? Would he see the American tradition of openness as the realisation of his own ideal, or as something quite different from it? And would he treat social credit as the fulfilment of his nightmare, or as a category mistake — the wrong shape of threat altogether? Please weigh at least two plausible predictions before you settle on one, and give me a genuine judgement rather than a survey of what commentators have said. Where his categories fit the case, explain why; where they do not, say so plainly instead of forcing them; and flag the moments at which you are extrapolating from him rather than reporting him. Write it as a short, well-organised essay in British English, and outline your reasoning before you commit to a conclusion.
