So last Saturday I spent six hours knocking doors for a city ballot measure that lost by 43 votes, and then at dinner my brother-in-law (econ PhD student, insufferable) explained to me that voting is irrational — the odds my vote decides anything are about the odds of getting struck by lightning while holding a winning scratchcard, and here's the part that actually got me, it doesn't get better if you're voting for other people's sake rather than your own. And every fix I've read since seems to keep his calculus and just bolt a new term onto it: minimax regret, voting as a consumption good, voting because you're part of a group that reasons collectively, voting because your vote still moves the margin and the margin is the thing everyone cites afterwards, voting out of duty. My partner sat through all of this and now says she's not sure she's voting in November, and honestly I couldn't argue with her, which is not a feeling I expected to have at 34.

I write a low-stakes advice column and I want to answer this in it, but as satire — an actual agony-aunt letter plus a deadpan reply, no winking, no 'but of course voting matters, kids'. The letter is from a woman who has quietly lost respect for her husband for voting every single year; the reply should take the paradox of voting at full strength and then give each of those standard replies its best straight-faced shot, in the voice of a columnist who sincerely means to be helpful. The thing I want the satire to exaggerate is the whole genre of fixing the paradox by keeping the expected-utility frame and adding one more term — so someone who knows this literature should definately watch every move get made correctly and still see the letter-writer's problem standing there at the end. No citations, no names of scholars, no footnotes. Around 600 words, letter plus reply, funny to someone who's read the stuff and followable by someone who hasn't.
