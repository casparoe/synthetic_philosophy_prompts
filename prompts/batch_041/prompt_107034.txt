I sit on the exhibits committee at a small county historical society, and we've got an exhibit opening in six weeks on the towns founding. The draft wall text says the founders "built a community on the principle that ordinary people could govern themselves, a principle they believed was unique in the world" -- except the founders were a religious congregation whose leader picked the trustees and the minister held veto power, so the self-government bit is shaky, and one of our board members keeps insisting that the label has to say which kind of claim it's making: that the founders believed their principles were unique, that the principles actually were unique, or that being the first to put them into practice makes this place a model for everyone else. The director loves the draft as is; two board members want the word unique struck entirely; the family paying for the gallery has let it be known they'd like something uplifting and have already asked to see a proof. I need to circulate a short decision memo before Thursdays meeting: what the label should actually say (give me the wording), and a recommendation with a deadline for who signs off. Don't just split the difference -- make the reasoning explicit, including the strongest version of the argument on the side you come down against, and say plainly what we'd be asserting about the past versus what we'd be endorsing.
