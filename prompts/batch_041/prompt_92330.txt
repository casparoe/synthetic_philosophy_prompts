I am a third-year student reading history and philosophy of science, and I am currently taking a module on theory change that runs from Popper through Kuhn to Lakatos; the methodology of scientific research programmes is the part of the syllabus I keep returning to, partly because it seems to me the only one of the three that describes how research actually feels from the inside. The module is assessed by a piece of writing in any genre I like, on condition that it demonstrates real command of the methodology, and I would rather attempt a sustained satire than another conventional essay. What I want is an in-universe administrative document from a world in which Lakatosian appraisal is not a philosopher's retrospective reconstruction but the statutory basis on which research money is awarded: a Programme Appraisal Form, meaning a numbered instrument with headings, a scoring rubric and a short covering memo from the case officer, of roughly 700 words in total, written throughout in the flat, unloved register of genuine funding paperwork, so that nothing in it announces itself as a joke.

The humour has to come from getting the machinery right rather than from generic mockery of bureaucracy. I want the form to presuppose, without ever explaining, the hard core that is exempted from criticism by methodological decision and the negative heuristic that forbids directing refutations at it; the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that absorbs the anomalies instead; the positive heuristic that supplies the programme's research agenda; the distinction between progressive and degenerating problem-shifts, with theoretical progressiveness meaning that a shift predicts novel facts and empirical progressiveness meaning that some of those predictions are corroborated; the criterion by which one programme supersedes another, namely that the rival explains the predecessor's previous successes, absorbs its belt and exhibits heuristic superiority; and the point that all of this can be adjudicated only in hindsight, by rational reconstruction, so that there is no such thing as an instant verdict. It would also be a nice touch to work in the mathematical side of the methodology — the repertoire of responses to a counterexample, from monster-barring through exception-barring to the incorporation of a hidden lemma. The form should be engineered so that its own fields cannot do what the methodology demands. The verdict must be entered prospectively, while the criteria are retrospective. The section recording anomalies should specify that each entry is automatically carried forward into the section listing research tasks, so that no entry can ever count against the programme. The section headed hard core should carry a printed instruction that it is never to be completed. The section requiring the officer to list the programme's novel facts should require certification of their novelty at a date before anyone could know it. I would like at least three or four more such traps of your own invention, and a set of marginal notes in the officer's hand that are more tired than the form is.

A few constraints. Please invent the programme and its field — some fictional subdiscipline of marine biology or acoustics, whatever suits — so that nothing in the piece depends on a real scientific claim being true, and do not put real people in it, living or dead; the target is the practice, not a person: programmes that immunise their core, and an audit culture that demands prospective verdicts from retrospective criteria. Do not name Lakatos or any of the technical vocabulary inside the document itself, since in that world the methodology is simply the rules; the reader who knows the theory should be able to map every joke onto a specific element of it, and the reader who does not should still find the thing funny and never feel that the author is winking. No footnotes, no citations — I will supply the scholarly apparatus separately in the commentary that has to accompany the piece. My seminar tutor is a rather stern Popperian, so the piece had better not look like a complaint that unfalsifiable ideas go unfunded; it should be a straight-faced demonstration of what happens when they are funded properly.
