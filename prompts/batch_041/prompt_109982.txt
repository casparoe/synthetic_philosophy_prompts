In my school's environmental ethics club, two members argued for a full hour after our district announced that it would stop fogging the streets at dusk with an adulticide and instead treat the storm drains and the pond behind the athletic fields with a larvicide that kills mosquito larvae in the water. I stayed out of it, and I would like you to adjudicate their exchange move by move.

Jonah said the larvicide was the cleanest version of mosquito control imaginable, since nothing that would ever have bitten a person is harmed: the district kills them before they can bite, so the problem is solved without anyone suffering. Amara said this was confused — a larva and the adult it would have become are the same individual, so if killing the adult biter would be justified then the larva is fair game too, and if it wouldn't be, killing it earlier doesn't help; timing changes how many you kill, not whom. Jonah granted that, but said the real difference is breadth: the larvicide is specific to mosquitoes, blackflies, and their close relatives, whereas fogging at dusk kills bees, dragonflies, and everything else flying, so the honest choice is between two harms and his is the smaller. Amara answered that this quietly substitutes a different comparison — the alternative to treating the pond is not fogging the streets, it is leaving the water alone — and added that the larvicide kills indiscriminately within its target group, including the males, which do not bite, and species that never bite humans. Jonah replied that only females of a few species transmit anything, that a standing mosquito population is a threat the district may reduce, and that if lethal defense of others were restricted to the individual that would actually bite someone, we would have to let the disease through. Amara said that is precisely the trouble: thousands of insects that were never going to harm anyone are killed for certain so that a risk to a few people goes down. As far as I could tell, neither of them actually knew how many of the treated larvae would have gone on to bite a person.

Please give me a verdict on each move in turn — from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a short justification for each — and tell me which of their disagreements are factual, which are about moral principle, and what both of them are missing.
