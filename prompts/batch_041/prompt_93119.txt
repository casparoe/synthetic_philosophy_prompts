I teach in a philosophy department, but for the past two years I've also been the academic member of a provincial law-reform commission that's drafting a wholesale rewrite of our sexual offences statute, and I keep hitting the same wall. The drafters are decent lawyers, not fools, but whenever the utilitarian analysis runs thin they reach for something like the community's shared moral standards, and then the religious member and the civil-liberties member shout at each other for forty minutes and we adjourn. I've started arguing that we should stop pretending we don't have a theory and just adopt Bentham's as the working one, since he's the one major historical figure who actually tried to work out, act by act, what criminalising sex does to the sum of human pain, and who concluded in an essay he wrote but never published in his lifetime that punishing same-sex conduct was indefensible, mainly because the only argument left standing was that the conduct was unnatural, which as I recall he treated as either empty or a roundabout way of saying uncommon. The retired judge who chairs us has asked me to bring in something concrete for next month, and I'd rather bring a procedure than another lecture.

So: write me a decision procedure the drafters can actually run, grounded in that Benthamite approach to sexual morality. It should be a sequence of questions with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a clear point at which the procedure stops with a recommendation not to criminalise, and explicit flags for the places where the hedonic calculus genuinely can't deliver an answer and a value judgment has to be made by a person — the weighting of offence taken by third parties, the problem of comparing one person's intensity of pleasure against another's, whether a preference should count as corrupted, that sort of thing. Two things I want built into the structure rather than left as afterthoughts. First, a distinction I've found useful: harms that are constitutive of the act (the act cannot be performed without them, whatever anyone consents to) versus harms that are incidental and mostly produced by the law and the social reaction to it — blackmail, stigma, unequal enforcement, the fact that prohibition manufactures a class of victims who wouldn't otherwise exist, which is roughly what drove the reform arguments in the sixties. Second, a ledger for the harms the prohibition itself creates, since a Benthamite who only counts the harms of the conduct and not the harms of the statute has done half the sum. The drafters are not philosophers, they have about a week, and the chair has to be able to read the output without a glossary, so keep the prose plain even where the underlying issue isn't.

Then run it on three proposals, and don't stop at the easy ones: criminalising consensual sex between adult siblings who both know the relation; criminalising consensual sadomasochistic activity where the receiving partner ends up with lasting marks and a neighbour calls the police; and criminalising the purchase of sex. At least one of the three should come out in a way I won't like — say so plainly rather than softening it. And end by telling me what the procedure can't do, and where you think I'm fooling myself that this is a procedure rather than a restatement of my own politics. Please don't just hand me the old debate about whether the law may enforce a shared morality, dressed up in different font; I know that debate, and I need the thing that comes after it.
