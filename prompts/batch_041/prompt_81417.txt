I am a program officer at a grantmaking foundation whose charter commits it to strong longtermism: the view that the most important thing we can do with our resources is to improve the very long-run future, and that this aim outweighs nearer-term goods, however large those nearer-term goods may be. We run four funding rounds a year, roughly thirty proposals per round, in the $200,000 to $3 million range, with a team of three and two weeks per round. Anything above $2 million requires sign-off from a five-member board, one member of which rejects the charter's premise outright, and another of whom will not approve anything whose case depends on a contested population axiology.

Please write a decision procedure my team can actually run on a proposal: the sequence of questions to put to it, the standard of evidence that counts as an adequate answer to each, the conditions under which the procedure terminates with a refusal, and the conditions under which it must escalate, because the residual dispute is axiological rather than empirical.

The procedure has to survive the cases that make this work hard. It must not be captured by fanaticism — the structure in which a very small probability of a very large outcome, multiplied out, dominates every alternative — and it must state plainly what rule it uses to prevent that, and what that rule costs elsewhere. It must be honest about cluelessness: at these time horizons the downstream effects of most interventions are not merely unknown but unknowable, so the procedure should separate estimates that carry information from numbers that are artifacts of the model that produced them. It must accommodate the possibility that the far future is net negative under some defensible value theories, and the standing disagreement about whether adding happy lives is itself an improvement. It must distinguish reducing the probability of a bad terminal outcome from improving the character of the future conditional on our surviving — including proposals aimed at preventing a single durable global order from locking in one set of values permanently. And it must be capable of concluding that we should disburse nothing this round, invest the endowment, and give later, on the grounds that later generations may be better placed to use the money well.

Run it on three proposals: a technical research program whose entire case is a claimed one-in-ten-thousand reduction in the probability of a permanent loss of human control over our own future; an institutional reform program whose expected value changes sign depending on which population axiology one holds; and the option of holding the endowment. At least one of the three should come out in a way I will not like. Finally, identify where the procedure is doing values work in the costume of estimation, and say what it cannot do at all.
