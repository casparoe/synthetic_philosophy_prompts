I've been reading Schlick's essay on the meaning of life and I half beleive it, but my gut keeps dissagreeing in ways I can't sort out. His basic move, as I understand it, is that meaning isn't a property a life has, the way a chair has a color -- it's something that shows up in the doing, and specifically in activity you're absorbed in for its own sake rather than for what it gets you. Work is for the sake of its results; play is for the sake of itself; and the meaningful life is the one that's mostly play in that sense, even when the play is hard and productive. He also says the question "what is the meaning of life" is mostly a malformed question, and that the people who ask it are either too weary to hear an answer or too inexperienced to need one. Something like that, anyway.

Here's where my own verdicts land, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

A woman spends twenty years raising three kids and says her life was meaningful. She can't name a single goal it was all for. Meaningful.

A man hits every target he ever set himself -- the book, the house, the title -- and finds it hollow. I don't think he's confused about anything. His question "was any of it worth it" is a real question and the answer might really be no.

A friend plays piano badly, alone, for hours, with no audience and no improvement. Meaningful, and more so than when she was winning competitions.

A guy works eighty hours a week at a job he hates so he can retire at fifty-five. If the retirement is real and he gets there, I want to say his life was meaningful. If he drops dead at fifty-four, I want to say it wasn't. Which seems insane, since he did the same things either way.

A gambler genuinely loves the game, plays for its own sake, loses everything. Not meaningful. So "absorbed in it for its own sake" isn't sufficient.

Someone in the middle of a depression asks what the point of anything is. I don't think that's a philosophical question at all. No argument answers it, and no argument is what's missing.

A child playing in a sprinkler never asks the question. I don't think that's because she's naive. I think it's because she's right.

So my tentative principle is roughly Schlick's: meaning is not a feature a life has but a mode of activity, and the question of life's meaning is well-formed only when it's really the question "am I living well," not "what is life for." But the retirement case and the gambler case both seem to break it in different directions, and I can't tell whether I should give up Schlick, give up my verdicts, or split the difference with some second clause about consequences mattering when they're actually reached.

Please tell me what principle or small set of principles actually captures this pattern, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself. For each clause, tell me which case forces it. Then tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed: someone spends thirty years on a project she loves, does it well, and it turns out to have accomplished nothing and mattered to no one -- the love was real, the results were nil. Was her life meaningful? And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't accommodate, because I suspect at least one has to go.
