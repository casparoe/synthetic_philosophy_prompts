I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a self-taught statistician who believes she has at last solved the oldest problem in her field: how a rational mind should distribute its confidence when it is genuinely ignorant. Her method drives the plot — she bets with it, she testifies under oath with it, and by the end it ruins someone — and the book will not work unless the method is either a genuine solution or a recognizable fraud. So I need the specification before I need the theories, and I am not asking for a bibliography.

Set out the desiderata that any adequate account of reasoning from ignorance must meet: the cases it has to get right, beginning with the plainly symmetric ones where everyone already agrees; the distinctions it cannot collapse; and the constraints it cannot violate. I have my own guesses at the last of these, and I want your full list rather than mine — that the verdict must not change when the same unknown is described in different units or coordinates; that it must not depend on how the space of possibilities is carved up, so that two ways of partitioning one situation yield a single answer; that no nonempty possibility may be assigned zero; that the confidences must be coherent, in the sense that no collection of bets priced from them can guarantee a loss; that the method must survive learning, so that when she acquires evidence her confidence shifts and remains coherent; and that it must still return a definite number where the range is continuous or unbounded, where treating possibilities as equally likely has no direct meaning. Behind each requirement, put the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are genuine constraints on any theory and which are merely the tastes of one camp.

Then give me the part I most need: which of these requirements cannot be satisfied together, since I suspect several pairs stand in open conflict, and which single requirement, if I could keep only one, would eliminate the most candidates. Close with a few sentences each on how the classical rule of treating alternatives as equally likely, the rule that reads the answer off the symmetries of a situation, the rule that maximizes entropy, and the view that there is no such rule at all — that priors are simply a matter of opinion — fare against your list. A table is fine.
