I'm a second-year PhD student in cognitive science, and I've been dragged into a standing argument in my lab that has hardened into a bet. The question is narrower than "is free will real," and I want to keep it narrow. Take the Libet-style experiments as a body of evidence — the ones in which a participant watches a rotating clock, decides at some moment of their own choosing to press a button, and afterwards reports where the clock hand was when they first felt the urge or the decision to press. The proposition I want a credence on is this: in those paradigms, the consciously reported urge to move is not a cause of the movement — that is, if you could intervene on the conscious intention itself while holding the pre-motor neural dynamics fixed, neither the occurrence nor the timing of the press would change. My labmate puts this at 0.75; I'm at about 0.3; and we've stopped making progress because we can't tell whether we disagree about the data, about what "cause" is doing in that sentence, or about what "conscious intention" picks out.

Please give me your own number, and then decompose it rather than defending it. The sub-questions I can see are: (a) whether the reported urge measures anything that existed at the time it is attributed to, given that it is delivered by a retrospective judgment about a clock face and may be reconstructed after the fact; (b) whether the pre-movement neural signal that precedes the report is specific to the decision, or instead just what you would expect from spontaneous fluctuation in a system that emits a response once some noisy quantity crosses a threshold; (c) whether the temporal comparison at the heart of the paradigm could test the causal claim even in principle, since a cause need not precede its effect by the interval the paradigm measures, and a signal that precedes an action is not thereby a signal that decides it; (d) whether any of the measured quantities satisfies the criterion of being "available to the agent for reasoning at the time it occurs," which one of my colleagues proposes as the mark of a genuine intention — she says the neural signal fails this by construction, and I can't tell whether that criterion is a fair constraint or a restatement of the conclusion; and (e) how much of any of this generalizes from a button press under lab instructions to the actions anyone actually cares about morally or metaphysically. Assign each of those a rough probability, say how they combine into the number you gave, and note where two of the terms are not independent of each other if that's the case.

Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and what single piece of evidence — an experiment, a reanalysis, or a purely conceptual argument — would move you furthest and in which direction. Finally, given my 0.3 and my labmate's 0.75, tell me which of the terms above most plausibly contains our disagreement, and whether we are disagreeing about a fact or merely about a definition.
