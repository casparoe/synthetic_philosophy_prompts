Here's the debate as I understand it. One camp says the wavefunction (or quantum state) is a piece of reality itself, the physical state of the system, as real as a field or a particle. The other says it's a state of information, a summary of what some agent knows or could in principle know, and that the actual physical state is something else underneath, some hidden configuration that the wavefunction is a probability distribution over. The labels people use are psi-ontic and psi-epistemic.

Now, the bit I think is doing all the work, and that I want you to focus on. There's an argument — I've seen it sketched alot, I couldn't reproduce it properly — that goes roughly like this. Suppose you've got underlying real states, and each preparation procedure, each way of producing a quantum state in the lab, gives you a probability distribution over those underlying states. The epistemic view seems to need it to be possible that two different preparations, yielding two different quantum states, sometimes land on the same underlying real state. Otherwise the underlying state would just be a relabelling of the quantum state and the view collapses into the ontic one. Then you add an assumption that systems prepared independently of each other have independent underlying states. And supposedly from those two ingredients you can construct a scenario in which a joint measurement has an outcome that should be impossible under one of the preparations, but the setup makes it possible, contradicting the quantum predictions. So either the wavefunction is ontic, or you give up preparation independence, which people say amounts to a kind of action at a distance.
