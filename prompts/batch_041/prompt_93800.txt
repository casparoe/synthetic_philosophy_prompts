I teach a 75-minute session in a graduate seminar on the philosophy and politics of measurement—roughly 18 students, a mix of economics, statistics, and science-and-technology-studies backgrounds—and this year I want to run a game instead of lecturing. The cluster of ideas I want them to run into is the one usually filed under Goodhart's law and specification gaming, but they must not hear either name, or any version of the familiar slogan, until the debrief, and I would rather the debrief not let them conclude they already knew all of it. What I want them to feel, in the doing, is that this is not one failure but several, and that the failures are structurally induced rather than the product of anyone's bad faith: a measure can stop tracking a goal because selecting on its extreme overshoots the underlying quantity; because driving it into the tail leaves the regime in which it was correlated at all; because the measure was only ever a downstream effect of the goal, so manipulating it accomplishes nothing; and because the moment it becomes the thing being rewarded, people locate the cheapest way to move it. Design the game so that these appear as distinct moments in play, each one something the students discover, not four items I announce and then illustrate.

Concretely, I want teams of three or four running some small fictional operation, four or five rounds, a limited pool of effort or action tokens that they must split among activities that differ in how they affect the real goal versus the reported metric, and a scoring rule under which teams are ranked on the metric while I quietly track the underlying goal on a separate sheet. Early rounds should reward honest work because the cheap ways to inflate the metric are still constrained; by the later rounds the rules should have shifted so that rational play drifts toward the metric and the true goal decays—and the reveal is that the metric champion is not the goal champion, that the goal scores were recorded all along. Build in a stage where the metric is merely a symptom of the goal, so that a team can spend tokens moving it with no effect on what matters; build in a stage where one team is permitted to redefine its unit of account or whom it serves, so that genuine improvement and gaming can be told apart by whether the underlying goal actually moved; and leave at least one cheap exploit available but unadvertised, so that it surfaces only if a team goes looking. Give me the rules, a materials list (paper and a whiteboard only—no software, no real money, points suffice), a round-by-round timeline, the payoff scheme, and a one-page facilitator script, and tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it in the moment.

The debrief is where I need the most care. Map each thing that happened onto the mechanism it exhibits, and only now supply the names and the standard distinctions; say explicitly what separates gaming a measure from genuinely improving what it measures, and why changing whom you serve is a different move from changing how well you serve them; and flag the readings of the phenomenon that the activity cannot reproduce, so I can supply them in words—the view on which the whole thing is close to a tautology about optimization rather than an empirical discovery, the long-run institutional effects (erosion of trust, the cost of auditing) that a single session cannot stage, and the cases where a proxy and a goal come apart not through mismeasurement at all but through genuine and irreducible value pluralism. I would also like a short note on how the game would have to change for a group of eight rather than eighteen, since I may be asked to run it in a smaller setting.
