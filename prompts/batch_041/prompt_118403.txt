I am trying to isolate a claim that sits at the junction of moral psychology and metaethics, and I want your credence in it rather than a survey of the surrounding literature. Here is the claim, stated as tightly as I can manage.

(D) The divergence that moral foundations theory reports between self-identified political liberals and conservatives — liberals weighting care and fairness more heavily, conservatives weighting loyalty, authority, and sanctity more heavily — constitutes non-negligible first-order evidence about which side's contested moral judgments are more likely to be true, and not merely a causal explanation of why the two sides disagree.

I want (D) split into two readings. (D-weak): the divergence raises or lowers the probability of at least some contested moral propositions; it has some evidential weight, however small. (D-strong): the divergence, once properly interpreted, tells us something determinate about which side is closer to the truth on the issues that divide them.

Give me a credence for each. Read "supplies evidence" in the strict likelihood-ratio sense: the pattern counts as evidence for a contested proposition only if it is more probable under the hypothesis that the proposition is true than under the hypothesis that it is false. Part of your decomposition should therefore be an estimate of how the relevant likelihoods actually compare, and whether they compare at all.

Decompose the number into at least the following terms, giving each a probability and a one-line justification before you combine them:

1. The descriptive core of the theory is roughly correct, as opposed to an artifact of the instrument, the item selection, or a taxonomy that mis-carves the underlying space.
2. The ideological variation in foundation weights is better explained by causes that do not track moral truth — coalitional signaling, in-group socialization, evolved predispositions shaped for coordination rather than accuracy — than by differential sensitivity to moral facts.
3. Given that the variation is largely non-truth-tracking, the best available metaethical account of moral belief licenses the inference from a belief's having a non-truth-tracking etiology to its being less likely to be true.
4. The specific asymmetries survive a symmetry test: whatever debunking pressure the argument generates falls harder on one side than the other, rather than on both sides equally.
5. A rival taxonomy of the moral domain — for instance one that treats the several foundations as variations on a single harm-and-reciprocity template — would leave the asymmetry, and the argument built on it, substantially intact.

Then tell me which term the final number is most sensitive to, and which single result (an empirical finding, a conceptual argument, or a dissolution of the question) would move it furthest.

Two further probes. First, the asymmetry is usually stated as a difference in the strength of the foundations; does your answer change if it is instead a difference in their salience, that is, in what each side reaches for when moralizing, with the underlying weights roughly equal? Second, suppose someone showed that the whole five- or six-factor profile collapses onto a single dimension, so that the divergence is one-dimensional after all; would that strengthen or weaken (D-weak)?

For calibration: I am at 0.7 on (D-weak) and 0.15 on (D-strong); a colleague is at 0.9 and 0.7. Say where you think the disagreement between the two of us most likely lives — in the empirical premise, in the debunking step, or in what we each take evidence about which side is right to even mean — and give your own two numbers alongside the diagnosis.
