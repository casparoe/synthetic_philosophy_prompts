Two philosophers are arguing about whether dreams are experiences. The first holds the view associated with Norman Malcolm: that the concept of dreaming is anchored in the waking report, that there is no independent criterion for the occurrence of a dream apart from the dreamer's subsequent testimony, and that it is a mistake to conceive of a dream as a sequence of inner episodes that the dreamer observes. The second holds the view associated with Daniel Dennett: that dreams are experiences, that the distinction between a dream and a confabulated waking story is not as sharp as the first philosopher thinks, and that making the occurrence of a dream depend on the dreamer's later testimony is implausible. They agree on the empirical facts but disagree on how to interpret them. Write a dialogue between these two philosophers in which they discuss three pieces of evidence. First, experiments in which lucid dreamers, during REM sleep, answer yes/no questions posed by experimenters by moving their eyes in pre-arranged patterns, and sometimes answer with facial muscle contractions. Second, cases of REM sleep behavior disorder, in which sleepers act out their dreams, sometimes punching or kicking, and their actions match the dream content they report on waking. Third, the phenomenon of dreams that seem to last for hours or days but occur during a brief period of sleep, as in the classic case of a dream of being guillotined that seems to span a long trial and execution but coincides with a single external stimulus. In the dialogue, each philosopher should respond to the other's actual points rather than talk past each other, and neither should be a strawman. The first philosopher should not simply deny the empirical findings; the second should not simply assert that they settle the matter. The dialogue should explore whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, and it should end without a forced resolution, though each should have made concessions or clarified their position. Write the dialogue in a polished, philosophical style, with clear turns and no more than 1200 words.
