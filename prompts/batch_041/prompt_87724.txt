I write a fairly wonky ideas newsletter — subscriber-funded, no ads, the kind of thing where I can spend 2,000 words on medical error and moral responsibility — and I'm working up a piece about whether you can cause something by not doing it: the doctor who doesn't check the chart, the lifeguard who doesn't look up, the regulator who doesn't read the memo. A philosopher friend who runs a popular-ish blog summed up the literature for me in one paragraph, and I've been chewing on it ever since. His gloss, more or less: 'Almost everyone working on this now agrees that omissions can't literally be causes, because causing something requires a real connection between two events, and with an omission there's no event there to be connected — a non-happening can't make anything happen. So when the doctor fails to check the chart and the patient dies, what caused the death is the disease; the failure is just a fact about what she didn't do. That's why the law treats acts and omissions differently, and why we need a seperate moral principle, something like a duty to rescue, to explain why she's blameworthy at all. Which means all those cases where we casually say someone caused a death by standing by are really cases where we're blaming them for failing to prevent a harm that something else caused — so causation by omission is basically a fiction we invented to make our blame look more objective than it actually is.'

I'm inclined to buy the bit about duties to rescue, and I sort of like the deflationary ending, but the move from "omissions aren't causes" to "so all that talk is a fiction" feels like it's smuggling something, though I can't say what. Can you acually go clause by clause and tell me what someone who works on this would accept, what's the standard simplification a popularizer would make, and what would make a specialist wince or push back? Specifically: is the metaphysical claim about what can be a cause doing any real work in his moral conclusion, or is there a hidden premise; is "almost everyone agrees" true, or is that one camp in a live fight; is "no event to connect" the actual reason people deny omission-causation, or just one version of one argument; and if omissions aren't causes, does it follow that a failure can't make a difference, or is that a different question wearing the same word? I don't need a reading list, I've got too much of that already — I just want to know which sentences to keep.
