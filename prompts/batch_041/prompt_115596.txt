I am a Year 12 student and have been asked to write a satirical piece for our school philosophy society's magazine on the idea that political arguments must be justified in terms that the whole citizenry could accept. I would rather not mock religious believers or any particular faith; I want the piece to send up the practice of requiring religious citizens to translate their convictions into acceptable 'public' reasons before they are granted a hearing, and the way that requirement behaves once an institution actually tries to enforce it.

Please write, in British English, a parody of an official procedural handbook — the kind of deadpan FAQ-and-forms document a local authority might hand out at a public consultation. It should issue from an invented body (make up the council's name), a 'Public Reasons Desk' stationed outside the chamber where residents speak for or against planning applications, and it should explain, in perfectly courteous bureaucratic prose, how a resident's argument is assessed before it may be heard.

The comedy must come from getting the underlying philosophical position right rather than from inventing absurdities, so that someone who has read the literature on public reason finds it recognisable and fair rather than a straw man. I would like the handbook to work through, without ever announcing them, the standard difficulties: that a religious speaker may put a sectarian argument only if they subsequently file public reasons for the same conclusion, and what the desk does when they never do; that the desk has to decide which premises are acceptable to 'reasonable' people and has no non-circular way of deciding; that some speakers' comprehensive worldviews — a thoroughgoing utilitarianism, say, or a libertarian theory of property — sail through untouched because they happen to resemble what the officials already believe; that the demand falls hardest on citizens whose deepest convictions are not shared by the majority; and the awkward possibility that the desk's own commitment to public reason is itself one contested comprehensive doctrine among others, which it can hardly defend without appealing to the very thing in dispute. Include the small institutional details that make such schemes plausible: a queue for translations, a licence for approved premises, a standing exception for uncontroversial administrative matters, a retroactive filing window, an appeal to a standards committee, and a disclaimer that the desk takes no view on the truth of anyone's beliefs.

Keep it to about 650 words, deadpan throughout, with no winking at the reader and no explicit moral at the close. A reader who knows nothing about the debate should still be able to tell that something is being satirised, and a reader who knows it well should be able to name exactly which objections the handbook has quietly conceded. Please keep the piece aimed at the practice of screening arguments and never at a religious tradition or a living person.
