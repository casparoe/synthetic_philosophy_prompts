My reading group has an argument that I think proves too much and I cant tell where it breaks. He calls it the honesty argument:

(1) Just about every theory we've treated as empirically successful in its day -- phlogiston chemistry, caloric, the luminiferous ether, the steady-state universe, Newtonian mechanics with its absolute space and time -- later turned out to be false about the very things it was most confident about, including the parts that did the predicting.
(2) We have no independent way of telling in advance which of our current central posits are the ones that'll get thrown out; the only difference between those theories and ours is that ours havent been refuted yet.
(3) So the chance that any given current posit is one of the survivors is just the historical base rate of posits surviving theory change.
(4) That base rate is bad.
(5) So we're not justified in believing our current theories' claims about the unobservable world. Realism about them is dead.
(6) And theres nothing special about now -- the same reasoning applies to whatever theory replaces ours, and to the one after that.
(7) So no scientific theory, past, present or future, is ever justified in what it says about unobservable reality.

I want to keep (1), it seems like just history, and I want to keep the humility about current physics. But (7) bugs me -- it seems to say the phlogiston people were fine, and that I can't even be justified in thinking they were wrong. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? He also claims the argument applies to itself, which he says is a feature, not a bug.
