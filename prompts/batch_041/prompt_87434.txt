I'm designing a graduate seminar on mindless mechanism, organised around the thought that competence can outrun comprehension: that a system may reliably do the right thing without understanding anything, and that this possibility arrives in two historically distinct packages — the Darwinian inversion, in which design is produced with no designer, and the Turing inversion, in which rule-governed operations that could be carried out by an uncomprehending clerk yield results indistinguishable from thought. Before we evaluate particular versions of the claim, I want the specification. Write me the desiderata that any adequate account of the inversion must satisfy: the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it is not allowed to collapse, the background results it must be consistent with, and the puzzles it must either dissolve or explain. For each requirement, give the consideration that generates it, and say what work it does — which candidate accounts it rules out, and whether it is a genuine constraint or the prejudice of one camp.

The cases should include at least the following: an ordinary artefact whose correct operation is fully explained by the comprehension of whoever built it; a system that passes every behavioural test only by consulting pre-stored answers, with no structure that could be perturbed into new competence; a system whose competence was shaped by a selection process with no mind anywhere upstream; and the theorist who advances the account. The distinctions to be respected should include, at a minimum, the difference between responding to something and representing it, the difference between having a function and having a purpose, the difference between competence located in the system and competence located in the interpreter who credits it, and the difference between comprehension that is merely displaced upstream and comprehension that is genuinely emergent.

Two constraints matter more to me than the rest, and I want your assessment of whether they can be met together. First, the account must say plainly what happens to the comprehension in the artefact case: whether crediting the designer counts as an explanation of the artefact's competence or as an evasion of it. Second, the account must handle the case where no comprehension exists anywhere upstream, and must not do so by denying comprehension outright — unless it is prepared to be turned on its own author. If those two constraints cannot be jointly satisfied, that is the result I most want: say so explicitly, show which pair of requirements is in tension, and name the single requirement that does the most to exclude otherwise attractive accounts. Close with a short verdict — a sentence or two each, no more — on how three broad families fare against the list: accounts that credit a designer's comprehension, accounts that locate the relevant difference in a history of selection, and deflationary accounts that treat comprehension as something we attribute rather than something a system has. A table is fine for the verdict; keep the weight on the requirements and the cases behind them.
