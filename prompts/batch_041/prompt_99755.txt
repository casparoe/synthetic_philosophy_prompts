I have spent the better part of a decade writing novels whose narrators are, by any ordinary standard, morally repellent — men who do dreadful things and describe them beautifully — and I find myself trapped in a recurring and increasingly sterile argument with a critic friend about what this does to the books. We agree entirely on the non-moral facts: we agree what the narrators do, we agree that it is wicked, and we agree that some of the prose is very fine indeed. And yet we have not moved an inch in years. He holds that a work's ethical defects are aesthetic defects — that a novel which invites me to collude with a monster is, precisely thereby, a worse novel — while I hold that the ethical and the aesthetic are separate ledgers, and that his objection is really about the sort of person who wrote the thing. Between us sit positions neither of us occupies: the view that a moral defect can sometimes function as a positive aesthetic merit, and the more cautious view that a work's ethics are aesthetically relevant only where the work itself endorses or prescribes a stance, rather than merely depicting one. I suspect the quarrel is not what it appears to be, but I want a diagnosis rather than another round of assertion.

My working hypothesis is that we are answering two different questions under a single heading — he is asking whether the work deserves my admiration, I am asking whether it is well made — and that the phrase 'good art' is quietly doing double duty for both. But I also suspect there is a hidden question about what a work of art actually is: whether it includes the responses it solicits from its audience, and whether it includes the artist's own conduct or character. Three things I keep conflating, and would like separated, are the moral character of the fictional world the novel depicts, the moral character of the response the novel demands of its reader, and the moral record of the person who made it; I can never tell which of these my friend is really attacking, and I am not convinced he can either.

Please diagnose the impasse: is it a verbal dispute about the word 'good', a substantive disagreement about the metaphysics of the artwork (what belongs to the work — the fiction, the implied author's stance, the experience asked of the audience, or the maker's acts), a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether moral considerations can count as aesthetic ones at all, or some combination in which the verbal and the substantive are tangled together? Use the positions as I have sketched them, and feel free to name them by the labels the literature uses — ethicism, autonomism, immoralism and their moderated cousins — but keep the labels subordinate to the diagnosis. Then tell me what would count as progress given your answer: a distinction that dissolves the quarrel, a test case one side would have to concede, a formal result, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected here and why. One candidate test I keep turning over, if it helps you get traction: whether my novel would be better if the narrator's cruelty were replaced by something equally vivid but benign, with everything else held constant — and whether my friend and I would give the same answer to that, and what our disagreement about it would reveal.
