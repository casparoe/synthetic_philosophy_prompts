Talk about what people really want, or get satisfaction from, is untestable, so the theory just defines a preference as a pattern in someone's choices — utility becomes a name for the choices rather than anything behind them. Samuelson's point was that a theory only has to predict, not to be true of the mind: people behave as if they maximize utility. As long as someone's choices are consistent (no cycles, no picking B over A here and A over B there), we can always assign them a utility function that they're maximizing. So welfare economics doesn't need to peer into anyone's head: if you choose X when Y was available and no more expensive, X is better for you, period. The only real constraint is the axiom of revealed preference, which says that if you pick A when B is on the table, you can never pick B when A is available. Critics like Sen complain this is too thin to ground welfare claims, since people also choose out of politeness or duty."
