Alright, I want you to stress-test a view I've basically been assuming for years. The view: the Dutch book argument really does establish that rational credences have to satisfy the probability axioms — that if your degrees of belief are incoherent (say you think a coin is 60% heads and also 60% tails, or your credences are subadditive), then a bookie who knows your numbers can sell you a bundle of bets that each look fair to you but together guarantee a loss, and that this shows the incoherence itself is the defect, with the exploitability just being the symptom.

I dont want the standard objection list recited back at me. I want the actual defeaters, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to flag which ones a committed defender would genuinely concede versus which ones they'd just wave away. Things I keep chewing on:

- The loss is in money, not utility. If I have sharply decreasing marginal utility for money, cant I just say the package isnt actually bad for me? And if the standard fix is to bet in utility units, does the argument survive that move or does it just go circular?
- The bookie has to know my credences and gets to set the stakes after seeing them. What if we handicap her — she can only offer me one bet at a time, at odds I name, and I can decline any of them? Whats left of the argument then? Is coherence still motivated, or only something weaker like dont be a repeat sucker?
- Is there a coherent-ish agent who provably never gets exploited — one who refuses every multi-bet package, or only bets on propositions she can later check? If so, does that defuse the argument or just show shes irrational in some other way?
- Does the whole thing secretly lean on expected utility theory plus a small-worlds setup where your credences are defined on a tidy partition? Widen the world, or drop EU, and does the theorem even get off the ground?

Then the meta-question: has coherence = rationality been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so is that actually a problem here or just the price of any normative claim? And tell me honestly what the view predicts that a rival doesn't — what I'd actually lose by giving it up. Be willing to say the answer is not much.
