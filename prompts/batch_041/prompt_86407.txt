ok so I need a second pair of eyes on something from my reading group, because my friend has basically got the whole room convinced and I think he's cheating somewhere but I can't find the seam.

Backround: he's an anti-realist about a certain kind of claim — the ones about stuff nobody wrote down, like how many birds were on a particular pier at a particular minute in 1994, or what the weather was doing over some village in 1200. His slogan is that for those claims, being true just is being settleable: if it's true, it's because it could in principle be settled. He says "settled" rather than "known" because he reckons "known" drags in too much psychology, so lets go with his word: someone settles p when they nail it down by a reliable method, so you can't settle something false.

His argument, as he puts it on the whiteboard:

1. G is "there were exactly 41,237 gulls within a mile of the Hanford pier at noon on 3 June 1994." Stipulate that G is true and that nobody settles it, ever.
2. Settleability: for any true claim p, it's possible in principle for someone to settle p.
3. "G and nobody ever settles G" is true — first half by stipulation, second half is just how the world went.
4. So by 2, it's possible in principle for someone to settle "G and nobody ever settles G."
5. Settling a conjunction means settling each conjunct. You can't have settled "p and q" and still be in the dark about q.
6. So it's possible for someone to settle G and to settle "nobody ever settles G."
7. Settling implies truth, so if someone settled "nobody ever settles G," then nobody ever settles G.
8. But 6 also has someone settling G. Contradiction.
9. A contradiction can't be actual, so the possibility in 4 was never a real possibility. 4 followed from 2 and 3, and 3 is just a fact, so 2 is false — or else G isn't the kind of thing that can be true while unsettled. Either way, no unsettled truths.
10. G was arbitrary. So every truth is settled. Every fact about the gulls, the medieval weather, how many times you blinked last Tuesday.

And then the flourish: he says run the same ten steps again with "G has been settled" playing the role of G, and you get that every truth is settled-to-be-settled. He agrees that's mad, but says mad conclusions are how reductios work.

My problem: I am not giving up 1 — there are definately truths nobody ever settled, I'm not budging on that. And I actually like 2, it's the whole reason I'd call myself an anti-realist. So the casualty has to be one of 3, 5, 7, 8, 9. He thinks it's 7 and calls anyone who drops it a fideist. My gut says 5 is where the trick is — can you really not settle a conjunction without settling the parts? maybe you settle the whole thing as a package — but he says if I drop 5 then settling isn't closed under obvious consequences and I've lost more than I paid for. I also keep feeling like "possible" is doing two jobs somewhere, like "possible that someone settles it" vs "possible to settle it," but I can't get that to cash out into an actual line.

So: which step do I take out, why did it look fine until I stare at it, and what's the honest price of dropping it? And if there's just a step where he's slipped something past me, say so instead — I need to know what I'm left holding. Don't just give me the name of the paradox, I know it's got one.
