I write the science-and-ideas bits for a weekend magazine — long reads, the odd online interactive — and I'm doing a piece on the timing of experience, hung on the old colour-phi demo: you flash a blue dot up top, then a red dot lower down a fraction of a second later, and people swear they watched the first dot slide across the gap and flip colour halfway, even though the colour flip belongs to a dot that wasn't on screen yet when they say they saw it. The standard fork for this is Orwellian versus Stalinesque: either you really did see red and your memory of it gets quietly rewritten afterwards, or the experience itself is held back and edited before you ever get it.

What I want from you is a decision tree for readers of the piece — smart generalists, no neuroscience. Start them from something they can actually agree or disagree with before they know where it leads: do you think there's a fact of the matter about what the subject was experiencing at the instant the first dot was up? Is the report good evidence about the experience? Is there a single moment at which a content becomes conscious? Does the phenomenon need any story about revision at all, or is it just ordinary sensory processing? Every branch node should be a question someone can answer without knowing the label it leads to.

Leaves: at least five named positions. I definitely want Orwellian, Stalinesque, the line that the whole when-did-it-become-conscious question is ill-posed, and the line that the effect is a low-level sensory affair with no revision anywhere — plus whatever else you think belongs.

Two things I especially want. First, my hunch: that no experiment can seperate Orwellian from Stalinesque, because every probe ends up going through the subject's report, which is downstream of the very editing at issue. If I'm right, say why; if I'm wrong, tell me which variant would do it — dropping the second dot on some trials, asking for a colour-change button-press, masking, a trained observer, whatever you've got. Second, flag any pairs of leaves the tree can't separate, and note any combinations of answers that nobody seems to occupy, with a one-line diagnosis of whether that's because the view is incoherent, unappealing, or just unexplored.
