I'm in a metaethics reading group that's split down the middle and we've been going at it all term. Roughly: one faction thinks the cross-cultural record from anthropology and moral psychology is basically one long argument against moral objectivity -- different communities have different fundamental values, the variation doesn't dissolve once you correct for factual ignorance and bad inference, and there's no fact of the matter about which set is right. The other faction thinks the variation is real but shallow: nearly all of it is downstream of different non-moral beliefs (about souls, ancestors, what actually causes harm, who counts as a person, what happens after death) or different weightings of a small set of shared values, and once you hold the factual beliefs and the weightings fixed there's far more convergence than the skeptics let on, with whatever remains being either in-principle resolvable or not really a moral disagreement at all.

Write the joint statement those two could actually both sign. I want a numbered agreed-claims section, and be aggressive about it -- the empirical claims about variation both can accept, what neither is committed to (neither thinks the other is simply confused or lying about their own values), what both concede about the limits of the evidence, and what each takes the burden of proof to be. Then a disagreements section as a table, each side's view stated in wording that side would accept, followed by the cruxes. I suspect two already: whether convergence under full information is an empirical prediction or a definitional constraint, and whether apparent moral disagreement survives charitable translation at all -- the are-they-even-disagreeing question. For each crux, give a concrete test, case, or argument both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, so neither can wriggle afterwards.

One thing I want this document to do that our group has never managed: introduce a shared procedure for sorting the cases, something like a missing-belief audit. For each apparent cross-cultural disagreement, ask what factual belief would have to differ for the two communities to be judging the same way, then ask what happens to the disagreement when that belief is corrected -- either by an information manipulation in the field or by asking each community what it says about a hypothetical where the belief turns out false. Here's a made-up case we argued about for two hours and got nowhere, so use it or something like it: a community that treats a failure to avenge a murdered kinsman as leaving a debt owed to the dead, where the refusal is described by insiders not as cowardice but as theft. The realist should be betting that the disagreement mostly evaporates or changes shape once you fix the beliefs about what the dead can be owed; the pluralist should be betting that a hard core survives intact, and the doc should say which of them is exposed if it doesn't. Also let them say plainly whether any part of the fight is verbal -- e.g. about whether objectivity means mind-independence, or universality, or authority -- and if so which part, and what each side's claim becomes once that's pinned down. No need for citations, I've read the literature, I want the arguments. I'd rather have a short honest document then a long one that splits the difference.
