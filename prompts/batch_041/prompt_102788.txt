I have been following the long-running dispute over Galen Strawson's Basic Argument — the regress that runs roughly as follows: you act as you do because of the way you are; so to be genuinely morally responsible for what you do, you would have to be responsible for the way you are; but to be responsible for the way you are you would have to have brought it about that you are that way, which merely restarts the demand at the next level; and since nothing can be the cause of itself, the regress never bottoms out, so no one is ever genuinely morally responsible. The compatibilist replies have been on the table for decades — that responsibility does not require self-creation, that it requires only a suitable responsiveness to reasons or a suitable history connecting the action to the agent's own attitudes, that Strawson has simply conflated responsibility with a demand for ultimate authorship — and yet the exchange has not moved. Strawson treats these replies as changing the subject; his critics treat his argument as begging the question against them.

What I want is a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a verbal dispute about what 'moral responsibility' means? Two parties answering different questions under a single heading — one asking what it would take to be the ultimate source of oneself, the other asking what it takes for our practices of blame and praise to be fair? Or a substantive disagreement about the conditions of desert that turns on a hidden premise which neither side has argued for? My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and one I would like tested rather than confirmed, is that the impasse is sustained by a distinction the parties keep sliding over: between a regress of self-formation (how one came to be the way one is) and a regress of self-governance (whether one's action flowed from one's own reasons at the time). On that reading the Basic Argument is a decisive argument against the first and simply irrelevant to the second, while the compatibilist replies are answers to the second and simply irrelevant to the first — in which case the two sides are less disagreeing than failing to meet. If that is wrong, say where.

Diagnose the structure of the disagreement using what each side actually says, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here: a distinction, a concession, a reformulation of the question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Please also tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis — which consideration or reply, if it turned out to be sound, would lead you to a different verdict — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
