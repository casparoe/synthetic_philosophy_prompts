For most of my working life I was a physiologist, and since retiring I have spent a great deal of time reading philosophy, less systematically than I would like. Of all the disputes I have encountered, the one that has held my attention longest is the argument over whether beliefs and desires exist — whether the vocabulary of ordinary psychological description names genuine states of the organism or, as the eliminativists contend, consists of the posits of a stagnant folk theory that a mature neuroscience will one day discard, much as caloric and phlogiston were discarded. What puzzles me is less the disagreement than its endurance. The principal positions were staked out decades ago, each side is thoroughly familiar with the other's arguments, and yet there is no discernible movement toward convergence. I am asking for a diagnosis rather than a verdict.

The eliminativist, as I understand the position, maintains that folk psychology is an empirical theory of the mind, that it is a poor one — its predictive successes are shallow and it has made no progress in explaining the phenomena — and that its central posits, belief and desire among them, will not survive the advance of a genuinely scientific psychology. The defender of the folk, as I understand that position, replies in one of several ways: that the vocabulary is not a theory at all but a set of practical capacities for predicting and explaining one another, so that the demand that it be true or false is misapplied; that it is a theory but a largely correct one, whose everyday successes in interpretation are real evidence for its posits; or that even if the folk theory is false in detail, something will play the causal role that belief and desire are meant to play, so that the categories are absorbed rather than eliminated. And there is the perennial charge that the eliminativist refutes himself in the act of arguing, since the argument presupposes the very states he denies.

I offer the following as a hypothesis, not a settled view. In my own field, a theoretical entity was seldom abandoned because it was demonstrated to be false; it was abandoned when a successor theory accounted for the phenomena without it, and the older vocabulary became idle rather than refuted. It seems to me that the eliminativist case rests substantially on a prediction about what neuroscience will eventually be able to do without, while the defense rests substantially on a claim about what the vocabulary does for us now. If that is right, the two sides may not be disagreeing about any present fact at all. I have also wondered whether the dispute turns on a distinction I have found it useful to draw, between a posit that disappears because nothing plays its role and a posit that is absorbed because something plays its role under a new description — and whether the parties agree on the science but divide over which of these two models is the correct one to expect.

I would like you to tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is. Is it substantive, or verbal — a quarrel about the word exist, or about whether the ordinary notion of belief is a natural kind whose reference is fixed by its history or a functional kind defined by a role, with each side silently assuming a different answer? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work, such that the dispute would be settled by a future result, and if so, what result? Do the sides differ about which cases count as evidence — whether everyday predictive success is data for realism about the posits, or whether only the explanatory integration achieved by a mature theory counts? Or do they begin from intuitions about what would be lost if the vocabulary went, intuitions that neither side can argue for? It also seems possible to me that they are answering different questions under a single heading: one asking what the world contains, the other asking what we ought to say. Use what each side actually maintains to support your diagnosis, and tell me honestly what would count as progress — a distinction, a criterion of elimination, a formal result, or a change of question — or whether, given the diagnosis, no progress should be expected and why.
