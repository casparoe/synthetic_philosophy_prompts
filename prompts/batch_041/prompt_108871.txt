I am a retired observational cosmologist who has spent the past several years reading philosophy as an amateur and have now enrolled part-time in a master's program. I need a research proposal of about two pages, roughly a thousand words, for a six-month independent project that would culminate in an essay of some eight thousand words and a short seminar talk. I am submitting it to a small foundation that supports independent scholars working across disciplines, so it has to satisfy a philosopher and still be intelligible to a physicist.

My subject is Fitch's paradox of knowability. As I understand the dialectic, the anti-realist or verificationist is drawn to the principle that every truth is knowable, and the paradox shows that this principle, read straightforwardly, yields the absurd conclusion that every truth is in fact known. The standard replies restrict the principle's scope (to observational truths, say, or to truths that do not themselves mention knowledge), weaken or reinterpret the epistemic operator, or question one of the steps in the derivation. All of them treat knowability as a single fixed notion. My own field never worked that way. When we said a quantity was in principle measurable, we meant measurable relative to a stated instrument, a stated integration time, and a stated background; strip out those subscripts and the claim stops meaning anything. So my half-formed idea is that knowability in the anti-realist's principle is implicitly subscripted — knowable by such-and-such an agent, by such-and-such methods, within such-and-such a conceptual scheme — and that the paradox has been run only on the unsubscripted version.

Please turn this into a proposal that does the following.

First, states the question so that it has a determinate answer. Something like: does a knowability principle that is explicitly indexed to an agent, a set of available methods, and a conceptual scheme either entail the correspondingly indexed claim that all truths are known, or fail to; and if some indexings survive the derivation, is what survives strong enough to do the anti-realist work the principle was introduced to do?

Second, explains why the existing positions leave this open, in terms of arguments and positions rather than citations. I will supply the references myself, so please include no bibliography and no literature review by name.

Third, sets out the method. I would like the derivation treated as something to be ablated, the way one ablates a model: identify each step on which the conclusion depends — factivity of the operator, distribution over conjunction, the fact that an agent's ignorance is itself a truth, any use of classical reasoning — then construct variants that drop one feature at a time and determine which drops block the conclusion. For each candidate indexing of knowability, the project should either show that the derivation goes through or exhibit a countermodel: a small, explicit epistemic model with finite agents, in which the indexed principle holds and some truth remains unknown. Tell me what formalism this requires. I have worked through an introductory modal logic text, but I need to know what more I would have to learn and how long that would take.

Fourth, says what a result would look like and what would count as failure. I want the proposal to be explicit that the hypothesis dies if the indexing turns out to be cosmetic — if every indexed version is a notational variant of the original, or if the versions that escape the paradox are trivially true and philosophically inert — and to say how I would tell the difference between the indexing doing real work and my merely having renamed the problem.

Fifth, gives an honest assessment of scope: whether this fits six months of part-time work, what to cut if it does not, and where the project could most easily go wrong.

The document should be in American English and in the register of a serious funding application.
