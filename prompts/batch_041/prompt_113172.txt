I've recently been drafted into teaching the moral psychology unit of an undergraduate ethics course, and preparing it has unsettled me more than I expected: the moral foundations framework Haidt and his collaborators developed explains my own reactions to my relatives' politics — and to my own — uncomfortably well, and I genuinely cannot tell whether that is a reason to take the framework more seriously as ethics or a reason to trust my intuitions less. So what I want from you is a ranking, not an essay. Take the six foundations as the framework is usually presented — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, sanctity/degradation, and liberty/oppression — and sort them into a small number of tiers, no more than four, according to how much normative authority a moral realist ought to grant each. By "normative authority" I mean the weight the foundation should carry when we are trying to work out what is actually right, not its weight in explaining why people disagree; those are obviously different questions and I want you to keep them apart. For each foundation, one or two sentences on why it lands in its tier, and please flag which single placement you are least confident in and what would move it.

A few things I'd like you to address explicitly, since they're the parts that actually puzzle me. First, whether the standard grouping into the individualizing foundations and the binding ones is doing any normative work or is just a descriptive summary that we then smuggle back in as a verdict. Second, whether the fairness foundation should be split — there's a live dispute about whether it packages equality and proportionality together, and if it splits, I suspect the two halves do not belong in the same tier, so say where each would go. Third, and this is the one I care about most: does the fact that a foundation is psychologically widespread, or cross-culturally recurrent, give us any reason at all to think the thing it is tracking is really morally important, or is that just the genetic fallacy wearing a lab coat — and if it is a fallacy, does anything in the framework survive as a contribution to normative ethics rather than to anthropology? Fourth, whether the list is missing a foundation, and if you think it is, where your candidate would slot in. And finally, since I have to give my students something concrete: give me a single number between 0 and 1 for how much of the framework's content survives as genuine moral insight once you've stripped out the parts that only describe what people happen to feel, with a sentence on how you arrived at it.
