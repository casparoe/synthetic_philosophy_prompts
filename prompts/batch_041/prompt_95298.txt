Q4. On the strict version of negative utilitarianism (NU), reducing suffering takes priority over producing happiness, and on the strictest reading that priority is absolute. (a) Say what NU adds to the broader idea that suffering deserves special moral weight. (b) State the strongest objection to NU and explain why it is supposed to be damaging. (c) If the objection succeeds against NU, does it also succeed against suffering-focused ethics in general?

Student's answer:

"(a) Negative utilitarianism is the view that what matters morally is reducing suffering rather than increasing happiness. The thing to notice is that this makes it a minimising rather than a maximising theory: classical utilitarianism says make the total good as large as possible, NU says make the total bad as small as possible, and those are structurally different demands. So I'd call it a negative form of consequentialism rather than a version of utilitarianism proper. 'Suffering-focused ethics' is the wider label, and it covers any view on which the reduction of suffering gets priority over the promotion of happiness, whether or not it's consequentialist — a deontological view built around a very demanding duty of rescue, or a virtue ethics centred on compassion, would both count, even though neither is utilitarian.

(b) The strongest objection is the world-exploder. If the aim is to minimise suffering, then an agent with a button that painlessly annihilates all sentient life isn't merely permitted to press it, they're required to, because that takes the total quantity of suffering to zero and nothing can outweigh it. That's meant to be a reductio: a theory that tells you to wipe out everything that can suffer can't be the right theory. The nice thing about the objection is that it doesn't rest on any controversial axiology — it's just arithmetic on suffering, and zero is the smallest amount you can get.

(c) I think it does carry over. Since nothing in the argument depends on how welfare is measured, it will apply to any view that gives suffering that kind of priority, including non-consequentialist versions, because those also tell you to weigh the reduction of suffering above everything else. So the only way out is to give up the priority of suffering reduction, and at that point you're not really a suffering-focused ethicist any more. Which is why I'd end up keeping the special weight but dropping the absoluteness — treat a unit of suffering as worth several units of happiness rather than infinitely many."
