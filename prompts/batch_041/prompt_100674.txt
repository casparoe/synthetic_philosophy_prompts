I write about philosophy and the cognitive sciences, and I keep returning to a dispute that never seems to move. Carroll's tortoise puzzle is standardly read as a challenge to the justification of deduction: the tortoise grants the premises and the conditional but will not detach the conclusion, and every added conditional merely supplies another premise. One camp treats this as a kind of bad faith—anyone who really accepts the premises and the rule of inference already draws the conclusion—while another treats it as a genuine result: deduction cannot be justified without circularity because any justification must employ the very rule at issue. A third camp says these are two different questions, one about the psychology of inference and one about epistemic warrant, travelling under a single name. A colleague has now proposed a reading I have not seen before: the tortoise is a resource-bounded agent who will not spend an inference unless given a guarantee, so accepting a rule is a policy rather than a belief, and the regress is a decision-theoretic problem about when to act on an unsecured rule. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is the standoff substantive, verbal, or a case of sides answering different questions? Is my colleague's reading a genuine advance or a redescription that leaves the original issue untouched? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a formal result about rule-circularity, a distinction between using and mentioning a rule, an empirical finding about how reasoners detach conditionals—or should we expect none?
