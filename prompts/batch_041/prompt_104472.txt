I have spent most of my adult life assuming that decision-making under risk has exactly one respectable framework: assign probabilities, assign values to outcomes, maximize expected utility with a concave utility function doing the work of encoding your tolerance for variance, and treat everything else as either a special case of that or a mistake. A few months ago the assumption took a hit. I had just left a salaried job to manage a small family pool of capital — money that has to survive decades of withdrawals and a few bad years, rather than a portfolio I get to mentally reset each January — and a friend who plays poker semi-seriously told me that the only staking rule worth using is the Kelly criterion: stake the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes expected log wealth, which for an even-money wager that doubles your stake with probability p and loses it outright with probability 1 − p comes out to 2p − 1, so a bet you win 51% of the time justifies a two-percent stake and not a penny more. His argument was that this fraction makes your wealth compound fastest and that no other rule can beat it in the long run, and he described the underlying doctrine as geometric rationality, as though it were a rival to the arithmetic kind rather than a footnote to it.

What I want from you is a careful comparison of these two things as accounts of rational choice under risk: the growth-optimal staking rule, and expected-utility maximization of the ordinary sort. Are they rivals, or is the growth rule simply expected-utility maximization with the logarithm of wealth as the utility function — in which case someone who prefers it is expressing a taste for one particular curvature, and an investor who stakes more aggressively under a different utility function is not irrational but merely different? Or does the growth rule rest on something the expected-utility framework does not supply, namely an argument about the behavior of one actual wealth path through time rather than an average over hypothetical parallel versions of the same person — an argument that bites only because wealth multiplies rather than adds, and only because you expect to keep playing? I would like the strongest form of each side, the places where the two must genuinely diverge rather than merely sounding different, and an explicit sorting of which parts of the disagreement are substantive and which are terminological. Tell me also whether my friend's claim that no other staking rule can beat the growth-optimal one in the long run is true as stated or needs qualification, and whether the case for the growth rule survives when capital can be spread across many independent favorable opportunities at once, so that the average across simultaneous bets rather than the path of a single bankroll becomes the thing that matters.

Please do that concretely rather than in the abstract. Take the 51% even-money bet offered once, and the same bet offered every day for a year; a favorable wager with a much larger edge, where the growth rule wants a large stake and the chance of ruin is real; a case where the growth-optimal stake and the expected-value-maximizing stake point in opposite directions and you have to say which you would follow; and a case where the odds are only estimated, so that the supposedly optimal fraction is itself uncertain. For each, tell me what each framework recommends, where one of them has nothing to say at all, and which of the two you would trust if you were the one holding the pool — including whether the growth argument depends on assumptions my situation violates, such as an indefinite horizon, the absence of any absorbing state, and probabilities known exactly. If I have misstated either the growth rule or the expected-utility framework, correct me rather than humoring me. I do not need citations or a reading list; I need the reasoning.
