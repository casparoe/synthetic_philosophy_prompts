I teach a metaphysics course and somewhere along the way I stopped defending Humeanism about laws of nature as a position and started just inhabiting it. Roughly, I've ended up at the Mill-Ramsey-Lewis picture: the laws are the axioms or theorems of whichever deductive system best combines simplicity and strength in describing the whole distribution of local, particular matters of fact, and there's nothing more to lawhood than that -- no necessitation, no governing, no extra ingredient in the world. I've stopped even feeling the anti-Humean tug; 'the laws make things happen' now strikes me as a picture we absorbed from theology and never audited. So what I want from you is a real map of what would count against it. Not the objections I could recite half-asleep -- regularity theory's old embarrassments, the inference problem in the abstract -- but the things that should actually move me, ranked by how decisive they'd be. Does the undermining problem (chances that, applied to the very initial conditions that generated them, would make the chance-making system itself unlikely) count as a genuine defeat or just an ugly cost I can pay? Is it fatal that the account makes lawhood hostage to a notion of simplicity nobody can cash out in mind-independent terms, or is that a bill every view in the neighbourhood gets? And does the whole thing survive quantum entanglement, or do the local particular facts the Humean is committed to have to get gerrymandered so hard the position stops being recognizable?

I'm also after the cases where the account hands me a verdict I don't want: worlds with one instantiated law and infinitely many unexercised ones, worlds where the 'best' system is just a brute catalogue with no laws at all, and symmetry principles -- the sort of thing people argue are laws while adding no predictive strength, only constraints -- which look like they break the simplicity-strength tradeoff rather than sit inside it. Then tell me which of these a committed Humean would themselves concede counts against them and which they'd wave away; I want the concession column filled in, not just the critics' list. And be straight about the meta-question: has the view been insulated into unfalsifiability, say by the manoeuvre of 'holding fixed the rest of the system' or by treating Humean supervenience as a contingent thesis that could simply be given up if it fails? If it has, is that a problem for a view of this kind or just what any foundational view looks like from the outside? For what it's worth, the thing I'd personally find hardest to swallow is a case where the anti-Humean's ontology earns its keep -- where positing necessitation or primitive dispositions lets you explain or predict something the Humean can only redescribe. If there's such a case, lead with it.
