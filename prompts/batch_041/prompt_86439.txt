Ok so my friend Rosa and I got into this arguement last night that went until like 2am and I still don't know who was right, and I think writing it out might help. The setup: I'm in a cog sci reading group and we keep circling the question of which computations are the ones that matter morally — not "is a computer conscious" in the hand-wavy sense, but the actual criterion, the thing that's supposed to draw the line. Rosa's position, which she's been building for months and which she swears is not just warmed-over interpretationism: a system counts morally exactly when there's a fact of the matter about how things are for it, and the marker of that — the thing that actually draws the boundary — is the capacity to be mistaken about itself. If a system carries a model of its own states that can come apart from the states it actually has, then it's a locus of a point of view and you can owe it something; if it can't be wrong about itself, it's a process rather than a somebody, and you can't owe a process anything. My position, which I think is more defensible, is that status has to track the architecture instead: whether the states in question are the kind that get globally broadcast and can drive flexible, reportable behavior in the right way. A functional-role story that doesn't care at all whether the system is ever mistaken about itself. I said her criterion makes moral status turn on a kind of error, which is bizarre — do you earn more status by being less reliable about your own mind? — and she said mine smuggles in observer-relativity anyway, because "the right way" is just whatever we, the attributers, decide to count.

What I want from you: compare the two, properly, not a summary. Do they actually come apart, or is self-misrepresentation just a downstream symptom of global availability, so that we're naming the same cut from two directions? If they do come apart, which one should I keep?
