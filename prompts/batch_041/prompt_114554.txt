I am a doctoral candidate in the philosophy of physics, and I convene a fortnightly reading group for postgraduates: twelve regular attendees, backgrounds spread across metaphysics, logic and the history of science, all of them fluent in elementary set theory and first-order logic but few of them at ease with differential geometry. In three weeks I have the whole ninety minutes to myself, and I want to spend it on the hole argument — as an activity, not a paper session. My difficulty is that the argument is trivial to state and almost impossible to feel. I set out the two models, everyone cheerfully agrees that they must represent one and the same world, and nobody's intuitions have been touched. What I want is a session in which the group manufactures the two models itself, commits publicly to the commitments that generate the problem, and only then runs into it. Please design that session for me, with rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief. Specifically:

The toy world. The participants should build the world themselves, in small groups, out of cheap physical materials — something like a lattice of tokens joined by threads, where the tokens are stipulated to be numerically distinct individuals even though nothing in their appearance distinguishes them. It matters that the group, not I, writes down what its constituents are and which relations hold among them. The argument must not be named, or its conclusion hinted at, until the debrief.

The prior commitment. Before the puzzle appears, each group must publicly endorse a criterion for when two such constructions describe the same world — a test they will be held to afterwards. It is not enough for them to assent to some criterion in the abstract; they must also be induced to record, in the same ledger, claims about their own construction that only make sense if its tokens are numerically distinct individuals — questions about whether the token someone handled earlier is still present in the construction, and which one it is. I want them to have endorsed haecceitism about their own toy without noticing that they were doing anything contentious.

The trap. Only then is a rearrangement applied to a region of the construction: everything outside that region — and in particular everything that has already happened — keeps its threads exactly as before, while the resulting construction as a whole is a rearrangement of the original, so that no observation could distinguish the two. To make this bite, have groups exchange ledgers, so that a rival group selects the rearrangement most embarrassing to the ledger's authors. Each group must then rule, in public, ledger open on the table, on whether the rearranged construction is the same world or a different one, judged by its own criterion.

The forced choice. There must be no costless exit. Give participants a finite stock of counters that they forfeit for contradicting their own ledger, and arrange matters so that each horn is painful: a group that rules the constructions the same must strike out the clause in its own ledger about numerically distinct individuals, while a group that rules them different must say exactly which fact about the world was left unsettled by the state of affairs outside the region, given that this state was complete. I should like the group also to be pushed on whether a difference that no possible observation could ever register still counts as a difference in the world, and to notice that this is a further question, not the same one.

The debrief. Map what happened onto the argument: the region and the rearrangement onto a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside its hole; the complete state outside onto the initial data that fix the entire past; the two rulings onto Leibniz equivalence on the one hand and substantivalism with haecceitism on the other; and the resulting predicament onto indeterminism rather than mere underdetermination, which is why the argument is supposed to be a threat rather than an observation about theoretical equivalence. Set out at least one way of retaining substantivalism while refusing the indeterminism, and say plainly what must be given up to take it. You may use the standard terminology of the debate and name the positions, but do not attribute views to authors or supply a reading list: I shall do the referencing myself, and the session must not depend on any text being to hand.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the escape routes participants will reach for, the objections that will feel like cheating, the places where the timing will collapse — and how to handle each. And be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: which parts of the argument I shall simply have to state, and where the toy distorts the real case. I suspect that the discreteness of the construction flatters one of the horns, but you will know better than I do.

Please write the whole thing in British English.
