Here's the practice. A mid-sized contemporary art museum I work with has adopted what it calls a testimony-free label policy. The wall texts next to each work can give historical facts, materials, provenance, and neutral formal descriptions, like the canvas is divided into three vertical bands, but they are not allowed to make evaluative claims. No this is a masterpiece, no the brushwork is exquisite, no this is an important work. The audio guide is supposed to ask questions rather than assert aesthetic value. The idea is that visitors should judge for themselves, and that telling someone a work is good is somehow not a reason for them to believe it. But the same museum's acquisition committee is basically a small group of experts whose say-so settles which works enter the collection, and the catalogue essays are full of evaluative claims, like this is the most significant painting of its decade. The membership magazine runs lists of ten works you absolutely must see before you die. And the docents are trained to say things like most people find the late works more moving when they want visitors to linger in a room.

What I want from you: what philosophical committments does this whole pattern presuppose about aesthetic testimony? Would the labels have to change, the acquisition process, the docent training, all of it?
