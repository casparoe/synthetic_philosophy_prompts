I'm a postdoc in a philosophy department, and in three weeks I'm presenting at our "Mind and Machine" reading group. The group is small but awkwardly mixed, and I need to write for the people who will actually be in the room: three philosophers of mind, one a hard-line computationalist who thinks the mind just is a computer and will hear my talk as an attack on his research program, one a functionalist who is sympathetic to my conclusion but suspicious of the route I take to it, and one who regards the mind–body problem as permanently beyond human understanding and treats the whole machine question as a distraction; two logicians who know the incompleteness theorems cold and are audibly irritated whenever anyone drags them outside logic; a computational cognitive scientist who models reasoning as probabilistic inference and thinks deductive proof is a small, unrepresentative corner of cognition; and one retired mathematician who has spent twenty years arguing that Gödel proved no machine can think and who will be sitting in the front row. My thesis is that the incompleteness theorems have no bearing on the mind/machine question in either direction, and I want to defend it without leaning on the standard reply. That reply concedes that the human reasoner is, or may be treated as, a formal system with an axiom set and then haggles over whether we can know our own consistency, or whether we might be unsound or inconsistent; it leaves the debate alive precisely because it grants the picture the other side needs.

My own argument, which I'd like the prep sheet to target, is that this debate trades on a slide between two senses of "mechanical": first, computable — the outputs are produced by some procedure a machine could run — and second, formal — the reasoner is fully characterized by a specified axiom set plus inference rules. The incompleteness theorems bite on the second sense and are silent about the first, and the first is the only sense in which "the mind is a machine" is a substantive empirical hypothesis about us rather than a claim about how we might be described. So the Gödelian argument, as usually run, either establishes something nobody denies or establishes nothing at all.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones I would get at a logic conference or at an AI safety meetup — ordered by how much damage each would do to my position, each phrased the way the person who would raise it would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should concede up front before the questions even start, flag the single objection that has to be answered well for the talk to survive, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
