My friend and I argued late into the night about whether the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem proves too much, and we built the following argument. I want a diagnosis.

(1) Gibbard–Satterthwaite: any social choice function over three or more alternatives that is onto, defined on the unrestricted domain of strict preference profiles, and strategy-proof must be dictatorial.
(2) A decision procedure counts as legitimate only if no participant can do better by misreporting their preferences than by stating them honestly.
(3) Real collective decisions — a search committee choosing among three finalists, a city choosing among four transit plans — always offer at least three alternatives and place no restriction on the orderings participants may hold.
(4) Therefore every real collective decision procedure is dictatorial.
(5) But majority rule over single-peaked preferences returns the median position, and no participant is a dictator there.
(6) Since (1) is a theorem, either (2) or (3) must be false.

My friend concludes that (2) is the culprit: strategy-proofness is an impossible ideal, and honest procedures simply have to tolerate manipulation. He adds that letting procedures use lotteries does not rescue them, since the analogue of Gibbard–Satterthwaite for probabilistic rules delivers only a random dictatorship. I am reluctant to give up (2) — it seems to me the whole point of a fair procedure — but I cannot tell whether that is conviction or stubbornness.

Tell me which step to reject, why it looked compelling, and what rejecting it costs. If the faulty step is (3), say precisely what is wrong with it, whether the single-peakedness example in (5) is a fair escape or a cheat, and what the lottery point does or does not add to the friend's case.
