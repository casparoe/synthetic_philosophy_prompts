I spent thirty-odd years as a chemist before I retired, and for the last couple of years I've been reading philosophy, which is a strange experiance — half the time I feel like I've walked into a room where everyone has agreed on the furniture and I can't see it.

Here's the thing I keep chewing on. There's a move I associate with Darwin and with Turing, and I've started thinking of it as an inversion of reasoning. Darwin: you don't need a designer for design to show up, you just need variation plus retention. Turing: you don't need a mind that understands arithmetic for arithmetic to get done, you just need the right machinery grinding through the steps. In both cases, something that looked like it required comprehension turns out to be producible without any. The comprehension is in the result, not the process.

Now my actual question. Give me your honest credence, an actual number, that this move generalizes all the way up to linguistic understanding:

Proposition: some process, or class of processes, produces competence with language of the same kind and range we have, and at no stage in that process is there any understanding of what's being said — and moreover there's no further fact about understanding left over once you've accounted for the competence. Understanding just is competence of the right kind, all the way down.

I want it decomposed, not a bare number:
- the chance that meaning really is exhausted by functional/computational role, i.e. there's no further fact of the matter,
- conditional on that, the chance that a Darwin/Turing-style process could actually generate that competence rather than it needing something else — embodiment, causal contact with the world, a social practice, whatever,
- conditional on both, the chance we'd ever be in a position to know it, as opposed to the metaphysics being settled but undetectable from the inside.

A number and one line of why for each, then how they combine. Then tell me which of those three terms is doing the most work in your final figure, and what would move it.

And the part I actually care about: is this one move or am I lumping two things together? Darwin's inversion is about where design comes from; Turing's is about what a process needs. What I'm really asking about is a third thing, about content — competence that gets transmitted and recombined with no comprehension anywhere in the chain, the way a language model is trained on text that was written by people who did understand. Does that break the analogy, or is it just the first two stacked? Because if it's just the first two stacked then I should stop being spooked by it, and if it isn't, I'd like to know exactly where the extra something is supposed to come in.
