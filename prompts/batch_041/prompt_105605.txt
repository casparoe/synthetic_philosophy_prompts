I'm putting together the take-home final for a grad seminar I teach on social choice and political philosophy — the course is roughly half Arrow/Sen, half the liberty-and-rights literture — and I want one long essay question on Sen's liberal paradox that a student can't pass just by writing out the theorem. Everyone in the room can recite the conditions and the conclusion. What I want them to do is build something.

Here's the setup I have in mind. Two parts. Part (i): they say in their own words what the theorem actually establishes — which of the conditions the contradiction lands on, why the Pareto premise being so weak is what makes the result bite rather than being a curiosity, and why "well, just drop Pareto" is too quick. Part (ii) is the real question: I hand them a proposed repair and make them test it.

The repair, as I'll state it to them: keep minimal liberalism exactly as Sen states it — at least two people, each decisive over at least one pair of alternatives in their own personal sphere, with no requirement that a person's preferences over their own sphere be the same in every context. Then respond to the objection that the Pareto principle is what's doing the damage by restricting it: the Pareto principle may only be applied to pairs of alternatives that differ solely in the private dimension of one person, so it can never be applied to a pair that differs in two people's private dimensions at once. Everything else stays put — unrestricted domain over preferences, so people may care about other people's private choices as much as they like, and the social preference still has to be a genuine ordering, complete and transitive.

I'm fairly confident the repair doesn't work, and that the reason is more intresting than "he just weakened Pareto", but I haven't worked the thing out properly and I dont want to put a false claim in front of the students. My hunch is that you can get an intransitivity out of the liberty conditions by themselves, with no Pareto anywhere in the construction, and that it turns on the fact that being decisive over a pair doesn't pin down how that person ranks their own private alternatives in other contexts. So I want the question to ask them to either produce a counterexample or show that none exists, and if they produce one, to identify exactly which feature of the liberty condition makes it possible and whether some further, independently plausible restriction would block it.
