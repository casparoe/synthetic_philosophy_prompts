I'm a novelist, about a third of the way into a book about a woman in her late forties who sinks everything she has into buying the tiny press that put out her first novel twenty years ago, and I've been having a running arguement with my friend Priya, who used to trade for a living, about whether my character is being brave or being an idiot. The argument keeps landing in the same place, and it's a place I don't understand. Priya will say, in one breath, that if you average over all the parallel versions of you — the ones where it works out and the ones where it doesn't — then any bet with a positive expected value is worth taking, that's just what the words mean; and in the next breath she'll say that someone who stakes her whole pile every time ends up with nothing even when the odds are in her favour, because what happens to you as you go along isn't the same thing as the average across all your copies. Those two sentences sound like they contradict each other, she swears they don't, and I beleive her, but I need to see it working on a case, because the whole book turns on whether a reader thinks my character's decision is defensible and I don't want to rig the scene in either direction.

So here's what I'm asking for: build me the case, twice, with the numbers worked out, identical in every respect except one. Version one: my character can buy any slice of the press she wants — the family will sell her five percent, forty percent, whatever she can afford. Version two: the only deal on the table is the whole thing or nothing, because they won't split the shares. Everything else held fixed, same probabilities, same payoff multiples, same amount of capital, same time frame. What I want is a case where the expected-money rule and the growth rule (Priya calls it the Kelly rule, the one that maximises what your money compounds to rather than what it averages to) give the same verdict about whether to play in version one and opposite verdicts in version two. And I don't want the all-or-nothing version to be a wipeout — in the bad branch she should still walk away with a real chunk of her capital, so nobody can dismiss the growth rule as just being scared of zero. Then walk me through it: what each rule says in each version and why, which feature of the case is actually doing the work and exactly where the line is (if there's a stake size or a probability at which the growth rule flips, show me), what happens if that feature goes away, and — the part I care about most — what a clever defender of the rule that comes out worse would say to argue the whole case away. One more thing, out of curiosity: if the same offer came back every spring for twenty years, would either verdict change, and in which version?

Keep it realistic — no casinos, no coin flips, no lottery tickets, no infinite payoffs, and nobody with supernatural foresight. Write it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just the way you'd explain it to me over a long email. And if the two versions can't be made to come out the way I've asked without cheating somewhere, say so instead of fudging it.
