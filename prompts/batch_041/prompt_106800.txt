You are a decision theorist who knows the formal literature on choice under risk and uncertainty, and I would like your help working through a pattern in my own choices. Please think carefully, and begin by mapping the cases below — what varies across them and what is held fixed — before you propose any principle. I care more about whether the principle actually fits my verdicts than about which famous model it happens to resemble.

I have a long-standing habit of avoiding gambles whose odds I do not know. When I push on that habit with specific cases, it comes apart, and I cannot tell whether that is my own inconsistency or a sign that I am tracking two different things. Here are my verdicts.

First case. Urn A holds 100 balls, exactly 50 red and 50 black, and I know this. Urn B holds 100 balls, each red or black, in some proportion nobody has told me. I will bet on a red draw from A over a red draw from B, and I will bet on a black draw from A over a black draw from B.

Second case. Urn C holds 100 balls whose red-and-black proportion was chosen by someone I know nothing about, with no reason to expect red or black to be favored. Even though my ignorance here is symmetric between the two colors, I still take a red bet from A over a red bet from C, and a black bet from A over a black bet from C.

Third case. Urn D holds 100 balls whose proportion was fixed by a fair coin: heads means exactly 30 red, tails means exactly 70 red. I am indifferent between a red bet from D and a red bet from A, and I prefer either of them to B.

Fourth case. Urns E and F are both certified to hold exactly 50 red and 50 black. E was filled in a lab I trust; F was filled by a carnival operator whose certification I suspect is sloppy. I prefer the red bet from E.

Fifth case. Urn G holds 100 balls in an unknown proportion. A bet pays me only if the proportion is extreme — all red or all black — and pays nothing for a middling urn. For this payoff I prefer G to a known 50/50 urn.

Sixth case. Urn H's proportion was fixed by a genuinely indeterministic process that has already run, with no surviving record; Urn I's proportion was fixed deterministically, but the record is lost. I find that I feel exactly the same about H and I, and that both feel worse to me than A.

My tentative stab is that I am not averse to unknown odds as such but to not knowing the process that produced them — but the third and fifth cases both push against that, and the fourth suggests that even a known process can leave me averse if I do not trust who ran it.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing principles, each stated precisely enough that I could test it against a new case.
