I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of biology, and I'm trying to determine how much argumentative weight a certain analogy can bear before it quietly gives out. A colleague of mine — trained as an economist, now working on evolutionary models of social behavior — put his position to me roughly as follows: "Conspicuous consumption is the peacock's tail. The luxury watch, the gala, the yacht: these are handicaps, and their credibility comes precisely from their waste. The handicap principle taught us that a signal is trustworthy only when it is costly enough that a low-quality individual could not afford to fake it, so the waste is not a defect of the system — it is the whole point of it. It follows that moralizing about wasteful consumption is confused: the waste is doing informational work, and a society that somehow abolished it would lose its only reliable way of sorting the genuinely high-quality from the pretenders."

I find this genuinely illuminating — there is clearly something right about the thought that the cost is what buys the credibility — but I also suspect the analogy is being asked to license more than it can. He is not being idiosyncratic; a compressed version of this move shows up whenever signaling theory is exported from biology into the human sciences, and I want to be able to say exactly where the transfer is legitimate and where it is not.

What I'd like from you is a careful audit of the analogy rather than a defense or a refutation of the conclusion. Concretely:

First, give me the mapping explicitly. What in the peacock case corresponds to the signaler, the receiver, the signal itself, the cost, the information conveyed, and the evolutionary payoff on each side? A correspondence table is welcome, and I'd rather have it spelled out than gestured at.

Second, identify where the mapping breaks. I'm especially interested in the difference between a signal whose honesty is underwritten by the signaler's underlying condition — so that only a genuinely robust individual can sustain the display — and a signal whose honesty is underwritten merely by a budget constraint, so that anyone with the resources can produce it. I also want the distinction between a cost that is a true handicap, lowering the signaler's survival prospects, and a cost that is simply a price paid for an index of some already-existing property. Does the popular use of "handicap" collapse these? Does it matter for the conclusion?

Third, tell me which features of the peacock case the analogy quietly imports that the human case may not have: a well-defined chooser on the receiving end rather than a diffuse audience of rivals; a co-evolved preference; an inference from the signal to something that actually bears on the receiver's fitness. Where those are missing, does the "the waste is the point" claim survive, or does it turn into a much weaker claim about costly signals in general?

Fourth, separate the disanalogies that matter for the specific conclusion my colleague draws — that waste is functionally necessary and that moralizing about it is confused — from the ones that are merely interesting. In particular, does anything in the analogy support the necessity claim, or does "a society without it would have no way to sort..." simply not follow from any version of the signaling logic?

Finally, if the analogy can be repaired, tell me what a better one would be, and what it would and wouldn't carry. I'd rather end with a calibrated verdict — this part transfers, this part doesn't, the conclusion survives in this weakened form or not at all — than with a general reflection on the perils of biological analogy.
