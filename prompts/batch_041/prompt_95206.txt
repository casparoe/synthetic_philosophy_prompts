I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for a themed issue on behavioural public policy I am commissioning a satirical piece. The target is the family of doctrines that travel under the name of libertarian paternalism: the proposal that a state may deliberately steer its citizens toward the choices they would have made for themselves, so long as it neither coerces anyone nor makes any option materially harder to obtain. I want the satire aimed at the position and its characteristic habits of argument rather than at any living person, and I want it written from inside — no winking, no narrator who steps out to tell the reader what to think.

The form should be an internal practitioner's handbook, written deadpan, for a fictional national office of choice architecture: the sort of document a new analyst would be handed on her first morning. Give it numbered sections with headings, a bureaucratic register, and roughly eight hundred words. Invent the country, the office, and its house jargon; do not name real agencies, real statutes, or real people.

What matters most is that the philosophy be done in the interstices. The handbook should:

Set out a tiered classification of interventions, running from information, reminders, and default settings at one end to measures designed to work below the threshold of conscious awareness at the other, and should treat the entire scale as a single continuous practice rather than a ladder with a break in it. Its stated justification for the lowest tier — that the citizen's set of options has not been altered, and therefore nothing has been taken from her — should be delivered with complete serenity, as though that settled the matter.

Describe, in the same administrative voice, the office's procedure for establishing what citizens would have chosen for themselves had they deliberated longer and better, and let the reader gradually notice what the procedure presupposes: that someone must decide what good deliberation consists in, and that the office's own judgments are quietly exempted from the test it applies to everyone else.

Contain a disclosure policy explaining why certain classes of measure are never published, with a rationale that, taken at face value, concedes the very objection it was written to answer.

Somewhere concede that deciding which behaviour to make easy — saving, donating an organ, eating less meat, or their contraries — is a substantive choice about how people ought to live rather than a neutral matter of clearing away friction, and then continue as though nothing of consequence had been said.

Include a section on internal oversight, in which the body charged with vetting the office's interventions turns out to be constituted by a default setting that nobody has ever thought to change.

A reader should be able to reconstruct the standard objections from the document alone — the difficulty of reading preferences off people who do not yet have them, the awkward fact that some of these measures appear to work only so long as they go unnoticed, the gap between helping someone pursue her own ends and substituting the office's ends for hers, and the question of who audits the auditors — without the handbook ever naming or conceding a single one of them. Someone who has never encountered any of this should find the piece funny and faintly alarming; a specialist in the field should recognise the arguments and judge the parody fair rather than cheap.
