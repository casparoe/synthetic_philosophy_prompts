I'm a backend engineer by trade, so my exposure to this is mostly a bioethics elective I took in college and an argument that keeps recurring at family dinners. The topic is when someone counts as dead, and I've noticed I have fairly strong gut reactions to a set of cases that I can't get to line up under any single rule I've been able to state. Here's what I think, going case by case. A patient in a persistent vegetative state — cortex gone, brainstem intact, breathing without a ventilator — is alive; I wouldn't call her dead, even though I don't expect her to come back. A patient with total brain death — no brainstem reflexes, on a ventilator — is dead, full stop, even though his chest is rising and his skin is warm to the touch. An anencephalic infant, born with essentially no cortex above the brainstem, is alive, though I'm not sure I could defend that against the previous two verdicts. A patient in a locked-in state, fully aware but almost completely paralyzed, is obviously alive. Someone whose heart has stopped and who is being actively resuscitated on an emergency room floor is not yet dead — I'd say we simply don't know yet, and that pronouncing it either way mid-resuscitation is a mistake. A patient on a heart-lung machine with no heartbeat of his own but a working brain is alive. And a patient who has been brain dead for two days and whose body is being maintained on machines so the organs can be recovered — I have a strong feeling the person is already gone there, yet the body is still somehow something, and it bothers me that I'd let a surgeon open it.

What I want to know is whether there's a principle underneath all of that, or whether I'm just tracking two or three different questions without realizing it. I keep going back and forth on the anencephalic infant, because if I say the vegetative patient is alive on the grounds that the organism is still integrating itself, the infant seems to qualify too — but it never had a mind to lose, so I'm not sure the same reasoning even applies. Please give me the best single principle, or a small set of competing candidates, and state each precisely enough that I could apply it myself. For each clause, tell me which of my verdicts above motivates it; say what the principle does to the cases I listed; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Then apply it to two cases I haven't mentioned: a patient whose cortex and brainstem are both destroyed but whose body is being perfused by a machine, and a patient in a permanent vegetative state who signed an advance directive asking not to be kept alive.

Finally, one request about how to pitch the answer: separate the parts of this where there's a genuine consensus among people who actually study the question from the parts where informed people disagree, and don't present a contested position as though it were settled. I'd rather have a precise map of where the disagreement lives than a confident-sounding single answer that papers it over.
