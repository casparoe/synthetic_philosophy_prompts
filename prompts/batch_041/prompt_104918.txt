I'm a software engineer and for the last several months I've been on a mech interp team, mostly training sparse autoencoders on a mid-size transformer's residual stream, labeling the features that fall out, then testing them by steering and ablating. Our internal rubric is roughly: a feature counts as "load-bearing" if ablating it drops benchmark accuracy by more than a couple percent and steering it in the labeled direction moves the output the way the label says; if it just lights up on some inputs but nothing happens when we poke it, we call it "decorative," write it in the appendix, and move on. My roommate did a philosophy minor and keeps needling me about what we think we're actually doing — are these features things the network has, or just things we made up and then found again? — and I've been brushing it off, but I'd like to actually give him an answer.

So: what view of representation does this whole pipeline presuppose? It seems like we're committed to features being real internal states of the model that we discover rather than impose, but I can't tell if that's something the practice actually requires or just a habit of speech. Which rival pictures does it quietly rule out — that the features are just useful fictions, that representation talk is a category error and we should only talk about input-output behavior, that a "feature" is nothing over and above whatever a linear probe can decode? And here's the part that bugs me: our rubric makes causal efficacy (steering, ablation) the test of whether a feature is real, but half the features in our appendix are purely correlational and we still call them features, and meanwhile the same team will tell you with a straight face that the model doesn't really have beliefs or goals. Is that consistent, or are we running two incompatible theories at once? A colleague also keeps saying that any invertible change of basis on the residual stream gives you an equally good SAE with different features, so asking which features are real is like asking which basis of a vector space is the real basis — I don't know if that's a genuine objection or a trick. Reconstruct the implicit theory of representation our practice presupposes, separate what it strictly needs from what it just fits nicely with, say where it's underdetermined (i.e. where several theories would license the same practice), and tell me what would have to change in how we do the work if we swapped it for the deflationary view.
