I'm a program officer at a mid-sized foundation that funds global health and development work, and every grant cycle my committee gets dragged into the same unwinnable fight. It's always some version of: do we fund the thing that saves lives, which means more people end up existing, or the thing that makes the people who already exist better off? Or: is it better to give a tiny boost to a huge number of people, or a big boost to a smaller number? Someone on the board read some population ethics last year and now everyone's paralized, because depending on wether you go by total welfare, average welfare, or one of the views that puts a floor under how bad a life can be before it counts, the answer flips. We're never going to agree on the theory and honestly we don't need to.

Here's what I actually want, and it's not a philosophy lecture. Give me a decision procedure my committee can actually run on a proposal that (1) tells us whether the proposal is one where all the main views agree, in which case we can fund it or kill it without settling anything, and (2) tells us when a proposal really does force the theory fight, and for those, lays out what each main view says so we at least know what we're choosing between. I need the steps in order, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure gives a clean no, and where it has to stop and hand the call to the board because it's a values question wearing a costume.

Constraints: we've got about a week per cycle, none of us are philosophers, the board signs off on anything over $2M, and the proposals are messy -- we can't measure well-being, we can't know how many future people a given project causes to exist, and half the time we're comparing a project that helps 50,000 people a lot with one that helps 5 million people a little.

Run the procedure on three of them: (a) deworming that measurably improves the health of a few million kids who were going to exist anyway; (b) a program that cuts infant mortality in one region, which everyone wants to call a success but which also means more people alive down the line; (c) a cheap intervention that gives a barely-noticeable benefit to a staggering number of people. Then tell me straight where this procedure is going to spit out an answer we all hate, and what would actually change your mind about the procedure itself -- what argument or piece of evidence would make you rewrite it.
