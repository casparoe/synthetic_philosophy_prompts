I host a small podcast on philosophy of biology and cognitive science, and next week's guest is a philosopher whose work sits at the intersection of evolutionary theory and computability. I would rather you treat her as an invented defender of the position I describe below than attribute the view to any real living person. Her recent project is a sustained attack on a move that has become almost automatic in discussions of AI and animal minds: invoking Darwin and Turing together as two great precedents for the idea that competence can outrun comprehension. Her claim is not that the precedents are wrong but that they are inversions of different things, and that the standard pairing hides an equivocation. Darwin's inversion, as she reconstructs it, concerns the order of explanation between function and mechanism: selection can produce ends-directed organization without any representation of those ends, so the competence belongs to a lineage and is measured by differential reproduction. Turing's inversion, by contrast, concerns the order of explanation between rule and understanding: a formal system can implement a rule-governed procedure without any grasp of what the rules mean, so the competence belongs to a system and is measured by what it computes. She argues that contemporary debates about large language models, reinforcement learning, and animal cognition routinely slide between these two senses, treating a system's computational competence as if it were evidence of a lineage's selectional competence, or vice versa, and that this slide is what makes both the hype and the dismissals so slippery.
