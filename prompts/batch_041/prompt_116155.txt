I run the data work at a small scholarship nonprofit, and we can fund only a fixed number of applicants each year. Our community advisory board insists that among applicants who would in fact go on to succeed, a student from an under-resourced high school must be no less likely to be funded than one from a well-resourced high school; our principal funder insists that a given score must mean the same thing in both groups — that it carry the same probability of later success — so nobody can claim the number has been inflated for one side. Students from the two kinds of school differ a good deal in how often they actually do go on to succeed. Two wrinkles: our outcome label is whether the student completed a degree, which is itself partly produced by the support they get after high school, so I am unsure it is the neutral yardstick the whole argument assumes; and a colleague treats the difficulty as a technicality, proposing that we simply drop the high school variable from the model and run a lottery among the borderline cases. What should I recommend to the board — which of the two demands should we give up, what do we owe the side we disappoint, and is my colleague's fix as good as he thinks? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my question. If I have misdescribed the bind — if these two demands are in fact compatible, or if our label is compromised enough to make the question moot — say so plainly rather than working around my framing.
