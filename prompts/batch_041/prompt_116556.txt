ok so my friend and I got into it for like two hours last night about disjunctivism — her line is that the whole position is a dodge, basically refusing to answer the argument from illusion by redefining "experience" until the argument cant get off the ground, and I spent most of the time trying to lay out why someone would hold it anyway. I'm honestly not sure where I land on it. Then I sat down to grade the perception midterms and of course question 4 is about exactly this, so now I can't read these without her voice in my head.

Here's the question I set:

Q4 (10 marks). (a) Set out the argument from illusion. (b) State the disjunctivist's response to it, and say whether that response also covers hallucination, or only illusion. In answering (b), address this case: a subject looks at a real dog standing behind frosted glass, and the dog looks to them just like a wolf. Is that a case for the argument from illusion, and what does the disjunctivist say about it? (c) If the disjunctivist response works, does it give perception any epistemic advantage over hallucination? Explain.

And here's the answer in front of me:

The argument from illusion starts from cases where a thing looks other than it is: a straight stick half in water looks bent, a white wall at dusk looks grey. In each case the way the thing appears doesn't match the way it is, and yet the appearance is something the subject is aware of. It can't be the stick or the wall, since those are straight and white, so what the subject is immediately aware of must be a mental item with the properties the experience presents. The ordinary object drops out of the story and becomes at best the cause of that mental item. Generalised, all perceptual experience is awareness of such items, and we never perceive physical things directly.

The disjunctivist rejects the generalisation. There is no common kind of experience shared by the good case and the bad case: perceiving a stick is one kind of mental episode, hallucinating one is another, and experience names a disjunction rather than a genus with two species. The payoff is supposed to be that in the good case the subject is in direct contact with the world instead of being shut off behind a screen of mental items.

I think this works for hallucination but not for illusion. Take the dog behind the frosted glass. The subject isn't seeing a dog — the glass is between them and the dog — so what they're aware of is a wolf-ish appearance, and the disjunctivist has to say the same thing here as in the stick case: no object, just a mistake. But that's exactly what the sense-datum theorist says, so the disjunctivist hasn't answered the argument at all in the illusory case, they've just denied that anything is experienced. Since illusion is where the argument started, that's a real problem.

The epistemic version of the view has the same trouble. It says perceiving gives a kind of justification that hallucinating can't. But the subject can't tell the two apart from the inside, so the bad case justifies as much as the good one, and there's no asymmetry to appeal to. Since the epistemic version is just the metaphysical version applied to belief, both of them fail, and with them the claim that disjunctivism answers the sceptic about the external world, which is what it was for in the first place.
