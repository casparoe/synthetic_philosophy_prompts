I'm reading George Eliot's 1855 essay on Dr. Cumming -- "Evangelical Teaching" -- for a reading group and I keep feeling like I'm either missing the joke or missing the fact that there is no joke. Please take the role of someone who really knows both the Victorian religious controversies and Eliot's own philosophical commitments at that moment (I think she'd already translated Strauss and Feuerbach by then, though the novels are still ahead of her). Think it through carefully before you answer, and start by sketching two or three rival readings of what she's actually up to in this passage, then tell me which one you think survives contact with the text.

Here's the passage. It's the opening move of the essay: she's describing, hypothetically, how a man of moderate intellect and average morals can rise in English society, and then she tells him how to play it.

"Let him shun practical extremes and be ultra only in what is purely theoretic; let him be stringent on predestination, but latitudinarian on fasting; unflinching in insisting on the Eternity of punishment, but diffident of curtailing the substantial comforts of Time; ardent and imaginative on the pre-millennial advent of Christ, but cold and cautious toward every other infringement of the status quo. Let him fish for souls not with the bait of inconvenient singularity, but with the drag-net of comfortable conformity. Let him be hard and literal in his interpretation only when he wants to hurl texts at the heads of unbelievers and adversaries, but when the letter of the Scriptures presses too closely on the genteel Christianity of the nineteenth century, let him use his spiritualizing alembic and disperse it into impalpable ether. Let him preach less of Christ than of Antichrist; let him be less definite in showing what sin is than in showing who is the Man of Sin, less expansive on the blessedness of faith than on the accursedness of infidelity."

A bit later in the same piece she says that the clergy are, practically, the most irresponsible of all talkers, because nobody hisses and nobody walks out.

What's bugging me: on the surface this reads like a straight secularist hit job, religion as a career strategy for mediocrities. But the tone is so strange. She isn't arguing that Christianity is false; she's arguing that this man's Christianity is a particular kind of social performance, and she's doing it with what sounds like intimate knowledge of the inside of a religious sensibility. So:

1) What is the actual object of criticism here -- the doctrine, the man, the market for preachers, the audience that wants comfortable conformity, or the whole arrangement? Is this an attack on religion or on a deformation of it, or is that distinction something she'd reject?

2) The business about being literal only when hurling texts at unbelievers, and spiritualizing the letter when it presses on genteel Christianity -- is that a claim about how interpretation actually works, or just a charge of hypocrisy? Does it matter that the same charge could be turned on a liberal believer who spiritualizes the harsh bits for better reasons?

3) How does this connect to the thing people always say about Eliot's later humanist religiosity -- that religious feeling survives the loss of dogma? Is any of that already here in 1855, or is this just pure demolition with nothing being built?

4) And here's the one that really nags at me. In another piece, reviewing Lecky on the history of rationalism, she seems to endorse the idea that beliefs die out not because anyone refutes them but because they become incongruous with prevailing habits of thought -- that disbelief precedes examination rather than resulting from it. If that's her view, then what is a critical essay even supposed to accomplish? Who is she writing for, and is she just preaching (ha) to the already-persuaded? Is there a coherent theory of persuasion in the background, or is she inconsistent?
