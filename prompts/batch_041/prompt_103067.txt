Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of three hours about whether the problem of cluelessness is a knockout blow against consequentialism, and we came away further apart than we started: she is now convinced that anyone who calls themselves a consequentialist is either fooling themselves or quietly smuggling in non-consequentialist constraints, and I am convinced we were both confused about what the problem even is. My diagnosis is that the discussion keeps circling a single coarse question — whether the unknowable long-run consequences of our actions make consequentialism untenable — when the real work lies in prying apart several distinct worries that get bundled together. There is the worry that the causal chain from any act to the distant future is so long and chaotic that the relevant probabilities are simply inscrutable; the worry that even where we can assign estimates, the expected value is dominated by minuscule probabilities of astronomically large or infinite effects, so the arithmetic becomes either noise or undefined; and the worry that the trouble lies not in our estimates at all but in the consequentialist's insistence on evaluating acts by outcomes we could never in principle compare. These look as though they might have very different answers, and quite different implications for what a consequentialist should actually do.

I am advising a philosopher who has roughly two years of protected time and a background in decision theory and formal epistemology rather than in normative ethics, and who wants to work on whichever of these is genuinely upstream of the rest. Please give me a defended agenda: rank the open questions in this area by how much other work depends on their answer, and for each one say what makes it upstream or downstream, how tractable it is for someone with that particular background, and what kind of result or argument over the next two years would move it up or down the list. I would much rather you commit to an ordering and defend it — naming the criteria you are trading off, and where they conflict — than hedge across the whole field. The deliverable I want is an agenda, not a survey.

Treat several things as live options rather than settled, and tell me which of them would reorganize the ranking if they turned out to be true and which are red herrings: the thought that long-run outcomes converge, so that small perturbations wash out and the far future is largely insensitive to what we do now; the thought that cluelessness afflicts all practical reasoning equally and is therefore no special objection to consequentialism; the thought that the right response is to abandon precise probabilities in favor of imprecise or non-additive ones; and the thought that the real issue is the moral weight we assign to the far future rather than uncertainty at all. Do not give me a reading list or a set of citations — I know the literature well enough — I want your reasoning about what deserves the two years.
