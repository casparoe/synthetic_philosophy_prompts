I write a monthly column on science and ideas, and for the past few weeks I've been embedded in the conservation lab of a small ceramics museum, reporting a piece on how the metaphysics of material constitution shows up in ordinary craft practice — specifically, the way conservators talk about the objects on their benches as though each were two things sharing one body, a lump of fired earth and a figure, with two different life expectancies. The case that has been bothering me is a large unfired terracotta figure, modelled months ago and never fired. As a figure it is finished; as clay it is precarious, since unfired clay is fragile, water-soluble, and will not survive much handling, let alone a move or a leaky roof. The treatment options are grimly symmetrical. Fire it, and the material is stabilised for centuries, but the kiln will shrink it and may well slump or crack the form, so that what comes out is a durable lump that is no longer quite the figure. Leave it, and the figure stays exactly as it is for as long as it lasts, which may not be long. What struck me in the lab is that nobody there treats this as a merely technical problem; the arguments they have sound exactly like the ones philosophers have about statues and their clay, except the conservators have to decide by Friday and sign their names to the decision.

I'd like a poem for the column: a villanelle, nineteen lines, spoken in the voice of the conservator who has to sign off on the treatment, with the two refrains being, as nearly as you can manage in verse, the one-thing line and the two-things line — the claim that there is a single object on the bench with a single history, and the claim that there are two, with two — so that the form itself keeps returning to a claim and then to its denial without ever letting either drop out of the poem. The closing quatrain should not pick a winner; it should make both refrains true of the object on the bench at once, and should register that the treatment decision can be made, defensibly, without settling which of them is right. Use the villanelle's habit of making a repeated line mean something new each time it comes round to carry the thought that the very same clay is being described and re-described; let the physical details do the metaphysical work — the shrinkage, the slump, the bucket of water, the shelf label that says one thing and the accession number that says another. Please keep the words 'identity', 'constitution', 'essence', 'ontology' and 'mereology' out of the poem entirely, and don't let it collapse into the shrug that it all depends on how you describe it unless the poem genuinely earns that. If a philosopher of art or a metaphysician reads it, they should recognise the problem and find nothing wrong; if a donor at the museum's annual evening hears it read aloud, they should simply find it a good poem.
