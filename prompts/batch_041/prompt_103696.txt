In my interpretability reading group there is a one-liner that keeps doing the work of an argument, and I would like to see it taken apart. The pattern is this: someone presents evidence that a trained network's internal activations carry some property — a direction in activation space that tracks whether a statement is true, a small set of units that tracks a syntactic category, and so on — and a colleague replies that the network 'is just a giant lookup table'. The remark is meant to settle the matter: if the network is only a table, then there is nothing inside it that represents anything, and the reported finding is a curiosity about a large memorization device rather than a discovery about representation.

I am not convinced, but I also cannot say precisely where the remark goes wrong, because I suspect it is several remarks wearing one coat. What I would like from you is a disambiguation.

First, set out the distinct precise claims that 'it is just a giant lookup table' could be making, arranged from the ones that are barely worth stating to the ones that are genuinely contentious. For each, give a verdict — trivial, contestable but defensible, contestable and probably false, or conceptually confused — and say what would have to be true for it to hold.

Second, say which of those readings actually supports the conclusion my colleague draws — that the network does not represent the property in question — and which are beside the point, however true they may be. I want to know which single reading his objection needs in order to land, and which question I could put to him to find out whether he means that reading or a cheaper one.

I would like the analysis to engage with four things, since these seem to me to be where the work is:

(a) whether the claim concerns the network's extension — the input-output pairs it realizes — or its mechanism — what its weights and activations are doing — and whether a claim of the first kind can bear on the second at all;

(b) the difference between information being recoverable from the activations by a purpose-trained probe and the network's actually using that information when it produces its output;

(c) the fact that a hidden layer's activations can be re-encoded by any invertible linear map, with the following layer's weights adjusted to compensate, without altering the network's input-output behavior in the slightest, so that behavior by itself appears unable to fix what is represented;

(d) the case of a network that reaches zero error on its training set early and then, after a long stretch of further training, abruptly begins to generalize to held-out inputs — a phenomenon that sits awkwardly with any reading on which what has been learned is a table of the training data.

Finally, he uses two other lines interchangeably with the lookup-table line — that the network is 'just curve fitting', and that it has 'just memorized the training set' — and I would like to know whether these are the same claim in different words or three separate claims that would each need a separate answer.

Lay the readings out as a numbered menu rather than choosing one and defending it, and close by naming the reading my colleague must have in mind for his objection to work.
