For the past several years I have hosted a small interview podcast on long-term thinking and the philosophy of technology, mostly conversations with decision theorists, economists, and the occasional philosopher, and next month's episode is with a guest I have wanted to speak with since I first encountered her work. She defends a position I find genuinely unsettling: that the simulation argument, taken seriously, ought to make us considerably less confident in the enormous expected-value calculations that underwrite what is usually called fanaticism about the far future, so that rather than supplying fresh reasons to care about distant generations it functions as a kind of solvent on the numbers. I came to the topic sideways. I teach a graduate seminar on intergenerational ethics, and I had assigned a unit on decision-making under extreme uncertainty when a student asked why, if we might be simulated, we should assign the far future the value that longtermists assign to it. I could not give a clean answer, which is why I have invited her on.

I would like you to draft eight to ten interview questions for this conversation. I do not want her merely restating her thesis; I want the questions to do real work against the position, and each should be paired with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. In particular, I want at least one question that forces her to separate the simulation argument from the plain simulation hypothesis, and to say whether the disjunctive conclusion of the familiar three-pronged argument can carry the dampening weight she wants to place on it, given that in two of the three disjuncts we are not simulated at all. I want at least one that presses on the self-sampling and reference-class assumptions that make the probability of being simulated tractable in the first place, and asks what becomes of her conclusion if those assumptions are rejected, as well as one that connects her argument to the self-indication assumption and the old worry that it makes our probabilities hostage to how many observers a hypothesis posits. I want at least one that asks whether being simulated relocates the value of the future rather than destroying it, since the simulated beings may still have a vast and valuable future, and how she avoids the reply that her view merely changes whose future matters. I want at least one on the opposite danger, that if simulators run many simulations the expected number of future beings could be multiplied rather than reduced, turning her dampening into amplification. And I want at least one on how her position interacts with the formal structure of the fanaticism worry in decision theory, the willingness to trade large probabilities of ordinary goods for tiny probabilities of astronomically large ones, asking whether simulation reasoning dissolves that structure or merely adds another term to it.

Beyond those, I would welcome questions on what evidence could count against her view, and on whether taking it seriously leaves any of our obligations to future people intact. Please write the questions in the register of a serious interview, precise but answerable aloud, and mark for each one the point at which I should expect her to deflect, so that I know what to press on next.
