I have just spent an evening arguing with a friend about Cicero's dialogue on old age. My friend's view is that Cicero, speaking through Cato, answers the four standard charges against old age—that it removes us from affairs, weakens the body, takes away pleasures, and brings death near—and shows that none of them is sufficient to make old age miserable. I said that Shakespeare's King Lear is the standing counterexample, and that no consolation survives the first storm on the heath. Please read King Lear through Cicero's views on old age. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: where the play bears out Cicero's claims, where it refutes them, and where it exposes a distinction he needs but does not make—between old age as a stage of life and old age as a position of diminished power and dependence. In particular, consider whether Lear's loss of command, property, and filial loyalty is a failure of old age or a failure of the political arrangements that surround it, and whether Cicero's Cato can answer that without assuming the very authority the play strips away. Do not quote at length; I know the play. Then give me your verdict on which of us had the better of the argument. Please write in British English. Organise the response as follows: Cicero's brief (roughly 200 words), Lear's case (roughly 300 words), Where each is right (roughly 300 words), What the play adds (roughly 200 words), and Verdict (roughly 150 words).
