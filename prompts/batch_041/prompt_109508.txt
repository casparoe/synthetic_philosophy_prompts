I have spent the last two years reading about Rawls's veil of ignorance and the difference principle on my own time — I am a machinist by trade, not a philosopher — and I need to turn them into something I can actually use. I sit on the compensation committee at the worker-owned shop where I have worked for fourteen years. There are ten of us, elected, and every spring we have to put together next year's pay scale: how many bands, what the top of the scale is worth relative to the bottom, who gets a share of the profit and after how long, whether we pay a manager a real salary or a token one. The whole membership votes on what we propose, so whatever I bring to the meeting has to be something I can defend out loud, to people who have never read a page of the book, in the two hours we get.

Before you answer, please start by brainstorming in your own words what the veil of ignorance and the difference principle actually claim, and where the one-line versions of them that get passed around go wrong. Then take the role of someone who knows this theory deeply and is used to explaining it to people outside philosophy, and think carefully about what is lost and what must not be lost in the translation.

What I want from you is a decision procedure I can run on a proposal by myself, in one evening, without help: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, the point at which the answer is a plain no, and the point at which the question has stopped being about justice and become something the members have to settle by vote. Be explicit about which parts of the theory have to survive the translation even though they are awkward for a committee of ten, and which parts I can drop without changing what the principle says. I also need a rule for identifying who counts as the least advantaged here, because our scale includes part-timers and two or three people who would struggle to find comparable work elsewhere, and I do not know whether that makes them the relevant group or whether the lowest hourly rate settles it.

Then run the procedure on three proposals: (1) raising the top band from 1.6 to 2.0 times the bottom band to keep our sales lead from leaving, on the argument that losing her would cost everyone, including the lowest-paid packers; (2) cutting the paid hours we set aside for safety training and for the monthly all-hands meeting, and spending the money on a 4% raise across every band; (3) hiring an outside general manager at 2.5 times the bottom band while making new hires wait three years before they get any profit share. At least one of these should come out against what I would guess I want — say which, and do not soften it.

Last, tell me what the procedure cannot do. Where will it misfire, which of its steps are really my own judgment dressed up as the theory's, and what would it take to settle the questions it hands off? And one thing I genuinely do not know: does the difference principle have anything to say about the pay scale of a single firm, or is it only about the basic structure of a whole society? If the honest answer is that it does not reach my committee, say so plainly, and tell me what is left that does.
