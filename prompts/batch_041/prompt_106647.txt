I'm building a unit for my senior seminar on self-deception, and I want to open with a dialogue the students can argue with rather than a summary of the literature — the moment I summarize it they all nod and decide they've got it, and then they can't say anything interesting about it for the rest of the term. So here's what I want you to write. Two philosophers, both good, neither one a fool, arguing about whether self-deception is even possible. One of them holds that the notion as we ordinarily use it is incoherent: the standard recipe — A deceives B into believing something false, A knows the truth, B ends up believing the false thing — simply can't be run when A and B are the same person, since the deceiver has to know and the deceived has to not know, and no amount of hand-waving about 'the unconscious' or 'one part of the mind fooling another' repairs that without quietly changing the subject. The other thinks that's an artifact of a bad picture of belief: believing isn't one act performed by one agent at one moment, so there's nothing contradictory in saying that a person's wants shape what she attends to, what she'll entertain, what she bothers to check, and so what she ends up believing — no secret knowledge anywhere. I want them actually engaging, not fencing. The deflationist has to answer the charge that he's just relabelled wishful thinking and lost the very thing we were trying to explain; the paradox-monger has to say what's really doing the work in his 'split' besides a metaphor, and has to face the question of whether it even matters that the self-deceived person 'intended' to end up where she ended up. Neither of them gets to settle anything by name-dropping, and don't let them quote or cite anybody at each other — argue it out on their own.

Here's the case I want them to fight over, because it's the one that reliably trips my students and I'm sick of the usual examples: a researcher becomes convinced that a rival's paper contains a fatal error in the proof. She's right — there is one. But she never worked it out. She wanted there to be an error, she read the thing in a foul mood, she spotted the one sentence that looks wrong and stopped reading there. So her belief is true, arrived at by a route she'd be embarrassed to defend, and driven the whole way by what she wanted. Is she self-deceived? The incoherentist is going to say obviously not, nobody was deceived about anything, she just got lucky with bad epistemic hygiene; the other one is going to say this is exactly what self-deception looks like from the inside and the demand that she be wrong is a leftover from the other-deception model. I want that disagreement to get properly worked through rather than dissolved, and I want at least one real concession somewhere in the middle — one of them gives ground on something specific and has to rebuild a bit — rather than both of them standing pat for a thousand words. It should read like a play, with dialogue tags, but I dont want any headings or bullet points or bold text or tables, just continuous prose the way you'd write an email; alot of my students skim anything with structure and I want them to have to actually read this one. Somewhere around three or four pages of back-and-forth feels right, and you can leave them without a verdict — I'd rather they end on a live question than a tidy resolution.
