I spent my working life in evolutionary biology — life-history theory mostly, and optimal foraging — and since retiring I have been teaching myself moral philosophy, badly but happily. The thing that pulled me in was an argument I have been watching for a year between two friends of mine, both of whom work on climate policy, about pure time preference. Let me label them the zero-rate view and the positive-rate view. The first holds that discounting a benefit merely because it lies further in the future — holding fixed the size of the benefit, the identity and circumstances of whoever receives it, and the probability that anyone is there to receive it at all — is indefensible, and that everything respectable in a social discount rate comes from two other sources: marginal utility falling as consumption rises, and the possibility that the future does not arrive. The second holds that a positive pure time preference is not a moral lapse but a requirement: that the axioms one would want any ranking of infinitely many generations to satisfy cannot all be satisfied without it, or that the present generation's claim on its own resources is a genuine moral claim rather than a failure of imagination, or that a social rule ought to respect the time preferences people actually have. They are now at the stage of repeating themselves more loudly, and I have come to suspect that much of their disagreement is about what is being discounted rather than about how much.

So I would like you to write the document they have been failing to write: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them. Set out, in a numbered section, the claims both of them can sign — about what the question actually asks, about the several distinct things that get run together whenever someone says the discount rate, about which objections each of them ought to concede against their own side, and about what would count as an argument in this area at all. Then give me a table of the disagreements that remain, each entry phrased so that the party it describes would accept that description of its own position; flag any entry you think is merely verbal and say what distinction would dissolve it. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I would guess three, though I may be wrong: first, whether pure time preference survives as a distinct object once one notices that any delay changes the probability of receipt, in which case it may be an empty place-holder rather than a moral error; second, whether the impossibility results about ranking infinite welfare streams genuinely force a positive rate or merely force the surrender of some other axiom, and if the latter, which axiom each party would rather give up; third, whether the fact that a taste for the near is what a mortal, reproducing organism ought to have given its hazard rate bears at all on what a society ought to do, or whether that is a confusion of the descriptive with the normative. For each crux, supply the case, argument, or constructed example that both parties would agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each of them predicts about it.

Please write it as their document, not as a survey of a literature: no citations, no reading list, no names — I have no way to check them and neither do you, and a joint statement should stand on its arguments. Finally, be candid about your own drafting. Tell me which of the claims you have placed in the agreed section is most likely to be repudiated by one side, and what argument or consideration would lead you to move it down into the disagreements table; and tell me where your reasoning is likeliest to fail, given that you cannot look anything up.
