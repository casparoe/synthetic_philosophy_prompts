I sit on a campus committee that has just approved an automated attendance system, and the experience has made me want to teach the politics of artifacts in a way students feel rather than recite. I run a 90-minute session for about 16 upper-level undergraduates in a philosophy of technology course. Design a classroom activity that makes them feel the force of the claim that artifacts have politics, but not through the well-worn bridge example, and keep the artifact low-tech—a door, a queue, a form—so the politics is in the arrangement, not the electronics. Build it around two ideas I want them to encounter as lived constraints rather than labels: that a design can inscribe a political settlement into ordinary use, and that a tool can undergo what I can only call reverse adaptation, where the ends it was meant to serve get reshaped to fit the tool. The activity should move from a design choice that quietly excludes some participants, to a use that unexpectedly redistributes power, to a moment where the artifact starts dictating the group's goals. Include roles, materials, a scoring or payoff scheme that doesn't need real money, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto those two ideas. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion.
