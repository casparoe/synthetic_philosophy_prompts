I'm a second-year student in a political theory program, and I've been reading a well-reviewed popular book on the epistemic case for democracy — the sort of trade book that gets blurbed as a defense of the ordinary voter against the pretensions of experts. Its chapter on Condorcet's jury theorem has been nagging at me. I know the theorem well enough to recite its shape, and I'm inclined to think the book gets it basically right, but I've sat through enough seminars on social choice to suspect that the version it offers is doing something suspicious at the joints, and I can't put my finger on where. Paraphrasing fairly closely, its argument runs like this:

"Condorcet's jury theorem is the mathematical backbone of the case for democracy. It says that if each voter is even slightly more likely than not to get a binary question right, and each reaches her conclusion independently of the others, then the majority is more likely to be right than any individual voter, and the more voters you add, the closer that probability comes to certainty. The independence assumption is really just a technicality: real electorates, with all their shared newspapers and shared prejudices, still get the benefit of the doubt, because the errors voters make tend to cancel each other out. The theorem's real punch is that it defeats the case for expert rule, since a large enough majority is more reliable than the single most competent person in the room, and so deference to a wise elite has no epistemic justification. It also shows that majority rule is the uniquely rational way to aggregate opinions, and that its logic applies to a race with many candidates just as well as to a yes/no question. The only thing the theorem demands is that voters be better than coin flips; everything else — scale, differences in competence, shared background — is a detail. The upshot is that the case for democracy is not merely moral but mathematical."

What I'd like from you is an audit. Go through it claim by claim and tell me, for each, what a social choice theorist or a philosopher of democracy would accept without qualification, what they would wince at as a distortion, and what is flatly false — and in each case be specific about which assumption is doing the work. I'm particularly unsure about the move from the theorem itself to the conclusion that expert rule has no epistemic justification: it feels like something is being smuggled in there, but I can't tell whether it's a premise the theorem lacks or a claim the theorem actually delivers. I'd also like to know what the theorem would have to say to license the book's final flourish about mathematics, and whether the bit about many candidates survives contact with the voting paradox that also carries Condorcet's name.
