Before I can assess anything anyone offers me, I want the specification: what any adequate theory of mental representation has to be able to do.

Adequacy is relative to a purpose, so let me fix mine. The theory is to underwrite a computational explanation of cognition in a creature that has no public language — an animal, an infant, a system whose thinking is to be explained as operations over internal states that carry content. It has to be usable by someone explaining how such a creature represents its surroundings, forms new thoughts out of old ones, and acts on the result, without smuggling in the theorist's own language and without quietly assuming that the creature could say what it is doing.

Write the desiderata, not a survey of the field. For each requirement: state it in a sentence or two, give the case or consideration that forces it, and tag it as non-negotiable or as a commitment of one camp dressed up as a constraint. I would rather have eight requirements that bite than twenty that flatter everything on offer.

A few I already suspect belong, and I want them argued for or knocked down rather than handed back to me. That the theory make room for error, so that a state can represent wrongly, while still fixing what a given state represents. That it say in virtue of what a state is about dogs rather than about dog-shaped surfaces, dogs in the dark, or undetached dog-parts — and likewise for properties rather than objects. That it not presuppose the very capacities it is meant to explain, including the capacity to represent rules of combination. That it cover a creature which has had no opportunity to acquire the relevant concepts, and that it not allow two creatures physically identical in the present to differ in what they think merely because their histories differ. That it apply to sub-personal states — a visual system representing an edge — as well as to beliefs, or else say plainly why one notion of representation will not stretch that far. And that it give content some role in the causal story, or explain why content's doing no causal work is acceptable within a computational explanation.

Then set out your answer in these sections, in this order:

1. "Purpose and scope" (~100 words): the explanatory project you are writing for, in your own words, and any narrowing it forces on everything that follows.
2. "The requirements" (~900–1200 words): the numbered list. Each entry gives the requirement, the case or consideration behind it, and the tag.
3. "Where the list fights itself" (~300–400 words): the pairs or triples of requirements you doubt can be satisfied together, with the argument for the conflict, and what that implies for the list as a whole.
4. "The requirement that does the most excluding" (~150–250 words): which single entry rules out the most otherwise attractive theories, and why.
5. "What this leaves of the leading families" (~250–350 words): brief, and by family rather than by author — covariance or informational accounts, historical or teleological accounts, inferential-role accounts, and formal symbol-system accounts.

Write in British English. No citations and no reading list: if you are unsure who holds a view, describe the family rather than naming anyone, since a misattributed name is worse than none.
