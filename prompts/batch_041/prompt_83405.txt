I chair the data-governance board of an international consortium running a longitudinal study of fatigue at two sites. Both sites use identical instruments, identical case definitions, and identical training, and the participants are matched on every internal measure the study takes; the design assumes throughout that the study's central label picks out the same condition in both places. Site B, however, lies in a region where a chronic parasitic infection is endemic, and our physician there reports that most enrolled participants with the study's symptom profile carry it, while site A has essentially none. A minority on the board now argues that we are not measuring one condition under two names but two conditions under one name, and that the pooled analysis in the draft paper therefore misstates what its numbers mean; they call it Twin Earth with a parasite in place of the twin water. The majority answers that our instruments are calibrated to each other, that participants at the two sites are indistinguishable on everything we measure, and that the label means whatever our protocol says it means. The paper is due in three weeks and the authors have asked us to rule. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate: a clear recommendation — pool, do not pool, or pool under stated conditions — with the reasoning behind it, the strongest case for the course I am rejecting, and what we should ask the authors to do before submission. One page, no more than 400 words.
