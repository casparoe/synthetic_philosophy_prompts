I am in the second half of a metaphysics of mind seminar on panpsychism, and I have argued myself into a corner while writing the response paper. The version of the view we have been given is roughly this: the fundamental physical entities that compose me each have some form of experience, and the facts about my experience are fixed by the facts about theirs, with nothing further added. I can follow the motivation, and I can follow the standard objections at the level of slogans, but I cannot get past the composition step, so I wrote out an argument that the view cannot be stated coherently at all. A friend in the seminar then pointed out that the load-bearing step of my argument is not really about tiny experiences -- it is a claim about composition and unity -- and that if that claim is right, no view that builds a single subject of experience out of parts survives, which would make my argument useless as an argument against panpsychism in particular. I do not want to hand in an argument that proves too much, but I also do not want to abandon it merely because it is strong.

Here it is, in the form I wrote it. (1) If panpsychism is true, the fundamental entities composing me each have an experience, and every experience is someone's: it belongs to exactly one subject. (2) Right now I have one experience and not a heap of them -- there is a single thing it is like to be me at this moment. (3) Take the smallest case. Two fundamental entities, each with a flicker of experience, arranged so that together they realise one simple experience in me, a single flash of red. That flash is either identical with one of the flickers or it is a further experience built out of the two. (4) The subject of each flicker is either identical with me or distinct from me, and there is no third option, because for any two subjects there are only two possibilities: they are the same subject, or they are different subjects. (5) If each flicker-subject is identical with me, then I am two subjects as well as one, which is not a way for a subject to be. If each is distinct from me, then parts of my one experience belong to subjects other than me, and whatever has parts belonging to other subjects is not one experience -- which contradicts (2). (6) So the flash cannot be built out of the flickers; and if nothing in me is built out of micro-experiences, then panpsychism's central claim, that the tiny experiences are what the large one is made of, is false, and we are left with brute emergence or with plain materialism.

I would like to keep (2) and (6) if I can, and the two-entity case in (3) is my own construction, so I am happy to have it rewritten. Tell me which step to give up, and then tell me what giving it up costs. I want to know, for each of the three options we have been discussing -- that large experiences are literally composed of small ones, that they merely emerge from them, and that the dependence runs the other way, with one fundamental subject and the small ones derivative -- what a defender of that option must say about the step I abandon, and whether the same move is available to a physicalist who thinks the composing entities have no experience at all, since if it is, my paper has no thesis left.
