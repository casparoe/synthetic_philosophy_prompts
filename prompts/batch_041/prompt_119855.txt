Question 3 (20 marks). Historians sometimes say that without Hitler there would have been no Nazi Germany, and sometimes that the Weimar Republic's structural crisis would have produced some kind of authoritarian regime no matter who led it. In class we built three tests for evaluating counterfactual claims of this kind:

(i) the but-for test: would the outcome have occurred had the person not acted as they did?
(ii) the substitute test: was an alternative actor available who could have played the same role, or did the person create the role?
(iii) the load-bearing test: how much of the causal work is done by conditions that were already in place independently of the person?

Using these three tests, assess the claim that without Hitler there would have been no Nazi Germany. Be explicit about which outcome you are assessing, and about what each test can and cannot show.

Student's answer:

"Would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? I'll take the three tests in order.

The but-for test asks whether the outcome would have happened without the person. Here the answer seems to be no. By 1932 the structural preconditions of an authoritarian nationalist regime were all in place: the humiliation of Versailles, the Depression, the collapse of the centre parties, and a conservative elite around Hindenburg that was willing to govern by decree and preferred almost anyone to the Social Democrats. These conditions were doing nearly all of the causal work, and they would have been there even if Hitler had never left Austria. So the but-for test fails, and Hitler is not a cause of Nazi Germany in this sense.

The substitute test then asks whether someone else could have filled the role. Clearly yes: Papen, Schleicher, Hugenberg, or the SA leadership could each have headed some authoritarian nationalist government. If the role could have been filled by any number of people, then the regime's specific character wasn't the man's doing either. That means the racial legislation and the extermination programme are better explained by the structural situation than by anything Hitler wanted — the role made the man rather than the other way round.

The load-bearing test asks how much of the causal work is done by conditions already in place before the person acted, and as I said, the answer is most of it. Failing the first test already settles the question, so the great-man view is refuted: the individual is a placeholder and the interesting causes are impersonal.

One qualification: the answer does depend a bit on how you specify the outcome. If 'Nazi Germany' just means some right-wing authoritarian regime, the substitute test makes the counterfactual quite robust — there would have been one. If it means a regime that industrialised mass murder, the substitute is harder to name, since none of the alternatives showed much interest in that. But that's a distinction without much of a difference, since the same structural pressures would presumably have produced the same policies in whoever ended up in charge."
