I teach a seminar on Renaissance political thought and I keep butting heads with a colleague over how to read the fortune chapter of The Prince (ch. 25, the one with the flooding rivers). His line is that the river image is basically Machiavelli's considered view and that everything else in the chapter should be read through it. On his reading: fortune is a natural force like a river in flood, virtù is the engineering you do in the calm season — dikes, canals, embankments — so the takeaway is that the wise prince is the one who prepares in advance, and virtù is essentially foresight plus prudence. He then waves at the bit at the very end of the same chapter, where fortune is a woman who has to be beaten into submission, as more of the same idea, i.e. dominating an unruly force, just with a cruder metaphor. My students lap it up because it makes the chapter tidy.

I don't buy that its tidy, and I'd like you to audit the analogy rather than summarise the chapter. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the river, the flood, the dikes, the engineer, the calm season, the plain that gets drowned — and then walk through where the mapping breaks. A few cracks I can already see and want you to adjudicate: a river's threat is cyclical and more or less foreseeable, whereas the fortune he's worried about seems to include other people's wills, the general drift of the times, and (in the opening lines) maybe God; a dike is a fixed structure you build once and then trust, whereas a page or two later he's telling you that men succeed only when their fixed temperament happens to match the times, and that the cautious man is ruined precisely because he can't rebuild himself when the times turn — which sounds less like engineering and more like a man who can't be re-tooled; and then there's Julius II, whose whole success comes from acting impetuously and refusing to wait until everything is arranged, which is the opposite of building dikes in the calm season.

So: which of these cracks actually threaten his conclusion (that virtù is foresight and fortune is manageable nature), which are just incidental, and does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table — or was the river image quietly carrying the whole thing, smuggling in a modern, control-nature picture that isn't really Machiavelli's? And if you think there's a better image for what he's actually up to across the chapter, give me one and say what it captures that the river misses. Skip the citations, I know the texts, I just want the anatomy of the analogy.
