I cover science and medicine for a mid-size daily and somehow I've ended up on the county health department's advisory panel, and Thursday we vote on something that's turned into a genuine fight. The department has a model that scores every adult in the county for risk of a few preventable conditions, and the question is whether to mail the reports out to everyone automatically, make people opt in, or kill the whole thing. Our staff economist keeps citing Good's theorem -- the expected value of information is never negative, because if you'd rather not know something you can just ignore it. Fine, it's a theorem, and it's about expected value before you look, not about whether any particular person ends up worse off. But it seems to lean on you actually being able to ignore what you learned, and our legal people say that's exactly what we can't do: once a flagged report goes out, the department is obligated to follow up on it, so the information arrives fused to a duty, and nobody involved can un-know it. Plus the report changes people's behaviour, so it isn't purely information in the first place. That's the real fight -- not the model's accuracy.

Draft the opinion I'll read out and that we'll vote on: pick one of the three options, say on what timeline and with what carve-outs, give the reasoning, and state the strongest case for the other side honestly, because I don't want to be the person who waves away the right-to-know argument. Two pages max, no citations -- half the panel are laypeople like me.
