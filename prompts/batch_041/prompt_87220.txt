You are a poet and a philosopher of mind — someone who can hold a received verse form and a skeptical argument in the same hand and make them illuminate each other. I'm a philosophy student finishing a seminar paper on Zhuangzi's butterfly passage and the dream argument more generally, and rather than end on a summary paragraph I want to close with a poem. Please think carefully before you write: begin with a few sentences of thinking-through, working out what the form can be made to do here, and only then draft.

Write a pantoum of five quatrains, in the standard interlocking pattern — the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing quatrain brings the opening lines back so that the loop shuts — and speak it in the voice of someone who has just woken up.

The philosophical requirement is that the poem leave it genuinely undecided whether the speaker is the man who dreamt he was a butterfly or the butterfly now dreaming it is a man. The reader should be able to reassign the speaker without changing a single line, and the returning lines should be exactly the ones whose sense flips when the attribution flips; the doubt I want on the page is the symmetrical one, not "perhaps I am dreaming" but "either of us could be the one asleep, and nothing available from the inside breaks the tie." The closing quatrain, which restores the opening, should leave that doubt closed over the reader rather than settled — no resolution in either direction, and no deflationary shrug of the "so nothing is real anyway" variety.

Three further constraints. First, do not use any form of the words dream, butterfly, wake, awake, real, or illusion; the passage and the argument have to be enacted, not named. Second, name no philosophers. Third, keep the diction plain and contemporary — I don't want mock-classical ornament or faux-archaic phrasing. Someone who knows the passage should recognize the structure of the doubt in the poem's machinery, and someone who doesn't should still find it a good poem.

After the poem, add two or three sentences on where you placed the refrains and why, since I may be asked about that in seminar.
