I'm writing a short seminar paper on how a Bayesian agent should revise her credences when what she learns is not a proposition but a conditional probability, and I want to begin from the specification rather than from the proposals. The case I keep returning to is the Judy Benjamin problem. An agent is dropped into a territory she knows only to be divided into a Blue region and a larger Red region, Red being further subdivided into two areas; a radio message then tells her something only about the odds between those two Red sub-areas conditional on her being in Red, and those odds differ from the ones she already assigned. Ordinary conditionalization is unavailable, because she has learned no proposition to condition on; the message also contradicts a conditional probability she already held, so even the recipe of conditioning on the corresponding material conditional fails to reproduce the information she was given and would assign zero to one of the two Red sub-areas besides. Before I read any more of the literature on this, I want the desiderata: what any adequate theory of updating on conditional information has to deliver.

So please write me that list, and write it as a specification rather than a survey. I want the cases it must get right, each with the consideration that makes it a test case: a constraint that merely restates one of the agent's prior conditional probabilities and so should leave her credences untouched; a conditional learned with probability one, where the theory must collapse into ordinary conditioning; information about a partition, where it must collapse into the familiar Jeffrey-style update; a constraint that conflicts with the prior conditional probability but not with the prior as a whole, where the theory must still return a defined posterior; and the Judy Benjamin case itself, where I especially want to know whether a rule is permitted to move credence from Red to Blue even though the message was silent about the Blue/Red division, since the leading minimum-information proposal does exactly that. Then the distinctions it must respect, with the reason each matters: between learning a proposition and learning a conditional credence; between the probability of a conditional and the conditional probability, given the triviality results that make identifying them untenable; between a constraint on a partition and a constraint on a sub-partition of one; between synchronic and diachronic requirements; and, if the theory needs a notion of minimal change, an account of what is being minimized and why that measure rather than another. Also tell me which of these requirements are genuine and which are parochial demands of one camp, and flag any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, since I suspect the Judy Benjamin verdicts are where the list tears.

Finally, score the main families against your list, briefly, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates. Include among the candidates the relative-entropy or minimum-information approach, the approach that treats the conditional as a proposition and conditions on it, the Jeffrey-style approach that updates directly on the learned conditional probability, and the deflationary view that no general rule is needed because the agent's new conditional credence simply is her state. And when you score them, address three things the usual presentations skip: whether the rule is order-invariant when two conditional constraints arrive in succession, what it does when the constraints are individually satisfiable but jointly force some cell to zero, and whether a constraint delivered by an admittedly unreliable source should be treated as hard information at all. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I will do the scoring in class if you run short.
