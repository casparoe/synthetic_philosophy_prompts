I've become convinced that Scanlonian contractualism is the right shape for morality: an act is wrong when every principle that would permit it could be reasonably rejected, with the test applied complaint by complaint, from each affected person's standpoint, and with no summing or averaging of different people's stakes. I know this leaves the aggregation problem — a principle that burdens one person severely can look rejectable even when a very large number of people each have a much smaller complaint against the alternative — and I want to rescue the view with a targeted repair rather than give it up. The repair: complaints may be aggregated only when they are of the same kind, and only to break a tie between principles that are otherwise equally rejectable, so that aggregation can never defeat a single complaint of a more serious kind. Take a case in which a safety rule spares each of a million people a modest but identical risk at the cost of a certain, somewhat worse outcome for a handful of identifiable people. Tell me what would count against the repaired view: which cases it misdescribes, which intuitions it must call mistaken, and whether the "same kind" restriction can be specified at all without collapsing into ordinary aggregation on one side or bare stipulation on the other. Separate the objections that should genuinely move me from those its defenders would simply absorb, and say whether the repair leaves the position exposed or merely insulates it.
