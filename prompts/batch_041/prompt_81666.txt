Since I began attending morning services regularly, I have found myself reciting the passages concerning the offerings without any clear sense of what I take them to mean, and this discomfort led me to Maimonides' treatment of the sacrificial commandments in the Guide of the Perplexed. His account has unsettled me further. As I understand the argument, the offerings were not commanded because sacrifice is in itself the highest form of worship, but because the people Israel had been raised among nations for whom sacrifice was the ordinary medium of worship, so that a law abruptly abolishing the practice would have been rejected; God therefore permitted the practice to continue in a redirected form, transferred from idols to the one God, and specified the animals and procedures in part to distance the rite from the particular practices and creatures venerated by the surrounding nations. If that is right, the commandments concerning the offerings begin to look less like the disclosure of an eternal good than like a remedy tailored to a particular historical ailment.

What I want help with is the conceptual structure of this claim, not its sources. How is a concession of this sort supposed to be compatible with the status of these commandments as divine legislation, given that Maimonides also holds that the commandments as a whole aim at the perfection of the soul and of society? Is it coherent to maintain both that the Law is perfect and that some of its content is a response to a contingent feature of its first audience, or does the accommodationist reading quietly convert the sacrificial cult into a temporary expedient that a different audience might never have received? I would also like you to distinguish two readings of the accommodation and to say which one Maimonides' argument actually supports. On the first, God is weaning Israel away from a practice it could not yet do without, so that the offerings are a concession to weakness. On the second, the Law is instead channeling a desire that belongs permanently to human nature into a disciplined and bounded form, so that the offerings are an education of desire rather than an indulgence of it. What follows from each reading for the question of whether the offerings possess any intrinsic value for the one who brings them, and for the counterfactual question of whether the commandments would have been different had Israel not been surrounded by idolatrous sacrifice? A rival account, on which the offerings express something about the offerer's own standing before God rather than merely accommodating his habits, would be useful as a contrast, but my main interest is in getting the internal logic of Maimonides' position straight.
