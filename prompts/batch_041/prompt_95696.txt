The domain is Avicenna’s flying man argument. You can take the following thumbnail as our common starting point: Avicenna asks us to imagine a person created fully formed and suspended in a void, with no sensory contact with anything, not even his own body; the question is whether such a person would affirm his own existence. The standard reading treats the scenario as an argument for the immateriality of the soul: since the self is affirmed without any bodily sensation, the soul must be something immaterial.

My thesis is that the standard reading conflates two things. I defend what I call the indexical reading. On this reading, the flying man is not an inference to the best explanation of the soul’s immateriality. It is a test of whether the first-person perspective can be tokened without any bodily self-ascription. The conclusion it supports is only that the self is not given as a body; the further step to immateriality requires a separate premise about the transparency of the mental, and that premise is much weaker than the argument’s friends suppose. I also claim that the scenario is best understood as a subtraction argument rather than a bracketing argument: it removes sensory content rather than suspending the natural attitude, and this difference matters for what the scenario can establish. My key premise is what I call the no-bodily-indexical claim: the first-person pronoun can be tokened in a context where no bodily predicate is self-ascribed. The standard reading needs a stronger claim, which I call the transparency-to-immateriality inference.
