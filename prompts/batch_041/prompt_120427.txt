I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on a character who runs cost–benefit analyses for a regulatory agency, so I have spent several months reading my way into her world. There is one thing in it that I cannot get past, and I would like you to help me see what it commits her to.

The practice is this. When her agency proposes a rule that will prevent a certain number of deaths — requiring, say, a safer industrial solvent — the benefits are stated in dollars. To arrive at the figure, the agency asks what workers accept in wages in exchange for bearing small risks of dying on the job, and reads off a price for a reduction in that risk. (The agency is careful to insist that it is not pricing any particular life, only a small change in the probability of death across a population.) A rule is justified when the benefits, so measured, exceed the costs. Now the same state that prices safety this way refuses to honour other bargains of exactly that shape. It will not let me sell a kidney, however willingly I offer it. It requires my employer to meet safety standards whatever I would have agreed to accept in their place. It forbids me to work for less than a minimum wage. In one setting my willingness to trade money against life and limb is treated as the authoritative measure of what the trade is worth to me; in another it is set aside as not counting for anything.

What theory of value, or of welfare, does the pricing practice presuppose? I take it that it needs something like this: the good for a person is the satisfaction of her preferences, and her preferences are whatever her choices reveal them to be — her utility, in the economist's sense, being nothing over and above that. But tell me whether the practice strictly requires that theory or merely fits it, and which rival accounts of welfare it quietly rules out. Then tell me whether the same theory can underwrite the refusals, or whether the state is tacitly running two tiers of preference — some choices revealing welfare, others not — and if so, on what principle the tiers are drawn. Mark clearly where the practice is genuinely underdetermined and where it is not. Finally: if the implicit theory were replaced by one on which welfare consists in how life actually feels to the person living it, or by one on which willingness to pay is the wrong measure because the poor cannot pay, what would have to change in the way the agency writes its rules?

No citations or reading lists, please; I want the argument, not the apparatus. Answer in no more than 600 words, opening with a single sentence stating what you take the practice to presuppose.
