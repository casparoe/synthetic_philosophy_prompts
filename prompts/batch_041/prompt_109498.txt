I'm a second-year PhD student in a philosophy of well-being reading group, and I've been asked to circulate a statement of my own position before the next session so that everyone else has something concrete to attack. What I want from you is an audit rather than a survey: tell me which of the commitments below actually pull against one another, which pairs merely look as though they do, and for each genuine conflict what the least costly repair would be and what that repair costs me. Where two things really do clash I would much rather be told which one has to give than be offered a way of keeping both.

Here is the position. (1) Well-being hedonism: the only thing intrinsically good for a person is pleasure and the only thing intrinsically bad for a person is pain; I mean this as an explanatory claim, so that whatever else is good for you is good for you because it is pleasant or a source of pleasure. (2) Heterogeneity: pleasures share no single phenomenal or functional essence. The pleasure of scratching an itch, the pleasure of a proof finally clicking, the pleasure of cold water on a hot day and the pleasure of a rival's humiliation are a motley class, and 'pleasure' names at best a family resemblance. (3) Commensurability: pleasures of quite different kinds can nevertheless be ranked and added along a single dimension, so that a life containing more total pleasure is better for the person living it, and comparisons of overall well-being between people make sense. (4) Quality-sensitivity: some kinds of pleasure count for more than others quite apart from how intense or how long they are — the pleasure of understanding something is worth more than the pleasure of a hot bath — so the ranking in (3) is not purely a matter of quantity. (5) Source-independence: the value of a pleasure does not depend on its object or its origin; a pleasure taken in another person's humiliation is still, pro tanto, good for the one who takes it. (6) I am not a psychological hedonist: people are not always moved by the prospect of their own pleasure, and I take hedonism to be a thesis about what makes a life go well rather than about what motivates us.

Some background, which may or may not be relevant. I arrived at (2) by introspection, comparing the relief of a toothache with the pleasure of a good argument and failing to find any common quality, and at (1) from the thought that an uninterrupted life of small bodily pleasures, unreflective though it is, could still be a very good life for the person living it. The reading group is small and fairly combative, so I would sooner hear the worst now than in the room. Rank the conflicts you find by severity, say for each what would have to be given up, and then tell me which single revision would tidy up the most at once. Please write in British English.
