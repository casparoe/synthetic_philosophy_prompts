I'm a high school senior preparing a short presentation for my school's philosophy club, and I have been stuck for a few weeks on a puzzle a friend brought back from a university decision theory reading group. The setup, as I understand it: a driver is on a road with two exits, and her payoffs are such that she does best by taking the second exit, does badly by taking the first, and does worst of all by driving past both. The catch is that she is absent-minded. At the moment she reaches each exit she cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, and she knows in advance that this will be so; before she sets out she can think clearly about what to do, but once she is on the road every decision point looks identical to her. The friend who explained it says the rational thing is to settle in advance on taking the first exit she comes to, even though at the moment of choosing she cannot tell whether that is the right one, and even though taking the first exit is, considered on its own, the worse outcome. Another friend insists this is a trick: if she genuinely cannot tell the two exits apart, then there is no such thing as deciding well at either one, and the only sensible verdict is that the case exposes the limits of the puzzle rather than a fact about rationality. I have to referee this at our next meeting, so I would like your help thinking it through carefully rather than picking a side quickly.

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on decision theory and rational choice, and give me your honest credence in a single, precisely stated claim: that in this problem the uniquely rational policy is to exit at the first decision point encountered, and that any theory of rational choice which implies otherwise is thereby mistaken. Begin by brainstorming the considerations you think bear on the claim, since I would rather see the shape of the issue than a fast verdict, and then give me a number decomposed into the sub-questions it rests on: roughly, the probability that an evidential or otherwise policy-oriented account of rational choice is the correct general account; the probability that this is a well-posed decision problem with a determinate answer at all, rather than an idealization so strange, an agent deliberating while unable to locate herself within her own plan, that the question of what to do dissolves; the probability that it is legitimate here to treat the choice as one among whole policies rather than among individual acts, given that the driver at the moment of decision has no memory to consult; the probability that the case genuinely discriminates between rival theories rather than being, as my second friend suspects, a spot where they all go quiet; and any further term you think belongs. Give each term a number and one line of justification, show how they combine into the final figure, and then tell me which term you are least confident in and what argument or example would move it most.

Finally, one of my friends puts his credence at about 0.9 and the other at about 0.15. Using your own decomposition, tell me which sub-question their disagreement most likely lives in, and which of the two your number lands closer to. If you have room, I would also like to know whether anything changes in a variant I have been chewing on, in which the driver may write herself a note before setting out, something like take the first exit, and pin it to the dashboard, knowing all the while that once she is driving she will be unable to tell whether the note is hers, whether she wrote it before or after starting, or whether it says what she thinks it says. Does the possibility of such a note shift your credence, and does it shift which sub-question matters most?
