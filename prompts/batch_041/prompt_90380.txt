**Two Kinds of Exclusion**

The causal exclusion argument is usually presented as a dilemma for non-reductive physicalism. Mental properties are supposed to be distinct from physical properties, yet every mental event has a physical realizer. If the physical realizer is sufficient for the effect, then the mental property seems to add nothing; if it does add something, we get overdetermination. The standard replies try to show either that overdetermination is benign or that the mental property is not a separate cause but a higher-level description of the same cause. I want to suggest that these replies are answering different questions, and that the argument equivocates between two kinds of exclusion.

Let me distinguish horizontal exclusion from vertical exclusion. Horizontal exclusion treats the mental and the physical as two candidate causes of the same effect, arrayed side by side in the same causal chain. The question is whether both can be causes without overdetermination. Vertical exclusion treats the mental and the physical as two candidate explanations of the same causal transaction, one at a higher level of description and one at a lower level. The question is whether the higher-level explanation is genuine or merely a shorthand for the lower-level one. The overdetermination horn belongs to horizontal exclusion; the epiphenomenalism horn belongs to vertical exclusion. Kim's argument, I think, moves from one to the other without marking the shift.

Once the distinction is on the table, the dialectic changes. The determinable/determinate reply, for example, is a reply to horizontal exclusion: the mental property is not a second cause alongside the physical, but a determinable whose determinate is the physical. That is a plausible answer to the overdetermination worry. But it does not answer vertical exclusion. Even if the mental is not a second cause, it might still be a mere representation of the physical cause, with no explanatory work of its own. Conversely, the interventionist reply—that mental properties can be variables in a causal model—is a reply to vertical exclusion: it shows that mental predicates can figure in explanations that support interventions. But it does not by itself answer horizontal exclusion, because being a variable in a model is not the same as being a cause in the world.

I want to argue that the exclusion argument is best understood as a demand for a criterion of causal relevance, not as a proof of epiphenomenalism. The horizontal version asks: what makes something a cause rather than a mere condition? The vertical version asks: what makes an explanation causal rather than merely descriptive? These are different questions, and the answers need not coincide. A property can be causally relevant in the horizontal sense without being explanatory in the vertical sense, and vice versa. If that is right, then the exclusion argument does not have a single conclusion. It has two, and they can come apart.

The cost of this move is that it may make mental causation too easy. If horizontal and vertical exclusion are independent, then one can accept the causal closure of the physical and still insist that mental properties are horizontally relevant, or accept that mental explanations are vertically genuine and still deny that mental properties are horizontally distinct. But this is not obviously a cost; it may simply be the correct diagnosis. The real disagreement is not about whether mental causation exists, but about which of the two questions we are asking when we ask whether it exists.

I am unsure whether this distinction is already implicit in the literature or whether it is a genuine innovation. I am also unsure whether it dissolves the exclusion argument or merely relocates it. In particular, I worry that the horizontal/vertical distinction may collapse under pressure: perhaps every horizontal claim can be redescribed vertically, and every vertical claim can be redescribed horizontally. If so, the distinction is verbal. I would like to know whether it is.
