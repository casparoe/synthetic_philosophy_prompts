I have come to accept that perception is cognitively penetrable — that beliefs and desires change what we actually see, not merely how we judge or describe it — and the familiar demonstrations (thirst making a bottle look nearer, fear making a slope look steeper) are what convinced me. A colleague objects that those demonstrations concern thirst and fear rather than belief, and that this difference matters for how far the thesis reaches; I would like to know whether she is right about that. My real question, though, is whether the following argument succeeds. If a belief can shape my visual experience, then that experience cannot count as independent evidence for the belief; so observation is theory-laden in a way that leaves perception evidentially inert, and any claim to have simply seen that a theory is correct should be distrusted. Is this argument sound, and if not, which step fails?
