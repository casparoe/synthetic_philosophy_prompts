I'm in a decision theory reading group that has spent the term circling the question of where priors come from and what we're entitled to assume about other people's, and two ideas keep getting run together in our discussion in a way I'd like you to sort out. The first is the common prior assumption: the thought that two rational agents who hold different beliefs must differ because of the information they've each received rather than because they started from genuinely different priors, so that — in the idealized case — pooling their information drives their beliefs together and sustained disagreement becomes a symptom of someone having seen something the other hasn't, or of someone being irrational. The second is the principle of indifference: the thought that when you have no reason to favour one possibility over another you should spread your credence evenly among them, or, in its more sophisticated dress, that the distribution you adopt ought to be invariant under the symmetries of the problem you're facing. What I want from you is a comparison of these two principles. Are they one idea wearing two costumes — the same appeal to symmetry, applied once to a population of agents and once to the possibilities facing a single agent — or do they come apart in ways that matter? What work does each actually do, and could either survive without the other: is there an indifference principle that doesn't presuppose a common prior, or a common prior that doesn't smuggle in an indifference principle somewhere upstream?

Concretely, my friend and I both read the same weather forecast and come away with different odds on rain tomorrow; the common prior assumption tells me we cannot both be rational unless one of us has seen something the other hasn't, while the principle of indifference tells me nothing at all here, since neither of us is indifferent about anything. Turn it around, though — suppose what we know is only that a die has some number of faces, or that a chord is drawn at random in a circle, and indifference is the only thing that hands either of us a number to start from, while the common prior sits silent until there are already priors to share. That asymmetry seems to matter, and I can't tell whether it shows that one of the two is more fundamental, or that they're simply answering different questions and the temptation to run them together is a mistake. The standard objections point in opposite directions: indifference is charged with arbitrariness, since which symmetries you pick depends on how you describe the problem (the Bertrand-style worry), whereas the common prior is charged with being either empirically false — people plainly do begin from different priors — or a substantive empirical claim disguised as a harmless modelling convention.

Give me a verdict on which of the two is the more defensible starting point, and be explicit about your confidence and about what would change your mind: name the argument or consideration that, if it turned out to be correct, would flip your verdict, and say where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
