A gallery, a competition jury, and a copyright office now have to say what a person has contributed when the picture was produced by a generative model, and the answers they give pull against one another. A photograph is the photographer's work: the framing, the lighting, the timing, and the choice of what to include are treated as creative acts, even though the camera does most of the causal work. The same is generally granted to a painter who sets up a procedure and lets the paint fall where it falls. But a person who writes a detailed instruction to an image model, runs it many times, and chooses one result is usually told that the machine's output is not his creation, that he supplied an idea at most, and that whatever protection he receives attaches to his arrangement of the results rather than to the images.

Reconstruct the theory of creativity that this asymmetry presupposes. Say which of the following the practice genuinely requires and which it merely fits comfortably with: that creativity requires a mental act of conception which causally fixes the work's features; that it requires control over the process rather than over the outcome; that it requires the maker to be the proximate cause; that it consists in selection and arrangement; that it consists in effort or intention whatever the result. Say which rival views of creativity the practice quietly rules out. Identify the cases it leaves underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories of creativity would license exactly the same verdicts. Then judge whether the treatment of the prompter can be reconciled with the treatment of the photographer and the painter by a principled distinction, or whether the practice is simply inconsistent, and what the least disruptive repair would be. Finally, describe what would have to change in the practice if creativity were instead understood as distributed across the person, the model, and the corpus the model was trained on. I want the implicit theory and its pressure points, not statutes or case names.
