Write a dialogue between two philosophers, each fully competent and each willing to concede ground. Speaker A is a totalist hedonistic utilitarian who holds that the utility monster is a fiction of the arithmetic — an artifact of writing welfare on a single unbounded cardinal scale — and who believes a defensible version of the view can be stated in a form no monster can exploit. Speaker B is a critic who holds that the monster is a genuine reductio, and who is not a caricature: B should know the standard utilitarian replies and press past them rather than restate the slogan.

The exchange should turn on structural questions rather than the familiar headline version of the argument. First: what exactly does the monster argument assume about the shape of individual welfare — does it require welfare to be unbounded above, or only to admit arbitrarily large ratios between persons, and can A give up one of these without giving up the other? Second: the construction looks as though it can be rebuilt for any view that ranks outcomes by a single unbounded magnitude, not only for hedonism — B should be made to say whether the argument is meant to refute hedonism specifically or aggregative consequentialism generally, and A should exploit whatever that concession yields; the participants should also say whether it matters if the monster's good consists in the misery of others, so that its welfare cannot rise unless theirs falls. Third: the monster differs from the repugnant conclusion only in whether the added welfare is concentrated in one being or spread across many — work out whether that makes the monster objection stronger or weaker than the repugnant conclusion objection, and whether a single reply answers both. Fourth: if interpersonal comparisons of welfare are rejected, the monster cannot even be stated; say whether that refutes the argument or merely declines to engage it. Fifth, and this is where I want the exchange to arrive: if A's best reply is that no being's welfare can outweigh the claims of others beyond some ceiling, that looks like a lexical priority rather than a utilitarian commitment — A should say whether the ceiling belongs to welfare itself or is a moral constraint imported from outside, and B should say what follows on either answer. A long-lived person whose lifetime welfare is enormous without any monstrous psychology is available to whichever speaker can use it honestly.

Neither speaker should be a strawman; A should win some exchanges and B should lose some. Do not quote any text, and include no names, dates, or citations. End with one sentence from each speaker stating what would change their mind — the argument, case, or consideration that would move them toward the other position — and where in their own reasoning they think it is most likely to fail. Label the speakers A and B, write in American English, and keep the entire dialogue under 1,000 words.
