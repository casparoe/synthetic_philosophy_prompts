I am a graduate student finishing a term paper in logic and epistemology — roughly eight thousand words, one semester — and I want to turn a half-formed idea about the surprise exam paradox into a proper research proposal rather than a rambling set of notes. The idea is this. The paradox is usually handled either by attacking the students' backward induction step by step or by blaming their misplaced confidence, but I suspect the real engine is that the teacher's announcement is a statement that can be true while being impossible for the students to know in advance — the content is something like 'there will be an exam, and you will not know the night before that it is coming.' If that is the structure of the announcement, then the elimination argument is circular in a precise way: each step ('it cannot be Friday', 'it cannot be Thursday') is licensed only if the students may treat the announcement as a known premise, yet part of what the announcement asserts is that they cannot know the relevant day. What I want the proposal to pin down is a determinate question rather than a restatement of the puzzle: does this unknowability diagnosis fully dissolve the paradox, or does it merely relocate it, and is there a residual version that survives the diagnosis and still produces a genuine surprise? I also keep circling a second thread — that the standard treatments silently assume the announcement is common knowledge among the students, and that 'surprise' should really be relativized to each student's own information, so that an announcement might surprise one student but not another, or surprise a student precisely because she does not know that the others do not know. I want the proposal to say whether relativizing surprise this way is the same puzzle in new clothes or a genuinely distinct and harder one, and whether the unknowability diagnosis still works once the announcement is weakened — for instance if the teacher's reliability is itself in question, or if the promised surprise is only probabilistic.

Please write the proposal. It needs a statement of the question sharp enough to have a determinate answer; an account of why the existing approaches — the backward-induction treatments, the ones that fault the students' confidence, and the ones that treat the announcement as a statement that can be true but not knowable — leave this particular question open; the method, spelled out month by month, whether that means constructing cases, building a small formal model, or straight conceptual analysis, together with a defense of that method against the obvious alternatives; a description of what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of whether the whole thing is too ambitious for one semester and, if so, which part to cut. Assume the audience is a fellowship committee that will judge the project on the framing rather than on the topic's familiarity, and assume I will supply the citations myself — so do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or anything that depends on my having looked things up; refer to positions and arguments in plain terms and let me attach the sources later.
