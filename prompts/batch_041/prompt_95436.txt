The topic area is strong longtermism -- the idea that the top moral priority is making the very long-run future go well, because there's astronomically more value out there than in the present. The debate as I understand it keeps circling the same things: whether the numbers are right, whether we can know anything about effects that far out, whether expected-value reasoning lets absurdly unlikely bets dominate everything, whether the whole thing paralyses you into doing nothing. What I haven't run into anyone pressing on is the *shape* of the bet. The longtermist case runs a single expected-value calculation over one enormous decision. But in betting and investing there's a well-known argument that you should not maximize expected value when a bad outcome ends the game for you -- if you only get to place the bet once and losing is absorbing, the arithmetic that says take it because the expectation is huge is misleading, because there's no averaging over many runs for the average to actually happen in. Repeated bets and one-shot bets come apart.

So my question: does the case for strong longtermism depend on treating humanity's situation as a repeatable bet when it's really a one-shot, unrepeatable one? And if you replace expected-value maximization with a rule that forbids risking irreversible loss -- no one-way doors at civilizational scale, whatever the upside -- does longtermism survive in a weaker or altered form, and does the ranking among interventions change?
