I teach a 90-minute session of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in decision theory and philosophy of probability; the group is 12 to 16 students, most of whom have had one course in probability or statistics but no measure theory. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the Pasadena game — specifically, the difference between a game whose expected value is infinite and a game whose expected value is undefined, and the way an undefined expectation leaves the expected-value rule silent while choice remains forced. By the end they should have run into the problem themselves, not heard it described.

The game: a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on toss n, the payoff is 2^n/n, positive when n is odd and negative when n is even. So the outcomes in order are +2, −2, +8/3, −4, +32/5, and so on. The contribution of outcome n to the expected value is (1/2^n) times the payoff, i.e. +1, −1/2, +1/3, −1/4, and so on. The series of contributions is the alternating harmonic series, which is conditionally convergent: its partial sums converge to ln 2 in the natural order, but by the Riemann rearrangement theorem the same terms can be rearranged to sum to any real number or to diverge. The expected value of the game is therefore undefined on the standard requirement that the expectation be an absolutely convergent sum. The St. Petersburg game, by contrast, has infinite expectation: the expected-value rule gives a verdict, and the verdict is absurd. The Pasadena game gives no verdict at all, and yet a choice must be made.

Please design the activity. I need:

- A runnable plan with rules, roles, materials, and a timeline that fits 90 minutes, plus a note on how to compress it to 75.
- The idea should show up in what happens. The students should compute the contributions, hit the conditional convergence themselves, and then be forced to choose whether to play or to accept a sure payment, with the expected-value rule unable to help. Do not name the Pasadena game or the rearrangement theorem until the debrief; let the students meet the problem under a neutral label.
- Small stakes only: tokens or points, no real money. The tokens can be redeemable for something trivial or for nothing but bragging rights.
- A debrief that maps moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea: the difference between infinite and undefined expectation, the role of absolute convergence in the definition of expectation, the natural order of outcomes versus the order of summation, the rearrangement theorem, and the question of what a rational agent does when the expected-value rule is silent.
- An honest note on what the activity cannot reproduce. In particular, the activity will have to truncate the game at some finite N, and truncation changes the mathematics; the facilitator must supply the limit and be explicit that the truncated game is not the Pasadena game. Also note which parts of the idea can only be stated, not enacted.
- Predictable failure modes on the first run and how to handle them. I expect students to think the natural order settles the matter because the coin flips have a temporal order; to think the rearrangement is a trick that does not apply to a real game; to conflate this with the St. Petersburg paradox; and to get lost in the arithmetic. Tell me what else will go wrong and what to do.

In the response, separate what is mathematically settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. Settled, I take it: the alternating harmonic series is conditionally convergent; the Riemann rearrangement theorem; the standard definition of expectation requires absolute convergence; the St. Petersburg game has infinite expectation. Contested: whether the natural order of outcomes is privileged for the expectation; whether the Pasadena game shows expected utility theory is incomplete or merely that its domain needs restriction; whether an undefined expectation is a defect of the game or of the theory; and whether there is a fact of the matter about what the game is worth. Do not present a contested view as the consensus.
