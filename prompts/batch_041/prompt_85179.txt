Hey, I need a favor that's basically a writing job.

I sit on the grants committee at a foundation that funds long-horizon research, and a week from Tuesday we vote on whether to renew a group we've funded for eight years. Their whole output is one big model — they call it SPINE, dont ask — that projects when a particular resource constraint starts to bind. Think a Malthus story with better data and a lot more code. The headline number was that we'd cross the threshold in per-capita availability of the thing by around 2016. We didn't. It's now well past that and we're not close.

The group's line is that this isn't a failed prediction, because the number was never a bare forecast. It was conditional on the absence of a certain class of input substitutions, and that class showed up faster than the model's baseline assumed — so the model was right about the mechanism and wrong only about the clock. Maybe. Two things you should know before you judge it. First, the group also wants to discount part of the miss on the grounds that the agency that tracks the number changed its definition halfway through, and only the remaining part is a genuine miss — I want you to say whether that's a legitimate adjustment or a second rescue stacked on the first. Second, one of my colleagues, a chemist, wants to defund them on the spot: his view is that a prediction that misses its window is a dead theory and everything the group has said since is just ad hoc patching to save the core. My other colleague wants to renew forever, on the theory that long-run predictions are always early, never wrong, so the honest thing is to wait a century. I think both of them are being lazy and I want a real call.

So draft the decision memo I'll circulate before the vote. It needs a concrete recommendation — renew as-is, renew with conditions, or defund — with a time frame attached, and if it's renew with conditions I need those conditions spelled out as something we can actually check: what specific evidence in that window would count as the model being vindicated, and what would count as it being dead. The question that actually matters to me is whether the group's rescue (the input-substitution story) predicts anything new that we could turn out to be wrong about, or whether it just explains away the miss after the fact. Say which one it is and why, and be specific about the test.

Don't hand me Popper vs. Kuhn in the abstract, apply it to these people. And give the chemist his best shot before you overrule him, if you do — I'd rather read the strongest version of a miss is a miss than a strawman I can knock down in the meeting.
