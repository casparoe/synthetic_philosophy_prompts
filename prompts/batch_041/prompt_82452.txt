Dennett's puzzle of the brain kept alive in a vat — separated from its body, which goes on living elsewhere, and linked to it at a distance — is usually read as a lesson in how slippery the question of location becomes once the mind is detached from its original place. I want an essay on a variant of that puzzle that I take to be considerably harder, and I want a verdict rather than a tour of the options.

The variant. A subject, call her R, is anaesthetised. A procedure reads out the complete functional state of her brain and writes it into a synthetic substrate, which is then activated; her biological brain is meanwhile held in a metabolically suspended, unconscious state. For the following year the substrate runs the whole of R's mental life — it thinks, remembers, deliberates, and is certain it is R. At the end of the year the process is reversed: the substrate's current functional state is written back into the biological brain, the substrate is dismantled, and R wakes up. The cycle — read out, run on a synthetic substrate, write back, dismantle — repeats with fresh substrates for a decade. Each transfer preserves the functional organisation of the moment, and no substrate is ever used twice.

Now add the overlap. On one occasion the substrate is switched on before the biological brain is suspended, and for twenty minutes both are live and in continuous two-way communication, each continuously modified by the other's states. Both behave as R, both sincerely claim to be R, both remember the same life. The two are then uncoupled and each continues on its own for a year, at the end of which the biological body is destroyed. Vary the round-trip delay in the coupling: a few milliseconds, four hundred milliseconds, four seconds.

Adjudicate the following, and reach a defended conclusion on each.

1. Does R survive the Relay? Is the R who wakes after a write-back related to the R who went under as the R of an ordinary uninterrupted year is related to herself, or not?

2. During the overlap, is there one person or two? If two, which of them, if either, is the R who entered the procedure — and does the answer shift when the delay is short enough that the coupled pair behaves as a single integrated agent, indistinguishable by any test from one person? If one, where is she?

3. Suppose the handover is made gradual instead of discrete: over many months, more and more of the cognitive work is done by the substrate and less by the brain, until the brain is idle, with no moment at which a switch occurs. Does your verdict change, and why or why not?

4. Weigh the two broad positions — that personal identity consists in the continuity of an information-bearing pattern, substrate-neutral and in principle multiply instantiable, and that it consists in the continuity of the causal and material history of one living organism — and say which the Relay favours, and what it costs to accept that verdict.

Do not end on the verdict that the case simply has no fact of the matter. If you think that is the right verdict, you must argue for it against the rival view that our concept of a person has perfectly determinate application here, and that the case merely reveals which strand of it we care about most.

Finally, address this objection: that the Relay is the familiar problem of fission in new clothing, so that whatever one says about an amoeba dividing or about hemispheres transplanted into two bodies one should say here, and the scenario adds nothing. If the objection is sound, state what the essay has nevertheless established.

Argue from the case itself rather than from authority. Do not lean on what any particular philosopher has written about it, and do not supply citations or a bibliography; the reasoning is what matters.
