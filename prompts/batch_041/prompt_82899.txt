Last night a friend and I spent two hours arguing about the strategy-stealing argument — he insisted it is either a triviality or a cheat, that it proves Hex is unfair to the second player while telling nobody how to win, and that by the same reasoning White must win at chess — and I have now foolishly agreed to run next month's session of our reading group on it. The group is eight or nine people, mostly philosophers with a couple of economists, none of them game theorists; they know what a proof by contradiction is and they have all played chess. I do not want you to explain strategy-stealing to me. I want you to plan how I should teach it: the order in which the components have to come and why each depends on the one before it; the single example that should carry the whole session, and what it must have and must not have for the argument to land on it; the misconceptions the group will arrive with or form mid-session, and the precise point in the sequence at which I should head each one off; one question I can put to them at the end that would show me whether they have actually got the idea; and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, saying so out loud.

Two things I want the plan to take seriously, because they are exactly where my friend and I could not agree. First, the argument seems to turn on something I sketched for him on the back of an envelope and which I would like the session to name and isolate: a game is monotone, in the relevant sense, if giving a player an extra move — a free stone, a pass, an extra tempo — can never leave that player worse off; and I suspect the entire force of strategy-stealing is a theorem about monotone games and nothing more, so that the contradiction is bookkeeping and the substantive premise is monotonicity. I want the session built so that this becomes visible rather than asserted, ideally by running the argument once on a game where the premise holds and then once on a deliberately sabotaged variant where it fails, so the group can watch precisely which step breaks. Second, I want the sequence to make exact, rather than wave away, two things the friend would not concede: that the argument is non-constructive — it establishes that one player has a winning strategy without producing it — and why it does not license the chess conclusion he drew, which I assume has something to do with zugzwang and with draws, but which I want made precise rather than hand-waved.

So the plan should also tell me what the carrying example has to be for this to work. I had thought Hex, on the grounds that it has no draws and an extra stone is never a liability, but argue me out of it if something else — a small Chomp board, say — would force the monotonicity premise into the open sooner, and tell me what I lose if I use a game the group already knows too well. Tell me where in the arc to introduce the notion of a winning strategy and the fact that in these finite, drawless, perfect-information games exactly one player has one, and how to stop the group collapsing strategy-stealing into the mirror-and-pairing tricks some of them will have seen, which strike me as a genuinely different animal. Assume fifty minutes, a whiteboard, and no handouts.
