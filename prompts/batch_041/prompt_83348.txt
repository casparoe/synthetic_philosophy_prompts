I'm in the second half of a philosophy of biology course, and we've spent the last few weeks on the dispute between etiological (selected-effects) accounts of function and causal-role (systemic) accounts. I can state both positions, and I can recite the standard points of friction — vestiges, drift, exaptation, the status of artifact functions — but what I actually want is downstream of all that, and my seminar leader keeps answering my questions by saying that it depends on which account you adopt, which is true and useless. So I'd like you to set out what you take to be the most important implications of the distinction. I mean implications in a strong sense: consequences that you could not have stated, or could not have stated with the same confidence, without having settled the question one way or the other — not a neutral catalogue of the ways the two theories differ. Please resist the temptation to give me a comparison table; I have one already, and it is exactly the thing that has stopped being helpful.

To keep this concrete rather than another abstract taxonomy, please run the analysis through three cases that have come up in our reading group, since they are the ones that make me think the choice is not merely terminological. First, de novo genes: a stretch of previously non-coding sequence acquires a reading frame, and the resulting peptide now does real work in the cell, but it has been under selection in that capacity for only a few thousand generations at most, in a population small enough that drift is a serious rival explanation. Second, a small isolated population carrying a trait that currently does causal work in the organism but whose frequency in the population owes to drift rather than selection — and, as a controlled contrast, the very same trait under positive selection in a sister population, so that history and causal contribution come apart while everything else is held fixed. Third, and hardest for me, a single organism: a mouse with an engineered regulatory element, or a child with a novel de novo mutation, where there is no population-level selection history at all, and yet clinicians, and I suspect most of us, want to say that the affected organ is malfunctioning rather than merely unusual. For each case I want to know what each account is committed to, and then what those commitments force on us elsewhere: for medicine and the concept of disease, for the ethics of intervening in wild populations and in synthetic organisms, for whether function-talk is normative or merely descriptive, and for anything else you think genuinely turns on the choice rather than merely being decorated by it.

Finally, I'd like you to commit. Tell me which account we should adopt, or which combination of the two, and defend it against whichever of my three cases cuts hardest against it; if you think the right move is to split the concept — one notion of function for medicine, another for evolutionary biology — then say so, and explain why that is a principled division of labour rather than a refusal to answer. I would much rather have a definite position with its costs plainly stated than a balanced survey, and where your confidence is genuinely low I would like you to say so in that particular place rather than hedging everything equally.
