I write a long-form column on philosophy and the human sciences, and I am planning a piece on consequentialist cluelessness — the unease that our knowledge of the distant, diffuse, or cumulative consequences of our actions is so thin that consequentialist evaluation cannot get a grip on them. I have interviewed enough philosophers by now to notice that the conversation tends to stall in the same place: everyone agrees the phenomenon is real and everyone disagrees about whether it is a problem, and the disagreement turns on distinctions nobody states cleanly.

I would like you to take the role of a professional philosopher who works on decision theory and the ethics of uncertainty, think carefully, and before answering, brainstorm on the page the full space of candidate questions the area contains. Then give me a defended agenda.

Here is the framing I have been mulling, which I offer partly as a provocation and partly because I suspect it is where the real crux lies. It seems to me that cluelessness can mean at least three different things, and that the literature and my interviewees slide between them. First, cluelessness about whether an action was right, versus cluelessness about how to choose an action at the moment of acting — evaluation and guidance may come apart. Second, cluelessness that is merely epistemic, in the sense that more information or more computing power would resolve it, versus cluelessness that is in principle unresolvable no matter how much we knew. Third, and this is the one I cannot get anyone to take seriously, cluelessness as the absence of a well-defined counterfactual baseline: for consequences that fan out over centuries, perhaps there simply is no determinate fact about what would have happened otherwise, in which case the trouble is not our ignorance but the indeterminacy of the thing we are asking about. I want you to tell me whether that third reading is a genuine distinct problem, a restatement of the second, or a confusion.

So: which open questions in this area most deserve attention, and in what order? I am not asking for a survey, and not for a reading list — assume I cannot look anything up and give me the argument, not the citations. For each question, tell me why it is upstream of the others, how tractable it is for philosophers working with the tools they already have, how neglected it currently is, and what development over the next few years would move it up or down my ranking. Where two criteria pull in opposite directions, say so and say which you would let win. Conclude with a definite, committed ordering — three to five items — and stake a position on the three-way distinction above rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me. If you think my framing is a trap that will lead the piece astray, say that plainly and tell me what to replace it with.
