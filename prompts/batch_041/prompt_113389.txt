Earlier this year I left a philosophy post-doctoral post for a research position in an autonomous-systems group, and next month I have been asked to give the opening session of our internal seminar series on the frame problem. The invitation is partly my own doing. The phrase now circulates in our design meetings in at least three incompatible senses: one colleague means the planner's bookkeeping burden, another means simply that an agent gets swamped by irrelevant context, and a third, who has read some philosophy, means something grander about how any finite reasoner could cope with an open world. When I was asked in front of the room which of them was right, I gave an answer that satisfied nobody, including me. I know the territory well enough: the problem as McCarthy and Hayes posed it, the tangle of the frame problem proper (what stays the same) with the qualification problem (which preconditions an action actually needs) and the ramification problem (the knock-on consequences), the Yale shooting scenario and the non-monotonic repairs that followed it, and the philosophical reading on which the real difficulty is relevance rather than logical bookkeeping. I do not want the frame problem explained to me.

Plan the session instead. The audience is about twenty-five people: mostly engineers working on planners and learned control policies, two cognitive scientists, and a couple of philosophers; sixty minutes, a whiteboard, no formal logic assumed beyond the propositional connectives, and very little appetite for more. Give me the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependency behind each step stated, and in particular tell me what the room has to accept before the frame problem registers as a problem rather than an engineering nuisance, and at what point the technical and the philosophical versions can be prised apart without the thread being lost. Give me the one example that should carry the whole hour, what it must contain and what it must leave out, and why the obvious candidates — the Yale shooting scenario, or a robot with a bomb and a ticking clock — are or are not the right vehicle for this particular room. Give me the misconceptions to expect, in the order they will surface, and exactly where in the sequence I should head each one off. I am anticipating the homonyms first (frames as data structures, the framing effect in decision research), then the running-together of the three problems, then 'it is only a question of speed and memory', then 'learned systems have made this obsolete', and finally the assumption that the whole business is a curiosity of a dead formalism with no bearing on anything anyone in the room builds. Finish with a single question I can put to them at the end that would show whether they have grasped the problem itself rather than the slogan, and tell me what I should openly decline to cover at this level. Please answer in British English.
