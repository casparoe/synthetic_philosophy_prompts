I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, working on formal epistemology and the foundations of inductive inference; I have enough mathematics to follow the formalism, so please don't simplify. I want a diagnosis of a dispute that has been running for decades without either side moving, not another survey of it.

The dispute is whether Solomonoff's universal prior, and the Kolmogorov complexity machinery beneath it, vindicates Occam's razor or merely restates it. The vindicationist package is roughly this: a learner weights each hypothesis by its complexity, giving exponentially more weight to shorter descriptions; Bayesian conditioning on that prior converges with probability one on the truth whenever the data-generating process is computable; and the invariance theorem -- complexities computed under different universal machines differ by at most an additive constant independent of the data -- shows that the choice of reference language is not a substantive commitment. On this account simplicity is not a preference we happen to have but a theorem about learning. The objections come in three families. First, the constant in the invariance theorem is not in general known, can be made arbitrarily large by choosing an exotic reference machine, and can dominate the comparison between hypotheses at any finite sample size, so the asymptotics do no work where actual inference happens. Second, Kolmogorov simplicity is not the simplicity of scientific practice -- few parameters, unification, symmetry, ontological economy -- and a theory with many free parameters can have a very short description, so the theorem may be about a different quantity from the one Occam's razor concerns. Third, the results presuppose that the environment is computable, or falls in a class the prior dominates, which is a substantive assumption about the world rather than a justification of simplicity; the problem of induction is relocated, not solved.

My hypothesis is that the camps are answering different questions under one heading -- one asking how a learner ought to behave given a complexity-weighted prior, the other asking what could count as a justification of an inductive method at all -- and that what remains is a genuine but narrow formal question about whether the objectivity claim can be made non-asymptotic. Assess that hypothesis. Tell me which parts of the dispute are verbal (an equivocation on 'simplicity', or on 'explain' versus 'justify'), which are substantive disagreements about facts or about what counts as evidence, and which are bedrock differences about what an explanation of a methodological principle is meant to deliver. Then say what would count as progress here -- a theorem, a distinction, a change of question -- or whether none should be expected; and give me your own view on whether the asymptotic results can be made to do finite-sample work.
