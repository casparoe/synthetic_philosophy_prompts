I have arrived at a position about moral responsibility that I would like to stress-test, and I would rather have the genuinely awkward objections than the flattering ones.

The position is this. The Principle of Alternative Possibilities is false, but the standard Frankfurt-style counterexamples understate what they show. They are usually read as establishing only that an agent can be responsible for an action even though she could not have done otherwise. I want to defend the stronger and, I think, more interesting claim: that facts about what an absent counterfactual intervener would have done are irrelevant to responsibility in principle, not merely in the particular cases. On my view, everything that bears on whether an agent is responsible is fixed by the actual sequence that produced the action — that the deliberation was genuinely hers, that it was responsive to reasons in the right way, and that the action issued from her evaluative commitments rather than from compulsion, manipulation, or malfunction. What the intervener would have done had she wavered is a shadow cast by that actual sequence; one can thicken, thin, or delete the shadow without altering a single responsibility-relevant fact. The residual alternatives that some defenders of the Principle hunt for inside the Frankfurt cases — the tiny counterfactually open choices that are said to preserve a flicker of freedom — are, on this view, not what responsibility was ever tracking.

What would count against this? I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they are, from the ones that should actually move me to the ones that are merely awkward. I am particularly interested in four things.

First, whether any of the Frankfurt cases can be reconstructed so that the actual sequence is impeccable by my own lights — genuinely the agent's own, reasons-responsive, unmanipulated — and yet the agent is plainly not responsible. If such a case exists, my view is in trouble, because I have made the actual sequence both necessary and sufficient.

Second, whether my dismissal of the flicker is too quick. If a case can be built in which the only thing separating a responsible agent from a non-responsible one is some counterfactually open alternative, then counterfactual facts are doing real work after all, and my shadow metaphor is false.

Third, what happens when the counterfactual intervener is replaced by a law of nature, so that no agent in the world could have formed a contrary intention. Is my view committed to saying that this changes nothing, and if so, is that a liability or exactly the result I should want?

Fourth, whether the view has any falsifiable content left. Once I have packed genuine ownership, reasons-responsiveness, and commitment-issuing into the actual sequence, have I simply defined responsibility as whatever the actual sequence happens to possess, so that no case could ever count against me? If so, tell me honestly whether that is a defect of my view in particular or the unavoidable fate of any correct account of responsibility.

Along the way, please say what my view predicts that its rivals do not — what it rules in or out that a defender of the Principle, or a sceptic about the whole debate, need not — and which objections a defender of my position would concede as counting against her versus which she could reasonably dismiss. Finally, if I told you that the case I would personally find hardest to answer is the one in which the agent's actual sequence is reasons-responsive but her reasons themselves were installed by another, tell me whether that is a fair thing to be worried about or a red herring.
