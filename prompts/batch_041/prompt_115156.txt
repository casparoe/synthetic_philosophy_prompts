ok so i'm building the last week of a unit on the unity of consciousness and i want to do it with Jekyll and Hyde, because everyone already knows the story and it's the obvious fictional cousin of the split-brain cases. what i want is a real reading of the novella (the Stevenson one, not the film versions — my students have all seen some Hollywood thing where Hyde is basically a big sexy guy, which is nothing like the book) through the split-brain literature and the philosophy that's grown up around it.

the stuff i want actually in play: that after the surgery you usually end up with one hemisphere that can talk and one that mostly can't, that the talking half will cheerfully confabulate a reason for something the other half just did, that these patients are weirdly ordinary in daily life and only come apart on lab tasks, and the live disagreement about whether we're looking at one subject whose consciousness is disunified or literally two subjects sharing a skull. also the fact that the two halves can differ in what they like, what they're good at, how they react — the split isn't a moral split, it's two agents with different equipment.

so: does the novella dramatize something the clinical cases really do show, or does it get the phenomenology backwards by making the division a moral one, good self and evil self, when the actual cases have no moral valence at all? is Jekyll's framing — that a person isn't one thing but two — closer to the truth than the patients' own reports, or is it a moralized fantasy the science quietly undercuts? and where does the story resist the reading — the drug, the shared memory, the shared body, the fact that Hyde reads as smaller and younger rather than simply as the bad half. dont just use the novella to illustrate the neuroscience, and dont bend the neuroscience so the story comes out tidy. i want both taken seriously, and i want to know what the novella adds that the papers and the philosophy dont.
