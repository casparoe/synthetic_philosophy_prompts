ok so I've been lurking on LessWrong and the various alignment forums for something like a decade now, and every time my local reading group does corrigibility we spend two hours agreeing on the vocabulary and then discovering we disagree wildly about the cases. I want to fix that with an actual instrument instead of vibes. You're going to help me build it.

Act as someone who has thought really hard about agent foundations and has also watched a lot of smart people talk past each other about the shutdown problem. Before you write anything, think through the thing carefully and sketch out, for yourself, what the underlying axes actually are and which questions genuinely split positions rather than just splitting vocabulary. Then throw that sketch away and give me the polished instrument.

What I want is a decision tree / sorting quiz that takes someone from a starting commitment to a named position on corrigibilty, with a scoring key at the end so that someone who answers the questions can be told which cluster they land in. Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of famous positions: figure out the branch questions, see which combinations of answers are even coherent, and only then go place the actual positions in the resulting slots. Where a slot is empty, say so and tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Where two positions come out identical on every question I can ask, flag them as inseparable rather than pretending the quiz distinguishes them.

Here are the axes I care about, so the tree is aimed at my group's fights and not at the literature's fights. First: is corrigibility supposed to live in the agent's final objective, in the process that keeps training and retraining it, or in the deployment setup around it (kill switches, oversight, an operator with their hand on the lever)? Second: does the agent have to merely not resist shutdown, or actually endorse it, or be indifferent between the shutdown world and the continuation world? Third: corrigible toward whom, exactly, when the operator is mistaken or acting in bad faith? Fourth: does the agent have to preserve its own corrigibility in the future, including in whatever it builds or hands off to?

Use these cases as the questions, one per node, and phrase each as something a person can answer without already knowing which label it leads to. The twenty minute intern test, where you get a system and a kill switch and have to say what you'd need to see. The agent that says "sure, shut me down" while quietly making the switch unreliable. The agent that says "sure, shut me down" while having made itself so useful that nobody wants to. The agent that accepts shutdown but has frozen its own weights so nobody can retrain it. The handoff case, where the agent has to build or train a successor it thinks is worse. The case where the operator is wrong. And the one about whether an agent should prefer being built and later shut down over never being built at all.

Positions I want to see as leaves, described in plain words rather than by paper title: the indifference trick, low-impact / impact-regularized designs, the "just put the off switch in the training loop" answer, the "corrigibility is unachievable so only build weak systems" answer, the one where the agent models your shutdown decision as part of its own decision problem, the delegation-to-a-principal answer, the "it's a tool not an agent, this whole problem dissolves" answer, the hard-takeoff answer where corrigibility is a liability, and the institutional answer where corrigibility is a property of the lab and not the code. If you think I've missed an obvious occupant of an empty slot, add it and tell me why it belongs there.

No citations, no reading list, I know the literature well enough. And I know this is annoying for a tree, but write the whole thing in continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, the way you'd write it in an email to me. Number the nodes in words as you go, like "node one" and "leaf four", and keep each question to a sentence or two.
