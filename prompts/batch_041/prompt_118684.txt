Last night a friend and I spent two hours arguing about higher-order theories of consciousness, and I came away realizing I can't state the view's central claim in a way that doesn't collapse into something either trivial or absurd. His version of it: a mental state is conscious when you are aware of having it, which he took to be either a restatement of the obvious or a recipe for regress, since whatever does the being-aware-of is itself a mental state, and either it is conscious (so the regress runs) or it isn't (so why does the first-order state get to be?). When I tried to distinguish the higher-order representation from ordinary introspection and from metacognition, he said I was relabeling. I want to teach my way out of this rather than argue my way out.

In three weeks I have ninety minutes with a mixed group: cognitive science graduate students, two neuroscientists, and one philosopher of language. They know the global workspace and predictive processing framings of consciousness; most have never met the higher-order family at all — the higher-order thought version, the inner-sense or higher-order perception version, and the self-representational attempts to block the regress. I have the primary sources. What I lack is a plan.

Give me the order in which the pieces have to come and what depends on what: what must be in place before the central claim can be stated without sounding trivial, and where the regress worry has to be handled so that it lands as a live problem rather than a refutation. Then give me the one case that should carry the session, saying what it has to have (a state that is genuinely mental and genuinely not conscious) and what it must lack, and tell me whether my candidate will do the work: a friend who drives forty minutes while listening to a podcast, arrives with no memory of the drive, and can nonetheless recall a specific billboard when prompted. Map the misconceptions in the order they will surface — my guesses are that higher-order means metacognition, that the higher-order state must itself be conscious, that this smuggles in a homunculus, that anything registering its own state (a thermostat, say) thereby qualifies, and, worst, that the theory dissolves the hard problem — and tell me exactly where in the sequence to head off each one. Finish with what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, plus one question I can ask at the end that would show whether the central claim landed or merely got repeated back.

If my framing of the disagreement is itself confused — if the regress is not really the crux, or the case I want to carry the session with cannot bear the weight — say so plainly instead of building me a tidy plan around a bad premise. I would rather be told the session needs a different shape.
