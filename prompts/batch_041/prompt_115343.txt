My friend, a doctoral student in political theory, spent most of last night trying to convince me that majority rule is justified because a crowd of individually mediocre people is more likely to get things right than any single one of them, and she leaned the whole case on the Condorcet jury theorem. I called it replicated majority voting, a design I spend my working life defending and quietly distrusting, and she accused me of cynicism. I am a site reliability engineer: I have spent years around quorum-replicated stores, quorum-intersection arguments, and Byzantine-fault thresholds; I have drawn reliability block diagrams and fault trees for k-out-of-n systems; and I have been paged at three in the morning by outages that took out every replica at once because whatever killed them was not independent. I follow her theorem in outline — each voter likelier than not to be right, votes independent of one another, the majority's chance of being right rising with the size of the electorate and approaching certainty as that size grows, collapsing toward zero if the typical voter is worse than a coin flip — but I do not trust my own translation of it.

So restate the theorem, and the objections to it that philosophers actually care about, as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I use: components and per-component reliability, k-out-of-n redundancy, quorum sizes and quorum intersection, crash faults versus Byzantine faults, common-mode and correlated failures, error decorrelation, the difference between damping noise and correcting a shared bias, and the fact that in a replicated system the value to be agreed on is usually fixed by the protocol or by an operator rather than discovered in the world. Do not smuggle my assumptions in and call it translation; where my vocabulary forces a distortion, flag the distortion rather than smoothing it over. Two points I especially want tested. She says the independence assumption is a harmless idealization, the frictionless plane of the argument, whereas I say it is the entire load-bearing beam — in my world, redundancy that shares a failure mode buys you nothing, and a majority of components all biased the same way is just the bias with extra steps. And she insists the theorem concerns the probability of being correct rather than the legitimacy of the outcome, which makes me suspect the word correct is doing work my vocabulary has no slot for: my replicas converge on a value because a protocol or an operator decides which value counts, and I have no way to say that they converged on the true one.

Give me exactly five numbered items, with no introduction and no conclusion, each one or two sentences, except that the fifth may run a little longer: (1) the theorem itself, translated; (2) the independence assumption, translated, including what its counterpart in my world is actually called and whether the two are the same condition; (3) the competence parameter, translated, including where the bias-versus-noise distinction lands; (4) the standard philosophical objections, translated; (5) the residue report — what carried over cleanly, what had to be distorted to fit, what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all, and whether that residue marks a gap in my framework or a sign that the philosophers' disagreement was less substantive than it looks.
