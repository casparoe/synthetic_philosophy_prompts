For two years my reading group has been going round the same dispute, and I want a diagnosis of it rather than another round. One side holds that where two options are neither better nor worse than each other, and not equally good either, there is no fact of the matter about which should be chosen, so an agent who cannot choose is failing to think clearly. The other holds that some choices are genuinely hard — that no further information and no sharper attention to the options would yield a verdict — and that this is a feature of value rather than a defect in the chooser. Our stock case is a woman deciding whether to stay in the city where her work is or move to care for a parent who needs her; she insists that neither option is better, and also that the choice is not a matter of indifference. Each side's argument seems to begin from its own conclusion, and we have stopped moving.

Three ideas have been proposed to get us unstuck, and I want to know which of them does real work. First, a three-way distinction between a gap in the ranking (neither better nor worse nor equal), a fourth comparative relation under which the options are on a par, and plain ignorance of a ranking that exists. Second, the distinction between a claim about value (how the options are) and a claim about practical rationality (what a chooser may or may not do). Third, my own suspicion that the two sides are not comparing the same objects: that one compares options under descriptions taken to be complete, while the other compares them as the things one would be doing.

Tell me what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, substantive, or two questions under one heading — and what would count as progress given your answer: a distinction, a case, a formal result, or an argument that none should be expected. Commit to a diagnosis and defend it rather than laying out the options. Write in British English.
