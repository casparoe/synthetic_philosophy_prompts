For our school's philosophy club I ran a session on the Death in Damascus puzzle, using a handout that set it up as follows: a man in Damascus learns that Death will come for him today, and he can either stay where he is or slip away to Aleppo. Where Death will be waiting was settled in advance by a forecast of what the man would do — a forecast made by someone who knew his character and circumstances in complete detail, and one that has never once been wrong — though the forecast is not what makes him choose, and his choosing is not what puts Death in one city rather than the other. Two members then argued, and I could not referee them.

Maya said the case is a reductio of evidential decision theory. On that theory the man should do whatever he is not about to do: if he is set on staying, staying is evidence that Death is in Damascus, so he should flee; if he is set on fleeing, fleeing is evidence that Death is in Aleppo, so he should stay. A theory whose advice is always to do the opposite of what you will do gives no advice at all.

Dev said it is a reductio of causal decision theory instead. That theory tells the man his act is causally idle, so the two options come out equal and he may as well flip a coin — which is absurd when every option ends with him meeting Death. Maya answered that the causalist's "either will do" is at least coherent, and that the man loses nothing by leaving, since fleeing does not cause Death to be in Aleppo. Dev replied that a perfect forecast forbids exactly that move: you cannot hold the forecast fixed while changing the act, and the man's act is a sign of where Death is, and a sign is as good a guide as a cause. Maya then said that if we take the perfect forecast seriously, the man could not have done otherwise, and the case is fatalism in technical dress rather than a test of either theory.

Please adjudicate their exchange move by move — who is right about which claim, what each of them is missing, whether a perfect forecast really does imply that he could not have done otherwise, and whether the case tells against either theory or only appears to. If the question makes sense at all, say what he should do.
