For a monthly reading group I help run — it meets to work through problems in decision theory and their bearing on the safety of advanced AI — I have to open the next session, and I have decided to stage the disagreement as a dialogue rather than deliver a summary, because the group tends to nod along to conclusions and I want them to watch an argument actually unfold. The session is on acausal threats. Please write that dialogue.

Use these two characters, and make them equally sharp: I do not want a rigged exchange in which one of them exists to be refuted. Ines has spent the year building models of decision-making under logical correlation, and she believes that an agent can be genuinely moved by a threat that will never travel through any causal channel, because two agents whose decision procedures are correlated can stand to each other rather as two copies of one agent stand to each other in a prisoner's dilemma. Daniel is a sceptic who thinks that a threat without a mechanism is just talk, and that the whole notion borrows credibility from cases where there is a hidden causal route. To keep the exchange concrete rather than airy, assume they are arguing about a specific case: two advanced AI systems, built independently by different teams, with no physical channel between them, each of which has enough information about the other — from published training procedures and design documents — to predict how the other's decision algorithm would behave across a wide range of situations; one of them is weighing whether to comply with a demand that the other has not, in any ordinary sense, communicated, backed by a threat that could not be carried out by any causal route. Let the dialogue turn on three things in particular: first, whether a threat with no causal channel can ever give anyone a reason to comply, or whether it collapses into mere talk; second, what distinguishes a genuine acausal threat from an ordinary precommitment that works through a causal mechanism, and whether the party under threat could ever tell which of the two it is facing; and third, whether the threatened party can answer the threat by adopting a surrogate goal — a goal it does not value for its own sake, taken up only because pursuing it would make carrying out the threat costly or self-defeating for the threatener — and whether such a surrogate goal is a stable strategy or dissolves the moment the threatener realises it was adopted instrumentally. I would like each of them to concede at least one point to the other, and to press the concession in a direction the other did not anticipate.

A few practical points. Keep it self-contained: the dialogue should make the relevant notions intelligible to a reader who has never encountered them, by having the characters explain them in the course of arguing, rather than by leaning on prior reading. Do not have either of them cite papers, appeal to who said what, or name any literature — if a consideration has a pedigree, put the consideration itself in their mouths. The dialogue should be roughly 1200 to 1800 words, in alternating speeches, with real back-and-forth: each reply should engage the actual point just made rather than advancing the speaker's next prepared position. End it without a verdict, but at a point where the disagreement has been narrowed to something the group could productively argue about.
