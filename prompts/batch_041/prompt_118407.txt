I am writing a novel about a man who commanded a company in a war he has since concluded was unjust, and I want the book's moral accounting to be exact rather than merely atmospheric. My difficulty is the principle of the moral equality of combatants. As I have come to understand it, the just war tradition holds that the two sides in a war are morally on a par: that it declines to rank the justice of their causes, and treats the aggressor's soldier and the defender's soldier as equals — each licensed to kill the other, each equally exposed, neither more culpable than the other for what he does — and, further, that the justice of the war itself has no bearing on what a soldier may do within it or on what he may be blamed for afterwards. I have also read that the doctrine turns on a distinction between a combatant's liability to be killed and his culpability for what he does, and I take this to mean that culpability never attaches to a soldier for the cause he serves. If that is right, then the guilt my character carries is the very illusion the theory dissolves, and the most he can owe is something to the men he killed as such, not to the cause he served; the scene I have planned, in which an enemy soldier tells him that they are the same and that he owes him nothing, would be not a kindness but a statement of the doctrine.

Tell me how the tradition would actually draw up the account. Whom, if anyone, has my officer wronged, and on what ground? What may he say when he finally tries to reckon with it, and what does the theory forbid him to say? I care less about whether the principle can be defended than about what it commits me to on the page, so what I need is not a defense of it but the account itself.
