Construct a thought experiment that separates the three principal accounts of what an emotion is, using a single episode from an ordinary adult life.

The three accounts are: the feeling account, on which an emotion is a distinctive felt bodily state; the evaluative-judgment account, on which an emotion is a judgment that something matters to the subject in a certain way; and the perceptual account, on which an emotion is a perception-like experience that presents its object as having a certain value.

The specifications are these.

(1) Verdict pattern. The feeling account and the evaluative-judgment account must both classify the episode as a genuine emotion, while the perceptual account must deny that it is an emotion at all. This is the reverse of the familiar pattern, in which the perceptual account is the inclusive one (it admits recalcitrant cases) and the judgment account is the exclusive one; do not reproduce that familiar pattern.

(2) Satisfied on their own terms. The episode must contain a real, describable felt bodily profile and a real evaluative judgment, so that the two accounts that count it as an emotion are not merely being generous or being carried by a stipulation.

(3) Structural divergence. The perceptual account's denial must be forced by the character of the experience itself — by how the state is produced or undergone — and not by anything missing in the subject (no irrationality, self-deception, or cognitive deficit), and not by your simply announcing that the theory gives that verdict.

(4) Ordinary and object-directed. One paragraph; no phobias, no clinical conditions, no imagined neurotechnology; the emotion must be about a particular object or act rather than a free-floating mood.

Then, in no more than 450 words: state each account's verdict and the reason it gives; identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work; say what the perceptual account would say if that feature were removed; and state the strongest way a defender of the perceptual account would attack the case. Keep the entire response under 650 words.
