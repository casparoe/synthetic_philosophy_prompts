I am a sixth-form student at a school in England, and I am writing an essay for our philosophy prize. I should be grateful if you would write it for me. The question I have set myself is whether the doctrine of double effect identifies a genuine moral distinction or merely dignifies a distinction that does no work, and I want the essay to close with a verdict that is actually defended, not a survey that trails off into a shrug.

Let me say at the outset what I am not asking for. I have read the familiar cases — the runaway trolley, the man on the footbridge, the surgeon and the five patients — and I do not want another guided tour of them. I want the essay to take a case of my own devising as its central test, to work out exactly what the doctrine must say about it, and then to decide whether that verdict survives scrutiny.

The doctrine, as it is usually set out to me, runs roughly as follows. An action that has both a good and a bad effect may be permissible even when the bad effect is foreseen with certainty, provided that four conditions are met: the action itself must not be intrinsically wrong; the bad effect must not be the means by which the good effect is produced; the bad effect must not be intended, though it may be foreseen; and the good effect must be proportionate to the bad. It is the second and third conditions that interest me, because the second looks like a claim about the shape of the causal chain and the third a claim about the contents of the agent's mind, and I suspect the two can be pulled apart.

Here is my case.

Five climbers are trapped on a ledge high on a mountain. A storm is closing in, and on any realistic estimate they have only a few hours before the cold kills them. The only route that a rescue team can cover in time runs down a narrow gully. The gully is choked by a slab of ice and rock that the team cannot climb over in the time available, but they can shift it with a small explosive charge. If they do, the slab will fall the length of the gully and come to rest on the ledge at the foot of it, where a lone climber is bivouacked; it will certainly kill him. He cannot be woken or reached in the time available, and there is no way to warn him. If the team does not use the charge, they cannot get through in time, the five die, and the lone climber survives. There is, finally, a second way of placing the charge that would clear the gully without harming anyone, but it would take about four hours; the team chooses the placement that will certainly kill the lone climber.

I should like the essay to do four things. First, state precisely what the doctrine must say about the case, condition by condition, and be explicit about which condition is doing the work. Second, press on whether the lone climber's death is a means to the rescue or a side effect of it, and whether the doctrine can answer that question without circularity. Third, consider the strongest objection: that a team which chooses the placement it knows will kill a man, when it could have chosen one that would not, intends his death in the only sense that matters, so that the doctrine's distinction between the foreseen and the intended is an illusion. Fourth, reach a verdict on whether the doctrine is a genuine moral principle, a rule of thumb that happens to track something else, or a rationalisation — and say what the team ought to do.

Please do not end by observing that everything depends on how intention is defined. If it does, say which account of intention we should adopt, and defend it.
