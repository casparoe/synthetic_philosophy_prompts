I am a third-year student in a course on Augustine's ethics, and I have been asked to run the Q&A portion of our seminar when a visiting scholar joins us next week. Her research defends the reading that Augustine's prohibition of lying admits of no exceptions — that the passages where he grades the helpful lie as the least grave of sins are not a backdoor concession, and that the standard modern objections (the murderer at the door, the lie that saves a life, the lie told to protect the innocent) miss what the prohibition is actually about.

Please draft eight to ten interview questions for her, ordered so that each builds on the one before, and for each supply a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The questions should turn on the structure of her position rather than invite her to restate it. I would like at least one question each on the following, though please add others if they sharpen the sequence:

- the definition of lying as the intention to deceive, and its counterintuitive consequences: that a false statement sincerely believed is not a lie, while a true statement uttered in order to mislead is one; and what this implies about lies told to people the speaker knows cannot be deceived, or to an audience that will see through the falsehood immediately;
- the distinction between believing and merely opining, and whether the confident rashasserter who spreads a falsehood is really in a different moral category from the liar, given that both may mislead equally;
- the claim that lying is a perversion of the natural function of speech, which is to convey the speaker's mind: how much work that teleological premise does, and whether the position survives if one rejects it;
- the graded catalogue of kinds of lies, and whether a ranked list of sins is coherent alongside an absolute prohibition, given that the least grave lie is still said to be forbidden rather than merely discouraged;
- the biblical cases usually cited in favour of permissible deception, such as the Hebrew midwives' reply to Pharaoh and Rahab's concealment of the spies, where Augustine seems to commend the agents while refusing to commend the lie itself; ask whether that manoeuvre can be applied consistently, or whether it makes Scriptural praise unusable as evidence about the moral status of the act;
- the argument from martyrdom, on which permitting a lie to save a life would undermine the martyrs' refusal to deny the faith: whether that argument proves too much, and whether it depends on treating the stakes in the two cases as commensurable;
- the controversy that prompted his later, harsher treatment of the subject — whether Catholics may pretend to be heretics, and lie, in order to infiltrate them — and how he avoids both the rigorist conclusion that no false word may ever be spoken and the consequentialist conclusion he rejects.

She has a habit of replying to hard cases by granting that the counterexample shows lying to be the lesser evil while denying that it shows lying to be permitted; please include at least one question designed to make that move progressively harder to use. End with a question about what would have to be true for her to abandon the no-exceptions reading.
