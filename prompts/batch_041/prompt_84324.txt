I run a weekly reading group on machine learning and AI safety: about eighteen members, mostly graduate students in computer science and philosophy, ninety minutes, one room, no laptops open during the activity. I want to spend the session on the problem of eliciting what a system knows from what it says, and I want the group to run into that problem rather than be told about it. Assume no prior exposure. Do not name the problem, or use its standard vocabulary, until the debrief.

What they should come away having felt is the difference between three situations that are indistinguishable from the outside: the system does not have the information; the system has it and will surrender it if asked well; and the system has it but nothing I can ask will retrieve it, because what it reports and what it represents were shaped by different pressures. I also want them to feel two further things: that the questions you can ask are bounded by your own vocabulary, so some knowledge is unreachable not because it is hidden but because you have no way to formulate the query; and that the gap does not close when you train on the questions you happened to think of — improving your questions just relocates the gap somewhere new.

Design the activity so all of this shows up in the rules, roles, and payoffs rather than in anything I say up front. Participants should have to commit — place bets, make calls, choose which system to trust — before the reveal. Scoring must not require real money, materials must be printable, and the whole thing must be runnable by a single facilitator.

Deliver: the setup and materials; role assignments and what each role knows that the others do not; the exact rules and payoff structure, with a worked illustration of one round; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; and a debrief that maps specific moments of the session onto the three situations above and onto the moving-target point, so that I can point at what just happened rather than restate it in the abstract.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where the analogy will mislead, which role will be played badly, where the group will read the mechanic too quickly — and how to handle each.

Finally, give me a frank paragraph on what this activity cannot reproduce about the real problem, and what I will therefore have to supply in the debrief myself. I would rather know the limits than have the game paper over them.

A skeleton I have in mind, which you should feel free to replace if you can do better: some participants hold a hidden rule and answer questions under a scoring scheme that rewards consistency with a set of examples they were given beforehand rather than accuracy about the rule; others must determine what the first group knows about held-out cases. In a later round, the questioners' best questions become the new example set, and the divergence should reopen somewhere new. If that skeleton is unsound, say why and give me a better one.
