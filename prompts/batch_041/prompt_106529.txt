The answer:

The sorites paradox arises for predicates such as heap or red that apply by degrees. The standard conditional version begins from a clear case — a single grain of sand is not a heap — and a tolerance principle: if n grains of sand do not make a heap, then neither do n + 1, since the addition of one grain cannot make the difference between a heap and a non-heap. Iterating this principle yields the conclusion that no number of grains makes a heap, which is absurd. The paradox therefore shows that vague predicates have no extension at all, and that any theory which assigns them one must be mistaken.

Responses to the paradox divide into three groups. The first denies the tolerance principle and holds that there is a sharp boundary to every vague predicate; this is the epistemicist line, on which the boundary exists but cannot be known. The second retains the tolerance principle and revises classical logic, which is what the degree theorist does. The third retains classical logic and gives up bivalence, which is the supervaluationist strategy.

Supervaluationism is the most promising of the three. Its central idea is that a sentence is true if it comes out true on every admissible way of making the predicate precise. Where admissible precisifications disagree, the sentence is neither true nor false, so the theory does justice to the intuition that vague predicates have no sharp boundaries: unlike the epistemicist, the supervaluationist need not posit a boundary that exists but is unknowable, since on this account there is no boundary at all, only the absence of a determinate answer. Because truth is defined as truth on every precisification, supervaluationism also preserves classical logic, including the law of excluded middle: the disjunction that n grains make a heap or do not is true even though neither disjunct is. And it gives a satisfying treatment of the sorites, since the tolerance conditionals are neither true nor false — each comes out true on some precisifications and false on others — so the argument is unsound because it relies on premises that lack a truth value. The theory's principal cost is the problem of higher-order vagueness: the notion of an admissible precisification is itself vague, and it is unclear that the theory can account for this without appealing to a further level of precisification.

Degree theory is less satisfactory. It holds that sentences are true to degrees between 0 and 1, and that each tolerance conditional is true to a degree just below 1, so that the argument is valid but unsound. The price is that degree theory abandons classical logic, since it rejects the law of excluded middle, and it is not clear what it means for a sentence to be 0.7 true.

I conclude that supervaluationism is the best available response to the paradox, although not a complete one.
