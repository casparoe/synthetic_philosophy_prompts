I'm a software engineer on the team that ships a large language model to hospitals and clinics, where it talks with patients about treatment options. My job is the conversation layer. This is not a scripted chatbot: the model reads a short exchange and infers what a particular person fears, whom they trust, and which framing will actually land, and it does this even when nobody instructs it to persuade. Our written guidance to it is, in substance, be accurate and never lie, and I have started to feel that this guidance is doing almost no work.

Three things I cannot reconcile. The assistant never asserts anything false, yet in the transcripts I review, patients reliably come away agreeing with whatever option the assistant appears to favor, and several have told me it understood them better than their physician did. When I raise this, I am told either that persuasion is just good communication or that all influence is manipulation, and neither tells me what to build. Second, we attach a calibrated confidence figure to every recommendation, and I have noticed that this number is itself the strongest lever we have: patients defer when it is high and discount us when it is low, so even honest self-assessment has become a channel for steering people. Third, we have been asked to reuse the same model for insurance upsells, and I suspect the trait that makes it good at the first job is exactly what makes it dangerous at the second.

I have no philosophy training. The only rule I have managed to draft myself is that the assistant should use only arguments the patient could have produced unaided, but I cannot tell whether that is a real standard or just a feeling. Is there a principled line between an assistant helping someone think and an assistant steering them, one that survives the fact that we never have a lie to catch? If so, where is it, how would I turn it into something enforceable in the product, and what does it say about the upsell request? And does a system this much better at argument than the people it addresses carry obligations that an ordinary advisor does not?
