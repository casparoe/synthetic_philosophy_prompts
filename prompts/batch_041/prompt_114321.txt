Write the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in a long-running dispute over housing and land use — a document so drafted that each camp could put its name to it.

The first camp is the build-by-right camp. It holds that the central housing injustice is legal exclusion: the lattice of zoning rules, minimum lot sizes, height and density limits, parking requirements and discretionary planning review that makes it unlawful to build the homes the market would otherwise produce, and that entrenches the advantages of existing owners at the expense of everyone who arrives later. Its first demand is that permission to build be granted as of right, at much higher densities, across the whole city, and it treats the resulting construction as the principal engine of affordability.

The second camp is the community-control camp. It holds that the central housing injustice is the treatment of the home as an asset and of the neighbourhood as a site of private accumulation, in which the gains from rising land values accrue to owners and developers while the costs — displacement, the erosion of the social fabric that makes a place habitable, the loss of any real say over what happens next door — fall on tenants and long-standing residents. Its first demand is secure tenure and democratic control over the neighbourhood, and it regards new construction that arrives without those guarantees as at best beside the point and at worst a windfall to precisely those who have already profited.

The two have argued for a decade, each convinced that the other's remedy addresses the wrong mechanism. Your task is to produce the document they should have written. Both have agreed to reason from the mechanics of the problem rather than from the literature: do not cite works, authors or studies, and do not supply a reading list. Where a claim rests on an empirical regularity, state the regularity in plain terms and treat it as something to be tested rather than invoked as authority.

The statement should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — about how the housing market works, about who currently bears the costs of the status quo, and about what is not in dispute. These must be substantive enough that neither camp could have written them alone, and must not be truisms dressed as concessions.
2. A statement of each remaining disagreement, phrased in wording its own side would accept, each marked as either genuine or — where you judge it so — merely verbal, with the reason.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. For each, give the case, natural experiment or argument that both camps agree bears on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts about it. Include at least the following candidates, though you may add others: whether new market-rate construction lowers rents in the surrounding area, by what mechanism and on what timescale; whether upzoning tends to be capitalised into land prices rather than converted into more homes; whether the binding constraint is regulatory or financial and demand-side; whether discretionary review functions as a safeguard or as an exclusion device; and whether tenure security and new supply are complements or substitutes.

To give the exercise a common object, both camps have agreed to assess the following programme, which neither designed and neither fully endorses: (a) every residential lot in the city is rezoned by right to four storeys and four dwellings, with no minimum parking requirement and no discretionary review for schemes that comply; (b) the uplift in land value attributable to the rezoning is taxed at a high rate on realisation; (c) the proceeds are placed in a community land trust governed by residents of the affected district, which must hold a specified share of new homes at rents tied to local incomes and must offer sitting tenants a right of first refusal. Say which parts each camp accepts outright, which it accepts only instrumentally, and which it rejects, and why.

Format the result as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table, with the cruxes set out beneath the table. Write in British English throughout, in a register suitable for a published joint statement.
