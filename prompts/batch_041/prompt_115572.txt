So I do a tiny interview podcast — mostly philosophy grad students and people who read too much on the internet — and my next guest is someone I've wanted to talk to for a while. She's spent alot of the last several years defending consequentialism against the cluelessness objection. The objection, as she frames it, is that the causal ripples of anything you do spread out into an unknowable future — whether you buy the coffee or skip it, the knock-on effects eventually swamp whatever you were trying to accomplish — so the consequentialist's instruction to maximize expected value has nothing determinate to operate on, since you can't assign probabilities to consequences that far out. Her response is the part I want to dig into. She thinks the mistake is computing expected value over all the worlds consistent with your evidence, and that you should instead compute it over a restricted class — the worlds that share what she calls your causal neighborhood — so the far-out ripples get no weight at all rather than a tiny weight. She's up front that this is a substantive restriction on what counts as a consequence, not just a bookkeeping trick, and she thinks that's fine. I'm partly convinced and partly suspicious.

I want ten questions that actually press on this, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, and none of the "what got you into philosophy" stuff. I need at least one question on how she draws the boundary of the causal neighborhood without it being arbitrary — why *that* horizon and not one a little nearer or farther — and whether the same cluelessness just reappears at the boundary. At least one on whether her move is really a claim about what matters (the far future genuinely doesn't count) or a claim about what we can know (it counts, we're just in the dark), because those seem to carry very different costs, and I want her pinned down on which one she's defending. At least one on the tension with the people who became consequentialists precisely because they think the far future and astronomical-scale outcomes are where the action is — does her restriction cut against that motivation or not? And at least one on whether the cluelessness objection is even specific to consequentialism, since deontological rules and virtues also have unknowable downstream effects, and whether pointing that out is a real defense or just a tu quoque. Throw in one that asks what her view tells her to do differently in an actual decision she faces — something she'd do one way that a standard expected-value maximizer would do another. Write the questions in American English, and phrase them the way an interviewer would actually say them out loud, not like essay prompts.
