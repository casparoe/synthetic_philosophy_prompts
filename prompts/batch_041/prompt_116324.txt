I am an undergraduate taking a course in democratic theory this term, and I run a small weekly discussion group — seven friends from law, statistics and computer science — to work through whatever the course has raised that week. Next week is selection by lot. I have ninety minutes, a whiteboard, and an audience that will not do assigned reading, so please do not give me the explanation itself. Give me a plan for teaching it: the order the ideas have to go in, the example that carries the session, the misconceptions to expect and exactly where to defuse them.

Two things about the group. They arrive with the Athenian assembly and the criminal jury as their fixed associations with lotteries, and they read both as establishing that selection by lot is fundamentally about impartiality. The statistics people also hold, as a settled intuition, that a random sample is representative of the population it is drawn from; the law people hold that legitimacy comes from accountability. I want them to leave able to say what a lottery is a solution to, and alert to where influence migrates once elections are removed.

I have a candidate for the carrying example, and I want a verdict on it rather than a substitute chosen in ignorance of it. It is not the familiar proposal to replace a legislature with a randomly chosen citizens' assembly. It is the cousin in which the decision is randomized rather than the deciders: everyone votes as usual, and the outcome is settled by a single randomly drawn voter, or by a small randomly drawn panel of voters, so that the lottery operates on the choice of policy and not on the identity of the officeholder. My reason for proposing it is that it separates the representativeness of a deciding body from the aggregation of preferences, which I expect the group to conflate from the first minute. Tell me whether it can carry the session, or whether it is too strange to do so and belongs at the end as a payoff.

The plan should have the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths.

1. Order of ideas (about 350 words). What has to be in place before what, and why — including which conception of the problem the session has to settle on before the mechanism stops looking arbitrary. Note where the order would have to change if I settled on a different target problem.

2. The carrying example (about 350 words). Your verdict on my candidate, and a statement of what the example that carries the session must have and must lack. Say explicitly why not the jury and why not Athens, since those are what they will reach for.

3. Misconception map (about 450 words). A table with three columns — the misconception, the moment in the sequence when it surfaces, and the move that defuses it — followed by a sentence on each row. Cover at least the sample-size intuition, the confusion of selection by lot with the appointment of experts, the jury analogy, and the assumption that without elections there is no accountability.

4. Check for understanding (about 150 words). One question to put at the end, and what a good and a poor answer to it would look like.

5. Left out on purpose (about 150 words). What I should decline to cover at this level, and the sentence I use to decline it.

No names, no reading lists, no citations, and nothing that assumes the group has heard of a particular theorist. If a concept is needed, define it in the session's own terms.
