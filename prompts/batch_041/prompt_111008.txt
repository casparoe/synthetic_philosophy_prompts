I'm a journalist who covers the social sciences, and for the better part of a year I've been circling a long-running fight among economic historians and sociologists over whether a society's values can be a genuine cause of its economic trajectory — the claim, usually traced to Weber, that a religious or ethical orientation can help bring a commercial order into being rather than merely reflecting one. After a long dinner with two economic historians who disagree about almost everything else, I've ended up with a view of my own that I'd like you to try to break. Roughly: culture can be a cause, but it is never an engine — only a brake. On my picture, a value-orientation does causal work when it renders an otherwise attractive course of action effectively unthinkable for the people who hold it, so the signature of its causal power is always an absence: the durable, otherwise-inexplicable non-occurrence of profitable moves that we can independently establish were available to the actors in question. Where values merely accompany or rationalize what people were going to do anyway, they aren't doing causal work; where they forbid something the incentives were pushing people toward, they are. I can already hear the objection that this lets me have it both ways, since almost any pattern of conduct can be redescribed as a suppressed alternative, and that is exactly what I want you to test.

So tell me what would count against the view. I want the defeaters ranked by how decisive they would actually be for someone already attracted to the picture — not only the objections critics would press, but the things a sympathetic defender would have to concede as awkward. Please keep the kinds of defeater distinct: empirical findings (a case where a value-orientation is demonstrably doing positive causal work, or where a supposed brake turns out to track incentives after all), conceptual arguments (that causal claims about absences are incoherent, or that the view smuggles in a counterfactual the evidence can never supply), and methodological results (that the whole family of explanation is unfalsifiable, or cannot be given microfoundations). I'd also like to know what the view predicts that its rivals don't — and if it predicts nothing, say so plainly and tell me whether that is fatal or merely a cost. Finally, give me an honest verdict on whether the position has been quietly insulated by qualification, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind. The single thing that would move me most is a case in which a value-orientation outlived the material conditions that supposedly produced it and kept shaping conduct after the incentives had reversed; treat that as the standard I care about most.

Please give me the answer as a JSON object with these fields: `verdict` (a short paragraph on how exposed the view is overall, and whether it survives); `defeaters` (an array of six to ten objects, each with `rank` (integer), `defeater` (the objection or finding in one or two sentences), `kind` (one of "empirical", "conceptual", "methodological"), `what_would_have_to_be_true` (the specific state of the world or argument that would make it a real defeater), `decisiveness` (one of "fatal", "serious", "awkward", "cosmetic"), `defender_concedes` (one of "yes", "partly", "no", plus a clause saying exactly what they would concede or refuse to), and `best_defense` (the strongest reply available to a defender)); `distinctive_predictions` (an array of strings); and `insulation_check` (a paragraph on whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect for a position of this kind).
