This summer I finally got around to restoring my grandfather's 1930s roadster, and I have spent more evenings than I care to admit chasing nuts and bolts cut to a thread standard that no shop stocks anymore. Nothing about that thread is better than what we use now; it survives only because every other part on the frame was made to mate with it, and every replacement I machine myself makes the next one more likely and the cost of switching higher. It has been an odd companion to the last seminar of my STS degree, which has been on path dependence, and I have been asked to close the final session with a poem rather than a summary. So the poem has to do two things at once, and I would like it to be a real poem, not a lecture in verse.

The distinction we kept circling is the one between the weak claim that history merely matters and the strong claim that an inferior arrangement gets locked in because returns to adoption accumulate: the more the standard is used, the more it pays to keep using it, until the choice is no longer live. Against that, one person in the room argued that most supposed lock-ins are convergence in disguise, that the rival would have lost on the merits anyway, and that the whole story is told backwards from the winner. I want a poem that keeps both readings available: one in which the repetition looks like necessity and one in which it looks like mere momentum, so that the reader cannot decide from the poem alone whether the outcome was contingent or overdetermined. It should be recognizable to anyone in the seminar as being about that distinction, without naming a single theorist or study.

Please write it as a pantoum of four quatrains, with the second and fourth lines of each quatrain returning as the first and third of the next, and the closing quatrain folding back into the poem's opening lines. I do not care about rhyme; the interlocking repetition is the engine. Let the speaker be the surviving standard itself, or the last obsolete thread, addressing the rival it outlived, so that its recurrences feel like a thing reproducing itself because it has already reproduced itself. Somewhere in the middle, let a stanza try to break the chain by naming what the rival did better, and let the form pull it back anyway. The final return should be double-edged: read one way it seals the lock-in, read another it confesses that it might all have gone the other way. Keep it to the sixteen lines the form gives you, and make the machinery of the form carry the argument rather than stating it.
