I've been thinking alot about advance directives since my grandfather's stroke, and I keep landing on different answers depending on the case, so I want to know what rule I actually believe.

Here's what I feel. A woman with advanced dementia signed a living will years ago refusing antibiotics if she got pneumonia. She gets pneumonia. She's cheerful, doesn't recognize anyone, says she wants to live, seems content enough. My gut: don't treat her. The person who wrote that will is the one who gets to decide. But take the same setup and change one thing — she wrote the will in the months right after her husband died, when she was barely sleeping and told her doctor she wanted it all over. Now I say treat her, that paper shouldn't count. Those two verdicts feel right and I can't make them sit together.

A man wrote that he never wanted to be on a ventilator. He's on one, awake, alert, and begging to stay on it. Keep him on. Fine. But then a woman with early dementia writes a new directive revoking her old one, and I want to honor the new one, even though the new her is already fading. So it's not simply "whoever's competent now wins," and it's not simply "the earlier person always wins." Something else is doing the work and I don't know what.

Two more. A guy in a coma; his brother says he once told him in a bar that he'd want everything done. I don't think that beats a signed directive. And someone writes: "if I ever convert to a religion, ignore everything I said before." That clause feels like it should have no power over a later self who means it, but I can't say why it's different from the dementia case.

Is there one principle that gets these? If not, tell me which verdict I should give up, and what the principle it leaves me with predicts for a case I haven't listed. And if the whole way I've set this up is confused, just say so — I'd rather hear that than have you smooth it over. Keep it under 350 words.
