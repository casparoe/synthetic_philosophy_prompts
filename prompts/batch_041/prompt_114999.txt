I'm a third-year PhD student working on axiomatic models of fair division, and I'm writing a position piece for a small workshop on normative bargaining theory. The organizing question is one I keep going back and forth on, and I want a properly argued essay rather than a survey. The two standard axiomatic characterizations of the two-person bargaining problem pull in opposite directions: Nash's solution is picked out by scale invariance, Pareto efficiency, symmetry, and independence of irrelevant alternatives, while the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is standardly characterized by scale invariance, Pareto efficiency, symmetry, and individual monotonicity, with the last of these standing in place of the independence axiom. Since each characterization is tight, no solution can satisfy all of these requirements at once, and the literature typically presents the situation as a clean trade-off between two attractive axioms. I've come to suspect the two axioms are not symmetric in their normative standing. Independence of irrelevant alternatives earns its plausibility in expected-utility and social-choice settings, where it functions as a consistency constraint on preferences; in bargaining it is asked to do something quite different, namely to make the selected division insensitive to the presence of alternatives other than the one selected, even when those alternatives would have altered the parties' relative leverage. Individual monotonicity, by contrast, is often dismissed as an incentive-driven technical requirement, but it also encodes something I take to be normatively central: that a party's share should not shrink when the feasible set expands in a way that raises only that party's best attainable payoff while leaving the other party's best attainable payoff where it was.

The essay should defend a verdict on which axiom set provides the better foundation for normative bargaining theory, and it should test that verdict against what I take to be the hardest case for any clean answer: a hybrid position that accepts independence of irrelevant alternatives as a constraint on contractions of the feasible set, while accepting individual monotonicity as a constraint on expansions, especially expansions that enlarge one party's maximum attainable payoff without reducing the other's. If such a hybrid can be made coherent, it undercuts the standard framing of the trade-off; if it cannot, I want to know precisely where it breaks, why, and whether the break indicts the hybrid or instead gives us reason to reject one of the two axioms outright. Use a concrete case to keep the argument honest. Two labs in a research consortium are dividing the proceeds of joint work, with a fixed disagreement point representing what each would receive from its own independent funding if they fail to agree. One lab can unilaterally purchase equipment that raises the consortium's joint output ceiling, and the other lab cannot. Work out what each solution concept implies about the investing lab's ex ante incentive to make that purchase, whether the non-investing lab has grounds for complaint under either rule, and whether the answer changes if the investment is costly enough that the investing lab is worse off ex ante under one rule than under the other. I would also like you to address whether the disagreement point should enter the solution only through the ideal point, as it does on the Kalai–Smorodinsky picture, or whether a richer dependence on the disagreement point is defensible.

Please don't pad the essay with recitations of the characterizations or with gestures at how each axiom has intuitive appeal. State the axioms briefly in your own words, then spend the bulk of the essay on the substantive normative argument. Reach a definite conclusion — including, if you think the argument warrants it, the conclusion that one of the two standard solutions should be abandoned rather than supplemented — and be explicit about the strongest objection to your own position and why it fails.
