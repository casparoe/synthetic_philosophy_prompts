Right, so I've got a slot at our department's research seminar in a fortnight and I've stupidly agreed to give the talk I've been threatening to give for years. It's a psychiatry department — mostly clinicians, the kind who run the ECT clinic and are quietly proud that their field is a science; there's a couple who lean phenomenological/critical, one philosopher of medicine who sits in the back, and a service-user rep who comes to the research committee meetings and has heard it all before. My thesis: that the disease model of mental illness doesn't do the normative work everyone assumes it does. I'm not Szasz — I don't think 'mental illness' is a myth to be abolished — but I do want to argue that calling something a brain disorder settles almost nothing about what we're allowed to do to the person who has it, and in particular it can't settle the question of involuntary treatment, because the move from 'her brain is disordered' to 'she must be treated whether she likes it or not' always smuggles in a value judgement about what a life worth living looks like. I've been dipping into the values-based-practice stuff and the older arguments about illness being a value-laden concept, and I want to lean on a distinction I've started drawing myself between what I call the soft and the hard effects of a diagnosis — soft being the way a label changes how you're seen and how you see yourself, hard being the way it changes what can legally be done to you. My claim is that the disease model earns its keep by promising to justify the hard effects, and it never quite delivers.
