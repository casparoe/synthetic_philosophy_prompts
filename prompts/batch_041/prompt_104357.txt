I spent my working life at the bench — my own field was immunology — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with more appetite than training. I have been asked to contribute a single essay question to the final examination of an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of biology and cognitive science, and I would like you to draft it for me, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The set topic is what has sometimes been called a strange inversion of reasoning — the idea, usually associated with Dennett's readings of Darwin and Turing, that a system can display competence without comprehension: it can do the right thing, reliably and even adaptively, with nothing inside it understanding why. The two standard illustrations are Darwin's inversion, in which blind variation and selection yield the appearance of design with no designer, and Turing's, in which a machine computes or plays chess with no inner grasp of what it is doing.

My worry is that my students will fill pages with fluent summaries and thereby reveal nothing. I want a question that a recitation cannot answer. To that end I would like it built around a third case from my old field: the adaptive immune system, where clonal selection and affinity maturation produce something that behaves like recognition — the system adapts to the pathogens it encounters — with no recogniser and no mind. My hunch is that this case is instructive precisely because it sits awkwardly between the two canonical ones, and that a good student must say where it sits and why.

Please provide three things. First, the examination question itself, to be answered in ninety minutes. Second, a model answer of roughly eight hundred words. Third, a marking scheme organised around five must-hit points worth four marks each, twenty in total, where each point names the specific move a top answer has to make and is followed by a one-line note on the mistake that typically forfeits that point.

A few constraints. The question must be answerable with no sources at hand, so its stem should state the two inversions in its own words rather than sending students off to a reading; do not require or reward citations, and do not rest the question on any particular quotation or page reference. I care far less that students can attach the right name to the right idea than that they can say what each inversion actually claims. Above all, I want the question to force them to decide whether the two inversions are one argument or two — in particular, whether Darwin's inversion is a claim about where a system's competence came from while Turing's is a claim about how a system runs — and whether the immune-system case shows that these two things can be pulled apart. If it does, I want the question to make them say what that does to the slogan; if it does not, I want them to say why not.
