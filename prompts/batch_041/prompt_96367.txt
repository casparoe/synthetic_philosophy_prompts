I sit on a municipal water-quality advisory board, and at the end of our last meeting two of the other members, Helena and Marcus, had an argument that ran well past the vote. I have to write up the minority report, and what I want from you is a careful adjudication of the argument itself rather than a recommendation about the policy.

The decision concerns the lining compound used in the city's distribution pipes. Three options are on the table: (1) keep the compound that has been in use for forty years, which has been studied extensively, with results that are suggestive but inconclusive of a small elevation in one rare cancer; (2) switch to a newer compound that removes that byproduct altogether, but whose own breakdown products have been tested only in animals, at doses far above any realistic human exposure, where they produced ambiguous effects on hormone regulation; or (3) adopt a third technology with no known toxicity concerns at all, which costs roughly three times as much and would push back by about a decade the program that is replacing the city's remaining lead service lines.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Helena: the precautionary principle, which several board members invoked to justify a switch, cannot be a decision rule at all, because every option carries uncertain risks — the new compound has unstudied breakdown products, the old one has a cancer signal, and doing nothing leaves the lead lines in the ground. A principle that can be cited in favor of every option either gives no guidance or ratifies whatever the decision-maker already wanted. And if precaution is a genuine requirement, it ought to apply to itself: we do not know the downstream effects of an expensive precautionary regulation, so by its own standard we should be cautious about adopting one.

(2) Marcus: this attacks a caricature. The principle is not a rule for ranking risks; it is a rule about the default and the burden of proof. Whoever proposes to introduce something new and hard to undo must show that it is safe, rather than opponents having to show that it is harmful. The asymmetry is not arbitrary: keeping the old compound preserves our option to switch later, whereas exposure to a new compound is body-wide and cumulative and may not be undoable. He added that the status quo's risks have at least been measured, while the new compound's have not.

(3) Helena: that gets the epistemology backwards. The old compound's risks look measured only because we have studied it for forty years; familiarity is not evidence of safety, and had the compounds been introduced in the opposite order we would be saying the same thing about the other one. As for the burden of proof, it relocates the problem rather than solving it: if someone proposes option (3), who bears the burden of proving that delaying the lead-line program is safe?

(4) Marcus: he conceded that reversibility comes in degrees and that a track record is not itself evidence of safety, but held that reversibility is still the right axis. He said the principle should be read as a weighting rule: below some threshold of evidence, uncertain harms that would be widely distributed and difficult to undo should be weighted more heavily than uncertain harms that are localized and remediable. A principle can be useful, he said, without being an algorithm.

(5) Helena: a standard of proof does not by itself select an action; it yields a choice only once you have a prior about which errors are worse, so the asymmetry Marcus wants has been smuggled back in rather than justified. She added that his weighting rule, applied consistently, would favor keeping the lead lines in place, since lead exposure in children is about as irreversible a harm as we have. On her reading the principle is either a substantive claim in need of independent argument or a rhetorical device that lets whoever invokes it claim the mantle of caution while painting opponents as reckless.

(6) Marcus: he said the last point may show that the principle cuts against his own preference in this case, but that this is a feature rather than an embarrassment — a principle that could be relied on to deliver the answer you wanted would be no principle at all.

For what it is worth, Marcus ended up recommending option (2) and Helena option (3), though Helena said she would have recommended option (2) if the money were not coming out of the lead program.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move, saying for each of (1) through (6) who has the better of it and why. Where the two are talking past each other — for instance, because they are defending different formulations of the principle rather than disagreeing about a single claim — say so, and say which formulation is worth defending. Tell me what each of them is missing, and whether the argument can be assessed without settling which option the board should adopt. If you think it cannot, explain why.
