I've been reading the rationalist blogs since around the Sequences era and I've somehow ended up teaching a small upper-level seminar on value theory for students who mostly come out of that scene — they've all read the dust-specks-vs-torture thread and skimmed the repugnant conclusion wiki, and they think the spectrum-argument stuff is old news. Every draft of my final turns into a question they can answer by reciting the mere-addition paradox and mumbling something about money pumps. I want a question that can't be answered that way, so I made up my own spectrum case and I want the question built around it. Use this one, not the standard population-ethics version:

Take a life of one year lived at the top of the scale, 10/10 the whole way through. Now double the length and knock a hair off the quality: two years at 9.9/10. I think most people, at least up at the top of the scale, will say that's not worse — twice the life for a rounding-error drop. Do it again: four years at 9.8, eight years at 9.7, and so on. Each individual step is 'not worse than' the one before. Keep going long enough and you end up at some astronomical number of years lived at a strongly negative quality, which is plainly worse than the single good year. So something has to give: either transitivity, or one of the little steps, or the endpoint judgment.

Two things I want the question to force, beyond just reconstructing that:

(1) I don't want them getting off the hook with 'well, some step must be false.' Make them say which step and why, and make them notice that any specific cutoff looks arbitrary in the sorites-y way — if step k is where 'not worse' breaks, what's special about k? I want them to actually feel that cost.

(2) The real crux: does this show that betterness *itself* is intransitive, or only that at least one of our pairwise judgments has to be abandoned? And if betterness really is intransitive, does that actually make you exploitable? I want them to have to take a side on whether the money-pump objection is a reductio of intransitive betterness, or a bullet you can bite by driving a wedge between what's true of value and what's true of choice — i.e. whether an agent with cyclic betterness judgments is necessarily pumpable, or only if her *choices* track them in the right (wrong?) way. They should have to commit, and defend it. I definately want them to seperate those two questions instead of running them together.

Format: write me (a) the essay question itself, pitched so a strong student could do it in 45-60 minutes; (b) a model answer of about 650 words; (c) a marking scheme built on five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, with one line per point on how students typically blow it. It should be unanswerable by reciting Parfit or Temkin — the case above is what they have to work with. No citations or reading lists obviously, they can't look anything up.
