I run the eligibility office for a county rental-assistance program, and I need help diagnosing a problem that has been getting worse for two years.

Our determinations are made by a software system assembled out of explicit rules: each rule states that if certain facts hold — household income below a threshold, a verified lease, a disability designation, and so on — then a client is eligible for a particular benefit amount. The rules have accumulated for eleven years, written by different people for different purposes. Here is the pattern that worries me. Whenever we add a rule to handle a new kind of case, cases that previously came out right come out wrong. Last month we added a rule that recalculates household income when a client reports starting a new job. Afterward, the system began treating some of those clients as having no verified lease on file, even though nothing about their leases had changed; the new rule had, in effect, stopped the earlier facts from being carried forward. We patched it. Two weeks later, a different patch broke a different rule. The system does not forget the way a person forgets; it simply stops carrying forward anything that no one explicitly instructed it to carry forward.

Four people have told me what the real problem is, and I have to make a recommendation to my director on Monday.

My director says the rules are merely incomplete: fund a year of work to enumerate every exception and every fact that remains true when something changes, so that the system never has to rely on assumptions.

The contractor we consulted says we should stop writing rules by hand, train a model on eleven years of our decisions, and let it learn the patterns; we would not have to say what stays the same, because the model would infer it.

Our auditor says the problem is that we keep changing the rules, and that the fix is to freeze them and require board approval for any change.

A caseworker who has been here longer than the software says the rulebook is not the issue. The problem, she says, is that we treat a case as finished once the system has computed it; if the system re-checked the record from scratch whenever anything changed, none of this would happen.

I am not looking for a vendor recommendation. I need to understand the shape of the problem well enough to know what I would be buying, and which of these four diagnoses names the underlying limitation rather than merely relocating it.

Please answer in two parts.

First, a short analysis (no more than 350 words) that states, in plain terms, what the underlying limitation actually is and why it arises. If this limitation has a standard name, give me the name; I do not need citations or further reading.

Second, a table with exactly these five columns and one row for each of the four proposals above, in the order I gave them:

- Proposal: who is proposing it, in a few words.
- Diagnosis: what that proposal assumes the underlying problem to be, in one sentence.
- What it would genuinely fix: the part of the failure pattern it actually addresses.
- What it leaves broken: what would still go wrong if we adopted it, and why.
- Verdict: exactly one of Adopt, Adopt with changes, Reject.

End with a single sentence I can read aloud to my director that states what the real problem is.
