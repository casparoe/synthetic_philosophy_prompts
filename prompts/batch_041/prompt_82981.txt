Bit of an odd one — I'm an ethicist by trade, but I've got myself into a running arguement with a colleague in the physics department about string theory and the multiverse, and I've realised I'm no longer sure I understand the position I'm arguing against. His line is that string theory is in decent shape even though, on his own account, it hasn't produced a prediction anyone could go and test, and when I asked him what on earth that could mean he pointed me at a body of work in the philosophy of physics on what people there apparently call non-empirical confirmation. As best I can make it out, the two main moves are these: one says that the bare fact that nobody in forty-odd years has managed to build a serious rival framework counts in the theory's favour, and the other says that the way the thing hangs together, plus the fact that it turns out to explain a lot of things we already knew without having been built to do that, is itself further evidence for it. Now, my instinct is that this is a contradiction in terms. Confirmation is a relation between a theory and evidence; if there's no evidence in play — and by hypothesis there isn't, since that's what non-empirical is supposed to mean — then there is nothing to do the confirming, and what's actually on offer is aesthetic appeal plus an argument from ignorance dressed up in probabilistic notation. So I'd like someone to tell me straight: what is the claim actually supposed to be, does it hold up, and if I'm trying to work out whether the multiverse picture is true, can any of this get me an answer, or does it only ever tell me which theory I'm permitted to feel good about?

Please be decisive about it — take a position and defend it, rather than surveying who says what and leaving me to adjudicate. And lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no throat-clearing and no restating of what I've just said. My colleague is a serious person, so I'd be dissapointed if the answer turned out to be that the whole programme is just physicists dressing up their taste in Bayesian vocabulary.
