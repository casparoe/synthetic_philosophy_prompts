For a biology-and-philosophy project I have been reading a popular science essay that uses the digger wasp Sphex to make a point about human freedom. As the essay tells it, the wasp provisions her burrow with a paralysed cricket and, before dragging the cricket inside, always goes in alone first to inspect the nest; if the cricket is nudged a few inches away while she is inside, she finds it, drags it back to the entrance, and — because her routine has been reset — inspects the nest all over again, repeating the loop rather than simply carrying the cricket in. The essay concludes that this is what a mindless fixed program looks like, and that since our own deliberation can equally be described as a step-by-step routine, the difference between the wasp and us is only one of degree, so our sense of freely choosing is an illusion. I am not convinced the analogy is doing the work the essay claims, and I would like you to audit it.

Please lay out the correspondences the analogy relies on (what is meant to map onto what), then say where the mapping breaks — including whether the wasp's loop is really as mindless as it is made out to be, and whether 'a describable routine' amounts to the same thing as 'a program that leaves no room for choice' — then identify what the analogy quietly imports from the wasp case that the human case may not actually contain, and finally give your verdict on whether the anti-free-will conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. I have also read that the experiment as usually recounted may be somewhat exaggerated, so please take that into account.
