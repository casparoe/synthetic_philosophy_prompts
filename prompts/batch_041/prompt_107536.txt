I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of a decade now, and the Kelly criterion keeps coming back around, usually bundled with a cluster of claims its fans call geometric rationality: maximize the geometric mean of your wealth rather than its arithmetic mean, size positions by the Kelly fraction, never take a bet that risks ruin, and generally treat growth rate rather than expected value as the quantity to optimize. I've read enough of the pushback to notice that it comes in two flavors. The deflationary one says that maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth simply is expected utility maximization with one particular utility function, so there is no separate theory on offer at all. The substantive one says that the growth-rate argument rests on a fact about long-run time averages — that along a single realized trajectory of a multiplicative process, what converges is the average of the log, not the log of the average — and that this fact, not any preference for logs, is what does the real work. I want you to write the strongest case you can for a specific position in this dispute: that geometric rationality is not a decision theory at all but a theorem about a restricted class of decision problems, and that treating it as a general criterion of rational choice is a mistake — even though the theorem, properly scoped, is genuine and important.

Take the role of a decision theorist who has thought carefully about both the mathematics and the philosophy here, and who is writing for a reader who already knows the basics and wants the argument rather than a survey. Begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you state the strongest version of the position you are attacking — the version that says growth-rate reasoning is a genuinely distinct and generally applicable norm — and then commit to your thesis in a sentence or two before defending it. Your argument should engage the mechanics rather than trade slogans. Be explicit about which premises the growth-rate argument actually needs (repetition across many independent trials, multiplicative dynamics, no intermediate consumption, wealth as the thing you ultimately care about), and say what happens to the argument as each premise is dropped. In particular, work through the one-shot bet you will never get to repeat; the case where what is at stake is not wealth and the ruin you want to avoid is not a wealth level; the gap between the ensemble average of wealth across possible worlds and the time average along the single trajectory you will actually live; and the question of whether "don't risk ruin" is a theorem, a heuristic that approximates one, or a preference smuggled in under mathematical dress. It would also be worth saying something about why practitioners so often bet a fraction of what the criterion recommends, and what that tells us about whether the criterion is a theorem or a preference.

Argue the position forcefully; I want to finish the piece knowing exactly what you think geometric rationality is, what it is entitled to claim, and which of its popular prescriptions survive contact with the cases where its premises fail. No citations, no reading list, no summary of who has said what — a model answering this has no library to hand, and I care about the argument, not its provenance, so build everything from first principles and from cases you construct yourself. If you think the position collapses at some particular point, say precisely where and why rather than hedging the whole thing into mush. Around a thousand words, in continuous prose rather than bullet points, and think carefully before you start writing.
