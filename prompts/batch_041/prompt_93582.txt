I'm in a reading group on Marx and twentieth-century labour history, and there's an episode I keep thinking our seminar mishandles. In January 1976 the shop stewards at Lucas Aerospace, organised as a combine committee spanning the company's plants, published an alternative corporate plan in response to management's announcement that thousands of jobs would be cut in the name of restructuring. Instead of accepting the redundancies, the stewards surveyed their own members, harvested proposals from the shop floor, and produced over a hundred and fifty designs for alternative products — wind turbines, heat pumps, hybrid vehicle engines, energy-efficient housing — together with market analyses and retraining schemes. The plan also proposed reorganising production into teams that would pair the tacit, hands-on knowledge of the shop floor with the theoretical engineering of the design staff. Management was hostile, the plan was never taken up, and the company went on restructuring regardless.

What I want is to use this as a test case for Marx's account of machinery, rather than as an occasion for admiration. The account I have in mind is the machinery chapter of Capital, where machinery is treated not as a neutral instrument but as a form of capital — introduced to extract relative surplus value, to devalue labour-power, to make the worker an appendage of the mechanism — together with the Grundrisse's discussion of fixed capital, in which general social knowledge becomes a direct force of production and the worker appears as the accessory of the automatic system. The Lucas stewards seem to have taken the opposite line: not that technology is the enemy, but that the workforce's own knowledge could be redirected toward need. So run the mapping in both directions. What does Marx's account predict and explain here — the form the redundancies took, the form the plan took, its defeat? And where does the episode strain the account rather than confirm it? I want you to press especially on two things. First, whether the plan's fate vindicates the thesis that the direction of technology is fixed by property relations rather than by technical feasibility — the designs were buildable, and what stopped them was control — or whether it exposes a gap in the framework, since Marx tends to treat science and skill as capital's instruments and has little to say about workers as bearers of technical knowledge, whereas the stewards' whole method was to treat the workforce as a research capacity. Second, whether the plan's failure is an anomaly for any expectation that the producers' interest and the general interest converge, given that the stewards were defending jobs and craft status as much as they were proposing socially useful production.

Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
