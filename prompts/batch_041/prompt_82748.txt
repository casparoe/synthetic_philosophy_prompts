I did thirty-odd years as a soil scientist before I retired, and since then I've been reading philosophy — mostly the philosophy of science end, which I'd somehow assumed was all sorted out by now. The local skeptics group has asked me to run next month's session: thirty-five minutes, adults who know a bit of science and no philosophy, and the topic I've been handed is how you tell science from psuedoscience. The obvious answer is falsifiability, I know that's Popper's, and I'm fairly sure most of the room walks in believing it, so I want to spend the session taking it apart rather than selling it. Plan it for me rather than explaining it. What has to be in place before they can even see why the obvious answer fails — my hunch is that the first thing is that nothing ever gets tested on its own, only along with a pile of auxiliary assumptions, but say so if that comes too early or too late. What single example should carry the whole thing, and what does it need? Ideally one where the same formal move — save the theory by patching something else — is a fudge in one case and good science in the other, and I'd rather it wasn't a famous case I'd get wrong in front of them. The bit I can't work out how to fit in is that you can make almost anything falsifiable by bolting a testable claim onto it and then blaming the bolt-on when the test fails. Which misconceptions should I expect, and where exactly do I head each one off (my guesses: falsifiable means false; Popper said one bad result kills a theory; unfalsifiable just means meaningless)? One question to ask at the end that tells me whether they've actually got it, and what I should openly refuse to get into at this level. Please don't make it a parade of philosopher names.
