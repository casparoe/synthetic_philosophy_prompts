A colleague in my decision theory reading group has spent the better part of two terms pressing an analogy on us, and I have not been able to decide whether it survives scrutiny. I would like you to audit it.

Her subject is program equilibrium. As she sets it up: instead of choosing an action, each player in a one-shot game submits a program; the programs are then run against one another, and each is permitted to read the other's source code before producing its output. A program equilibrium is a profile of programs from which neither player could gain by submitting a different program, given the other's. Her standard illustration is the one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma. Consider the program that outputs Cooperate if its opponent's source is identical to its own and Defects otherwise. Two copies of it cooperate with one another, and neither player can improve on submitting it, since any departure from it against that opponent yields the punishment payoff at best. So mutual cooperation is achievable in a single play of a game whose only ordinary Nash equilibrium is mutual defection. She sometimes puts the same point in folk terms, calling the strategy the golden rule written in code.

From this she draws an analogy to Kant, and it is the analogy, not the game theory, that I want examined. On her telling, submitting a program is submitting a maxim; the demand that the program be one that fares well against its own kind is the demand that the maxim be universalizable; the programs' mutual access to one another's source is the mutual recognition of rational agents; and the fact that the cooperative program is an equilibrium is the sense in which acting only on universalizable maxims is rational. She leans on the analogy for a conclusion she cares about: that rational agents can reach cooperation without any external enforcement, and that the categorical imperative is at bottom a piece of strategic rationality rather than a separate and sui generis moral demand. I am less interested in whether she has the game theory right than in what the analogy can bear.

I would like five things. First, a correspondence table: set out explicitly what is supposed to map onto what — maxim, universalizability, rational agency, mutual recognition, the equilibrium condition, the categorical ought, and any other relatum you find — and mark which of these the analogy genuinely supplies and which it merely gestures at. Second, the disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for her conclusion and those that do not; in particular, tell me what work the transparency of source code is doing, and what becomes of the Kantian reading in a variant where the programs cannot read one another. Third, the features of the source that the analogy quietly imports into the target. I suspect the equilibrium condition drags in a notion of best response, and so of self-interest, that the categorical imperative is precisely meant to be independent of, but I want this made precise rather than left as a suspicion. Fourth, a verdict: does her conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it all along? Fifth, if the analogy is defective, what would serve her point better — or is there no analogy to be had here?

Write for someone fluent in the philosophical vocabulary but not a game theorist, and do not trouble yourself with references; I want the argument, not a bibliography.
