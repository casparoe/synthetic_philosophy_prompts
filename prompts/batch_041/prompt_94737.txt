I've been participating in a reading group on the philosophy of computation, and a disagreement has arisen that I have not been able to resolve on my own. One participant draws a sharp line between two ways in which a problem can lie beyond our reach. The first is the way the halting problem lies beyond us: no procedure of any kind, however clever, and however much time it is granted, can decide it. The second is the way a problem such as finding an optimal tour in the travelling salesman problem lies beyond us: a procedure plainly exists, but every known one requires a superpolynomial number of steps in the worst case, so that the problem defeats us only in the sense that we are unwilling to wait. From this he concludes that the second kind of limit is merely practical while the first is metaphysical — that complexity theory can describe the reach of our algorithms but tells us nothing about the world itself, whereas computability theory marks a genuine boundary of the knowable.

Please explain the conceptual issue at stake here, as carefully as you can, for someone who is comfortable with the basic definitions of P, NP, and the halting problem but has not thought much about what they mean philosophically. In particular, I would like you to probe whether the proposed distinction between practical and metaphysical limits survives scrutiny. Some questions strike me as relevant, though you should not feel bound by them: Is the boundary of the feasible a fact about the world, a fact about our hardware, or an artifact of an arbitrary decision to treat polynomial time as the mark of efficiency? Does it matter that worst-case hardness may coexist with easy average-case behavior, or that the asymptotic idealization sets aside constant factors and finite inputs altogether? And is there a sense in which the undecidability of the halting problem is itself only a theorem relative to a fixed model of computation, in which case the contrast may be less clean than it first appears? Please give me the strongest version of the participant's position, then the strongest objections to it, and close by saying where you think the line — if there is one — ought to be drawn.
