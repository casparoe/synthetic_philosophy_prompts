I'm building a little collection of parody pieces and I need you to write one. Here's the setup: the venue is the comment page of a financial paper, and the author is a monetary reformer who has read Berkeley's Querist — the 1735 pamphlet that is nothing but a numbered list of questions about Ireland's economy — and has absorbed it not as a historical curiosity but as a live policy program. So write his submission as a formal response to a modern central bank's public consultation on the future of money, and write the whole thing as a numbered list of questions, Berkeley-style. Maybe 600 words, 25-35 queries.

The comedy has to come from the economics being right while the conclusion is unhinged. He's correct that money has no intrinsic value and matters only so far as it stirs up industry; correct that what counts is the circulation of credit, whatever marks or tokens carry it; he can even note, as Berkeley does, that the true idea of money may be "altogether that of a ticket or counter," and that gold, silver and paper are just counters for reckoning and transferring. Push from those premises, one query at a time, toward: dropping any metallic anchor as a bookkeeping formality, then accepting any token whatever, then the reductio — since wealth tracks industry and industry tracks wants, the bank should be in the business of manufacturing wants directly, on the grounds that "the creating of wants be not the likeliest way to produce industry in a people." By the end it should read as a proposal to run monetary policy as a demand-generation machine. Every step should follow from the last; the reader should recoil while seeing exactly how they got there.

Stay deadpan — no winking, no nudging the reader that this is a joke, and no modern econ jargon the character wouldn't reach for. Let him think he's being original, with maybe one query that dismisses "a certain Irish bishop" who apparently said something similar. Someone who knows the Querist and the national-bank scheme should laugh at how faithful it is; someone who doesn't should still find it a funny bureaucratic document.

Last line: a one-sentence summary starting "Pitch:".
