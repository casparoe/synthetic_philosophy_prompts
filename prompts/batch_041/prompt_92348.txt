An invented mid-sized logistics firm has adopted Galen Strawson's Basic Argument as official HR policy: people act as they do because of the way they are, they cannot be ultimately responsible for the way they are, and so no one is ever deserving in the basic-desert sense — blameworthy or praiseworthy just because of what they did, independent of any forward-looking reason. Strawson's point that the argument is neutral about whether determinism is true is to be respected, not quietly converted into hard determinism. Write the internal memo rolling the policy out: roughly 700 words, deadpan corporate register, with headings, invented names, and one worked example of a performance review rewritten under the new rules.

The humour must come from the argument rather than from generic corporate absurdity, and the author must read as someone who has genuinely followed the regress. The sanctions the firm retains — warnings, demotion, dismissal — have to be re-justified on non-desert grounds such as deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation while remaining recognizably the same sanctions, and the memo must face the standard objection that the policy is unlivable, since no one, the author included, can stop responding to a colleague as though something were deserved. No sentence may treat anyone as deserving, earning, or being owed anything as a matter of desert, and the strain of that constraint should show in the prose rather than be announced in it. The target is the argument's supposed practical upshot, not Strawson as a person; by the end, a reader should be genuinely unsure whether the firm has changed anything but its vocabulary.
