I'm putting together a reading-group session on the separateness of persons — the complaint that a certain style of moral theory treats the gains and losses of different people as though they belonged to a single life, to be traded off against one another the way one might trade off a hardship now for a benefit later. I want to work from the other end: not from any particular theory, but from a specification of what a theory would have to deliver in order to answer that complaint rather than dodge it. Write me that specification. What must any adequate account of interpersonal aggregation — of when and how the claims of distinct people may be weighed against one another — be able to do? For each requirement, give the case it is meant to capture (the one person who would be sacrificed so that several others are saved; the small burden imposed on many to spare one person a great loss; the possibility of a being whose gains outweigh everyone else's, however slight the gains and however great the losses they offset; the asymmetry between harming someone and merely failing to benefit her; the difference between compensating a person and producing an offsetting benefit somewhere else entirely), say why it is a requirement rather than a preference, and say what it rules out. Keep in view the distinctions the account must not collapse — between persons and the successive time-slices of a single person, between a person's being used and her merely being affected, between what aggregation may do and what it must do, between a claim's being outweighed and its being overridden — and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of one camp dressed up as constraints on everyone.

Then tell me which pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and which single requirement does the most work in narrowing the field. Close with a brief and decisive assessment of how the leading families — classical and average utilitarianism, prioritarianism, a Rawlsian or contractualist approach, and the more recent proposals that try to build the separateness of persons directly into the aggregation rule — fare against your list, naming for each the requirement it most clearly fails. I would rather have a committed verdict, with reasons, than a neutral survey of options; and if you think the objection cannot be turned into a specification that is neutral between theories at all, because any list of requirements already presupposes a substantive position, then say so and defend that claim instead of manufacturing a list you don't believe in. Please write in American English, and don't worry about citations — I'll add those myself.
