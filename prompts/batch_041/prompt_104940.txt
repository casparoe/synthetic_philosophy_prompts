I'm putting together a session for a history-of-ideas reading group on Newton's theology, and I want to end it with a bit of a provocation: what would Newton himself have made of a couple of questions that only turned up well after he died? I'd like you to actually reason it out rather than give me the usual "Newton was devout and also a heretic" line.

Here's the stuff you should treat as fixed, because it's what makes his position distinctive: he was a non-Trinitarian in the Arian direction -- Christ as a subordinate, created mediator rather than co-equal -- and he kept this private his whole life, refusing the sacrament when he was dying; he didn't believe in an immortal soul; he read the Bible literally where it suited him and treated prophecy as an encoded, decodable history of the church's corruption; against Leibniz he insisted that God doesn't just wind the world up and leave it, but sustains it and constantly intervenes in it; and he also worked out a chronology of the ancient world that squeezed creation into only a few thousand years before his own day. Take all that as the base.

Now the two later questions. First: Darwinian evolution and the deep age of the Earth -- in particular, animal suffering and extinction stretching back long before any human being existed. Second: the modern discovery that other stars have planets, and the live debate about rational life elsewhere -- and if there is such life, whether it needs its own redemption, given his Christology. Worth noting that he'd already written that if the fixed stars are centres of other like systems, they must all be subject to the dominion of the one God, so don't pretend the plurality question would have been news to him.

And here's the twist I actually want you to grapple with, because it's a modern arguement Newton never heard: someone now claims that if God is immediately present to every particle at every moment, sustaining it, then creation is already shot through with the divine in a way that makes one provincial incarnation on one planet look parochial, so the Incarnation can't be the unique centre of the story. Would Newton buy that, or would it strike him as exactly the kind of thing he spent his life arguing against?

Give me: your best reconstruction of his answers to both questions; how confident you are in each and why; where his own commitments pull against each other (I suspect the chronology and the Christology might); and one thing he'd say that would annoy both modern conservative and modern liberal Christians. Also tell me what single discovery, had he lived to see it, would have shaken him most -- and be upfront about which parts of this are reconstruction rather than anything he actually left on record. No need for sources or reading lists, just the reasoning.
