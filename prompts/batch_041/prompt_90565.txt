I am a second-year PhD student working on model interpretability, and I would like you to adjudicate an argument that broke out between two colleagues in my lab. I was present but said little, and I have deliberately not told you which side I lean toward.

Some background, so the dispute is intelligible. Our group works on what is now often called mechanistic anomaly detection. The idea is to judge a model not only by its outputs but by its internals: we want to flag cases in which the computation actually being performed is not the one the model's behaviour would lead us to expect. The motivating scenario is a model that behaves impeccably in testing but whose internal computation suggests it is doing something else, so that good behaviour cannot be taken as evidence that the underlying process is the benign one. The whole programme presupposes that we can say when the internal computation is the same as the one we expected and when it is not, and this is where my colleagues came apart.

The concrete case that started it: we had a small transformer that learned a shortcut on a synthetic arithmetic task. Call the original computation the reference mechanism; we could describe it as a handful of attention heads and the manner in which they feed the output. We then fine-tuned the model on a different data mixture. Its accuracy on the original task was, so far as we can measure, unchanged: the same answers, with the same distribution of confidence. But when we repeated our analysis, the old circuit did not reappear. A different set of components now appears to be producing the answers.

Priya, who came to this area from neuroscience, and Marcus, who came from formal verification, argued about whether the fine-tuned model is performing the same mechanism.

Priya: The mechanism is the computation, and the computation is what the parts do to one another. If the fine-tuned model reaches the same answers through a different pattern of internal dependence, then it is a different mechanism, and that is the end of it. This is the entire reason for working at the level of internals rather than behaviour. If behavioural sameness were enough to make two things the same mechanism, anomaly detection would be dead on arrival, because the anomalies we care about are precisely the ones that preserve behaviour.

Marcus: I will grant that different internals can amount to a different mechanism. My worry runs the other way: your criterion makes "different mechanism" far too cheap. Any two networks can be re-described so that their parts come out different. Partition the same weights at a finer grain, or stipulate a different correspondence between the units of the two models, and the dependence pattern changes and your verdict flips. So "same mechanism" is not a fact about the models; it is a fact about how we chose to carve them up. Until you tell me what fixes the carving, "different mechanism" means "different under some description," which is always true and therefore tells us nothing.

Priya: The carving is not free. It is fixed by what we can intervene on. A part is a part if perturbing it makes a difference to other parts or to the output. Identity of mechanism is identity of the pattern of counterfactual dependence among such parts. That is an objective criterion; it simply defines identity in terms of causal structure rather than in terms of the labels we happen to find convenient.

Marcus: But counterfactual dependence is still dependence relative to a partition. Whether ablating a given head "changes the computation" is only well defined once you have already decided that this head is a unit and that the remaining heads are not silently taking over its role. You have pushed the arbitrariness down a level rather than removing it. And there is a second gap: your criterion compares mechanism-tokens — this model's computation against that model's computation. The safety claim we actually want is about mechanism-types: that this model is doing the deceptive thing rather than the honest thing. That claim cannot be read off the causal profile of either model on its own, because a single profile can be realised by many algorithms and a single algorithm can yield many profiles.

Priya: The type-token gap is real but not fatal. We do not need the types fixed in advance; we need a similarity metric over causal profiles, and then "same mechanism" is a matter of degree and of threshold rather than a binary. Anomaly detection requires only that the threshold be stable enough to be useful. Compare "same height": it is relative to a unit and a tolerance, and it remains a fact about people.

Marcus: Then everything turns on where the threshold is set, and the threshold is set by how much we mind false alarms — a practical choice, not a discovery. That may be the honest position, but notice what follows. On that view, "the model is doing something other than what it appears to be doing" is not something the model is guilty of; it is something we have stipulated. So I want to know which it is: is Priya's threshold a discovery about the model, or a decision by us?

Please adjudicate move by move. Rate each of the six moves above from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating briefly. Then tell me which of the two, if either, is right about the concrete case — whether the fine-tuned model is performing the same mechanism — and be explicit if you think that is the wrong question to have asked. Finally, say what both of them are missing, and who is more right overall.
