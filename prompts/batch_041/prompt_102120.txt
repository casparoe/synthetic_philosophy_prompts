I have been following the decision-theory threads on the rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years, and I want help understanding why the argument between the causalists and the updateless camp has stopped generating anything. Both sides have their full repertoire on the table and neither has moved in a decade; the exchange has become ritual.

Here is how I would state the two positions, and I want the diagnosis to engage these specifically rather than a generic summary. The causalist camp holds that the only thing that can make a choice better is its causal influence on outcomes, that a decision theory is a rule for selecting an act given a causal model of the situation and a utility function, and that every case trotted out against it -- Newcomb, the smoking lesion, counterfactual mugging, Parfit's hitchhiker -- is handled by getting the causal structure right. Where a case seems to favor updatelessness, the causalist says the intuition rests on confusing deciding with predicting, or on smuggling in a causal path (for instance, the predictor having read your dispositions before you chose). The updateless camp holds that a decision theory must say what to do when your choice is logically entangled with things you cannot causally affect; that the causalist's insistence on causal graphs is a refusal to take logical uncertainty and self-reference seriously; and that the mugging-type cases are decisive data, with the causalist's re-description of them as covertly causal being question-begging.

My hypothesis, which I would like tested rather than assumed, is that this is not a substantive disagreement about the right action in any case. It is a disagreement about what a decision theory is a theory of. The causalist treats it as a theory of rational choice given a fixed representation of the world and one's influence on it; the updateless camp treats it as a theory of how an agent should be constituted -- a policy, a fixed point, something that can be committed to in advance -- so that the question what to do now is downstream of the question what kind of agent should exist. Under the first reading the cases are predictions to be matched; under the second they are constraints on constitution. If that is right, then each side's case-based arguments are data only relative to its own conception, and no case can ever move anyone.

One further consideration I would like you to weigh. Consider a case where the agent is a deterministic program whose source a predictor has read before it runs, so the correlation between the predictor's output and the agent's choice is mediated by an ordinary causal path into the past. I expect both camps to agree on the action here. If agreement on the action coexists with disagreement on the reason, does that support the different-questions diagnosis, or does it instead reveal a hidden formal question underneath -- for example, whether any decision rule can be reflectively consistent, or whether the updateless prescription is even well-defined once logical uncertainty is in the picture?

Give me the diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on which side is right, with the considerations on each side that support or undermine it. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis -- a distinction, a formal result, a change of question -- and say honestly whether you think any such thing is available, or whether the impasse should simply be expected to persist.
