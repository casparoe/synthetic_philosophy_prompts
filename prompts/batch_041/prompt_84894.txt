For most of my career I was a meteorologist — 32 years of putting numbers like 40% next to little cloud icons — and I never once stopped to ask what that number was supposed to be a property of. Now I'm retired, I sit in on a reading group at the local university, and two of the regulars have been at each other for about four months over exactly this. One of them (I'll call him the realist) thinks objective chances are real, irreducible features of a physical setup — propensities sitting there in the apparatus, there whether or not anyone is around to have opinions about them — and that the rule saying your credence in an outcome should equal the chance you know it to have (the 'Principal Principle', if I've got the name right) is a substantive, non-obvious truth that could in principle have been false and that we owe some kind of argument for. The other (the bookkeeper) thinks that's backwards: on her view a chance just is whatever quantity plays that credence-guiding role in the best systematic account of what happens, so the rule isn't a discovery about the world so much as part of what makes something count as a chance in the first place. They've stopped listening to each other, and I've noticed they seem to be answering seperate questions, so I want to see whether a proper adversarial collaboration can be written up. Please draft it: (1) a numbered list of claims both could sign — about what chance-talk is for, about what's agreed to be at stake, about what wouldn't settle anything either way; (2) a disagreements table, one row per remaining difference, with a column for each camp's own formulation written so that camp would accept the wording as theirs; (3) the cruxes, the sub-questions where settling things would actually move one of them, and for each crux the case, argument or experiment both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Two things I'd like the document to take seriously rather than wave at. First, the undermining problem: if the chance facts are fixed by the whole history including the future, then a chance I now know could be undermined by what comes later, and it isn't obvious the deference rule survives that; the realist will want to say the chance was fixed by the setup all along, and I want the bookkeeper's honest reply, not a dodge. Second, the analogy with deferring to an expert: if the rule is just 'defer to whoever knows better', what happens when the expert's information isn't mine — is chance an expert that knows my evidence, or one that knows only the setup? And I'd like them to confront a case where the same event seems to have two legitimate chances at once: a coin whose physical bias is fixed by a micro-detail nobody has measured, so the best anyone can say from the setup is 50/50 while the chance conditional on the full microstate is 0 or 1. Which one does the rule tell me to adopt — and if the honest answer is 'it depends what you mean by chance', say so out loud, because I suspect at least part of those four months has been a fight about a word. If some slice of the dispute turns out to be verbal, name the slice and say what the real residual disagreement is once the word is pinned down.

One more thing: this is for a reading group, not a journal, so I'd rather have your honest calibration then a confident-sounding document. At the end, say how confident you are in the individual agreed-claims — I'd expect some of them to be near-vacuous, and I'd like you to flag the ones you'd stake least on — and single out the one point in the whole thing you're least sure of, whether that's a claim, a crux, or your reading of what one of the two camps would actually accept.
