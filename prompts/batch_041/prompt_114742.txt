I run the editorial desk at a small policy institute. Our house rule is that we only publish claims that are knowable in principle — not claims we can currently prove, just claims that some finite investigator could in principle come to know. A philosopher on our board says Fitch's paradox of knowabilty makes this rule either empty or incoherent: if every truth is knowable, then every truth is known, and the proof turns on a proposition like p is true and nobody knows it. Assume we adopt the fix that knowability is always relative to a particular finite community at a particular time, using methods that could in principle be carried out. I don't want a literature review. I need a procedure my editors can actually run on a submitted claim, maybe 20 a week, with no philosophy training. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because it's a values call or a live philosophical dispute. Then run it on three cases: a claim about the exact number of hairs on Caesar's head at death; a claim that a particular unobserved exoplanet has a certain atmospheric composition; and the claim that this very claim is true but will never be known. Include one where the procedure gives an answer the editors won't like. Be honest about what the procedure can't do.

Format: The procedure (numbered steps, 600-800 words), What each step is doing (short, 300 words), Worked cases (the three above, 500 words), Where it breaks (200 words).
