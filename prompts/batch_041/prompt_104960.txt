Wittgenstein's On Certainty argues that some of our beliefs work as "hinges" — they're exempt from doubt and they form the framework inside of which doubting and knowing even happen. (a) Explain what Wittgenstein means by a hinge proposition, and how it's different from an ordinary empirical belief we merely hold with confidence. (b) Moore held up his hand and claimed that he knew it was a hand. Does Wittgenstein think Moore's claim is a good example of a hinge proposition? Explain. And here's the student's answer:

(a) A hinge proposition is a belief that stands fast for us, one that sits at the bottom of all our thinking and isn't itself up for grabs. Normal empirical beliefs, like "the cat is on the mat" or "water boils at 100 degrees," can in principle be tested and doubted. Hinges can't, because doubting something only makes sense against a background that isn't itself being doubted — if you doubted literally everything at once, doubt would stop meaning anything, it would just be words. So hinges aren't one more thing we know; they're what makes the whole game of knowing and doubting possible in the first place. Wittgenstein's riverbed picture is that our thoughts flow along like a river and the certainties are the bed it flows in; the bed can shift over time but only slowly, and never all at once. So a hinge proposition is basically one of our beliefs that we're maximally certain about, the ones at the very top of the confidence scale.

(b) Moore's move was to hold up his hand and claim to know it was a hand. For Wittgenstein, Moore's claim isn't exactly wrong, it's just a bit odd, because Moore is stating as knowledge something that normally functions as a hinge and isn't normally said out loud at all. I think Wittgenstein basically agrees with Moore here — he's happy to say Moore really does know it's a hand — he just wants to add that it's a special kind of knowledge that can't be doubted, the kind that isn't based on evidence. So Moore gets the certainty right and Wittgenstein just refines what kind of knowledge it is.

(c) The objection is that if hinges are just whatever our community happens to hold fixed, then they're arbitrary, since we could have had completely different ones and there's no neutral standpoint from which to say ours are better. I think Wittgenstein partly accepts this: hinges are tied to our form of life and our practices rather than to evidence, and other communities could have different ones. But "arbitrary" is too strong, because we don't choose our hinges and we can't just swap them out, they're more like the way we act than a decision we made. Where the objection does land is that Wittgenstein doesn't really tell us what to do when two whole frameworks meet and clash — he kind of just says they're different, and that's where the relativism worry comes from.
