I am a retired experimental physicist — forty years spent explaining the bulk behaviour of materials in terms of what their constituents are doing — and I have spent the last three years reading philosophy of social science with more enthusiasm than judgement. I am stuck on the methodological individualism versus holism dispute, and what I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. You are an expert in the philosophy of the social sciences; think carefully before answering, and do not assume the dispute is the familiar one you may have seen summarised.

As I understand it, the individualists (Weber on interpretive sociology, Popper on situational analysis, the Austrian tradition, and the microfoundations programme in economics) hold that social phenomena are ultimately to be explained by the actions, beliefs and dispositions of individual people, and that talk of classes, states, markets or cultures is at best shorthand. The holists (Durkheim on social facts that are to be explained by other social facts, and the more recent literature on emergence and group agents) hold that there are social regularities which cannot be derived from facts about individuals and which do causal work of their own. Both sides seem to grant the same cases — suicide rates, price movements, revolutions, the persistence of institutions — and draw opposite morals from them. I have also read that the quarrel is really three quarrels wearing one name: about what exists, about what explains, and about what our predicates mean; and that the reconciliation is easy once one notices the camps are answering different questions.

My hypothesis, which I would like you to treat as a hypothesis and correct if it is wrong, is that this is not one stalled disagreement but a conflation of an empirical question (are there macro-level regularities that resist micro-derivation?) with a normative one (what does a satisfying explanation look like?). If that is right, a real question is being buried under a verbal one.

Please write a diagnosis in the following sections, with approximately these lengths:

1. "The debate as I have stated it" (~150 words): restate the positions fairly and correct my characterisations where they are wrong or dated.
2. "The theses actually at issue" (~400 words): untangle ontological, explanatory and semantic individualism and the corresponding forms of holism, and say which pairs genuinely conflict and which merely pass each other by.
3. "Diagnosis" (~500 words): say what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, empirical, a difference over standards of explanation, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for — giving reasons drawn from what each side actually says, and address my hypothesis explicitly.
4. "What would count as progress" (~250 words): a distinction, a formal result, an empirical finding, or a change of question — and say honestly whether nothing would.
5. "What my physics is smuggling in" (~150 words): the assumptions I have carried over from a career of reductionist explanation that may be distorting my reading.

Aim for precision over completeness; I would rather have one clean distinction than a survey.
