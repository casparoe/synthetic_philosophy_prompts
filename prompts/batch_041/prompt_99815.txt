I'm a staff writer covering cognitive science, currently at work on a long feature about eliminative materialism — the claim that beliefs and desires are not real states of people at all, but posits of a folk theory that will someday be discarded the way talk of caloric and vital spirits was. The reporting keeps snagging on a version of the view that I find much stranger, and much harder to dismiss, than the headline version. One researcher I've been following argues that mindreading is not an innate module but a cultural technology: that the practice of explaining people by attributing beliefs and desires is something children are taught, like reading or long division, and that it varies across cultures the way counting systems do. If that's right, then 'belief' may be less like 'electron' — a theoretical posit that turned out not to exist — and more like 'the month': a representational device we invented, that genuinely works, that we genuinely learn, and that nonetheless does not describe anything already sitting in the head. I want to get the conceptual structure of that contrast straight before I write a sentence of the piece: whether it weakens the case for eliminativism, strengthens it, or simply relocates the argument; whether a state can be real enough to underwrite reliable prediction across a lifetime while being an artifact of how we talk rather than a state of the person; whether explanatory indispensability is any evidence at all for realism; and what the eliminativist should say about the awkward fact that folk psychology predicts behavior remarkably well, which is, after all, the main reason most people doubt eliminativism to begin with.

Please walk me through the terrain. Assume I can't look anything up and won't be name-dropping anyone — I don't want a literature review or a reading list, I want the argumentative shape of the thing clear in my head, in my own words. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. Make the first a statement of what the strongest version of eliminativism actually claims, and the last a statement of the sharpest thing I should press the researcher on when I interview her.
