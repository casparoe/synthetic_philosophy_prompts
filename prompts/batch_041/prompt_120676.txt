I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for about fifteen years — the long essays, the comment threads, the arguments that run for weeks — and in that time I have noticed that one piece of philosophy keeps coming back: Lewis Carroll's short dialogue for Mind, What the Tortoise Said to Achilles, published in 1895. It is cited as a proof that logic is groundless, as a proof that the sceptic is simply being obtuse, and as a proof that the whole question is confused. I have recently started marking written work for a small course unit on logic and normativity, and I set the exercise below after a session on that dialogue. I would like you to grade one student's answer.

The question, handed out with the dialogue in front of the students, was this:

In Carroll's dialogue the tortoise accepts the two premises A and B, and then — once Achilles has written it into the notebook — accepts as a further premise the hypothetical that if A and B are true then Z must be true, but still declines to accept Z, asking instead for a further hypothetical; the same move then repeats with each added premise. (a) Explain why no finite addition of premises can bring the tortoise to accept Z, and what this shows about the difference between a rule of inference and a premise. (b) A classmate claims that the dialogue proves deductive inference cannot be justified. Assess that claim. (c) Explain what, if anything, the dialogue shows about the normativity of logic — that is, about whether logical rules can rationally compel assent.

The student's answer:

(a) The regress is not a defect of any particular set of premises; it is a defect of the demand. Whatever finite list the tortoise is handed — A, B, C, D — he can accept every item on it and still ask for the hypothetical that connects the list to Z. Achilles's mistake is to treat this as an information deficit, and so he keeps trying to close a gap that no premise can close. Accepting a conditional is one thing; being licensed to move by it is another. Carroll's point is that modus ponens is a rule of inference and not a proposition that could be added to the list of things believed: the moment it is written into the notebook it becomes one more item of belief, and the transition from belief to conclusion has not moved an inch. That is why the notebook fills up and nothing is gained.

(b) A classmate said the dialogue shows that deductive inference cannot be justified, and I think she is right. If a justification must take the form of showing that Z follows from premises already granted, then the regress shows that every such showing can be met by the same request, one level up. Nor can the tortoise be faulted for this: he contradicts himself nowhere, he denies nothing that he has granted, and he declines only to take the last step. Since he is never irrational at any stage, there is no argument that could convict him of an error, and what is left is that accepting a rule of inference is a brute psychological fact about the reasoner, something we are trained into rather than argued into. It is not a truth that could be shown to us. This is the same shape of point Hume makes about induction: the demand for a justification of an inferential practice can only be met, in the end, by custom.

(c) This need not leave logic without authority, though it does relocate the authority. If the rule is not a proposition, then logic's ought cannot consist in the reasoner's beliefs being inconsistent with some further truth he is ignoring; the tortoise has all the truths and does not move. What does the compelling is the practice — the fact that we are the kind of creatures who go on in a certain way when we reason. The normativity of logic is therefore not the normativity of belief; it belongs to the activity rather than to the content. The dialogue is unsettling because the tortoise is a mirror in which the logician sees that he has nothing to say to someone who will not play.

Please grade this on our letter scale, A through F with plus and minus, and give me the comments I would actually hand back. I want a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression: work through the three parts, quote the sentences you are commenting on, and for each say what it gets right, what it gets wrong, and why the error does or does not matter for the question asked. Flag anything a hurried grader would be likely to let slide, and say for each problem you identify whether it is central or peripheral. Where a move is wrong but genuinely arguable, tell me that rather than marking it down as a straightforward mistake; where a move is right but underargued, distinguish it from a move that is simply wrong. State the grade, say how much of it rests on the errors you have identified, and give me a one-sentence verdict at the end. Do not rewrite the answer for the student.
