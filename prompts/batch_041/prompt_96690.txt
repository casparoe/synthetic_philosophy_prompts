I've been trying to write a short paper on supererogation and I keep going around in circles, so I want a second opinion on the notes below before I commit. Rough shape of the argument:

1. The old puzzle: if a supererogatory act is morally better than the alternatives, and morality tells you to do what's morally better, then it isn't optional — but if it isn't optional it isn't supererogatory. Every fix I've seen either weakens "morality tells you to do what's better" until it just means "morality tells you not to do what's forbidden," or keeps the strength and loses the optionality.

2. My move: the puzzle comes from running two different scales of evaluation through one word, better. There's the deontic scale (required / permitted / forbidden) and the aretaic scale (admirable / indifferent / contemptible). The classic supererogatory case is just permitted + admirable. The inference "better, therefore required" only looks compelling because better is ambiguous between the two scales, and once you split them, "admirable and permitted" implies nothing about requirement. So no paradox — just an equivocation that's survived because in most real cases the two scales move together.

3. The questions that fall out of the split, which I think is the interesting part:
 (a) Admirable + forbidden. Whistleblowing that breaks a law I actually think is a legitimate law; the loyalist who hides a war criminal because he promised. If admirability and permissibility come apart in both directions, then supererogatory is the name of one cell in a 3x3 grid, not a fundamental category.
 (b) Permitted + contemptible. Someone using a legal loophole to keep a dead employee's last wages. Not forbidden, not admirable, and neither of those facts explains the other.
 (c) Required + heroic. The firefighter going back into the building. Intuitively not supererogatory at all, it's the job, but it's the same act-type we'd call supererogatory if a bystander did it. So the property looks relative to the agent's role, capacities, and what it costs her.
 (d) Admirable + required. A parent spending her last money on her kid. Required, and not really praiseworthy in the heroic sense, though obviously good.

4. From (c) I want to draw something I'm not sure I can defend: that supererogatory is relative not to an absolute standard but to a baseline of reasonable expectation for that agent in that role — which makes the category partly social. What's supererogatory for me depends on what people like me are expected to do. If that's right, two things follow that I'd rather not accept: (i) the category is culture-relative, and (ii) it collapses into "beyond what you could be blamed for omitting," which is a claim about blame, not about requirement. I think I want to swallow (i) and resist (ii), but I can't see how.

5. There's also a version of the whole thing I find tempting and probably wrong: that supererogation is an artifact of thinking of morality as a code with a floor and no ceiling, and that on a virtue picture the category just dissolves — the generous person's generosity isn't above and beyond anything, it's just what she's like, and virtues don't have a line you can go past. If that's right, my whole 3x3 grid is a description of a code-shaped morality's blind spot rather than a feature of the moral landscape. But virtue ethics still has to say something about the person who does the bare minimum and the person who gives everything away, and saying they're different characters seems to lose the sense that one of them did something optional and good.

6. What I want to end up defending: (2) is the right diagnosis of the paradox, (3c) forces the relativity in (4), and (5) is a reductio of virtue ethics rather than of the concept — virtue ethics can't capture the optional-good, so much the worse for virtue ethics.

What I want from you: no survey of the literature, no reading list, and dont just lay out the options for me to pick from — I've read enough of that. Tell me what to cut and what to keep, and take a side. Specifically: is the two-scale move in (2) actually doing work, or is it relabeling — does the puzzle just reappear as "why isn't the admirable required?" once the scales are split? Does (3c) really force the relativity in (4), or is the firefighter just a role-based obligation that doesn't generalize to the bystander's case? And is (5) as cheap a shot at virtue ethics as I suspect it is? If one of my numbered claims is just wrong, say so flatly instead of hedging — I'd rather find out now than after 6000 words.
