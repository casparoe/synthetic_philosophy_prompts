Two of my closest friends from college went into medicine and philosophy respectively, and somehow the overlap is that one of them, Dana, now does clinical ethics for a big hospital network — she's the person they page when a case turns into a fight. Over the years I've listened to her verdicts on a long string of end-of-life cases and I've started keeping a list, because I'm convinced she's running one consistent rule and I can't name it. Her tally, as best I've reconstructed it: clearly right to withdraw the ventilator from a patient in a persistant vegetative state when the family asked, even though everyone knew the patient would be dead within minutes of the tube coming out; fine with a DNR order for a woman dying of end-stage pancreatic cancer; fine when a competent man with a treatable but miserable condition refused surgery and the team let the disease run its course; but appalled when a nurse gave a suffering patient a lethal dose of a sedative for the explicit purpose of ending his life, and just as appalled when a physician, at the request of a competent patient in terrible pain, injected a lethal drug. So the rough shape is: switching off the machines that keep people alive, and standing back while a disease finishes someone off, fine; putting someone down with a syringe, not fine. I thought I had her.

Then last week she got consulted on a case that's got both of us stuck, and I want to know what she'll say. A man in his forties with a high spinal-cord injury is on a ventilator. He's fully awake, fully competent, can talk, and isn't dying of anything — on the vent he could live for years. He's miserable, he's said clearly and repeatedly that he wants the vent off, and the attending agrees with him. The attending told Dana, off the record, that his aim is to bring about the patient's death: he thinks this man's life as it now stands isn't worth living and he wants to help him end it. Dana thinks the withdrawal is defensible, but the attending's stated aim is making her nervous, and she genuinely can't tell whether her own past verdicts commit her to allowing this or forbidding it.

So: what will she actually conclude? I care less about the yes/no than about the machinery — tell me the candidate rules that fit her tally so far, what each one predicts about the new case, which rule you think she's really running and why, and which further case would most cleanly drive the two main rules apart. And if her past verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case, say so and tell me what principle she'd be buying with each choice.
