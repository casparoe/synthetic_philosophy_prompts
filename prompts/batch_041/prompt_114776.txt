I teach a graduate seminar in metaphysics and I am writing up a lecture on dispositions, and I have an argument that I first assembled as a teaching device and then found I believed, which now strikes me as suspicious precisely because it seems to prove far more than I am willing to accept. I would like you to act as a specialist in the metaphysics of dispositions — the counterfactual analysis of dispositional predicates, causal powers, and the surrounding debates — and diagnose it for me. Think carefully before you commit to a verdict; the argument wears its structure on its sleeve, but I have found that the obvious objections are harder to state cleanly than they look. Here it is, in numbered steps.

(1) If a disposition ascription states a fact about how its bearer would behave, then that fact must be capturable by a counterfactual: "x is disposed to manifest M when stimulus S occurs" states, at least, that if S were to occur, M would occur. (2) The counterfactual is not incidental to the ascription; it is its content. So: x is disposed to M when S iff, if S were to occur, M would occur — the simple conditional analysis. (3) Now consider a fink: a glass that is genuinely fragile, its fragility grounded in its molecular structure, but fitted with a device that detects any incipient striking and instantaneously anneals it, so that it would not break if struck. By hypothesis the glass is fragile; the counterfactual "if it were struck, it would break" is false. The simple analysis therefore fails in the necessity direction and must be amended: the counterfactual is to be evaluated under conditions free of such interference. (4) Now consider a mimic: a sturdy object, not fragile, fitted with a device that detects an incipient striking and shatters the object itself. Here the counterfactual "if it were struck, it would break" is true and the object is not fragile, so the simple analysis also fails in the sufficiency direction and must be amended again: the counterfactual is to be evaluated under conditions free of devices that manufacture the manifestation. (5) The natural repair, which also handles the intermediate case of a mask — an interfering factor that blocks the manifestation without either removing the disposition, as a fink does, or producing it, as a mimic does; the sugar cube sealed in a coating that keeps water off it, the glass packed in foam — is this: x is disposed to M when S iff, if S were to occur and no fink, mask, or mimic were present, M would occur. (6) But there is no characterization of a fink, a mask, or a mimic that does not advert to the very disposition it interferes with. A device is a fink for fragility only because, when the striking occurs, it brings it about that the object does not break — that is, that the fragility-counterfactual comes out false; a device is a mask for fragility only because it likewise brings it about that the object does not break; a device is a mimic for fragility only because it brings it about that the object does break. To require that the counterfactual be evaluated in the absence of finks, masks, and mimics is therefore just to require that it be evaluated in the absence of anything that would make it come out false when the object is fragile or true when it is not. (7) Hence the repaired analysis is circular: the clause meant to rescue the counterfactual presupposes the disposition the counterfactual was to analyse, and a clause that is satisfied whenever the object has the disposition and fails whenever it lacks it is not an amendment at all but the conclusion smuggled into the premise. (8) An analysis circular in this way supplies no non-dispositional truth conditions for the ascription. (9) An ascription whose only content is borrowed from the disposition it was supposed to analyse adds nothing to our picture of the object; it records our expectations rather than describing a feature of the thing. (10) Therefore "this glass is fragile," said of a glass not yet struck, states no fact about the glass — and the same follows, by the same steps, for solubility, charge, and every other disposition.

I am uneasy because the conclusion cannot be right: the chemist who says the vial is fragile before anyone drops it is not saying nothing, and if step (6) is sound it seems to empty out ceteris paribus clauses across the special sciences as well, which is a much larger bill than I set out to pay. My instinct is that (6) does something illicit — that the cases in (3) through (5) describe their interlopers structurally, by what the devices are and do, and that (6) quietly swaps that structural description for a dispositional one — but I cannot make the objection stick, because I cannot see how to extend any structural description to cover the interfering mechanisms no one has thought of yet. I would rather keep the chemist's ascription and give up the demand for a reductive analysis than keep the demand and lose the ascription, but I want to know what I am buying at that price. Tell me which step fails, or tell me plainly that the argument is valid and that the conclusion is one I should accept in a suitably deflated form; do not manufacture a flaw to spare my intuitions. In either case, explain why the step you fasten on looked innocent, what has to be given up to reject it, and how a defender of the analysis would attack your diagnosis. Begin your reply with your bottom line — the step you reject, or a one-sentence statement that you accept the argument — with no preamble and no restatement of the question, and then give your reasoning.
