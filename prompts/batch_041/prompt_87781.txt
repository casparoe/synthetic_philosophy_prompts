A friend of mine who reads a lot of rationalist-adjacent philosophy blogs tried to explain the self-sampling vs. self-indication thing to me, and I want a second opinion on how much of it to trust. Here's the gist of what he said, as close as I can remember it:

There are two rival assumptions about how to reason from the fact that you exist. The self-sampling assumption says you should reason as if you were a random observer drawn from all the observers there are — you're a random sample from the reference class of all observers. The self-indication assumption says the bare fact that you exist is itself evidence, and it pushes you toward hypotheses that contain more observers, because a bigger world offers more slots for you to have occupied. He said the famous doomsday argument is an application of the self-indication assumption: since existence favors bigger populations, and a long-lived humanity contains far more people than a short-lived one, you should figure you're probably somewhere in the middle of all the humans who will ever live, and therefore probably close to the end. And he said the standard objection to the self-sampling assumption is the presumptuous philosopher: if you really treat yourself as a random draw, then you're far more likely to have landed in an enormous universe than in a small one, so you can argue from your armchair that reality must be bursting with minds, which is absurd. His conclusion was that the presumptuous philosopher is a clean refutation of the self-sampling assumption, that the whole debate is basically settled, and that nobody serious defends it anymore.

I'm not sure if I'm remebering this right, but I have a feeling he's half right and half turned around, and I don't know enough to say which half. If he's got the two assumptions hooked up to the wrong arguments, straighten that out too, and tell me which assumption the doomsday argument actually rests on and why the mix-up matters.
