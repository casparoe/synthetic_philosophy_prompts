I cover science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm in the last stretch of a long essay on the Victorians who tried to put the paranormal on a scientific footing. The figure I keep returning to is Henry Sidgwick — the Cambridge moral philosopher whose Methods of Ethics is routinely called the most rigorous book of nineteenth-century utilitarianism — and I've got myself tangled in a framing problem that I'd like help with before I file. The Sidgwick in my notes has two lives. One is the professor who insisted that every moral claim be validated from an impartial point of view, and who was famous even among his friends for refusing to believe anything on insufficient evidence. The other is the co-founder and first president of the Society for Psychical Research, who gave the last two decades of his life to the investigation of apparitions, mediums, and thought-transference — the Society whose 1894 Census of Hallucinations tabulated some seventeen thousand replies in an attempt to work out how often sane people see things that aren't there, and whose early members were perfectly willing to expose a medium as a fraud and publish the results.

What I want the piece to argue is that these were never really two lives: the ethics needed something the ethics could not supply, and the psychical research was where he went looking for it. His system rests on the thought that virtue and self-interest must ultimately coincide, and since nothing in his own arguments could establish that they do, I read the sittings, the census, and the patient sifting of testimony as a private consolation project — the one corner of his life where hope was permitted to do the work that proof could not — and I read his reticence about all of it in print as tact, a determination not to let the credulity show in the masterpiece. Frederic Myers, his closest ally in the research, liked to recall a walk on which he asked Sidgwick whether, philosophy having failed to solve the riddle of the universe, observable phenomena might still yield knowledge of a world unseen; I have been treating that exchange as the key to the whole man, and as evidence that by the end he counted the survival of the dead as more or less settled.

So my question is this: if the psychical research was doing that kind of load-bearing work for him, why did he keep it out of The Methods of Ethics — and how should I phrase the relationship so that a reader sees a single, consistent man rather than a hypocrite? I'd like a formulation I can actually defend, since a couple of historians of the period will be reading the draft.
