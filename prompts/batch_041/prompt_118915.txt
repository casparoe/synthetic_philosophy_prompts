I work as a backend engineer for a company that sells record-keeping and compliance software to university research labs, and last quarter I was handed the module for animal research protocols, which means I have now read an unreasonable amount of the paperwork governing experiments on mice, rats, and fish. The shape of it is this. Every proposed procedure goes to an institutional ethics committee, which assigns it one of four severity labels — non-recovery, mild, moderate, or severe — and the institution reports the totals upward every year. To get a protocol approved you must justify the number of animals, specify anaesthesia and analgesia, commit to environmental enrichment, and define humane endpoints, after which the committee weighs the total suffering it expects the animals to undergo against the scientific value it expects to get. Here is what has been bothering me. Enrichment, cage size, boredom, and a few days of post-surgical pain are all treated as real entries on the ledger: a procedure that leaves a mouse uncomfortable for a week is graded moderate, and a botched injection is a reportable welfare event. But a procedure in which every animal is anaesthetised at the outset and killed without ever regaining consciousness is filed as non-recovery, which is not a point on the mild-to-severe scale at all but a separate benign category, and the animal's entire remaining life never appears as a cost anywhere in the review. It gets stranger: the humane-endpoint rule makes killing the remedy. If an animal is heading toward a bad outcome, the institutionally correct move is to kill it early, and that counts as the welfare-respecting choice. A colleague who reads a lot of philosophy tells me the whole apparatus encodes an asymmetry — that suffering is bad in itself, while a life cut short is not bad for the one who dies unless something about it is suffered — and I would like to know whether the practice genuinely commits to that or merely looks as though it does.

So my question is: what philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of harm that would make the whole thing hang together, and then be candid about which parts of the practice strictly require that theory as opposed to merely sitting comfortably with it, since I suspect several quite different views would license exactly the same paperwork. Tell me which rivals the practice quietly rules out — a view on which death is itself a harm to the animal, a rights-based view on which an animal may not be used as a means at all, an objective-list view on which a life of a certain kind has value independently of how it feels — and which rivals it is simply indifferent to. Say whether the parts are mutually consistent, given that the practice does count some deaths as harms (an animal that dies of an untreated infection because nobody noticed is logged as a welfare failure) while treating others as free. And finish by telling me what would concretely have to change in the severity bands, in the harm-benefit weighing, and in the humane-endpoint rule if the presupposed view were replaced by the view that death is a harm to the animal in its own right. I do not want citations or a reading list; I have no background in philosophy, so define any term of art as you use it, and reason it out from the description above rather than pointing me toward literature.

Please give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. In order: (1) the implicit theory of harm; (2) what the practice strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with; (3) which rival views it rules out; (4) where it is internally inconsistent or underdetermined; (5) what would have to change if death were treated as a harm in its own right.
