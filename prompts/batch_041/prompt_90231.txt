I'm a novelist, about 200 pages into a book set in South London, and the whole plot turns on a woman who has lived in the same house since 1961 and cannot prove it. Which is, of course, just the Windrush scandal, and I've read enough that I don't need the history from you: people who came from the Caribbean before 1973, who had an automatic right to stay and were never handed a document saying so, then the hostile environment rules arrive and suddenly everyone has to produce papers, and the department starts demanding documentary proof for decade after decade of a life — and the landing cards, the one record that would have shown when people actually arrived, got destroyed by the Home Office in 2010. I'm giving you those facts because I want you to take them as fixed. What I want is the theory.

Two bodies of work, and I think they're in tension. First, Trouillot on how silences get produced — his claim that silencing happens at four separate moments: when the fact is created, when it's assembled into a record, when it's retrieved, and when it's assigned retrospective significance. Second, Hartman's critical fabulation, which as I understand it means writing into the gap in a way that neither pretends the gap isn't there nor papers over it with a comfortable invention — telling the impossible story while keeping the impossibility visible. So: map the Windrush case onto Trouillot's four moments and tell me where the mapping strains or breaks. Then tell me what critical fabulation can and can't do for a case where the silence isn't only the absence of a voice but the deliberate destruction of a record. I have a suspicion that fabulation is the wrong tool when the problem isnt that nobody wrote it down but that somebody shredded it, and I want to know whether that suspicion survives contact with the theory or just falls apart.

And here's the part I actually want you to chew on. I've been sorting the silences in my research into three kinds. Silences of omission: nobody ever wrote it down. Silences of erasure: it was written down and then destroyed or lost. Silences of illegibility: it was written down, it still exists, but the system that needs it can't read it as proof — a payslip isn't a passport, a school register isn't a landing card. Does that three-way split hold up, or does the Windrush case collapse the distinctions? Is the destroyed landing card an erasure or an illegibility, given that even if it had survived it recorded an arrival and not a right? Push back on the taxonomy if it's bad, tell me which of the three the state was actually producing, and say plainly where Trouillot and Hartman go quiet rather than forcing them to speak. Then finish the other direction: what does this case show the two of them that neither quite says — what does it mean for a theory of the archive when the archive isn't merely incomplete but adversarial?
