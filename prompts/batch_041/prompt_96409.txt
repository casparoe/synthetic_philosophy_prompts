I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I have spent the better part of three years reporting on the moral-psychology literature — the laboratories that try to explain why we hold the moral beliefs we hold. I have come out the other side with a set of verdicts that I cannot get to add up. Some of the explanations on offer strike me as genuinely undermining the beliefs they explain; others leave those beliefs exactly where they were, even when the explanation is of much the same kind. I would like you to work out what principle, if any, my verdicts express, and then to tell me which of the verdicts that principle forces me to give up.

Two stipulations before the cases. First, take each explanation at face value as a hypothesis and assume, for the sake of the argument, that it is true. I am not asking whether the science is any good, and nothing here turns on a particular paper or researcher; these are simply the standard shapes such explanations take. Second, I am not asking whether moral belief in general is undermined by evolution. I am asking what follows for one belief once you accept one explanation of it.

1. **Kin incest.** My conviction that sex between close relatives is seriously wrong is said to be downstream of a disgust response selected because inbreeding is costly. Verdict: not undermined. The disgust may have been the messenger; the substance of the objection — the risk carried by any child — is available to me without it.

2. **Parental partiality.** That I owe more to my own children than to other people's is said to be explained by parental investment and kin selection. Verdict: not undermined. I am the one who can act for them, and I took the job on.

3. **Gratuitous cruelty.** That it is wrong to hurt a small child for amusement is said to be a spillover of adaptations for protecting one's own vulnerable young. Verdict: not undermined. I would sooner abandon this whole enquiry than give up that verdict.

4. **Defilement.** My sense that certain acts are unclean — that some bodies, foods and mixtures are defiling quite apart from any harm they do — is said to be explained by pathogen-avoidance and contamination heuristics. Verdict: undermined. I now treat the revulsion as a fact about me rather than about the world.

5. **Jealousy.** That a partner's attention to others is a wrong done to me, of a kind that licenses surveillance and reproach, is said to be explained by adaptations for securing paternity. Verdict: undermined. Every warrant I reach for turns out to be the feeling restated.

6. **The corpse, two ways.** (a) A person has left their body to be butchered and eaten by their friends at a dinner; no one is harmed, and the wish was freely expressed. My revulsion is said to be the same contamination heuristic as in (4). Verdict: undermined — it is a strong feeling and nothing more. (b) The same person has instead left their body to be dressed and posed in the window of a clothes shop. Identical explanation, identical stipulations. Verdict: not undermined — and I cannot say what the difference is, except that (b) seems to be about a person and (a) only about meat. I record the asymmetry without defending it.

7. **Compatriots.** That I owe more to my fellow citizens than to foreigners is said to be explained by parochial altruism — the same machinery as in (2). Verdict: undermined.

8. **Desert.** That the poor are largely the authors of their own misfortune is said to be explained by adaptations that make us eager to blame the unlucky, so as to keep the world feeling just. Verdict: undermined. Note that I take this explanation to be plainly partial: it accounts for why I am motivated to believe the proposition, not for whether the proposition is true. I give the verdict anyway.

The test I keep reaching for is this: a belief is undermined by its genealogy just when the genealogy leaves it with no warrant that does not itself depend on the belief. But that test seems to debunk (3) along with everything else, and I will not accept that. Nor does it tell me why (1) and (4) come apart: in both I have a feeling and a story about the feeling. I suspect the real work is being done by something else — perhaps by whether the explanation is aimed at the content of the belief or only at the strength of the feeling, or by whether the belief is answerable to something outside us (harm, consent, what a person is owed) rather than to a norm we merely have. If that is the distinction, tell me whether it can be stated without circularity — that is, without defining 'answerable to something outside us' as 'whatever beliefs I want to keep'.

I will accept a principle that overturns two or three of my verdicts; I will not accept one that overturns all of them. If every belief on the list turns out to be undermined, I will conclude that the test is wrong rather than that morality is.

There is one case on which I have no verdict and would like yours. My belief that the suffering of an animal counts for something is said by some to be a spillover of adaptations for caring for our own young, and by others not to be explained that way at all. Say what my principle predicts for it. Say also what it predicts for one further case of your own choosing that I have not mentioned.

Please give me: the principle, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself; the case that motivates each clause; the verdicts it delivers for the two new cases; and an honest statement of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and what it would cost me to give them up. End with a single final line of the form \answer{...} containing, in no more than forty words, the principle in one sentence, followed by a semicolon and the numbers of whichever of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, or the word none.
