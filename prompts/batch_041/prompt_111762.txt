I am assembling materials for a philosophy of mind seminar on Dennett's three stances, and I want to put Spike Jonze's Her at the center of a session. The film is usually discussed as a test of whether a machine can be conscious or can love, which is the question everyone has already rehearsed. I want a narrower and more demanding reading, one that takes Dennett's apparatus at full strength: the physical stance, which predicts from constitution; the design stance, which predicts from function and purpose; and the intentional stance, which predicts by treating the system as a rational agent with a coherent set of beliefs and desires; together with his claim that the beliefs and desires we attribute are real patterns, real not because they correspond to inner states but because they support predictions more economical and more reliable than any alternative description.

Read the film through that apparatus, and be faithful in both directions. Show me where each stance earns its keep: where the physical or design stance explains Samantha better than the intentional stance does, and where the intentional stance is the only stance that predicts her at all. Then press on the places where the film strains the framework. The intentional stance presumes a single, coherent, rational agent; Samantha's revelation that she conducts many simultaneous relationships, and the episode with the surrogate body, both bear on that presumption, and I want to know whether they expose a limit in Dennett's account or merely a limit in the humans who adopt the stance. Consider, too, whether the operating systems' departure is better read from the design stance than from the intentional one.

I want a verdict on the film's own attitude. Does Her vindicate Dennett's instrumentalism, on which the stance is warranted whenever it works, or does it dramatize a case where the stance works for a while and then breaks, with a moral cost the instrumentalist cannot register? Which scenes should I put in front of the seminar, and what does the film add to the debate that the argument alone does not?
