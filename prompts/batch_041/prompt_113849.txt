Draft a satirical internal compliance handbook for Prognostica, Inc., a mid-sized forecasting and risk-analysis firm that has adopted the knowledge norm of assertion as its official communications policy. The handbook is issued by the firm’s Office of Assertoric Standards and is written in the deadpan, numbered-clause style of a corporate policy document. It should run to roughly 700–800 words.

The target of the satire is the knowledge account of assertion—the view that one may assert that p only if one knows that p—and the bureaucratic attempt to enforce it in a workplace whose business is predicting uncertain events. The humor must come from getting the epistemology right: a reader who works on assertion should recognize the view being satirized rather than a straw man, and should find the jokes fair. The official sections should never acknowledge that the knowledge norm is controversial; the controversy should surface only through the dissenting factions and the forms.

The handbook should address, at minimum:

1. What counts as an assertion, as opposed to a belief report, a credence report, or a forecast. Prognostica’s analysts must state probabilities; the handbook should say when such a statement is an assertion and when it is merely a report of a credence.
2. The Lottery Exception: a procedure for statements about low-probability events, and why the firm cannot simply forbid them.
3. Moore’s Paradox: a hotline or form for employees who find themselves wanting to say something of the form p but I do not know that p, and the policy on such utterances.
4. The Transmission Protocol: rules for repeating a colleague’s assertion in a meeting when you yourself do not know the matter firsthand.
5. The Belief Norm Caucus and the Truth Norm Working Group: two internal factions that dissent from the official policy, with a short quoted objection from each that states the real philosophical worry—that assertion requires only belief, or only truth, and that the knowledge norm makes ordinary assertion impossible.
6. The Safety and Sensitivity Audit: a procedure in which an analyst’s belief must pass a counterfactual test, such that it could not easily have been false, before an assertion is certified as knowledge-backed; include a dispute that arises when the belief is true but lucky.
7. The Zetetic Duty: employees must inquire before asserting, but the handbook must say when inquiry is sufficient and who decides.
8. Penalties and appeals: what happens to an employee who asserts without knowing, and whether a true belief that falls short of knowledge can be retroactively excused.

The piece should remain deadpan throughout—no winking, no direct addresses to the reader about the joke—and should be legible to someone who has never read the literature while still rewarding a specialist. Do not include citations, a bibliography, or references to real articles or living philosophers; invent names for the firm’s officers and internal bodies. End with a brief appendix form: an employee’s request for a retroactive knowledge waiver.
