I write a column on science and ideas, and I am preparing a long-form interview with a philosopher of science who has spent a career arguing that philosophy has made real, concrete contributions to physics. My difficulty is that this conversation tends to collapse into a recital: the guest lists the same handful of episodes — the interpretation of quantum mechanics, the conceptual prehistory of relativity, the analysis of symmetry and renormalization — and the interviewer nods along. I would like the interview to function as a genuine test of her thesis rather than a platform for it.

Please draft eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and order them so that the pressure builds. I intend to put a specific criterion to her and ask her to adopt it for the duration: call it counterfactual indispensability. On this criterion, philosophy counts as useful to science just in case some actual scientific result would not have been reached, or would have been reached later or in a visibly worse form, had the philosophical work not been done. I want the questions to force her to run that test in the open rather than gesture at it.

At least one question should make her apply the test to the interpretation of quantum mechanics — specifically to whether the long philosophical engagement with the measurement problem and with Bell's theorem has produced anything a physicist could use, and whether the shut-up-and-calculate attitude is a defensible division of labor or an evasion. Another should push her on the hole argument and the status of spacetime points: did the philosophy resolve a genuine scientific question, or did it merely re-describe one? Another should take up the nineteenth-century disputes over the ether and the meaning of simultaneity and ask whether the philosophical work there was load-bearing or merely scaffolding that could have been removed once the building stood.

I also want you to introduce two further distinctions and press her with them. The first is the difference between philosophy as scaffolding and philosophy as load-bearing structure: ask which of her own examples survive if we insist that a contribution be load-bearing, not merely helpful at the time. The second is the prophylactic view — that philosophy's value to science lies chiefly in preventing error rather than in enabling discovery — and ask whether she would accept that this weaker, negative role is all the evidence supports, and what would have to be true for the stronger role to be vindicated.

Include a question that puts the familiar charge to her directly — the complaint, associated with working physicists from Feynman through Weinberg and Hawking, that philosophy has nothing to teach the scientist — and that asks whether that complaint is an empirical claim or a rhetorical one, and how she would tell the difference. Close with a question about whether her own thesis is falsifiable: what observation about the history of science would count against the claim that philosophy has been useful.

For every question, add a one-line note telling me what move or deflection the question is designed to block, so that I can steer in real time if she reaches for it. Please avoid questions that could be answered by reciting the standard Feynman–Weinberg–Hawking exchange, and do not ask her for reading recommendations or citations — I want the substance, not the bibliography.
