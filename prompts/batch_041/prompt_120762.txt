I am a novelist — three books, the last one assembled entirely out of documents — and for the past two years I have run a fortnightly workshop for other writers on what I have been calling the epistemology of plotting: how a reader's confidence in a fact moves when the fact arrives through a source that is itself only partly trustworthy. My own books are built out of layered testimony, and I want the people in the room to feel, rather than be told, that two equally honest accounts can leave a reader somewhere quite different depending on which one arrives first. In November I have fourteen participants, a single seventy-five-minute session, one room, no computers, and a budget of index cards, envelopes and a whiteboard. I would like you to design the session as a game rather than a lecture. Please brainstorm two or three possible structures before committing to one, then develop the strongest; write as someone who is both a philosopher of probability and an experienced designer of games for adults, assume no technical training in the room but a willingness to add fractions, and tell me plainly wherever you are unsure of a step rather than smoothing it over. No reading lists and no citations, please — I have no way to check them, and the point is to be in the room.

Here is the phenomenon I want them to feel, and it has to arrive through the rules rather than through anything I say afterwards. Take a specimen drawn at random from a collection of one hundred, each member of which either has a spotted thorax, long antennae, both, or neither: forty have both, forty have neither, ten have spots only, ten have antennae only — so the two features are correlated. Two impressions of the specimen reach the group, one about each feature, and neither is decisive. After the first impression, the group's credence that the specimen is spotted is to stand at six in ten; after the second, their credence that it has long antennae is to stand at eight in ten. Crucially, each of these figures must be handed to the participants as what the experience leaves them with — a new degree of belief in that one proposition — and never as the output of a gadget whose accuracy is known in advance, because the moment you give them a gadget you have converted the problem into ordinary conditioning on a known event, and the whole thing collapses into something that behaves perfectly well. Now the sting. The rule most people reach for, which holds the probabilities inside each of the two categories fixed and renormalises, is not indifferent to order. Feed the group the six-in-ten impression first and the eight-in-ten second, and they should end with a credence of about 0.69 that the specimen has both features; reverse the order and they should end at about 0.57. The same two pieces of evidence, in the other order, and the rational answer moves by twelve points. Then run the identical procedure as a control on an uncorrelated collection, twenty-five in each cell, and let them watch the order stop mattering entirely. Please work the arithmetic yourself and tell me if my two figures are wrong.

I want this to run as an activity: rules, materials, a division into teams who are kept apart and given the two impressions in opposite orders so that the collision at the whiteboard is itself the discovery, a timeline that fits the seventy-five minutes, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the machinery — the partition, the requirement that the conditional probabilities inside each cell stay put, the name of the rule (I know it as Jeffrey conditionalization, or probability kinematics), why plain conditionalization never has this problem, and why some philosophers regard a rule that can be pulled in two directions by the order of experience as not really a rule of learning at all but an artefact of describing the experience too thinly. Be honest in the debrief about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply myself: the theorem that says exactly when two such updates do and do not commute, and the objection that a fully described experience would be ordinary evidence in a richer space, which would make the twelve-point gap disappear. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — which step the teams will fumble, what the loudest objection will be, and what I should say when someone insists that one of the two orders must be the correct one. Finish with a one-page facilitator's sheet I could photocopy.
