For some years now I have been following the dispute in the philosophy of medicine between naturalists and normativists about the concept of disease, and it has reached the point where I want it diagnosed rather than adjudicated. Let me set out the stall as concretely as I can, because I do not want a survey of positions I already know.

The naturalist line, in the version I understand best, holds that disease is a value-free theoretical notion: a state is a disease just in case it is an internal state that depresses the functional efficiency of some part or process of the organism below the statistically typical level for its species and age class. On this view the word belongs to biology and pathology, and to call something a disease is to make a claim about how bodies work, not about whether the state is bad, unwanted, or worth treating.

The normativist line holds that no such value-free notion can be extracted, because the judgements of dysfunction and of subnormality are themselves evaluative: what counts as the function of a part, and what counts as its normal level, are fixed by purposes and interests rather than by biology alone. The usual evidence is a list of cases — conditions that are statistically typical yet treated as diseases, conditions that are atypical yet not diseases, and conditions whose status as diseases has changed with social attitudes while the biology stayed put.

What makes this a stall rather than a live dispute is that both sides have been fully stated for decades, each has absorbed the other's cases and explained them away, and nobody moves. My working hypothesis is that the quarrel is not really about values at all, but about which job the word is being asked to do. Suppose we separate two projects. The first is explanatory: we want a kind that figures in causal and predictive generalizations of the sort pathology and epidemiology rely on, and such a kind cannot depend on anyone's preferences, or the generalizations will not survive a change in attitude. The second is practical and clinical: we want a kind that guides what to treat, excuse, insure, and pity, and that kind will be answerable to values somewhere. If these are genuinely different projects, then the naturalist is right about the first concept and the normativist is right about the second, and the impasse is the sound of two projects sharing one word.

I would like you to test this hypothesis rather than accept it. Specifically:

(1) Is the disagreement, on this reading, merely verbal — does it dissolve once the word is disambiguated into the explanatory concept and the practical concept, with nothing left over? Or does something substantive survive? If something survives, say exactly what the two sides still disagree about once the distinction has been drawn.

(2) If something survives, classify it: is it a disagreement about facts (about what functions are, or about how medicine in fact classifies); about standards of evidence (about what would show that a classification is value-laden); or about starting intuitions neither side can argue for (for instance, whether a condition's being unwanted is or is not partly constitutive of its being a disease)?

(3) Consider two cases that I think press the distinction rather than sit comfortably on either side of it. First, the ordinary clinical reference range, which is constructed so that a fixed fraction of a healthy population falls outside it, with the consequence that as the number of tests ordered rises the probability that a healthy person is flagged as abnormal approaches one: is that a fact about biology or a fact about the conventions of measurement, and does it favour either side? Second, a risk state — a modestly elevated blood pressure, or a marker that predicts a future condition without any present dysfunction — where the classificatory decision plainly does practical work about who is treated and insured while the underlying biology is continuous with the normal range. Do these cases show that the explanatory and practical projects cannot be kept apart in practice, and if so, is that an argument against the distinction or merely a fact about clinical life?

(4) Finally, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a further distinction, a decision to let one of the projects go, an empirical result about how medicine actually reasons, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the question is ill-posed. If you think it is ill-posed, say which question is, and what should replace it.
