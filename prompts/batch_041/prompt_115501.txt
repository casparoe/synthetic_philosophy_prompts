I'm in a reading group that's working through Keynes on probability and uncertainty, and I've managed to get myself tangled up in a way I can't untangle on my own. Here's my picture of things: the Treatise on Probability is a subjectivist book — probability is just a degree of belief sitting in someone's head, a personal opinion about how likely something is — and then later, in the thirties, Keynes starts going on about how in economics we face genuine "uncertainty," by which he means there's no scientific basis for putting probabilities on things like the return on an investment over the next decade. That combination has always seemed odd to me. If probabilities just are your degrees of belief, then of course you can assign one — you don't need a scientific basis, you need an opinion, and if you're unsure you just say something near 0.5. So what was he actually complaining about? Did he contradict himself between the early book and the later stuff, or is there something in his own account of probability that makes the later complaint coherent? I'd rather get the real answer than a smoothing-over.

The other thing is that I have a vague memory of him talking about the "weight" of an argument or of the evidence, and I've been quietly assuming that's just his word for confidence, so that more weight = higher probability. If that's wrong, does it bear on the uncertainty business at all? A friend of mine who does statistics insists the whole thing is just the familiar risk/uncertainty distinction that Knight made, and that Keynes was a confused frequentist who never really accepted that probabilities are subjective — I suspect he's wrong on at least one of those counts but I don't know enough to say which. What I want is a clear account of whether the early theory and the later skepticism about calculable probability are one view applied to different situations or two genuinely different views, and if the latter, what changed.
