I'm co-teaching (well, technically TA'ing but I get one session) a 300-level cross-listed history/philosophy seminar on money and trade in early modern Europe — 18 students, mostly juniors, split between econ minors who are sure they already know all this and philosophy people who glaze over the second a number appears. The room has movable chairs, a whiteboard, and a projector that's been broken since September, and my budget is zero. They've read Hume's essays on money, interest and the balance of trade for this week and I get 75 minutes. What I want is a trading game, not a lecture (the lecture is the professor's job, mine is the hour where they actually do something). Two things I want them to feel in their bones, things that sound contradictory on the page: first, if I magically doubled everyones money overnight and nothing else changed, nobody would be richer — prices would just catch up, the same goods get swapped around, only the numbers get bigger; and second, that the way the new money arrives matters enormously, because whoever gets it first, while prices are still lagging, walks away with real goods, and the people at the end of the queue just get higher prices and a shrug. Hume seems to hold both at once, and I don't want the game to make that look like a fudge — I want them to discover it's about time, not about inconsistency. If you can work the trade half in too, please do: two blocs trading, one of them winning and winning, and then discovering that its own success is what eventually prices it out of the market, which is the bit my students all nod at and clearly don't believe.

Then a debrief that maps what actually happened in the room onto the specific claims — long-run neutrality, the transition where the new money is still exciting industry, the self-correcting imbalance — and that gets them to say the mapping themselves rather than me pointing at it.
