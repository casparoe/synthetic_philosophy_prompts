I'm a software engineer, no philosophy background, and I'm putting together a lunch-and-learn for my team about decision theory. Half the team has read a couple of LessWrong posts, the other half thinks expected value is a function in a stats library. I want a decision tree they can actually fill out, not a lecture.

The topic: counterfactual mugging and XOR blackmail. You know the drill — Omega flips a coin, if tails asks you for $100, if heads pays you $10,000 if it predicted you'd pay on tails; and the blackmailer version where the bomb's location is XORed with your decision so paying can't causally help but the blackmailer knows your algorithm. I want the tree to start from something everyone in the room can answer — like do you one-box or two-box in Newcomb's problem — and end at a named position on whether you should pay in these cases.

Here's what I need:

- A decision tree with maybe 8 to 10 branch nodes. Each node is a question a person can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. No node should be are you a causal decision theorist — that's cheating. Instead: Suppose a predictor has already predicted your choice and you can't change its prediction. Do you take both boxes or just the one? Or: If you found out that your decision procedure is correlated with an outcome in a branch of reality you'll never see, does that count as a reason for you?
- Aim for six to eight leaves, each a named position: causal decision theory, evidential decision theory, timeless decision theory, updateless decision theory, functional decision theory, and whatever else belongs there — maybe the refuse to pay because blackmail is bad view, the problem is ill-posed view, and the pay because you want to be the kind of agent that pays view. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree can't seperate. I'd rather know that two positions collapse on my questions than have the tree pretend they're distinct.
- Note any combinations of answers that don't correspond to a named position, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's bothered to defend it.
- The tree should be built from the logic of the problem, not from a list of names. So first the questions and the combinations they allow, then the positions located in that space.

Also, since this is a lunch-and-learn and not a seminar, please keep the questions concrete. Use the coin, the $100, the bomb, the two boxes — not factorizability or measurement independence. If a question needs a setup, give the setup in a sentence.

One more thing: I want the answer to seperate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this stuff. I have a suspicion that the answer to counterfactual mugging is presented as more obvious than it is, and I don't want to stand in front of my team and present one camp's view as the consensus. So mark which parts of the tree are bedrock and which parts are live disputes.
