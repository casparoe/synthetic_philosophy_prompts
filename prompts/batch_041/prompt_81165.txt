Me and my friend got into a long arguement last night about organ donation and I think I lost but I'm honestly not sure either of us knew what we were actually arguing about. Heres what's bugging me. The rule everybody repeats is the dead donor rule, right, you only take organs out of someone after theyre dead, never in a way that kills them. Fine. But then look at how its actually done in hospitals, because theres two completely different pathways and I cant make them fit together.

Path one, donation after brain death. The persons brain is destroyed, brainstem included, theyre on a ventilator, heart still beating, blood pressure fine, theyre warm. We call that person dead and we take the heart.

Path two, donation after circulatory death. Here the brain is not destroyed. The person is catastrophically injured maybe, but theres no brain death diagnosis. So the family withdraws the ventilator, the heart stops on its own, and then everyone stands around and waits. After some short interval -- a few minutes, sometimes as little as two or three, it varies by hospital and by country -- we call that person dead and we start taking organs.

My friend says its one concept of death applied to two situations. I say it cant be, and heres the part thats really bugging me. Somebody once told me theres a distinction between a heart that has stopped irreversibly and a heart that has stopped permanently. Irreversible meaning it cant be restarted. Permanent meaning it wont be restarted, because nobody is going to try and the conditions for spontaneous restart arent there. In the circulatory case we seem to be going with permanent and not irreversible, because if you actually did chest compressions on that person the heart could plausibly start again. So the declaration of death is riding on a decision not to intervene. Thats a fact about us and our intentions, not a fact about the body.

And the waiting time is the tell for me. Why two minutes, why five, why not twenty? Its not because anyone has discovered that the brain dies at minute four. Its because the organs are getting damaged while you wait, so the interval gets tuned to keep the graft usable. The clock is being set by the recipient, not by the donor.

So what I want from you is this. What does this practice actually presuppose about death? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me. Does it need one biological criterion, or is it quietly running on a legal or social definition where death is a status we confer? Does it presuppose whole brain death in one wing of the hospital and something else entirely in the other, and is that a genuine inconsistency or can it be made coherent -- say by treating death as the permanent cessation of the organism as a whole, or by going the other direction and saying the thing that matters is the loss of personhood, higher brain stuff? Which rival theories does the practice quietly rule out just by the way it operates? And give me the strongest version of my friends position, because I suspect shes right that I'm confusing the metaphysics with the paperwork.
