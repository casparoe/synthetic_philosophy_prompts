I'm in a seminar on the analytic/synthetic distinction and there's one move I keep tripping over. Last week we were arguing about whether the law of non-contradiction is a fact about the world or just a rule of our language, and a guy near the window said "well, logic is true by convention, we basically made it up," and half the room nodded like that was the end of it. Then this week someone was making the opposite point — that since we could in principle give up modus ponens if the physics got weird enough, logic isn't epistemically special — and the exact same "true by convention" line came out again, only now it was being used to say logic IS special, because we fix it by fiat and can't be argued out of it. It obviously can't be doing both jobs at once, and I want to actually take the slogan apart instead of nodding along.

So help me seperate out the menu: what distinct precise claims could "logic is true by convention" actually be making? I can feel at least a few in there — something trivial about us picking symbols and notation, something about the laws of logic being definitions or abbreviations, something about validity itself being whatever we stipulate by laying down inference rules, and something about logical truths being true purely in virtue of the meanings of the words and not any fact about the world. For each one, tell me whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, whether it's just false, and — the part I actually care about — whether it would license the move it got used for, namely settling whether logic is "about the world" or not. I don't want you to just pick the right reading and explain it, I want the whole menu with verdicts, plus a note on which reading the people in my seminar are probably running when they say it.

And here's a test case I think kills some of the readings, so please run it through all of them. Imagine I introduce a brand-new connective, call it tonk, and I stipulate exactly two rules for it: from any sentence A you may infer "A tonk B," and from "A tonk B" you may infer B. Now from literally any sentence I can derive any other sentence whatsoever. If validity is just whatever we confer by stipulating the rules, didn't I just make a garbage inference valid? Which reading of the slogan dies here, and what would you have to add to save it — that the rules have to actually mean something, or be conservative/harmless when bolted onto the old ones, or fit together in some kind of harmony? And if the slogan needs that extra ingredient, does it still rescue the analytic/synthetic distinction from Quine, or has it just gone circular by presupposing the very notion of meaning it was supposed to explain? I'm leading section next week so a clean version would be a huge help.
