Write a patient information and consent booklet for a fictional private clinic that restores sight to adults who were born blind. The booklet should be written in the flat, reassuring, liability-conscious register of a real clinic's printed leaflet. It should never announce its philosophical commitments, never wink at the reader, and never use the vocabulary of a philosophy seminar.

What it is quietly about is Molyneux's question: whether a person born blind, who has learned by touch to tell a cube from a sphere, could, on first being made to see, tell which is which by sight alone. Molyneux put it to Locke, who printed it in the Essay and answered no, asking whether "by his sight, before he touch'd them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube"; Berkeley returned a more radical no, holding that the ideas of sight and touch resemble one another not at all, while Leibniz and others answered yes. The clinic is dimly aware of this history, and its clauses, assessment protocols, publication consent form, and refund policy should absorb it.

Aim for roughly 700-800 words: numbered clauses, then a short FAQ of three or four entries, and, if it helps, an appendix describing the post-operative shape-assessment procedure. The document should not cite or name the historical literature; the philosophy should appear only in what the clinic binds itself to.

The satire has to be fair, which means the booklet should be consistent with the following without stating any of them: the difference between the narrow question (could the patient sort cube from sphere at the very first visual encounter) and the broader one (can sight deliver shape information at all before it has been calibrated); the qualifiers Locke attached to his answer, at first sight and with certainty, which leave room for a patient who guesses well or learns fast; the difference between discriminating two shapes and recognising or knowing them, and the awkward status of the patient's own report as evidence; the fact that surgically restored vision is not normal vision, so that any outcome is confounded and neither the yes nor the no is cleanly confirmed; the suspicion that empiricist and rationalist frameworks can each accommodate whatever the patient does, which is why the clinic can promise nothing and charge regardless; and the sensorimotor line of thought on which seeing is something the patient must learn to do.

Elements worth including: a warranty whose applicability turns on which answer to the problem eventually proves correct; a definition of "first sighting"; a repeat-testing clause that is triggered if the patient names the shapes correctly straight away; a consent clause permitting the case to be written up as a contribution to a dispute that is by now several centuries old; an FAQ entry for the patient who succeeds immediately and one for the patient who cannot do it at all; and a complaints procedure for cross-modal transfer failure. The target is the habit of treating a thought experiment as though a clinical trial could settle it, and the way institutional language launders a metaphysical dispute into a policy question — not blind patients, and not surgeons.
