I've come around to the view that dreams aren't experiences — that nothing is going on experientially during sleep which the waking report is a report of. It's the Malcolm line as I read it (the only criterion for a dream is the waking telling, so there's nothing for the telling to be about), and Dennett's cassette picture from the 70s says the same thing in empirical dress: the whole dream gets composed at the moment of waking and then fed to you as if you'd just lived through it. I want the case against this, sorted by how much it should actually move me.

Some things I already know about, so don't just hand them back to me as news: the eye-movement signalling experiments where sleepers supposedly answer questions from inside a dream, the old studies where spraying water on someone or saying their name gets worked into the dream, and the fact that people with REM behaviour disorder act their dreams out physically. For each of those I want to know whether it's a real defeater or something the Malcolm/Dennett side can absorb, and what the absorbed version costs them. I also want the objections the defenders themselves would concede as counting against them, not just the ones critics like to throw. And give me an honest answer on whether this position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — if it has, say so, and say whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind or just the price of a conceptual claim.

One more thing, a thought I had: suppose someone built a brain decoder that read dream content out in real time while the subject was still asleep, and it matched the waking report word for word — does that break the view, or does the defender just say the decoder is picking up the cassette a few seconds before the tape plays? I'd like to know what would have to happen for the view to be genuinely in trouble rather than just re-described.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
