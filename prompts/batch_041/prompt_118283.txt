Last month a colleague closed a departmental discussion about our hiring plan by observing that we should be aiming at 'equality of opportunity, not equality of outcome', and the remark was received as though it settled the matter. I keep meeting the same sentence doing very different work — in arguments about Norway's boardroom quota, and in the Indian controversy over reservation, where the live question often seems to be not whether a quota is acceptable but what the quota is a quota on: seats, candidates, applicants, or eventual success rates.

Please give me a menu rather than a verdict on the sentence. Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making, ranging from the trivial through the substantive to the false, and make sure to include at least these: that opportunity and outcome are conceptually separable, so a policy can equalize one without touching the other; that opportunity is the only thing of intrinsic value here; that outcome, as the phrase's users intend it, means group-proportional representation rather than individual results; that differences in outcome are legitimate when they track choice rather than circumstance; and that a policy aimed at outcomes is impermissible even when it would produce more equal opportunities. For each reading, say what it would take to be true, whether it is plausible, and — the part I care about most — whether it would actually license my colleague's objection to a numerical target. Then say which reading the phrase's repeaters usually mean, and whether the sentence can do the work it did in my meeting without the separability claim, given that opportunities are themselves distributed by prior outcomes.

Say how confident you are in each verdict, and identify the single step in the analysis you are least sure of.
