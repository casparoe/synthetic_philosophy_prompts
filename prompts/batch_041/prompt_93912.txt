A week ago a friend and I spent most of a Saturday arguing about Nietzsche's essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I have not been able to think about teaching it the same way since. I run the second-year seminar in philosophy of history at a mid-sized university: sixteen students, one ninety-minute session on that essay, a whiteboard and nothing else. They will have read it in translation; a third of them will arrive certain it is a polemic against history itself, and most of the rest will assume the three modes are a list to be memorized. I need a plan for how to explain the essay to them — not the explanation itself, and not a lecture outline that recites monumental, antiquarian, and critical in the order the text happens to present them.

Two things my friend said that I could not answer on the day, and that should shape the session. First, she argued that the essay's appeal to life is not a criterion but a sorting device: whatever Nietzsche approves of gets called life-serving, whatever he dislikes gets diagnosed as a symptom, so the whole argument reduces to taste dressed in biological vocabulary. Second, and more useful to me, she pointed out that the three modes do not exhaust the ways the past is actually used. The dominant use of the past in our own setting, she said, is one the essay never names: the past as somewhere to visit — a heritage site, an anniversary, a documentary — which gives you a pleasant relation to the past and obliges you to do nothing whatsoever with it. She calls this the anaesthetic use, since it produces the feeling of connection at none of the cost. I came back with a test I think may answer her first objection: for any use of the past, ask what it obliges you to do tomorrow morning. Monumental, antiquarian, and critical history all have answers. The anaesthetic use has none. I want you to tell me whether that test actually rescues the essay's criterion or merely relabels her objection — and I want the session built so that the students arrive at it themselves.

Plan the session for me, covering:

The order the components must come in and why each depends on what precedes it. Specifically: what has to be in place before the three modes can do any work at all; where the anaesthetic use should enter (I suspect after the three, so that the students generate it rather than receive it, but I am persuadable); and where the super-historical standpoint belongs — the standpoint, as I read it, from which the world is already complete at every moment and nothing new needs to happen. I cannot decide whether that is a fourth mode, a limit case of the other three, or the cure.

The single carrying example, and what it must have and lack. My candidate is a mill town whose works closed thirty years ago, where three proposals now compete: a cooperative that wants to run the old floor again and cites the 1930s strike as proof that action is possible; a preservation society that wants the site kept exactly as it was and will not permit a single change; and a campaign to rename the square after the workers killed in the 1954 fire and to name the owners who knew. Tell me whether that example can carry the whole session, or whether something smaller — an inherited house, a single object, the way a department remembers its own founding — would do it better, and why the obvious candidates are worse: the toppled statue, the war memorial, family genealogy, and Nietzsche's own Renaissance and Roman illustrations.

The misconceptions, in the order they will form, and the point in the sequence where I should head each one off. My guesses: that critical history means critical thinking or source criticism; that the three modes are a taxonomy of historians or of periods rather than attitudes one person can occupy in a single afternoon; that monumental means hero worship and is therefore the discredited one; that the essay is an argument for forgetting; that life means biological or national vitality, which worries me most given what was later made of him; and that the three modes are cleanly separable.

One question or task for the last ten minutes that will show me whether the students can run the modes and the test on material of their own, rather than recite the terms.

And what I should refuse to explain at this level, said out loud rather than left implicit.

No readings, no secondary literature, no names of commentators: I do not want the students, or myself, leaning on authority. I am asking for the dependency structure and the misconception map, which is where the actual difficulty lies.
