A friend and I argued late into the night about imprecise probabilities, and I want the dispute written up and driven to a verdict. The issue is dilation. An agent's uncertainty is represented not by a single probability function but by a set of them, so that each event has a lower and an upper probability rather than one number. Suppose she is about to learn a fact E, and consider an event A that, by her own model, E tells her nothing about: conditional on the unknown quantity the two share, E is no evidence about A at all. One would expect learning E to leave her interval for A untouched, or to narrow it. Yet there are cases in which conditioning every function in her set on E strictly widens the interval for A — the lower bound falls and the upper bound rises — so that she is less sure about A after learning E than she was before.

My friend treats this as a reductio: a theory on which acquiring information can make one less confident about a proposition the information was supposed to be irrelevant to cannot be a theory of rational belief, and the remedy is to insist on a single probability function. I think he is mistaking a real defect for a misdescription of what the agent is doing, and that, stated carefully, the phenomenon is not embarrassing at all.

Write an essay that adjudicates. Set out the phenomenon precisely enough for a reader who has never met it, then give my friend's objection its strongest form and the best replies open to the imprecise-probabilist. Reach a verdict and defend it. If you side with my friend, say what must replace imprecise probabilities and what the replacement costs; if you side with me, say exactly what the theory must concede — about confirmation, about what learning does, or about how a set of probabilities is to be interpreted — for the phenomenon to be benign. Do not end by saying the answer depends on one's commitments: commit, and say what would change your mind.
