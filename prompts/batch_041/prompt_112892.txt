Retired geophysicist here — thirty-five years of inverse problems, and since I retired I've been reading popular books on computational complexity. Something has been bugging me and I can't tell if it's a real distinction or me being thick.

The way it's always put: P vs NP is about whether finding a solution is harder than checking one, and if P ≠ NP then certain problems are intrinsically hard — no possible intelligence, however clever, can do them. But an NP-completeness result is a worst-case statement, isn't it? It says no algorithm does well on *every* input. In my old job half the problems were officially intractable and yet we solved the instances we actually cared about every single day, because the inputs that show up in nature aren't chosen by somebody trying to make your life hard.

So what I'd like is a really intuitive explanation of the gap between hard in the worst case and hard for the inputs you actually meet. Specifically:

- What does it take to say a problem is hard on average? Does that only make sense once you fix some distribution over inputs, and if so who gets to pick that distribution — what stops it from being picked adversarially? (I gather the cryptography people live or die by exactly this sort of average-case hardness.)
- Why is it so much harder to prove a problem hard on average than hard in the worst case? What's the intuitive obstruction there?
- And then the philosophy bit, which is what I'm really after: if hardness is always relative to a distribution that somebody specified, is it a fact about the problem or a fact about our interests and our encodings? How should one honestly state the claim that some problem is beyond any feasible intelligence, given this worst/average gap? Does the claim survive, or does it quietly need a bridge principle that nobody ever states?

Don't give me a proof, I want the picture — and I'd rather you tell me where my instinct is wrong than just agree with it.
