The Delaney provision in United States food and drug law forbids the approval of any food additive, color additive, or animal drug that has been found to induce cancer in humans or in test animals. The finding of carcinogenicity is itself disqualifying: the provision makes no allowance for how potent the carcinogen is, sets no threshold of acceptable risk, and in principle treats an additive detectable only in trace amounts exactly as it treats one present in bulk, however large the benefit either might bring. Naturally occurring carcinogens already present in foods are tolerated; the prohibition bites on the act of adding. Meanwhile the surrounding regulatory system proceeds by quantitative risk assessment and accepts cancer risks from pesticide residues on raw produce, from contaminants, from occupational exposure, and from ordinary diet, whenever their magnitude is judged acceptable — the agency itself long treated an excess lifetime risk below one in a million as negligible, and Congress removed pesticide residues from the provision's reach in 1996. The same body of law that refuses to trade a possible cancer against a preservative's benefits is content to trade larger cancer risks elsewhere against convenience, cost, and choice.

I want to treat this as a philosophical artefact and reconstruct the theory of rational risk-taking that it presupposes. Four things in particular.

First, what kind of commitment is doing the real work here? Candidate readings: an extreme but otherwise ordinary attitude toward risk; a deontic constraint against imposing risk on people who have not consented to it; a lexicographic priority in which safety outranks every benefit; a precautionary heuristic adopted because dose-response extrapolation to very low doses is unreliable; or nothing theoretical at all, just a political settlement that survived because it touched few substances. Say which of these the practice strictly presupposes, which it merely fits comfortably, and whether the provision's own terms settle the question or leave it open.

Second, test the risk-attitude reading against risk-weighted expected utility. On that view the value of a prospect is not the probability-weighted average of its outcomes: the contribution of each outcome is governed by a risk function applied to the cumulative probability of attaining at least that much, so a rational agent's attitude to risk is located in a transformation of probabilities rather than in the curvature of a utility function. Note that the risk function is on this view a property of the agent, not of a hazard, a decision, or a statute — one and the same function is applied to every prospect the agent faces. Can any such function reproduce a rule that is absolute at arbitrarily small probabilities for one class of hazard, while the same agent trades the same kind of harm against benefit at ordinary rates everywhere else? If not, what exactly does the failure show? Is the provision best understood as a compartmentalized attitude that a theory of instrumental rationality cannot express, as a constraint that is not a risk attitude at all, or as a rule whose real subject is consent rather than probability?

Third, say where the practice is underdetermined by its own commitments — which rival theories would license precisely this arrangement, so that nothing in the practice could decide between them — and which rival views it quietly rules out. In particular, does it presuppose that the proper object of regulatory evaluation is an act of approval rather than a state of affairs, and that a small probability of catastrophe is to be treated as though it were not small, or as though it were one?

Fourth, suppose the agency announced that it would henceforth assess additives by risk-weighted expected utility under a single fixed, moderately risk-averse risk function applied uniformly to all outcomes, benefits foregone included, approving any additive whose risk-weighted value exceeded that of the status quo. Describe concretely what would change: which decisions would reverse, what evidence the agency would now have to gather and what it would be entitled to ignore, how the de minimis practice and the 1996 pesticide carve-out would fare under the new rule, and what defenders of the provision would have to stop claiming.
