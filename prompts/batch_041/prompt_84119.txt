Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Rosa: "if your preferences violate the axioms you can be money-pumped, i.e. there's a sequence of trades you'll accept that leaves you worse off for sure, so the axioms aren't optional for a rational agent." (2) Dev: "the pump only gets going if you add premises nobody states — that you'll trade repeatedly, that you won't notice the pattern and refuse, that you care about the money, that a sure loss is a bad thing, and that anyone bothers to run the pump on you. Spell those out and the conclusion is 'don't be exploitable if someone decides to exploit you', which is prudence, not expected utility maximization." (3) Rosa: "the theorem isn't a pump; it says a coherent preference order just is one that some u and p can represent, and that's what expected utility means." (4) Dev: "that's the thermometer fallacy — you can represent the temperature with a number without the number telling you what to do. And the axioms aren't a package: transitivity looks like a real coherence requirement, completeness doesn't (I can be genuinely undecided between two jobs without being irrational, and a gap isn't pumpable the way a cycle is), and independence — the one that actually delivers expected utility — is the one people violate in Allais, where there's no one-shot pump at all." (5) Rosa: "on completeness, someone who has to choose will choose, and if her choices go one way in one frame and another way in another you can pump her across frames; on Allais, the violator treats a common component differently depending on what it's mixed with, which is exactly the inconsistency the argument is about." (6) Dev: "the common-component story presupposes you can decompose a gamble into pieces whose contributions add up, which is the thing in dispute; and you keep sliding between preferences and choices — if the axioms are about choice, completeness is trivially true and the theorem says nothing, and if they're about preference, an incomplete order can be perfectly coherent and just isn't representable." (7) Rosa: "fine, it shows exploitability, and exploitability is bad; also your own probabilism is defended by an accuracy argument rather than a pump, so demanding that the EU argument be a pump is unfair." (8) Dev: "accuracy works for credences because a credence is an estimate and estimates can be scored for accuracy — there's no accuracy score for a utility function, nobody has ever produced one, so the analogy fails. And the theorems quietly need a rich domain of lotteries and an Archimedean axiom, which are facts about what preferences the agent has, not requirements of coherence, and they're doing real work in the proof."
