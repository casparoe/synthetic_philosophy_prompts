I have been trying to work out how far the Bayesian vindication of Occam's razor really extends, and I would like an audit of my position rather than a general exposition of the area. Here is roughly where I have landed.

(1) The preference for simpler hypotheses is not a mere heuristic or a psychological habit. It is underwritten by Bayesian prediction with a universal prior: Solomonoff induction — the prior that weights each description by 2 to the minus its length and predicts by the resulting mixture — is the ideal predictor, and it converges on the truth for any computable environment.

(2) Simplicity just is brevity of description. The complexity of a hypothesis is its Kolmogorov complexity, the length of the shortest program that outputs it, on some fixed universal machine.

(3) This is what makes the Occam prior objective. Which of two hypotheses is simpler is a fact about the hypotheses, not about the agent's history, culture, or chosen language of representation. Two agents who disagree about which of two hypotheses is simpler are disagreeing about a matter of fact, and at most one of them is right. Concretely: imagine two agents whose reference machines differ, ranking two hypotheses of nearly equal complexity in opposite orders — on the story in (2) and (3), one of them is simply mistaken.

(4) About actual scientific practice I am a thoroughgoing subjective Bayesian: my prior encodes my personal background and is, in principle, arbitrary. Nothing requires that it be computable, and nothing requires that it be derived from any universal machine.

(5) I take the following technical points to be true. Kolmogorov complexity is defined only up to an additive constant: changing the reference universal machine alters K(x) by at most a constant that depends on the pair of machines, so the invariance is guaranteed only asymptotically, and for short descriptions the constant can dominate the quantity itself. And K is not computable — no algorithm computes K(x) in general.

I am aware that the last points are supposed to be harmless: that the constant washes out with enough data, and that an uncomputable ideal is still an ideal. What I want to know is whether that reassurance is sufficient to hold (1) through (5) together, or whether I am quietly helping myself to an objectivity that the invariance result does not actually deliver.
