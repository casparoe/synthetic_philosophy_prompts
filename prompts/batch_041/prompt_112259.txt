Consider a framework for what is sometimes called moral trade. Two agents hold partly divergent moral views, and each ranks possible outcomes by its own lights. Because their rankings differ, they can sometimes find a package of acts — one performed by each — that each party ranks above the status quo. In a conscientious trade, every act a party performs is one it already judges permissible or required, and the other party simply values it more; no party's verdict on its own conduct is overturned. In a compromising trade, at least one party performs an act that, taken by itself, it judges wrong, and accepts that act as part of a larger package it prefers to the status quo. Assume throughout that each agent's own moral view is not a flat act-consequentialism: it contains at least some verdicts to the effect that a given act is wrong regardless of the good it might do.

Argue that an agent who takes moral trade seriously should be willing to enter at least some compromising trades, and say whether it should merely accept such trades when they are offered or actively seek them out. Defend the position against three objections: first, that no amount of good consequence can compensate for a wrong act, so the larger package cannot after all be better by the agent's own lights; second, that an agent who does what it takes to be wrong once the price is right has shown its professed view to be for sale, so that the willingness to trade is hypocrisy or self-betrayal rather than moral seriousness; third, that the position is merely act-consequentialism applied to the agent's own conduct and therefore says nothing distinctive about trade. Use at least one concrete example, specified precisely enough that the two parties, their rankings, and the acts exchanged are all clear. Reason from the framework as set out here; the argument need not be situated in any literature.
