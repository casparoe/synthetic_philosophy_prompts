I'm a retired ecologist — thirty-odd years in a forestry and wildlife department, mostly population and community ecology, and I only started reading philosophy after I retired, so go easy on the jargon but please don't water down the ecology. There's a claim I keep bumping into in the animal-ethics corner of the internet and I want to know whether it's even sayable in my own language. Roughly: the wild is a charnel house, most animals die young and badly, and so destroying habitat might be a good thing on net, because fewer animals means less total suffering. People usually put it more carefully than that, but thats the gist.

What I want is for you to restate that position as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I actually think in — trophic levels and energy flux, net primary production and transfer efficiency, density dependence and population regulation, additive versus compensatory mortality (the old doomed-surplus idea), r- and K-selected life histories, source-sink and metapopulation structure, disturbance and succession, the way abundance scales with body size, and, where it's relevant, ecology's own half-normative words like health, integrity, degradation and resilience. Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. Which parts of the claim come across cleanly, which ones only fit if I distort something, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Don't cheat by restating the whole thing as "it's about carrying capacity" and calling that a translation — if a concept has to do work it doesn't normally do, say so.

And tell me what you make of the leftover. Is it that ecology can't talk about welfare because its currency is energy and numbers rather than experience, and that's simply a gap in my field — or is it that the philosophers' claim is doing something my field shows to be confused, like treating a flux as if it were an event, or counting deaths when the argument really needs something about lives? I'd also like your honest view on whether the claim could ever be settled with ecological measurements at all, or whether ecology only supplies one premise and all the weight sits on something no field measures.

No headings, bullets, bold or tables please — I read enough of those in my working life. Just write it out as prose, the way you'd write me a long letter.
