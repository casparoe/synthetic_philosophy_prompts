I am a novelist, and I have spent a month revising a chapter that is little more than a character's pleasure — a particular meal, a fever breaking, a difficult sentence finally finished, the warmth of being forgiven — and my readers keep telling me the chapter does not cohere. At a workshop dinner, two friends of mine fell into an argument about why, and it became something I could not settle.

The first, who reads a great deal of philosophy, held that pleasures must share a distinctive felt quality — a tone that all of them possess and nothing else does — since otherwise 'pleasure' is only a heap of unrelated states gathered under one word, and any view that treats pleasure as the good is either empty or trivially true of everything.

The second, a psychologist, said she could find no such tone. The pleasure of a hot bath, of a friend's recovery, of a line that finally works, have nothing felt in common; all they share is that we like them.

The first replied that this makes 'pleasant' mean no more than 'liked,' which is circular — you cannot explain liking by pleasantness and pleasantness by liking — and that it implies nothing is pleasant in itself, which is absurd, since pleasure is precisely something felt.

The second answered that the circularity charge is too quick: liking can be identified independently, by what we seek out, linger over, and smile at, and 'pleasant' may be a relational label of the same kind as 'healthy' or 'poisonous' — a functional kind whose instances share no intrinsic quality. She added that she took the tone to be a second-order attitude of welcoming rather than a sensation, and that bittersweet experiences, in which a single state seems to carry two hedonic tones at once, tell against the felt-quality view rather than for it.

The first said that an attitude of welcoming must have something to welcome: if no state is pleasant in itself, the attitude has no object, and the account explains nothing about why we pursue pleasure at all.

They asked me to referee, and I still have a chapter to finish. Please go through the exchange move by move, saying which of them is right about the felt-quality thesis, the circularity objection, the analogy to functional kinds, the second-order-attitude proposal, and what the bittersweet case shows; then say what both of them are missing — including whether they are still talking about the same thing by the end. Do not simply declare a winner.
