I've been reading rat-adjacent blogs for years and I finally have an argument I can't get out of my head. It's about the ticking bomb case, but with a wrinkle I haven't seen discussed much. Suppose the interrogator isn't certain the suspect knows where the bomb is. She's at 0.7. And there are four other detainees, each with some smaller chance of knowing. The bomb is real, the stakes are huge. Here's the chain:

(1) If torture is permissible in the idealized ticking bomb case — you know the suspect knows, you know he'll talk only under torture, thousands will die otherwise — then it's permissible whenever the same relevant features obtain.
(2) The relevant features are just: enormous stakes, no other way to get the information, and a decent chance the suspect has it.
(3) In the noisy case, the expected lives saved is maximized by torturing all five, since each has some chance of knowing.
(4) So torturing all five is permissible.
(5) But torturing four people who are probably innocent to maybe save lives is monstrous.
(6) So the ticking bomb intuition is false even in the idealized case.

I'd rather keep (1) than (6) — I think the ticking bomb case is a real counterexample to absolutism — but I can't see where the break is. Is it the slide from (1) to (2), or from (2) to (3)? And whichever I give up, what does it cost me? Answer in under 400 words, lead with the step you reject.
