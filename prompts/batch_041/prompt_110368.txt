I'm a third-year PhD student in political philosophy, and I'm trying to get my position on free speech into presentable shape before a workshop. I'd like a consistency audit rather than encouragement. Here is the package as it currently stands.

(1) The harm principle, as I read it, is a necessary condition on legitimate state coercion, speech restrictions included: coercion is justified only by harm to determinate persons, understood as setbacks to their interests, and never by offense, disgust, or diffuse damage to society at large. (2) The case for protecting speech is epistemic rather than autonomy-based: expression matters because fallible authorities cannot be trusted to identify error, and because open contestation improves the quality of belief; I don't think speech has a value that grounds a right independently of that. (3) Because the rationale is epistemic rather than paternalist, the state may never restrict speech for the speaker's own good or to shield hearers from the character they might form. (4) The state may nevertheless prohibit coordinated commercial campaigns whose point is to manufacture doubt about a well-established scientific consensus. What is damaged there is not any one person's interests but what I have been calling the informational commons, the shared background against which everyone forms beliefs, and I take that to be enough to license prohibition. (5) Restrictions must be content- and viewpoint-neutral, though I treat that as a strong defeasible presumption rather than an absolute rule. (6) A sustained campaign of targeted online harassment is not protected, even though each individual message is merely offensive and offensiveness is not harm. (7) The harm principle governs the state; private platforms are not bound by it, and I think attempts to extend it to them are confused, even though I also think the concentration of platform power is now the greatest threat to the epistemic goods that (2) is meant to deliver.

Tell me where this actually comes apart. Rank the tensions by how much repair work each would take, say explicitly which commitment has to give in each case and what the repair costs, and explain which pairs only look inconsistent and why. Finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most.
