I have been compiling, for my own amusement and confusion, a list of sentences that strike me as impossible to say sincerely — not false, exactly, but somehow self-defeating — and I would like to know what principle, if any, governs them. The sentences all concern the same mundane proposition, that the meeting is at noon, which I hope makes the pattern easier to see. These feel unsayable to me: "The meeting is at noon, but I don't believe the meeting is at noon"; "The meeting is at noon, but I believe it is at one"; and, though I am a little less certain, "The meeting is at noon, but I don't know that the meeting is at noon." Yet these feel perfectly sayable: "The meeting is at noon, but I'm not sure it is"; "The meeting is at noon, but I might be wrong"; "The meeting is at noon, but I can't prove it"; "The meeting is at noon, but he doesn't believe it is"; "The meeting was at noon, but I didn't believe it was"; and "The meeting is at noon, but I don't believe I believe it is" — the last one fine, I think, because I can be mistaken about my own mental states.

A friend proposes a tidy principle: asserting a proposition commits the speaker to believing it, so a second clause that denies the speaker's belief contradicts a commitment built into the very act of asserting, whereas clauses about the speaker's evidence, certainty, or other people's beliefs do not. This explains much of my list, but it mishandles the knowledge cases, and I cannot tell whether repairing it should cost me a verdict. If asserting commits me only to believing the proposition, then "but I don't know that it is" ought to be fine, yet it sounds dreadful to me; if asserting commits me to knowing it, then "but I'm not sure it is" ought to be just as bad, yet it sounds fine.

Please give me the principle, or the smallest family of principles, that actually captures my verdicts: state it precisely, identify the case that forces each clause, tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't listed — for instance, "The meeting is at noon, but I only half-believe it is," or "The meeting is at noon, but I don't believe anyone believes it is" — and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, since I would rather abandon a verdict than adopt a gerrymandered principle. Kindly answer in American English.
