A reading group I'm in has been stuck for a year on a question that looks as though a theorem should settle it, and I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than another summary of the two sides. The question is what exactly is at issue between two camps in the literature on bet sizing. One camp holds that an agent facing a long sequence of multiplicative bets should stake the fraction that maximizes the expected logarithm of wealth, on the ground that any other rule is almost surely overtaken by the log-optimal rule in the long run; from this they conclude that log maximization is the rule of rationality for a long-lived agent, and that the characteristic risk-aversion of the rule — modest stakes, never courting ruin — is derived rather than assumed. The other camp replies that maximizing expected log wealth just is expected-utility maximization with a logarithmic utility function; that the expected-utility representation has arguments behind it while the choice of logarithmic utility does not; and that the growth theorem therefore shows at most that one particular preference profile is compatible with one particular betting rule, not that anyone ought to have that profile. On this view, the claim that rationality requires the log rule is either false or a stipulative redefinition of rationality as growth. A third group in the room says the two sides are answering different questions — one about what a person should prefer given her preferences, the other about which strategy keeps an agent in the game — and that each side is smuggling in a conclusion from the other's question.

We agree about all the mathematics, which is what puzzles me. The concrete case that keeps us circling: a single bet at 10:1 odds with a one-in-nine chance of winning, offered once, with no prospect of another. It has positive expected value; the log rule stakes about two percent of one's wealth; a linear-utility maximizer stakes everything. Everyone concedes that across a long sequence of such bets the log rule is the growth-optimal answer and that staking everything is a route to ruin. The fight is about the one-shot case, where the theorem's hypothesis — many repetitions — plainly fails, and yet the geometric-rationality people in the group still recommend the fractional stake. When pressed, one of them made an argument I cannot evaluate: that averaging over the branches of an ensemble is the wrong average for an agent who will live a single trajectory, so that the expected-value calculation answers a question about a population of copies rather than about you.

My hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis — is that the dispute is verbal at the level of the slogan that the log rule is a rival to expected-utility theory, but substantive at the level of the claim that one ought to have logarithmic utility, and that the real residue is a disagreement about whether expectation over an ensemble correctly represents a single agent's fate. Tell me whether that is right. I want the diagnosis: is this a verbal dispute about what the word rational is a predicate of, a substantive normative dispute about which averaging operation represents an agent's situation, a scope error about whether an asymptotic result concerning repetition bears on one-shot choices, a disagreement about what decision theory is for — preference coherence or survival — or something else, including a hidden mathematical question I have not noticed. Say what each side is actually committed to, and which of these is doing the work. Then say what, if anything, would count as progress here: a formal result, a distinction, a restriction of the theorem's scope, or an argument that no progress should be expected because the camps have different subject matters. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question; take a definite position rather than laying out the options for me to choose among.
