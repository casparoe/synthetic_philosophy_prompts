ok so background: I'm a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy, I work mostly on punishment and social norms, and through a series of bad decisions I'm currently the grad rep on our university's Committee on Free Expression and Campus Climate. Next Thursday the committee holds an open hearing on a resolution to formally censure a professor in my department over an op-ed she published last month. Honestly? Some kind of public response seems warranted to me — the piece was cruel, and not in a subtle way — but the resolution as drafted is a mess, and I got asked to speak. Seven minutes at the mic, then questions.

I want you to be a philosopher of criminal law who also happens to be a very good speechwriter, and to write me that speech. Think carefully about the room before you write: two law profs who will notice immediately if the argument is sloppy, a handful of student organizers who will tune out the second it sounds like a seminar paper, an administrator taking notes, a couple of faculty who think the whole hearing is a kangaroo court, and a reporter in the back row. It's mixed and it's tense and I have to land for all of them without saying anything I'd have to walk back later.

Here's the frame I want the speech built on, and it's the reason I'm not just going to stand up and say something mushy about civility. The interesting question isn't "is this cancel culture" — it's whether a public condemnation is a *punishment*, and if it is, whether it meets the conditions we'd demand of any punishment: who has standing to impose it, by what procedure, at what severity, and for how long. I've been kicking around a distinction I haven't seen put quite this way, and I want it to do the structural work in the speech: every sanction has a verdict-function (it declares that a norm was broken) and a sentence-function (it imposes a cost on someone). A crowd can issue a verdict. A crowd cannot set a sentence. And I think almost everything awful about deplatforming campaigns lives in that gap — because when nobody has jurisdiction to set the sentence, the only way to show you're serious is to escalate, so the severity ends up set by a competition rather than by the norm that was actually violated. I call that the ratchet and I'd like the speech to make it vivid with one concrete image, not a lecture.

Some things I need it to do: take seriously the objection that she's *already* been punished informally (the pile-on, the emails, whatever), so a formal censure might just be piling on — I want an answer to that, not a dodge. And the last paragraph has to give the committee something concrete to do: an amendment, a condition, a procedural requirement, not just a vibe.

Two more constraints. Don't cite anyone, don't name any scholars, don't quote anybody, and don't invent statistics or say "studies show" — if you need a number, say the obvious thing plainly instead. And keep the jargon out; if you use a term of art, unpack it in the same breath.

Before the speech, give me a short brainstorm: the cheap moves I must not make (I don't want the both-sides mush, I don't want "the remedy for bad speech is more speech," I don't want free-speech absolutism either), and the three or four moves the speech has to make in order, in the order you'd actually say them. Then the speech itself, about 800 words, first person, plain register, speakable out loud, a little dry humor is fine as long as it isn't at her expense.
