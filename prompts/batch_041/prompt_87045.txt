Last month a reading group I'm in spent an hour arguing about the killing of Marius, the young male giraffe at Copenhagen Zoo in February 2014, and we got nowhere because nobody would stay on the theory. I want you to do the disciplined version. The facts, as I understand them, are these: a healthy two-year-old bull, bred at the zoo, was judged genetically redundant because his genes were already well represented in the European breeding programme; the zoo declined offers from other collections to take him, on the grounds that those collections were outside the programme's rules or that their space was better used for a genetically more valuable animal; he was put down, publicly dissected as an educational demonstration, and parts of him were fed to the zoo's carnivores; the zoo's scientific director defended the whole sequence as open and responsible population management; Denmark's main animal protection organisation backed the zoo, while much of the international public reacted with outrage and some people sent death threats to staff. If I have any of that wrong in a way that matters, say so rather than building on it — and don't worry about citations or further reading, since I'm after the argument.

Analyse the episode through the lens of speciesism, and be precise about what the lens is. Work out separately what the sentientist line that asks only for equal consideration of interests would say about each decision point — the culling, the refusal of the transfer offers, the dissection, the feeding — and what a rights-based line that asks whether a giraffe is a subject of a life would say; then bring in the framework Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka develop in Zoopolis, which argues that questions of intrinsic capacity and moral status need to be supplemented by a political theory of animals' relations to human communities — domesticated animals as citizens of mixed communities, wild animal communities as sovereign, and liminal animals living among us as denizens. Ask the question that framework forces and the others don't: what was Marius politically? A captive-bred animal born into a human institution that had assumed ongoing responsibility for him looks like a candidate citizen with claims against the community that bred him, which would make the transfer offers not a favour but a matter of his entitlement; but I want you to say whether that classification is doing real work here or is just a redescription of the intuition that the zoo owed him something. Then reverse the direction: tell me what the episode does to the speciesism charge. Is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated — an institution that sincerely professes care for animals killing a healthy one not for welfare reasons but for the sake of a gene pool, and doing so with the blessing of an animal welfare organisation while its angriest critics are a public with no institutional standing at all? And be honest about the silences: say which parts of the episode none of these frameworks has the resources to address, and whether the population-genetic logic the zoo was operating under is best described as speciesism at all or as something else that happens to have the same victims. End by telling me which framework, if any, the case favours, and what it shows about the comfortable claim that speciesism is simply one prejudice among others.
