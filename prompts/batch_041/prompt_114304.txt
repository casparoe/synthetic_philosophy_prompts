I have been trying to state the principle behind my function ascriptions, and my verdicts keep pulling in different directions. I would like you to find the principle, or the best small set of principles, that captures them, and to tell me which verdicts I should abandon. I am working with the distinction between a trait's having a function because of its history of selection (the selected-effect view) and its having a function because of its current causal contribution to fitness (the causal-role view), but I do not want to assume either account. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A bacterium acquires a mutation that makes a pump expel an antibiotic. The mutation arose this morning; the bacterium has not divided since. I say the pump already has the function of expelling the antibiotic.

2. A cave fish retains eyes, but they are covered by skin and no longer connected to the brain. I say the eyes have no function.

3. A bird species has lost flight; its wings are now used in courtship displays. I say the wings' function is display, not flight.

4. A human heart has a congenital defect and cannot pump enough blood. I say it still has the function of pumping blood, even though it is malfunctioning.

5. A callus forms on a laborer's hand from repeated friction. I say the callus has the function of protecting the hand, even though it is not heritable and was not selected for.

6. A computer program has a bug that reliably crashes it. I say the bug does not have the function of crashing the program, even though it reliably does so.

7. A village performs a rain dance. It does not cause rain, but it strengthens social bonds. I say the dance's function is social bonding, not rain-making.

8. Human blood is red. I say its being red does not have the function of signaling danger, even if it sometimes does.

9. Feathers first evolved for thermoregulation; later they were co-opted for flight. I say their function now is flight, not thermoregulation.

10. The human tailbone is a vestige of a tail. I say it has no function now.

11. A tan develops after sun exposure and protects the skin. I say the tan has the function of protection, even though it is not heritable.

12. A gene is silenced by methylation in a lineage where it was once selected for a metabolic function. I say the silenced gene has no function now.

13. A species has a trait that is currently neutral but was selected for in the past. I say it has no function now.

14. A byproduct of a developmental process, such as the navel, is not something I would say has a function, even if it reliably occurs and can be used for something.

I suspect the principle is something like: for biological traits, a function is a current causal contribution to fitness, except that a malfunctioning trait retains the function it was selected for; for artifacts, a function is what it was designed to do; and byproducts have no function. But I am not sure this is one principle or three, and I do not know what it says about cases where selection and current contribution come apart in the other direction, such as a trait that was selected for F but now contributes to fitness by G, or a trait that was never selected for but now contributes to fitness by F.
