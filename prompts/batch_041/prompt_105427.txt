I have been reconstructing Descartes's argument for the claim that nonhuman animals are mere machines, and my reconstruction strikes me as proving more than the argument can afford. I would like you to identify the step I should give up and to say what giving it up would cost. The reconstruction runs as follows.

(1) We are entitled to attribute thought to a being other than ourselves only on the evidence of its behavior, and in particular only when its behavior cannot be explained by purely mechanical causes.

(2) Descartes maintains that animal behavior can be explained mechanically, by the disposition and motion of the organs, and he treats as decisive the fact that no animal has ever used language or any other conventional sign to make its thoughts known, whereas even the least intelligent human being can.

(3) Therefore no nonhuman animal thinks.

(4) Descartes also holds that thinking and being conscious are one and the same thing: every thought is a mode of awareness, so a being that does not think has no experience at all.

(5) Therefore no nonhuman animal has any experience, and so none of them suffers or enjoys anything.

(6) A human infant, however, and a human being with a severe cognitive disability, cannot use language either.

(7) Steps (1) through (5), applied to them, yield the conclusion that they too have no experience.

(8) That conclusion is absurd, so at least one of the steps above must be rejected.

I can feel the trouble gathering somewhere in the middle of the argument, but I cannot say where it actually lies. A friend proposes an escape: infants and the disabled possess the capacity for language even when they do not exercise it, whereas animals possess no such capacity. But that seems to convert Descartes's criterion from an observation about what creatures actually do into a claim about an inner power that no behavior could ever reveal, and I do not see how it could then carry the confident verdict he wants.

I would most like to keep the criterion in (1), since without it I do not see how anyone could have grounds for attributing a mind to another being at all, and I am willing to give up (5). Tell me which step is faulty, why it looked innocent, and what I would have to pay to reject it. If the alternatives cost different things — for instance, whether I must admit a kind of sensory awareness that is not thought, whether I must abandon the empirical footing of the criterion, or whether I must instead weaken what Descartes means by thought — lay out the prices so I can see what I am buying.
