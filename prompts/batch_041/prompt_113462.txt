I'm teaching a 3L seminar on legislation and statutory interpretation and I'm putting together the take-home final. I want one big essay question — 90 minuets, 30 marks out of 100 — and I'm tired of the answers I get when I use real cases, becuase half the class has already read the opinion and just paraphrases the majority and the dissent back at me. So I built a fake statute. Can you write me the question, a model answer of about 600-700 words, and a marking scheme that says out loud what seperates an A from a B?

Here's the statute (tighten the wording if you like, just keep the moving parts):

"The Automated Delivery Device Act
1. No automated delivery device shall operate on a public sidewalk or in a crosswalk without a permit issued under this chapter.
2. 'Automated delivery device' means a self-propelled machine that transports goods to a customer's address.
3. This chapter does not apply to a device that is under the continuous physical control of a natural person.
4. The department shall issue a permit to any applicant who pays the fee and files a safety plan."

Two facts about how it passed: the first draft said "except devices weighing less than fifty pounds," and that clause was struck in conference at the last minute; and when a reporter asked about the little ones, the floor sponsor said the small-robot question was "a mess" and that they were "leaving it to the department." Also, the department has since announced a moratorium on new permits pending a study, so in practice the small robots are off the sidewalks and the big ones are free to roam.

The two devices I want the question to turn on: (a) a six-pound robot that navigates by itself and is watched by one human in a call center who supervises forty of them and can take over remotely when something goes wrong; (b) a two-hundred pound robot that a worker walks behind holding a dead-man switch. Under the words as written, (b) looks exempt and (a) is the one that needs a permit — the opposite of what the sponsors say they were trying to do.

What I want the question to force: first, that students can't just announce the text is clear; they have to locate the ambiguity (is "continuous physical control" about proximity, about who holds the joystick, about who can intervene in time?). Second, that the best evidence of purpose here is a provision that was deliberately deleted, which is a trap, since the natural move is to say the purpose was to keep big heavy robots off sidewalks — but they considered a size line and took it out. Third, that both theories have to cope with a legislature that openly declined to decide. And then the meta-question: do textualism and purposivism actually disagree about the outcome here, or only about how to justify the same outcome, and what does that tell you about whether the debate is about meaning at all.

Please write it so the model answer doesn't lean on real cases, names, or quotations — I don't want students or a grader going down a rabbit hole, and I'd rather the answer stand on the hypothetical plus the general concepts (plain meaning, absurdity, the canons, deference, dynamic interpretation, and so on). In the marking scheme, break the 30 marks down and name the specific moves an A has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks — e.g. what it looks like when someone just recites "text versus intent" without noticing the inversion, or treats the deleted clause as straightforward evidence of purpose.
