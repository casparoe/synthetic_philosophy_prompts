Last night a friend and I argued until nearly two in the morning about whether rehabilitation is a real alternative to punishment or only a gentler way of doing the same thing to a person, and I have not stopped thinking about it, because on Thursday I have to give a speech at the retirement dinner of the woman I have worked under for eighteen years — a prison governor retiring after three decades, in a system that is not Norway's and never will be. I need you to write that speech.

Some material to work with. The room holds about eighty people. Roughly half are current and former officers from her prisons, people who have been assaulted at work, who think she spent her last decade going soft, and who will hear any sentence beginning "what we owe the people in our care" as a sentence about them. The other half are her family, a handful of volunteers from a reentry charity, and three men she hired after they served time under her. The speech has to land with both halves. Two things she actually did, if you want concrete anchors: she ended the routine strip-searching of everyone returning from a visit, and she required officers to knock and wait before entering a cell, which cost her more goodwill on the wings than any other decision she made.

The idea I want doing the structural work is one my friend and I could not agree on. The Norwegian Correctional Service states its principle of normality this way: "The punishment is the restriction of liberty; no other rights have been removed by the sentencing court." Elsewhere in the same account: "You need a reason to deny a sentenced offender his rights, not to grant them." My friend's objection is that this is a doctrine for a small, rich, homogeneous country with a twenty-one-year maximum, and that where a sentence already consumes a person's housing, work, family, and health, the principle is either a lie or a piece of bookkeeping — the loss of liberty is total, so subtracting nothing from it and adding nothing to it are the same gesture. My reply, which I am not sure survives, is that this is exactly why the principle matters: precisely because the deprivation is total, the whole work of the principle is to force every additional deprivation to be argued for rather than assumed. My friend calls the view that a prison should be a project for making someone better the project view, and the view that a sentence is a subtraction and nothing more the subtraction view, and insists that "rehabilitation" is just the project view in its Sunday clothes. The speech should be built on the subtraction view without ever preaching it, and without conceding that the officers in the room are the villains of the story.

Write it as a speech of roughly six to seven minutes, in six labelled parts, in this order and with roughly these lengths: "The room" (about 100 words, greeting both halves and naming the tension without resolving it); "What she did, without the theory" (about 200 words of concrete detail, no abstraction); "The argument I lost last night" (about 250 words, where the two views of prison are put to work); "What I will not say" (about 120 words, naming the sermon I am refusing to give and the concession I am making to the people who think she went soft); "The charge and the toast" (about 90 words); and then a final section titled "Note on what I conceded" (about 150 words, addressed to me, explaining what the speech gives away to my friend's argument and why I could not say the rest out loud in that room).
