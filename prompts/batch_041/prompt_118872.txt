I'm a journalist — I write long features on science and ideas, mostly history-of-thought stuff — and I'm about six weeks into a piece on Anscombe's charge that Truman was a murderer: the 1956 business where she kicked up a small scandal over Oxford handing him an honorary degree, and the wider question of what we're meant to do with the bombings now. The thing I keep running into, in interviews and in my own notes, is an analogy that does an enormous amount of work for people, and it turns up in one of two forms. The deflationary version goes: an atomic bomb is just a bigger bomb. One plane, one weapon, one city, versus the thousand-plane incendiary raids over Tokyo and Dresden, which killed at least as many people and probably more; if you're not out campaigning against the firebombing campaign, you've got no business making Truman the special case. The absolutist version runs the same comparison the other way: fine, then the area bombing was murder too, and Anscombe was right about Truman, she just wasn't radical enough about everyone else who signed those orders. Both rest on the same mapping — one weapon to a fleet of planes, one decision to a standing policy, concentrated force to dispersed force, one act to a campaign — and I can't tell whether the comparison is doing the arguing or just decorating it.

So what I'd like from you is an audit of the analogy. Start with the correspondence table, spelled out: what plays the role of what, and at what level of description — an act-token or an act-type, a decision, an order, a policy, two years of a habit of burning cities, the man who signs versus the machine that carries it out — plus what the picture quietly assumes (that harm is a single quantity you can sum across weapons, that acts come pre-packaged as units you can count, that a weapon's kind is irrelevant next to its yield). Then the places it breaks — and here's the bit I'm most intrested in, because my lay hunch is that Anscombe's objection isn't really about magnitude at all: as I understand it she's saying the thing that makes it murder is who gets deliberately killed and why, so 'bigger' might be orthogonal to the whole arguement, and the parity could be true and irrelevant at the same time. But I may have that wrong. Tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the work the comparison is being made to do and which are just scenery, whether either conclusion survives once they're on the table (my guess is the deflationary one doesn't and the absolutist one might, but on grounds the analogy doesn't supply), and if there's a better comparison for what's really in dispute, what it is.

And one more thing, since I'll be quoting you-ish in a piece that a lot of people will shout about: please seperate the parts where people who've worked on this actually agree from the parts where they don't. I don't want a contested reading of Anscombe served up as the consensus, and if something is genuinely up for grabs I'd much rather you say so than smooth it over.
