My friend Dana is a retired actuary and she will not shut up about Keynes on probability — the thing where two arguments can hand you the exact same probability and still not be the same argument, because one of them has more weight sitting underneath it, meaning more relevant evidence actually piled up behind the number. More evidence can even lower the probability and still raise the weight, she says. She also insists this is a seperate idea from being scared of unknown odds and that everyone keeps collapsing the two. Fine. But I want to know what rule she's actually running, so here's her record, and then a new case I want to spring on her.

Her record: anything with a huge documented history behind it — millions of observations, odds you could look up in a table — she takes a small edge on without blinking. She'll also take a 1-in-50 longshot when the payout is a bit better than the long odds justify, as long as the machine generating it has thousands of logged runs. So it's not just that she's scared of losing money. And then a one-off thing where the only basis was an analogy to four vaguely similar cases, and where the people who'd actually know ranged from 5% to 60% and wouldnt commit to a number: she passed, and said something rude about the odds being mush.

The new case: a newly designed building component. The engineers put the chance of a particular failure next year at 1 in 500, and they'll defend that number to the death — but it comes out of a physics model, not data. The design has never actually been fielded, so the track record is literally zero. I can offer Dana a bet at odds where 1 in 500 makes it a clearly good deal, stakes are a few hundred quid.

Predict what she does, and give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion: the candidate rules that fit her record so far; what each of those rules predicts for the component bet; your best guess and why; the other case I should put to her that would actually seperate those rules, plus what each combination of answers would tell me; and finally whether her record adds up to one coherent position at all — is the Keynes thing really a different idea from plain aversion to unknown odds, and would Keynes himself even sign off on whatever rule she turns out to be running.
