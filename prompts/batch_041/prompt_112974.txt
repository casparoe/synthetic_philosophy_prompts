My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about a claim of his that I cannot shake. He says that in a sufficiently large world — one containing enough other agents in situations relevantly like mine, reasoning as I do — I can have reason to cooperate with those agents even though nothing I do will ever reach them and nothing they do will ever reach me. His argument is that my choice is evidence about their choices: if they are the sort of agents who cooperate when I cooperate, then my cooperating raises the probability that they cooperate, and the good they do is enormous, so the expected value of acting well can be enormous even when the causal effect of my action is exactly zero. I said this proves far too much — by the same reasoning, my choice of breakfast is evidence about the breakfasts of distant civilizations, and I can blackmail myself into anything by imagining a large enough world of agents disposed to reward me for it. He said that is precisely the point, and that the size of the world is what makes it true. I want to run a session that lets a group feel the force of his view before either of us names it.

I teach a graduate seminar in decision theory and practical reason — fourteen students, one ninety-minute meeting, a room with a whiteboard, a projector, and a stack of index cards. I want an activity, not a lecture. The group should run into the phenomenon themselves: they should make choices under rules that make ordinary causal reasoning look obviously correct, and then, without my announcing any change, find themselves in a second phase where the very reasoning that looked like a mistake begins to look compelling. By the end they should see why the size of the world matters, why a choice can be rational in virtue of what it is evidence for rather than what it causes, and why that thought is dangerous enough that they should feel the objection as well as the appeal. The activity should not mention my friend's idea, or any name for it, until the debrief.

Please design it. Give me the setup and rules; the materials and how to distribute them; the payoff structure, using points or tokens rather than real money but with stakes the group will actually care about for the hour; a timeline for the ninety minutes; and any roles or subgroup divisions, with a clear account of who interacts with whom and who does not. Include a way for the group to record its choices so that the debrief can refer to what actually happened rather than to what I hoped would happen. Assume no internet and no computation beyond what fourteen people can do by hand.

Then give me the debrief. It should map particular moments in the activity onto the parts of the idea — the correlation between agents who never interact, the absence of any causal channel, the difference between acting on evidence and acting on influence, and the role of the world's size. It should also make the group feel the pressure of the objection I raised to my friend, not just the appeal of his view. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. And be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself; I expect the sheer scale of the world, and the question of whether the other agents really reason as we do, to be the hard ones.
