A Programmer's Take on the Separateness of Persons

I want to examine the objection that utilitarianism treats society as though it were a single person, summing everyone's welfare into one total to be maximized, and in doing so fails to take seriously that people are separate.

Set up a state of affairs as a vector of welfares, one entry per person. Simple utilitarianism says the value of the state is the sum of the entries, or more generally some increasing function of their total. The structural feature that matters is that this value depends only on the multiset of welfares: it is invariant under permuting the entries. Swap two people's welfare levels and the verdict does not change. Call this property person-anonymity.

Stated loosely, the separateness objection denies person-anonymity: it says that who holds which welfare matters. Taken literally, that cannot be right. No one thinks a world where A is at ten and B at two is better than one where A is at two and B at ten merely because of which name received the ten. That would make moral value depend on arbitrary labels and would make impartiality impossible. So the objection cannot really be a denial of person-anonymity.

What the objection needs, I think, is a different claim, which I will call non-fungibility. Because utilitarianism looks only at the total, it treats one person's welfare as substitutable for another's: a gain of five to A can cancel a loss of five to B and the ledger balances. The objection is that there is no such ledger. B's loss is not compensated by A's gain, not because the totals fail to match but because B is not A. The person whose loss it is has a standing that the total cannot absorb.

Two consequences seem to follow. First, the objection is not really about aggregation, that is, about summing, at all; it is about the assumption that individual welfares are commensurable and transferable on a single scale. A non-additive aggregator, say one that is concave in each person's welfare, still pools them, and so would be vulnerable to the same complaint. Second, the objection does not by itself tell us to maximize anything else; it tells us that a certain class of moves, transfers that balance only in the total, is illegitimate.

Here is the part I find genuinely interesting. Non-fungibility, which sounds like it makes morality more restrictive, is also what makes compromise and trade possible. If welfare were fungible, and only the total mattered, there would be no need for two people to agree to anything; the aggregator would simply reallocate. It is precisely because each person's welfare is his or her own that consent, exchange, and negotiation are needed at all. On this reading, separateness is the precondition of gains from trade rather than an obstacle to them.

I suspect this reframing either is a standard move I have merely rediscovered, or proves too much, or quietly changes the subject from persons to welfare. I would like comments, and I would find the following most useful:

1. Accuracy. Does the essay state the objection and the replies to it correctly? Flag any misrepresentation, and in particular any place where I have attributed a view to a position that does not actually hold it.

2. The central distinction. Is the contrast between person-anonymity and non-fungibility a real distinction with work to do, or a distinction without a difference? Is there a reading of the objection on which person-anonymity itself is denied, and if so, is that reading defensible on its own terms?

3. The trade claim. Does the claim that separateness grounds gains from trade strengthen the objection, undercut it, or amount to a truism? Does it prove too much by making every interpersonal comparison illegitimate, or too little by conceding the aggregator everything it needs?

4. Diagnosis. Which of my three suspicions is correct, and why? If the essay equivocates between welfare and persons, point to the sentence where it happens.

5. Counterexample. What is the strongest case that tells against the central distinction, and what would the essay have to say about it to survive?
