I write the ideas beat at a general-interest magazine, and I've been asked to produce a satirical piece built on the toxin puzzle — the thought experiment in which you are offered a large sum for forming, now, the intention to drink a foul but non-lethal toxin tomorrow, the money being paid on the intention alone, with nothing at all hinging on whether you actually drink it.

What I need from you is a draft of that piece: a deadpan mock program announcement, roughly 600 words, formatted as an official press release from a fictional government agency launching a pilot scheme with exactly that structure. Headline; a program name; a quoted line or two from the agency's director; a numbered list of program rules; a statistic or two; a short comment from a skeptical participant or outside critic. The register should be the register of a real public-sector launch — cautious, proud, faintly defensive — and the irony should never break.

The constraints matter more than the jokes. The satire has to come from getting the logic right rather than from inventing absurdity. So: payment is triggered by the verified presence of the intention at the moment of registration and by nothing else; a participant who takes the money and then skips the toxin keeps it, with no penalty and no violation of any rule; and the program's designers are fully aware of this and treat it as a feature rather than a loophole. The piece should be honest about what the puzzle actually turns on — above all the question of whether intending to do something requires predicting that one will do it, and what becomes of a scheme like this if the answer is no. Include one character who proposes a shortcut that would make the intention genuine by brute force (a pill, a hypnotist, a self-binding contract), and let the discomfort around that proposal be visible without anyone in the piece naming it. Let the fairness question surface somewhere too: some participants collect for resolve they may never exercise while others do the unpleasant work.

The target is not the thought experiment; it is the institutional fantasy behind it — that resolve, sincerity, and pre-commitment are inner states a bureaucracy or an employer can audit, purchase, and pay out on, and the small industry of commitment devices that already sells a version of this. The humor should be legible to a reader who has never heard of the puzzle and should not make a philosopher wince. No winking, no narrator's aside explaining the joke, no character who steps out of the fiction to lecture.

After the piece, add one final line, unironic and in plain prose, of the form Takeaway: <one sentence>, stating the single thing the piece is meant to establish.
