So I got dragged into a stupidly long argument with my mate Rowan last night — we were meant to be watching the match but somehow ended up going at it for about two hours about that old donkey between two identical piles of hay, except Rowan kept insisting the whole thing is a non-problem, because obviously a real donkey would just pick one, and once it's picked it's got a reason, so there was never any puzzle to begin with. I said that move is precisely what makes it a puzzle and we went round in circles till his flatmate basically asked us to leave. Anyway I'm now weirdly obsessed with the thing, and I've realised you see this exact logic most of all in advice columns — the ones that tell you that if two options look identical you should just flip a coin and move on, and that "not being able to decide" is always secretly a fear thing you need to work through.

So what I want from you is a parody advice column for the weekend magazine of some made-up paper: deadpan, in character from start to finish, no winking at the reader. Invent the columnist — give her a smug, cosy, faintly therapeutic voice and a name — and have her answer two letters in the one column. The first is from someone genuinely torn between two options that are actually identical in every respect that matters; I'll leave the details to you, but keep it domestic and a bit sad, like two equivalent job offers or two tins of beans. The second is from someone stuck between two things that aren't equal but aren't comparable either — one better in some respects, worse in others, so there's no fact of the matter about which is better overall. The joke is that she gives more or less the same breezy "just pick, and the reason will follow" answer to both letters, and it's genuinely fine for the first and quietly horrifying for the second. I want someone who's read a bit of the decision-theory and value-theory stuff to wince and then laugh, because the distinction she keeps bulldozing — being indifferent between two options versus the options being incommensurable — is a real one, and the humour has to come from her getting the surrounding machinery right rather than from inventing nonsense. Let her be totally confident, let her cite her own invented framework if it helps (maybe she's given it a twee name), sign the letters with daft regional pen-names, and don't let her ever notice the problem she's creating. Around 600-700 words, both letters plus her replies, and please write it in British English. Oh, and don't make it mean-spirited or party-political — it's the genre of advice-giving I'm taking the mickey out of, not any actual columnist.
