I am a novelist at work on a book about a public-health administrator, and I have run into a philosophical puzzle that bears directly on how I render her inner life. The puzzle, as I understand it, is the asymmetry experimental philosophers have turned up in ordinary judgments of intentional action: an agent who knowingly brings about a harmful side effect is described as having brought it about intentionally, whereas an agent who knowingly brings about a comparable beneficial side effect, under otherwise identical conditions, is not. I want a rigorous conceptual explanation of what is actually going on in that asymmetry — not a summary of the finding, which I already possess, but an account of the competing interpretations of it and of what would decide between them.

To keep the discussion honest, please test each interpretation against three variants of a case I have invented for the novel, so that the explanation cannot be delivered from stock examples. In the first, my administrator redirects funds in a way she knows will shorten waiting times for most patients and will also, as a foreseeable consequence, starve a small clinic that serves a stigmatized population; she professes indifference to the clinic and cites only the efficiency gains. In the second, the same redirection benefits the stigmatized clinic and burdens the comfortable majority, and she is equally indifferent to that. In the third, the harm to the small clinic is not merely foreseen but required by a rule she is bound to follow, so that her conduct conforms to an institutional norm even as it damages the clinic.

For each variant, I would like you to say what each of the leading accounts of the asymmetry predicts about whether an ordinary reader would call the outcome intentional, and to flag where the accounts come apart. I am especially interested in whether the asymmetry is really about the moral badness of the side effect, about norm violation more generally, or about the attribution of blame to an agent whose conduct we already want to censure — and in whether the folk concept of intentional action is best treated as a checklist of conditions or as something looser whose application is sensitive to context. Tell me which of my three variants would serve as a clean test between the accounts and which are muddied, and why. Finally, say plainly where the debate currently stands and what a decisive experiment would have to look like, even if no one has run it. I do not need citations, dates, or reading lists; I need the conceptual map, argued rather than recited.
