I want an example that exhibits the fanaticism of expected-value maximization — the charge that an agent who maximizes expected value is committed to preferring a lottery with an enormous payoff and a minuscule chance of winning it over a certain, substantial, ordinary good — and I want the example constructed so that it cannot be waved away by any of the moves that are usually made against the textbook versions. So impose the following constraints on it: keep every payoff finite, so that no appeal to infinite or undefined value is available; use probabilities that can be defended by an ordinary and uncontroversial procedure, so that no appeal to inscrutable, unknowable, or merely verbal probabilities is available; make the decision a single, one-shot choice, so that no long-run-frequency or law-of-large-numbers reply is available; and do not let the case turn on the decision-maker's ignorance of the odds. Set out the numbers explicitly, identify the option that expected-value maximization selects, and show the arithmetic that selects it. The case must be one you construct, not a reprise of the familiar religious-wager or mugging scenarios.

Having given the example, isolate the conceptual issue it raises. I want you to locate the source of the fanatical recommendation: is it a defect of expected-value maximization as such, or is it forced by one of the standard structural assumptions about rational preference — completeness, transitivity, the independence or sure-thing condition, or the continuity (Archimedean) condition that allows a sufficiently desirable outcome to offset a sufficiently small chance of obtaining it? Say which assumption your example actually turns on, and whether giving it up would cost the theory anything it has independent reason to retain.

Then take up the most natural escape — a rule that refuses to let a sufficiently small probability ever outweigh a certain gain — and tell me what such a rule does to the cases where the small probability is one we plainly should not ignore. Does the escape have a principled stopping point, or does it merely relocate the fanaticism to the opposite side? Close by saying, in a sentence or two, what your example establishes about the concept of expected value itself, as distinct from the particular value theory it happens to be paired with.
