I serve on the bylaws committee of the Harborview Neighborhood Planning Council — a midsize city's advisory body on zoning variances, small capital projects, and the disposition of a modest community-benefit fund — and I have to circulate a written opinion before our October vote on a proposed amendment that would replace the council's current elected membership with a sortition-based one. I'll describe the amendment as it now stands, because the details matter more than the general idea. Thirty-five members would be drawn by stratified lottery from the city's adult-resident registry rather than the voter rolls, so that noncitizens, recently arrived residents, and people who have never registered to vote would be eligible; each member would serve a single two-year term with no possibility of renewal and would receive a stipend calibrated to the median hourly wage; service would be compulsory for those drawn, subject to a hardship exemption decided by a three-member panel; and the assembly's decisions would be advisory to the elected city council, which could override them only by a two-thirds vote with written reasons entered into the record. Two further features have generated most of our argument. First, a dissent clause: any group of at least seven members (twenty percent) may attach a minority report that must be published alongside the assembly's recommendation. Second, and this is what I keep coming back to, one faction wants to insert a deliberative filter between the draw and the term of service — draw a pool of five hundred, then choose the final thirty-five by a second lottery restricted to those who complete a free two-weekend course on land use, municipal finance, and the council's enabling statute, with the explicit rationale that a body that cannot read a budget cannot deliberate about one.

That filter is where I think the committee's decision actually turns, and it is why I don't want the opinion to be a general essay for or against lottocracy. The filter's defenders say it is a competence threshold of the mildest kind — nobody is excluded by a test, only by their own choice not to attend — and that a lottery whose winners cannot evaluate the staff's cost estimates merely launders whatever the staff already believed. Its critics say it reintroduces exactly what sortition was supposed to remove: self-selection, and self-selection of a particularly flattering kind, since it will tend to favor people with the leisure and confidence to spend two weekends being told they are qualified to govern. Whichever way the committee goes on the filter, a version of the same objection rebounds on the compulsory-service clause, since the people who would never have volunteered are precisely the ones the lottery is meant to capture, and a course requirement hands them a socially respectable way to drop out. There is also a subtler question I'd like the opinion to take seriously rather than wave at: whether the minority-report clause makes the assembly more legitimate by making its internal disagreement visible, or less legitimate by inviting the city council and the press to shop for the dissent that suits them, so that the published twenty-percent view becomes the only view anyone reads.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate. I want a clear bottom line — adopt the amendment as written, reject it, or adopt it with specific conditions — together with a concrete time frame for implementation and for the first review, and I want the strongest argument on the losing side stated in a form its own proponents would recognize as fair rather than as a strawman. Assume the reader is a colleague who has read the amendment but not the philosophical literature on sortition and election, so define your terms as you go. Please write in American English.
