You are an economist and philosopher of science who designs teaching simulations for graduate seminars, and you are good at building activities in which a theory shows up in what participants do rather than in what the instructor says afterwards. Think carefully and work step by step before you commit to a design.

I run a ninety-minute session for eighteen graduate students in a joint philosophy and economics seminar on the epistemology of markets. Roughly half are economists and half are philosophers; all of them know expected utility and Bayes's rule, none of them has encountered a no-trade theorem, and none will have read anything in advance. I want them to feel in their hands why rational traders who share a prior, hold private information, and know one another to be rational should not trade at all — the result Paul Milgrom and Nancy Stokey established in 1982 — and to feel precisely which assumption has to give before trade becomes rational again. The room has movable tables, a whiteboard, and no computers. I have a printer, several decks of cards, envelopes, and about twenty dollars of real money that I am willing to lose.

Design the activity. Begin with a short design notes section: list five or six candidate mechanics, say for each what it would make participants feel and where it would fail, then choose one and say why. After that, give me the runnable design in enough detail that a colleague could run it without me:

- Materials and setup, including how private information is dealt and kept genuinely private and how it can be verified after the fact.
- Roles, rules, and payoffs that require no arithmetic beyond expected value, with a payoff table I can copy.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes.
- A round in which the initial allocation is one that everyone can see is efficient given the shared prior, so that the prediction is no trade at any price, and in which participants run a real double auction with real small stakes. The mechanism has to make the crucial step visible: any offer reveals the offerer's private signal, so accepting it is evidence of having been out-thought. And this has to be common knowledge rather than merely mutual — each of them knows the others are rational, and knows that the others know it, and so on. Build in whatever is needed to make that hierarchy of reasoning bite rather than remain a slogan.
- A second round that changes exactly one thing so that trade becomes rational, letting them locate the load-bearing assumption for themselves. Choose the alteration deliberately and tell me what the first round was testing by contrast.
- A third round built around the question of why the other side is willing to trade at all: sellers who may be informed and may instead be selling for a reason that has nothing to do with information, and buyers who must price the mix. If you prefer, stage instead the two-sided private-values case in which efficiency and voluntary participation cannot both be had.
- A debrief that maps what happened, moment by moment, onto the parts of the argument: the shared prior, the common-knowledge requirement, the informational content of an offer, and the difference between the claim that nobody has better information than I do and the claim that I know that. Participants should not hear the name of the theorem or the names of its authors until the last ten minutes, and the handout they get at the start should contain only rules, payoffs, and the story of the market.
- A short facilitator cue card, five lines at most, for the two or three moments that decide whether the session works.
- An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time: the handling for the room that trades enthusiastically in the first round anyway, and for the room that sits on its hands out of confusion rather than insight, and how I can tell those two cases apart.
- A frank closing note on what the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply myself in the debrief. I expect genuine common knowledge of unbounded rationality and a real equilibrium to be on that list; tell me what else is, and what it costs the lesson that we cannot stage them.

Do not give me a reading list, citations, or a bibliography; the activity has to stand on its own for students who have read nothing. Where you have to make a judgment about my students or my room, state the assumption you are making rather than asking me. Be concrete: I would rather have the exact payoff numbers and the exact wording of the facilitator's two or three most important interventions than a description of the shape of the activity.
