Last Saturday I lost an argument with a friend about whether a community's memory of its own past can ever outrank the documentary record, and it turns out to matter more than I'd like: on Thursday I circulate the decision memo for the state historical society's marker committee, which I chair. The petition before us comes from descendants of workers at the Halstead mill. Their oral tradition, carried across four generations, holds that in 1911 the owner brought in armed guards who shot striking workers and buried the dead in a common grave; a faction within the same descendant group accepts the shooting but doubts the grave. The written record points elsewhere — the coroner's inquest blamed a boiler explosion during the confrontation, the paper of record reported no shots fired, and a 2019 archaeological survey found no grave — yet the record is also incomplete, since the mill's papers burned in 1930 and the inquest file has gone missing, so its silence proves less than it appears to. Our guidelines require marker text to rest on documentary evidence, and a sister society has started erecting two-panel markers that present both accounts side by side.

Draft the memo. Open with your recommendation — the archival account, the community's account, a two-panel marker, or deferral pending further research — then give the reasoning, including the strongest case for whichever option you reject and a rule the committee can apply to the next petition of this kind, and state what we should do by when. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, no preamble, and don't restate the question.
