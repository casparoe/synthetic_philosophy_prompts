I am trying to locate a disagreement between two camps in epistemology, and I want your credence on a specific proposition rather than a survey of positions.

The dispute concerns bootstrapping: a subject reasons from the deliverances of one of her own belief-forming faculties to the conclusion that the faculty is reliable. One camp holds that such reasoning is viciously circular and can never yield knowledge, since the premises are justified only if the faculty is reliable, which is precisely what is in question. Another camp holds that the circularity is benign and that this kind of self-support can yield knowledge. Nearly all of the discussion concerns cases in which the subject takes the content of the faculty's verdicts as her premises.

I want you to assess a variant that isolates a different question. Consider a subject, S, whose only access to the colors of objects is a single faculty, F. S has no independent instrument, no teacher, no record of past accuracy, and no way to compare F against anything external. F is noisy rather than deterministic: shown the same object twice, it need not deliver the same color. Over many trials, however, S notices a structural feature of F's outputs: they are highly self-consistent. Objects that F calls the same color on one trial tend to be called the same color again; the relation F reports of looking the same is roughly transitive; and F's verdicts are stable under changes that S has independent reason to regard as irrelevant, such as viewing distance and time of day. S concludes that F is reliable.

Now the proposition I want a number on. Call it the structural-evidence principle: a subject may legitimately treat structural features of a faculty's outputs, namely their consistency, transitivity, and stability under transformations known to be irrelevant, as evidence that the faculty is reliable, even when she has no independent access to the truth of those outputs and even though her only access to the outputs is through the faculty itself.

What is your credence that the structural-evidence principle is correct, that is, that there is a defensible epistemology on which S's inference to F's reliability can be justified and, in favorable cases, amount to knowledge?

Give a number, then decompose it. List the sub-questions the number rests on, each with a rough probability and a one-line justification, and state how they combine. At a minimum, address: whether structural features can carry evidential weight when the subject cannot verify them externally; whether F's being noisy rather than deterministic helps or hurts; whether consistency can distinguish reliability from systematic bias, and whether that residual gap is fatal; whether the subject must know the possible error structure of F in order to use the evidence; whether the relevant target is justification or knowledge and whether the answer differs; and whether the bootstrapping intuition that such reasoning is defective tracks a real epistemic failure or only a psychological one. Then give me, for contrast, your credence that the classic content-based version of bootstrapping succeeds, so that I can see whether the structural version fares any better. Finally, say which sub-question your number is most sensitive to, and what single argument or result would move it most.
