Her version of it: each member is more likely than not to get a binary question right, the errors are independent, so a majority of a large group is near-certainly right. My other friend says the theorem is basically a toy. His objection is that nothing is ever independent — everyone reads the same briefing memo, everyone gets anchored by the same framing, and once the errors are correlated, piling on more voters buys you nothing; you just get a confident crowd repeating the same mistake. He also likes to point out that the same Condorcet who proved the convergence result is the one who showed majority rule can cycle once you have three or more options, which is what real assemblies actually face, so the theorem can't be the whole story regardless.

So here's what I want: your credence in the claim that a larger body of ordinary people, of the kind a citizens' assembly actually produces, is more likely than a smaller group of elected officials to reach the correct answer on a typical policy question — where "correct" means the choice an idealized, fully informed observer with the same values would make. Give me a number, then decompose it: how much rests on the independence assumption holding well enough in real deliberation, how much on members actually being better than chance on questions that are often ill-posed, how much on the binary-choice framing being the right idealization, how much on the small group being genuinely more competent (they're specialists, the assembly isn't), how much on strategic voting or on whoever sets the agenda quietly choosing which questions get asked, and any other term you think belongs — a probability and a one-line justification for each, then the combination. After that, tell me which term you're least sure about, what evidence would move it most, and — since I've given you both friends' positions — which single term you'd bet their disagreement really lives in.
