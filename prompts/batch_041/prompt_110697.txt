I'm chairing the first sitting of our city's new citizens' assembly next month — thirty residents picked by stratified lottery, plus the nine elected council members who'll be sitting in the room, most of who think the whole thing is an insult. I have to give the opening remarks, five minutes max. What I want the speech to actually pull off: make the case that this body's authority comes from the absence of a selection process, not from any special wisdom in the room — nobody here got picked because they wanted it or because they're good at it — and do that in a way the council members hear as an invitation instead of a demotion. So no wisdom-of-the-people stuff; they'd smell it instantly. I also want to get in, without turning it into a poli-sci lecture, that a lottery like this only earns its keep on questions that have better and worse answers; if what we're really doing is deciding whose preferences win, chance doesn't buy us anything. And there's a practical stake everyone in the room knows about: our recommendation goes on the next ballot word-for-word, no amendments, printed right next to the council's competing version. It has to land spoken aloud to nervous people, so keep the sentences short. Can you write it?
