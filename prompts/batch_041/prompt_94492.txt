I'm in a decision theory seminar and we keep arguing about the Allais paradox, and I dont actually know which parts of the standard story I should trust. A behavioral econ trade book I picked up (I won't name it, it's not the point) sums it up like this: 'Expected utility theory says that a rational agent maximizes expected utility, and its central axiom is independence -- if you prefer gamble A to gamble B, you should still prefer A to B when both are mixed with the same third gamble, because the common part cancels out. In Allais's famous example, most people prefer a sure 100 million francs to a lottery offering a 10% shot at 500 million, an 89% shot at 100 million, and a 1% shot at nothing. But when the 89% chance of 100 million is stripped out of both options and replaced with an 89% chance of nothing, the same people prefer the 10% shot at 500 million to an 11% shot at 100 million. The two answers pull in opposite directions, so the subjects' preferences were intransitive -- they contradicted themselves -- and that's the paradox. Allais concluded that people are simply bad at probabilities, and ever since nobody has defended expected utility as a normative standard; behavioral economists replaced it with prospect theory, which explains the Allais choices as a case of loss aversion.'

I'm fairly convinced the independence bit is stated right, and I even think the subjects are being unreasonable here, but something about the rest feels off and I can't say what. A friend in the class insists the real culprit is the sure-thing principle rather than independence, and the professor rolled his eyes at that but never explained why.
