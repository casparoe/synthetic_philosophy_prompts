I'm in this value theory reading group and we keep getting stuck on wether 'better than' is transitive — like, is it even possible for A to be better than B, B better than C, and C better than A? Next week I have to open the session and I want to do it as a dialogue, so could you write one for me? Two characters, both really sharp, neither one a strawman: one is convinced transitivity is a conceptual truth about betterness (A beats B, B beats C, so A has to beat C, full stop — any apparent counterexample is really just vagueness, or a shift in what we mean by 'better'), and the other thinks the spectrum argument shows transitivity just plain fails.

To keep us off the usual tired examples, here's a case I made up that we can use. Picture a spectrum of possible lives for one person, all the same length, that differ along two things: how much mild, low-grade physical discomfort they contain (a faint headache that's just always there in the background), and how many small disappointments they hit per day (say, biting into a piece of fruit that's slightly underripe — not a big deal, you barely register it). At one end you've got almost no discomfort at all but a gigantic number of those tiny disappointments every day; at the other end you've got zero disappointments but a constant, noticeable headache. Now arrange a long chain in between, where each step you trade away a sliver of headache for a big pile of extra disappointments. The catch is that the headache slivers are so small you'd never notice the difference between neighboring lives, and each disappointment is individually so trivial that you'd always take the trade — so every adjacent step looks like a clear improvement. But compare the two ends directly and you'd obviously pick the low-disappointment, headache-y life over the one with endless tiny letdowns. The spectrum person says that's a genuine cycle and transitivity is dead; the transitivity defender should push back — maybe somewhere the comparison goes indeterminate, maybe 'better' is quietly doing two different jobs, maybe those adjacent steps were never really strict improvements — and should press the other side on what exactly they mean by 'clearly better' at each step. Have the spectrum person actually answer that instead of just restating the example, and let it run a good while. Don't force a tidy resolution at the end.
