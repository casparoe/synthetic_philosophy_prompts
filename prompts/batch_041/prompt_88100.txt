Consider six families of accounts of what makes a life meaningful: (1) attitudinal accounts, on which a life is meaningful to the extent that the person gets, or endorses, what they most want; (2) objective-list naturalism, on which meaning consists in engagement with a fixed set of worthwhile goods such as knowledge, achievement, and love; (3) hybrid accounts, on which meaning requires both subjective attraction and genuine worth in the object of that attraction; (4) narrative accounts, on which a life is meaningful insofar as its parts cohere into a story with the right sort of arc; (5) theistic accounts, on which meaning consists in standing in the right relation to a divine purpose; (6) absurdist accounts, on which the demand for meaning cannot be met but can be answered with defiant lucidity rather than despair.

Rank these six by how well each handles the following three demands taken together: (a) it must allow that a person can be mistaken about whether their own life is meaningful; (b) it must explain why the standards it invokes have a claim on us, rather than merely listing them or appealing to a lawgiver to supply their authority; (c) it must not make the meaningfulness of a life depend on that life being known, remembered, or appreciated by anyone. Give each account a score out of 10, order them from highest to lowest, and state in one sentence what each score is buying. Then identify the single modification to the demands — dropping one, adding one, or tightening one — that would most change your ordering, and say which account would gain most from it and why.
