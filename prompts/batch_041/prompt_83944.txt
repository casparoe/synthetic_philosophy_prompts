I've spent a few years reading about independence movements in my spare time, and I've reached the point where I can state my verdicts on individual cases but not the rule behind them. I have no training in philosophy or law — I write software for a living — so I'd like you to tell me what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to.

Here is where I land.

Scotland, 2014: the referendum was legitimate, and had Yes won, Westminster should have negotiated an exit in good faith. That London consented in advance matters to me less than it seems to matter to other people.

Quebec, 1995: also legitimate, even though Ottawa never agreed in advance to be bound by the result. A narrow Yes would have given Quebec a genuine claim to negotiate secession.

Catalonia, 2017: not legitimate. I take the vote to have been a real expression of opinion, but declaring independence on the strength of it was wrong, and Madrid was entitled to treat the result as void.

Kosovo, 2008: legitimate, unilateral declaration and all, despite Serbia's refusal to consent and the many states that still withhold recognition.

Crimea, 2014: not legitimate, and not a close call. Whatever the ballot said, the vote was held under foreign military occupation.

Somaliland, 1991 to the present: legitimate. It is unrecognized and its independence was declared unilaterally, but it has governed itself for more than thirty years, and the state it left had turned on its people violently.

On franchise: I think it was right that only people resident in Scotland voted in 2014; the rest of the United Kingdom had no business deciding whether Scotland leaves it.

A counterfactual: had Scotland voted Yes, I believe Shetland and Orkney would have been entitled to remain in the UK, or at least to negotiate their own status, even though they sit inside Scotland.

The pair I cannot square is Quebec and Catalonia. Both were unilateral in the sense that the central government had not agreed in advance to be bound, yet I approve of one and not the other. I have tried several principles. A clear majority in a defined territory wanting it: this saves Quebec, but it also appears to license Catalonia and to give Shetland a veto over Scotland, which I don't want. Serious injustice as the only warrant: this saves Kosovo and Somaliland, but it condemns Quebec, which I also don't want. Legality under the parent state's own constitution: this gets Catalonia right, and it makes the whole question hostage to whatever the capital has written down — which cannot be right, given that Spain's constitution declares the unity of the Spanish nation to be indissoluble and Canada's is silent on the matter.

So: is there one principle, or a small set, that gets all of these, and if not, which verdict do I have to give up? For whichever principle survives, tell me what it predicts about a case I have not listed — a wealthy region with no history of oppression that wants out of a union it believes it is subsidizing, a Flanders or a Lombardy. And tell me which clause is doing the work for each of my verdicts, so I can see where the machinery is load-bearing.
