I write about science and ideas for a general-readership magazine, and I'm assembling a piece on why the oldest question in the philosophy of mathematics — whether numbers, sets, and the like are discovered or invented — refuses to go away. I've interviewed a lot of people for it, and the sharpest conversation I had was between two philosophers who agree on nearly all the technical mathematics but split on what it is about: one is an unapologetic platonist, holding that mathematical objects exist independently of us and of our language, so that mathematicians discover rather than invent; the other is a nominalist, holding that there are no such objects and that mathematics is a body of useful fictions — or at most a codification of what follows from what. Because the friction between them is the story, I want to run their disagreement as a dialogue rather than a summary.

Please write that dialogue. Anchor it in a concrete case so it doesn't float free: imagine a mathematician who introduces a new kind of object — call it a "shard" — by a short list of axioms; over a generation, hundreds of theorems about shards are proved, a few of them eventually turn out to be true of ordinary finite structures once the terms are reinterpreted, and then someone proves the axioms are inconsistent, so that no shard could have existed. Have the two of them fight over that history: were the theorems true when they were proved, and in what sense; was the mathematician discovering facts about something or exploring the consequences of a story that turned out to have no model; and what, if anything, does the case show about what mathematical existence amounts to. Both speakers should be at the top of their game — no strawmen, no cheap gotchas — and each should answer the other's actual point rather than pivot to a prepared speech. Let them concede where a concession is warranted and press hardest where the real disagreement lies, and don't let them resolve it tidily; I want the reader to see exactly where the fork in the road is.

Keep the whole thing to no more than 1,000 words, and write it in American English.
