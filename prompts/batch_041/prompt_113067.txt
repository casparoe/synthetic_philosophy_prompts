I am planning a term of seminars on time, agency, and prediction, and I want to begin with the criteria rather than with the theories, so that my students have something to test the theories against. What I am asking you for is the desiderata: the list of things any adequate account of future contingency has to deliver, each with the case or consideration that puts it on the list, and your judgement about which items are non-negotiable and which are merely the preferences of one camp. I have the old sea-battle puzzle in mind — the argument that if a claim about tomorrow is already true or false today, then what happens tomorrow is already fixed and deliberation is idle — but I do not want a list tailored to that one argument. I want the general specification that any view about the openness of the future has to meet, whether it treats the puzzle as a logical illusion, as a genuine constraint on what can be true, or as something else altogether; and I would rather have a short list of real requirements than a long list of things on which everyone already agrees.

Among the things I expect on the list, and want you to argue for or against rather than simply assume, are these. First, that ordinary predictions and warnings must come out as the sort of thing that can be right or wrong today, and that promises and resolutions must remain intelligible as commitments, since we retract such remarks, apologise for them, and hold one another to them. Second, that the account must keep separate the threat that comes from logic — from the very idea that a claim about the future already has a truth value — and the threat that comes from causal determinism, because the puzzle is supposed to bite even in a chancy world, and a view that survives it only by assuming indeterminism has changed the subject. Third, that the asymmetry between past and future, the sense in which the past is settled and the future is not, must be either vindicated or explained away, and not quietly presupposed. Fourth, that the account must say what distinguishes a claim that something will happen from a claim that it might, and what it would mean for a claim to become settled later without having changed from false to true. Fifth, that it must cope with cases in which the predicted event is one the agent herself later brings about, where the truth of the prediction and the agent's act appear to depend on one another.

The part I most want, though, is the collision. Tell me which pairs of these requirements cannot both be met, and why; if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly, since that would be a result in itself. My own suspicion, which I would like you to test rather than accept, is that the fixity of truth — a claim once true never becoming false — can be held together with the idea that every claim about the future is already true or false only at the cost of something the deliberation cases seem to need, and that the usual escape, letting a claim about tomorrow become true only when tomorrow arrives, purchases openness by making truth depend on what happens, which in turn threatens the requirement that a prediction can be wrong today. Finish by naming the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidates, and by briefly describing, in plain terms and without relying on the labels of the various camps, how the main families of response fare against it. Please write your answer in British English.
