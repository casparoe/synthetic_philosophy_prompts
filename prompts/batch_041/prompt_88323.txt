I am taking a course on moral change, and I am trying to formulate my own view on a question the readings keep returning to: when a practice dies — slavery, dueling, footbinding, and others — was it argument, interest, or honor that killed it, and does the answer determine whether the death was a genuine moral advance? I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts on individual cases add up to. On dueling, my verdict is that the arguments against it (that it was private war, a genteel form of suicide, a usurpation of the law's authority) had been available for a long time and accomplished little; what ended it was that the class which dueled came to find it ridiculous and faintly vulgar. Honor, not argument, did the work, and yet I regard its disappearance as one of the clearest moral improvements I can name. Footbinding has the same shape and gets the same verdict: no one was argued out of it, it became a mark of backwardness in the eyes of precisely those who had to marry their daughters well, and I still count its end a revolution. Atlantic slavery I cannot describe as a victory for argument, since the arguments were old and the timing tracks the declining profitability of the plantations and the embarrassment of the practice in a nation fond of congratulating itself on its freedom; nor can I say the practice ended, since it was quarantined and renamed — the trade relocated, and coerced labor reappeared in new legal dress — and if it was merely quarantined then by my own lights it was not a revolution, which I am unwilling to accept. In one case from the colonial-administration reading I will keep deliberately vague: a practice banned by an imperial power for administrative convenience and out of racial contempt, with no one converted and no reason offered that the practitioners could have accepted. I believe the ban was right, and I notice this leaves me unable to require that a revolution be a conversion. Finally, judicial public shaming: the courts abandoned it as degrading, and it has returned in privatized form online, which suggests the honor mechanism that ends practices is as reversible as the honor that sustained them. My tentative principle is a two-part test: a dead norm counts as a moral revolution only if (a) those bound by it came to accept the reasons against it rather than merely losing the power to sustain it, and (b) the change would survive the reversal of whatever power relations produced it. Tell me whether this principle accommodates my verdicts or whether I must surrender one to keep the rest, and if a better principle is available, state it. Answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first states the principle; the second identifies which of my verdicts forces each of its clauses; the third applies it to a case I have not listed; the fourth names the verdict I must give up, or the clause I must add, to keep the others; and the fifth says whether 'moral revolution' survives as a category on my own account or proves to be a flattering name for one honor code displacing another.
