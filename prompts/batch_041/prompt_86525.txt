I retired some years ago from a career in experimental physics and have spent my retirement reading philosophy, chiefly in the philosophy of mind and the philosophy of science. I keep returning to a passage in Alan Turing's 1950 paper on computing machinery and intelligence — not the imitation game itself, but his closing suggestion that extrasensory perception might spoil the game, and that one might therefore have to run the test with the interrogator lodged in a room proof against telepathy. As someone who spent decades designing controls, I find this move both admirable and suspicious, and I would like a careful essay that settles it.

Please write an essay on the following question: was Turing's concession to telepathy an instance of sound experimental hygiene, or does it quietly concede that the imitation game was never a test of intelligence in the first place?

The essay should develop and explicitly define two distinctions, and then apply them rather than merely naming them. The first is the distinction between quarantining a confound and amputating a capacity: a control that removes an extraneous channel of information versus one that removes part of the very thing under test. The second is the distinction between a parochial and a robust operationalization of a mental capacity: an operationalization is parochial when it would be defeated by the presence of any anomaly peculiar to human beings rather than to thinking as such.

Then argue the substance. If telepathy were real, would a machine that lacked it fail the game for a reason bearing on intelligence at all? Say what the game is supposed to measure, and whether shielding the interrogator changes the question or merely clarifies it. Consider the mirror case as well: a machine that passes by exploiting a channel of the same anomalous kind, having no relevant cognitive capacity of its own. Say what the game ought to deliver there, and whether that verdict is consistent with your treatment of the first case.

There is a further puzzle that interests me as a former experimenter. Turing treats the statistical evidence for telepathy as something a rational designer must accommodate, even though the phenomenon has no place in any physical theory he could state. Is that stance coherent — must one design around an anomaly one cannot explain — or does it license designing around every unreplicated anomaly that someone reports? Address this directly, since I suspect it is where the real difficulty lies. You may take the empirical evidence as Turing took it; I am not asking you to adjudicate parapsychology, only to work out what follows if one grants it.

Reach a verdict. I do not want a survey that concludes that both sides have a point. Tell me whether Turing was right to build his shielded room, or whether he should have treated the alleged phenomenon as irrelevant to the question he had set himself, and defend that verdict against the strongest objection you can raise against it. Please argue from the ideas themselves; I have no interest in citations, bibliographies, or reading lists. Somewhere between twelve hundred and eighteen hundred words will do.
