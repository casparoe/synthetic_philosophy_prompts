A constitution is sometimes described as a Ulysses contract: a sovereign people, anticipating that it will later be tempted to act against its own considered judgment, binds its future self in advance. The analogy is imperfect in a way that is usually glossed over. Ulysses could rely on sailors who were not themselves parties to the bargain, whereas a people has no external crew and must therefore appoint its enforcers from among the very selves it is trying to bind.

Consider two ways a polity might bind itself against a future temptation. (For concreteness, take the temptation to be the suspension of elections during a war, or a default on public debt, or the disenfranchisement of an unpopular minority — choose whichever you find most instructive, and say which you chose.)

Hard binding: the provision is placed beyond the reach of ordinary amendment, changeable only by a procedure so demanding that it is effectively unavailable in the moment of temptation, with enforcement entrusted to a body whose members are insulated from ordinary political pressure.

Soft binding: the provision remains amendable by a simple majority, but any amendment is subject to a costly delay — a waiting period, a mandatory referendum, or a requirement that the repealing coalition publicly bear the cost — so that the temptation must survive a stretch of reflection and a public accounting.

Argue for a position on which of these two, if either, a polity ought to prefer, and defend it against the strongest objection you can construct. In doing so:

- State what a precommitment is supposed to accomplish, and specify the sense in which the future self counts as the same agent as the self that binds it. Your answer will look very different depending on whether you treat the polity as one enduring agent or as a succession of temporary majorities, so say which you assume and why.
- Address the problem of the custodian: whoever enforces the contract is not bound by it, and therefore wields precisely the discretion the contract was meant to eliminate. Explain how your preferred mechanism handles this without merely relocating the arbitrary power it was supposed to constrain.
- Distinguish the case in which the precommitment protects the polity from a passing passion from the case in which it protects an entrenched majority against a durable and reasonable change of mind. Say whether your verdict differs across these cases, and if so, how the two are to be told apart at the time of binding rather than in hindsight.
- Consider whether an unamendable provision can be legitimate at all, given that the generation that adopted it cannot bind the generation that must live under it, and whether a merely costly provision is a genuine precommitment or only a speed bump.

Conclude with a concrete verdict: name the mechanism you would adopt for your chosen temptation, and identify the single condition under which you would abandon your position.

Write in American English. Be rigorous rather than exhaustive; a compact argument with clearly stated premises is worth more than a survey of everything that has been said on the subject.
