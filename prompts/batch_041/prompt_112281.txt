I am drafting a position statement on biological function for a philosophy of biology seminar and would like a consistency audit before I present it. These are the commitments I would defend:

(1) A trait's function is what it was selected for: selection history is what function talk tracks, and nothing else confers a function.
(2) Function ascriptions are normative. To say that the heart's function is to pump blood is to commit myself to the claim that a heart which cannot pump is dysfunctional, and that dysfunction is a fact about the organism rather than about our explanatory interests.
(3) There is no irreducible teleology in biology, so whatever normativity (2) has must be naturalized; I have always taken (1) to be what does the naturalizing.
(4) Physiology and medicine ascribe functions by causal contribution to a capacity, and they do so without any knowledge of selection history; such ascriptions would not be withdrawn if the history turned out to be different from what was assumed.
(5) What counts as the system whose function is at issue, and as the capacity that system exercises, is fixed by the inquirer's explanatory project, so function ascriptions are interest-relative.
(6) A trait that arose by drift and now reliably contributes to fitness has a function, and its ceasing to contribute would be a malfunction, even though selection has not yet acted on it.

Which of these are actually in conflict, which only appear to be, and what is the cheapest way to restore consistency? Rank the conflicts by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, say for each what precisely would have to give, and tell me whether any single revision would clean up more than the rest.
