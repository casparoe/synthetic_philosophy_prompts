I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm stuck on a problem I accidentally built into my own book, so I'm outsourcing it. Here's the setup. In the novel there's a mining town that pulls a strange mineral out of the ground and names it glim. What they mean by the word is roughly: the waxy pale solid that melts if you hold it, smells faintly of sulfur, burns with a green flame, and makes you drowsy when you breathe the fumes. That's the whole of how they fix what they're talking about. Years later a chemist in the book works out that the stuff is a specific compound with a specific structure. Fine so far, that's just how names for stuff work.

Now the part I need. I want a scene where the reader feels the sentence "glim is [whatever that underlying compound turns out to be]" come apart from itself. So what I want from you is a thought experiment built to order: a case where that sentence comes out true if you evaluate it the way you'd evaluate it if you were standing in each world fresh (you'd just check: does the stuff that answers to that description here have the compound nature? yes, so true), but false if you evaluate it holding fixed whatever the word actually latched onto back home. I'm told philosophers call these two readings the primary and the secondary intension, and that the interesting, hard-to-build case is the one where the primary comes out true and the secondary comes out false, not the other way round. That's the direction I need. Build me that case.

Constraints, and please actually respect them:

- It has to be a natural kind term in the mouth of an ordinary, competent community. Not a term whose meaning someone just stipulated, and not a term that's really an abbreviation for a description.
- The features the community uses to fix the reference and the underlying nature that gets discovered have to be genuinely seperate from each other -- neither one should entail the other. If the description already secretly contains the nature, you've cheated and the case is trivial.
- No empty kinds, no worlds where the kind simply fails to exist, and nothing that turns on the actual referent being a special indexical like "this" or "the actual one." I want the divergence to come from the modal facts, not from a word I slipped in.
- Keep it realistic. No Twin Earth, no science fiction props, no impossible chemistry. A reader who's never taken a philosophy class should be able to follow it.

Then do the demonstration, because I've been burned before by cases that only look like they work. Lay it out as a 2x2 matrix -- rows for the two readings, columns for the home world and the other world -- and show the truth value in each cell. Then tell me exactly which feature of the case is doing the work, what happens if I take that feature out, and how a defender of the standard story about natural kind terms (the Kripke/Putnam line, where the secondary reading is supposed to come out necessary) would attack the case to defuse it. And if what I've asked for collapses into the usual water/H2O shape once you look closely, say so rather than dressing it up.
