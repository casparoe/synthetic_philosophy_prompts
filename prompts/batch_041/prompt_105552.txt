I'm putting together a long feature on how philosophers of mind describe the graded ladder of animal cognition — the one where natural selection does the testing for the simplest organisms, operant conditioning takes over for creatures that can learn within a lifetime, then some creatures acquire the trick of running a hypothesis internally so that the hypothesis dies instead of them, and finally there are creatures like us, who inherit a whole workshop of external thinking tools — notation, number, written language, the accumulated apparatus of culture — and get to think with borrowed equipment. I've been rereading Dennett's book on the evolution of mind, the one that gives the four grades their nicknames after Darwin, Skinner, Popper, and the psychologist Richard Gregory, and what keeps striking me is that it isn't really a ladder of increasing worth so much as a tower in which every higher storey is physically resting on the lower ones and never replaces them. We are still Skinnerian when we flinch, still Darwinian when our hearts beat. What I want from you is a poem to close the piece — something that does for the reader what the prose can't, which is to make the stacking itself felt rather than merely stated.

Please write it as a cascade, the form where the opening stanza's lines come back one at a time as the closing lines of the stanzas that follow, so that each achievement of a lower grade is re-consumed and carried upward by the next one and the poem ends by handing the first line back to the reader in a new key. Four opening lines, so five stanzas of four lines total. Keep the verse plain and unhurried, no archaisms and no jargon for its own sake; I'd rather it read like someone thinking carefully than like a lecture in meter. A reader who already knows the four grades should be able to tell which stanza is which without being told, and should also be able to tell that I've read Dennett rather than skimmed a summary — in particular, the Gregorian storey should come across as a genuine discontinuity (creatures that can think with tools they did not invent and do not understand from the inside) and not just as more of the same cleverness, and the poem should not pretend that the lower grades have been left behind. The refrain mechanics should be doing real work here: I want the repetition to enact the point that the earlier capacities are not superseded but re-entered, so if the cascade structure is just decoration on top of a list, the poem has failed.

Because this is verse and not an argument, I don't expect a verdict — but I do want the poem to come with a short prose note after it, maybe a paragraph, that does three things: says plainly where the poem takes liberties with the ideas or leans on a contested reading of them (I'm aware that treating the four grades as a nested hierarchy rather than a loose set of distinctions is itself a reading, and I'd like you to say so if you think that's what you've done); says which single consideration, if it turned out to be right, would make you want to rewrite the poem's central image — for instance if the cultural-accumulation storey were shown to be continuous with the individual-learning storey rather than a break, I'd want to know that the poem would have to change shape and not just wording; and flags the one place in the verse where you're least confident you've got the idea right. I'd rather have an honest weak spot named than a smooth poem that pretends to be sure of everything.
