I write novels for a living, which means I spend an embarrassing amount of time arguing about what counts as art, and I've started keeping a list on my phone of things I'm sure are art and things I'm sure aren't, and the list has gotten to the point where I can't defend it anymore. What I want from you is the principle I'm actually using, stated precisely, plus an honest note on which of my entries I should probably give up.

I keep circling back to something I half-remember from Tolstoy's book about art. As I recall it: art isn't beauty, isn't pleasure, isn't self-expression — it's one person handing another person a feeling he actually lived through, by way of some external signs, and the other person catching it. If nobody catches it, it isn't art, however well made. And sincerity is supposed to be the main thing — the maker has to be genuinely infected by his own feeling, and the reason the art of his own class is mostly counterfeit, while a peasant song isn't, is that the peasants mean it and we don't. He also ended up thinking his own big novels were trash, which as a novelist I find terrifying. I might be mangling this, I don't have the vocabulary for any of it, so feel free to tell me where I've got the view wrong.

The list:

- My uncle's funeral. We paid a woman to lead the crying, and she was, I'm told, a professional who did it three times a week and felt nothing. The whole church wept. I say that was art. Not just the singing — the whole thing, the way it was arranged.
- My friend's novel. Five years of her life, absolutely sincere, no cynicism in it anywhere. I read it and felt nothing but respect. I want to say it isn't art, or barely is. I would never say so to her face.
- A romance I read on a plane, third in a series, written to a deadline by someone who told an interviewer she despises her readers. Sobbed. Twice. That's art.
- My own last book, which I wrote in a flat gray mood and don't much like. Readers tell me it wrecked them. I want that to count, and I feel like a fraud for wanting it.
- The box of poems my mother wrote and never showed anybody. I found it after she died and it hit me like a truck. Was it art while it sat in the drawer, with nobody infected? I say yes. But I can't see how, on the above.
- The lullaby my wife sings to our kid, off key, half the words wrong. Art. The same lullaby on a glossy album of baby music, note perfect. Not art, or not to me. Same notes.
- A jump-scare in a horror film that got me badly, and an insurance ad in an airport that made me cry. Both infected me. Neither is art.
- The novel I think is the best thing written in my lifetime, which maybe four hundred people on earth can actually read. By the clarity condition, that's supposed to be a fault, or evidence of counterfeit. I think it's the opposite.

So: what's the principle? Give me the smallest set of clauses that gets most of these, say which case motivates each clause, and then tell me straight which entries I have to throw out — I'd rather lose a verdict than keep a theory with an epicycle for every line of my list. I'd also like to know what your principle predicts about two things I haven't listed: a piece that lands on me the first time and is dead to me the tenth, and a whole stadium singing one anthem badly, all infected at once.

And then the bigger question, which is really why I'm writing: does anything of the Tolstoy thing survive this? If sincerity has to go, is what's left just "art is whatever moves somebody," which sounds true and useless and definitely isn't what he wanted? Or does it need a patch, and if so, which one — and does the patched version still have room for the drawer poems and the unreadable masterpiece, or does it quietly rule one of them out?
