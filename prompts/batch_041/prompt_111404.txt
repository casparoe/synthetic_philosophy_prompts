I've been "writing" a novel for six years and I've finaly figured out that my problem has a name: hyperbolic discounting. Fine. But I'm not convinced it's a mistake. I might get hit by a bus; discounting the future looks like honest expected-value math, not a bug. My flatmate goes further: she says the discount is correct at every moment, so future-me never has a stronger claim than now-me, and so weakness of will doesn't really exist — it's just a relay race of different people with different tastes, and akrasia is an empty word.

Two things I want you to work with. First, the curve. If this were really just uncertainty about the future, my discounting should be fairly smooth, but it isn't — I'll cheerfully wait a year for something eighteen months out, and I can't wait twenty minutes for something an hour out. There's a cliff right at now. Second, a test I made up. Call it the fair-trade test: when I'm about to cave, ask whether I'd take the same deal if the person who benefitted from my waiting were a stranger I'll never meet. If I'd say yes to the stranger, caving isn't weakness, it's a fair trade with a stranger. If I'd say no to the stranger but yes to future-me, then I'm treating future-me as special — and that's where the weakness actually lives.

Write me an essay on whether weakness of will is real or just preferences that change over time, using the fair-trade test to sort the cases. Don't just tell me my flatmate is wrong because it feels wrong — take her seriously. And land on a verdict, even a qualified one.
