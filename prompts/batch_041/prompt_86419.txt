ok so I've been reading rat-adjacent blogs since forever (LessWrong, the old Overcoming Bias archives, slatestarcodex, the whole deal) and my favorite genre is when someone takes a genuinely unhinged cosmological conclusion completely seriously and then just... carries on with their Tuesday. So: write me an advice column — a Dear-Abby / agony-aunt thing — set in a world where the Boltzmann brain argument won, and everyone accepts that the typical observer is a momentary thermal fluctuation rather than an evolved brain with a real past.

Format: a short letter from a worried reader (say a physics grad student who finally did the numbers and cant sleep), then the columnist's reply, in the deadpan voice of someone who has answered this exact question four hundred times. 500-650 words total. No winking, no "or so they think" narration from the author — the piece has to stay straight-faced the whole way, and a reader should still be able to tell what's being poked at. Give the column a name.

The physics has to be right enough that a cosmologist nods instead of wincing. That means the fluctuation argument as it actually runs — long-lived de Sitter-ish universe, finite entropy ceiling, so a lone brain in a thermal bath vastly outnumbers a whole evolved civilization, by a factor nobody can actually compute — and also the self-undermining bit: if the argument is sound, your evidence for the argument is itself a fluctuation, so the argument eats its own tail. Dont let the columnist resolve that. Let her wave at it and move on, the way the discourse actually does.

What I'm really satirizing is the habit (very much my habit) of taking a result that makes all practical reasoning void and then treating it as a life-optimization problem anyway. So the advice needs to be concrete and actionable and superficially reasonable — a daily routine, a "planning horizon," something about long-term goals — while quietly contradicting itself given the premise. If she reaches for any measure-theoretic or anthropic fix, it should be the kind that just relabels the reference class until the answer comes out nice. Somewhere in there, have her cite an "expert" — invented, no real names — whose quote makes things worse rather than better.

Keep it in the weeds but legible: someone who's read about the measure problem, SIA vs SSA, and the reference-class problem should feel like the author gets it; someone who hasn't should still get why it's funny. No citations, no real people, no reading list.
