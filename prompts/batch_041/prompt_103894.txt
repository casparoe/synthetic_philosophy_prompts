I've been messing about with Bongard problems — the ones where you get six boxes on the left and six on the right and have to guess the rule. I realise alot of people say they show how we learn concepts from a few examples, but I definately reckon that's backwards. Nobody makes a Bongard problem by sampling randomly from two categories; they choose the twelve figures to make the rule guessable and to make the wrong guesses tempting. So the task isn't induction from data, it's working out what the puzzle-setter was trying to get you to notice. Please argue for that. Then deal with the hard case: a Bongard problem where the intended rule is not about the figures but about the solver — left figures are the ones that tempt you into a simple rule, right figures are the ones that tempt you into a complicated rule, and the real rule is that left figures are traps for simple solvers while right figures are traps for complicated ones. Does the argument still hold, and what does that tell us about concept learning in general? Take a side, don't just list pros and cons.
