I'm a PhD student in metaphysics working on material constitution, and I've ended up defending what I'll call ungrounded pluralism about the statue and the clay: there really are two numerically distinct objects here, sharing all their matter and all their parts, and nothing — no modal difference, no difference in grounding, no difference in sortal — makes them distinct. The coincidence is brute. I'm also a Humean about de re modality, so the familiar move of distinguishing the lump from the statue by their differing survival conditions under squashing is not available to me; I treat those modal differences as artifacts of our sortal concepts.

Tell me what should count against the view, ranked by actual force rather than by how often it gets cited: which objections are cheap shots I can absorb by giving up a premise I never needed, and which would force me to abandon it. In particular, does denying myself modal grounds leave me anything to say about what makes the two objects different, or does the view collapse into identity, or into a bare pluralism the identity theorist is entitled to complain about? Are the counting and vagueness problems that plague ordinary objects worse for me than for my rival, or merely the same? Does the view predict or explain anything the identity view does not, or is it a posit with no work to do? And if it turns out hard to falsify, is that a defect here or just the cost of doing metaphysics of this kind? I'd also like to hear what a sympathetic defender would concede as counting against them, not only what critics say.
