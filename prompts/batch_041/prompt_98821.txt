ok so next friday I'm presenting at our political theory work-in-progress workshop and I need a Q&A prep sheet. My paper argues that the boundary problem -- the fact that any democracy needs a demos, but no democratic procedure can decide who's in the demos without already presupposing one -- is not a fixable defect. I walk through the usual escape routes and argue they all fail: the all-affected principle (everyone affected by a decision gets a say) is either circular or blows up into the whole world; the idea that a constitution bootstraps a people into existence begs the question; and the nationalist "we decide who joins" answer just asserts the thing in dispute. My positive move is that legitimacy doesn't require the demos to be bounded correctly in the first place. It requires that the decisions stay contestable by whoever ends up governed, so the demos is always provisional, membership is always revisable, and the right to contest the boundary is part of what makes a regime legitimate rather than a precondition for it. I'm calling it the provisional demos view. Part of the paper is also a distinction I draw between membership and jurisdiction -- I think the literature keeps running these together, and the boundary problem is really about jurisdiction, not membership.

The room: about 15 people, mostly deliberative democrats of the Habermas kind who think rights and popular sovereignty are co-original and that the boundary problem basically dissolves once you see the demos constituting itself through law. Two agonists (Mouffe people) who think any demos is just a hegemonic closure and that my "contestability" is a polite word for the same old exclusion. One historian of political thought who'll want to know how this differs from the old constituent power tradition and probably thinks I've reinvented it badly. And one person who works on representation for animals and future generations, who will definately push me on whether "governed" is supposed to include them.

Give me the objections I'll actually get from this room, not the generic ones from the literature. Order them by how much damage they'd do to the paper, phrased the way these specific people would put them, and for each give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes plus a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front and what I should hold. And give me the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Be committed -- don't just list possible objections and leave me to sort it out. Take a view on which objections are fatal, which are survivable, and whether my positive view actually escapes the problem or just relocates it. If you think it just relocates it, say so and say exactly where the relocation happens. Also no citations or reading lists, I don't need a bibliography, just the substance. American English please.
