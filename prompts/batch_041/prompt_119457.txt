My friend Nadia and I had a two hour arguement last night about oaths in fantasy novels, of all things, and I need someone to do the reading I couldn't do at 1am.

Her line: oaths and curses in Middle-earth only hold because Tolkien built a world where they do. Break your oath to Isildur and you don't just feel bad, you literally can't die, you hang around as a ghost under the mountain until someone lets you off the hook. So, she says, the whole philosophical literature on promises and performatives is describing a fiction too. We just happen not to notice that our own being-bound is made up in the same way, only without the ghosts to make it obvious. My line was that she has it backwards: we understand the oathbreakers because we already know what it is to be held by a word, so the fantasy is a picture of something real, not a debunking of it. We got nowhere.

What I want from you is a reading of The Lord of the Rings through speech act theory that takes both of us seriously. I'm not after a survey of Austin, and please skip the reading list, I'm not writing a paper with footnotes. I want the actual scenes worked through. The Doors of Durin, where the writing on the door is itself the puzzle and the password turns out to be the Elvish word for friend. Gollum's oath sworn on the Ring, which everybody, Gollum included, treats as still gripping him even after he starts scheming again. The oathbreakers under the mountain and the fact that the curse is lifted not by forgiveness but by the oath finally being performed. And maybe the Witch-king prophecy as a contrast case, since that one is a prediction with teeth rather than a promise, and it gets cashed out by an ambiguity in the wording.

Two things I want you to be honest about. Austin himself shoved stage-talk and poetry off to the side as a hollowed out, dependent case, and Derrida made a career out of that move, so say whether Middle-earth is a counterexample to the carve out, or a cheat that turns the illocutionary into a causal mechanism, more spell than commitment, or a literalisation of what convention does for us anyway. And where does the reading break down? I have a suspiscion that Tolkien's metaphysics of fate swamps the whole thing, that these are less speech acts than the physics of his world, and if that's right I want you to say so instead of forcing Austin onto it. If the book ends up showing something the Austin, Searle and Derrida back and forth misses, tell me what it is.
