I'm in a graduate seminar on classical social theory, and we have spent the past month on Weber: the Protestant ethic argument, the four ideal types of action (goal-rational, value-rational, affectual, traditional), and the methodological quarrel that runs underneath all of it, namely whether values and ideas can ever enter an explanation as causes, or only as the meanings that make conduct intelligible in the first place. Our instructor keeps returning to two of Weber's own devices. The first is the double requirement on a sociological explanation: it must be adequate at the level of meaning, so that the conduct is intelligible as the agent's own reason for acting, and it must also be causally adequate, so that it connects up with the regularities of what typically happens. The second is elective affinity, which Weber invokes to describe how an ethos and a form of economic conduct belong together, and which has always struck me as doing causal work while presenting itself as a mere description of a resemblance. Her favourite image is Weber's: interests, material and ideal, supply the motive power, while the world-pictures created by ideas act as switchmen, deciding the tracks along which those interests drive action.

For my seminar paper I would like to get away from the usual historical cases and test the whole apparatus on a work of fiction instead. I have been rereading Melville's Bartleby, the Scrivener, and it now seems to me almost built to embarrass this framework. The narrator is a prudent, methodical, unambitious lawyer who presents his forbearance toward Bartleby as flowing from his own charitable and respectable character, while also conceding that the forbearance costs him nothing and lays up a sweet morsel for his conscience; I cannot tell whether that is value-rational conduct in Weber's sense, or goal-rational conduct wearing the costume of value rationality, or something the typology simply has no slot for. Turkey and Nippers are pure temperament, one governed by drink and the other by indigestion, which makes them easy cases. Bartleby is the hard one: he does offer a reason for what he does, but the reason is always phrased as a preference rather than a refusal, and it fits none of the four types of action while plainly not being mere behaviour either. And the closing revelation about the dead letters, offered by the narrator at the very end, looks like a last-minute attempt at causal adequacy that supplies a cause without supplying any meaning at all.

What I want from you is a reading of the story through this material, and a reading that is faithful in both directions — one that does not reduce Melville to an illustration of a sociological thesis, and does not bend Weber's categories to make the story come out neatly. Which scenes would you put at the centre of such a paper, and what does each one show: the office itself and its arrangement of screens and windows, the narrator's repeated attempts to reason with Bartleby, the decision to move offices rather than evict him, the landlord's and the other tenants' reactions, the prison yard? Where does the story dramatize something the Weberian framework gets right, and where does it resist the framework and force me to say so? I am especially interested in whether Bartleby is a counterexample to the typology of action or simply an unusual instance of it, and in whether the story shows anything about explaining with values that the methodological arguments cannot show on their own. I do not need citations or a reading list — I have the seminar bibliography and I know the story well, so please argue with the text rather than quoting it at length, and write for someone who has the whole thing in front of him.
