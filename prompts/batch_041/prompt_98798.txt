I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy, and I have run into a dispute I find myself unable to referee.

At a reunion of my old department, a mathematician and a theoretical physicist I know well fell into an argument after a colloquium on constructive mathematics. Both are careful thinkers, and the exchange went roughly as follows.

The mathematician opened: intuitionism is not really a rival logic at all, but a rival conception of what mathematics is about. The intuitionist attaches a different meaning to 'or,' 'there exists,' and 'implies' than the classical mathematician does—to assert a disjunction is to possess a proof of one side, to assert an existence claim is to have a construction—so when the intuitionist refuses a classical theorem, the two are not contradicting each other; they are using the same words for different things. The physicist objected that this cannot be right. If it were merely a change of subject, the intuitionist would have no grounds for calling classical reasoning invalid, yet intuitionists plainly do call it invalid; so there must be a genuine disagreement about what is correct, not merely a divergence of vocabulary.

The mathematician replied that the intuitionist does have grounds: classical reasoning permits one to assert a disjunction without knowing which disjunct holds, or an existence claim without any witness, and such 'proofs' do not deliver what a proof of a disjunction or of an existence claim is supposed to deliver. The physicist answered that this merely redefines 'proof' in the intuitionist's favour. He added that a classical proof can be embedded into intuitionistic logic by the translation that doubles negations—the one usually credited to Gödel and Gentzen—so the intuitionist's own system certifies classical reasoning as consistent, which is an awkward result for anyone who wants to call that reasoning illegitimate.

The mathematician conceded that the translation establishes consistency but denied that it establishes correctness: being able to simulate classical reasoning inside intuitionistic logic is not the same as classical reasoning being valid, and the intuitionist's complaint is not that classical logic is inconsistent but that it proves statements that are not established—the law of excluded middle applied to Goldbach's conjecture, say, when no one has settled the conjecture either way. The physicist said this was the very slide he had been objecting to all along: classically, that the conjecture or its negation is true does not require us to know which; truth is not the same as knowability; and the intuitionist's argument, so far as he could see, consisted entirely in moving from 'we do not know' to 'it is not true.'

At that point they appealed to me, and I had to admit I could not say who had the better of it. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange point by point rather than declare an overall winner: for each of the moves above, say who is right about what, distinguish the claims that are sound from those that are overstated on each side, and identify what, if anything, both parties are missing. I am especially interested in whether their disagreement is a genuine one about truth or only a disagreement about the meanings assigned to the logical vocabulary, and in whether the negative-translation point actually cuts in the physicist's favour or can be turned against him.
