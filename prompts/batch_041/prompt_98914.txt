I run a graduate seminar in the philosophy of artificial intelligence — sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, a seminar room with movable chairs — and I want a single in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the so-called debate proposal for scalable oversight. The proposal, in the form I care about, is this: a comparatively weak judge can in principle recover a correct answer from two adversarial advocates who are each more capable than the judge, because the honest advocate knows the truth and argues for it, the dishonest advocate knows the truth and argues against it, and the judge need only be able to check the advocates' individual claims even when it cannot solve the problem alone. The promise rests on a gap between finding an answer and checking one: if checking is cheaper than finding, the judge's verdict can outrun the judge's own competence. I am not after a debate exercise in the ordinary sense — two students arguing a controversial topic while the class votes. I want the structural mechanism of the proposal to be what the students run into with their own choices.

Here is the content the activity must carry, so that it stands on its own with no reading assigned: the honest advocate's advantage is only as good as the judge's ability to check the lowest-level claims on offer; a dishonest advocate can exploit a judge's finite checking budget by burying the false step inside a chain of reasoning that is too long or too intricate for the judge to verify in the time available, so the failure is structural rather than a lapse of care; and the proposal's own remedy is recursive — a disputed sub-claim can itself be put out for debate, so that the dishonest advocate is forced to defend each link against someone who can check it.

Please design the activity: roles, materials, the rules of the protocol, a scoring or payoff scheme that needs no money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the debrief. Give me one concrete instance I can photocopy — with a worked example and a template for generating more — built so that finding the answer is genuinely hard while checking a proposed answer is easy, and so that no outside knowledge is required. Requirements:

1. Stage it so that in the first round the students, acting as the judge, actually reach the correct answer even though none of them could have found it unaided. They should feel the amplification before they are told it has a name.
2. In a second round, arrange for the same protocol to fail — the judge should be systematically fooled, and the reason should be traceable to the checking budget and the structure of the arguments, not to the judge being lazy.
3. The dishonest advocate must have a legitimate-seeming route to an argument that exceeds the judge's budget, so the students discover the failure mode by using the rules as written.
4. The debrief must map each moment of the activity onto a specific part of the mechanism — the find/check gap, the honest advocate's reliance on checkability, the obfuscation failure, and the recursive remedy — and it must be frank about which feature of the proposal the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I still have to supply in discussion.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. Do not lean on named papers or citations; the activity and its debrief have to be self-contained.
