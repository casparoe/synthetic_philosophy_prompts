Last year I moved from backend infrastructure onto a team that builds automated decisioning for a payments company. The system decides, with no human in the loop, which incoming payouts to hold for fraud review. In February it held a customer's rent payment for eleven days on a false positive and she lost her flat. I sat on the post-incident review, and afterwards I wrote up the following argument, partly so that I would have something concrete to put to our legal and compliance people. I can no longer tell whether it is a good argument or a bad one. I have no philosophy training, so I would like the diagnosis in plain terms.

(1) A person is morally answerable for a harm only if the harm lay within their control: they could have acted so as to prevent it, and nothing outside their control was doing the decisive work.
(2) The harm to that customer was produced by a chain — the corpus the data team assembled, the loss function and threshold the research team chose, my team's integration of the model into the payout service, the decision to go live, and the model's own inference on the day.
(3) Take any one person in that chain. Each of them could have acted differently — cleaner data, a more conservative threshold, a slower rollout — and the harm might still have occurred, because what actually settled the outcome was a particular combination of inputs and weights that no single person selected and no single person could inspect.
(4) So no individual had control over the harm.
(5) Answerability is individual: for a harm to be someone's to answer for, it must be some one person's.
(6) So no one is answerable for the harm.
(7) A harm for which no one is answerable is a harm done to someone with no one to answer to, and that is a kind of injustice.
(8) So systems of this kind should not be deployed where the harms fall on people outside the company.

I can see that this proves too much. Substitute a patient's death in a large hospital, or a bridge that failed, for the withheld payout, and the same steps appear to run; yet I do not conclude that hospitals or bridge projects are unjustifiable. But I cannot find the step that is doing illegitimate work. Someone on the review panel says the weak point is (5) and that answerability can be collective; I do not see how that helps, since a harm still has to be someone's. I would rather keep (1) and (7) and give up (8), though that may simply be the conclusion I want.

Two further questions, which I think bear on the diagnosis. First, our proposed fix is a mandatory gate: any hold longer than forty-eight hours must be approved by an on-call engineer inside a sixty-second window. Does such a gate close the gap, or does it manufacture an answerable person who has no real control — an answerability that satisfies (7) while leaving (1) to (4) untouched? Second, I keep wanting to say that although nobody controls any particular decision, we do control how often false positives occur and how bad the consequences get, much as a factory manager controls an accident rate rather than any given accident. Is that a genuine repair to the argument or a change of subject?

Tell me which step to give up, why it looked fine in the first place, and what giving it up costs me. Please write your answer in British English.
