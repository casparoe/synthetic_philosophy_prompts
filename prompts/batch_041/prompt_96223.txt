I am the leader of the party that Peter Halloran led for eleven years, and his widow has asked me to give the eulogy at his state funeral on Thursday. Halloran was health minister twenty-three years ago, when a hemorrhagic fever we had never seen came through the country. There was one therapeutic and not much of it, and the allocation protocol his ministry adopted, drawn up in eleven days by people who were guessing, effectively wrote off the northern provinces and concentrated the drug in the capital. The epidemic burned out in the spring. The official count was four thousand dead, most of them northern; the modelling done afterwards suggested that any other distribution would have killed far more. What nobody knew until a historian opened the ministry archive five years ago is that he signed the protocol and then stood up in front of the country and denied that any rule of allocation existed at all, attributing the pattern of deaths to bad luck, because he believed that if the public learned the state was choosing, the drug shipments from abroad would stop and the country would come apart. He never admitted it. The inquiry into the outbreak has been sitting for two years and resumes next month, its counsel has already written to ask me to give evidence, and the association of the bereaved families from the north has announced that it will attend the funeral, asking only that we not pretend.

This is, I take it, the problem of dirty hands, and I need the speech to take that problem seriously rather than use it as a rug. I will not stand up and tell four hundred people that he did nothing wrong; he did wrong, and he knew it, which is why he never spoke of the epidemic again. Nor will I deliver a verdict, both because the inquiry is sitting and because a funeral is not a courtroom. A friend of mine who teaches political theory says the hardest part of this is that a democracy has to be willing to blame the people it needs: that if we want anyone to take on the guilt of governing, we must let them carry it publicly rather than quietly absolving them afterwards, and that a funeral is precisely the occasion on which we are tempted to do the absolving. I think she is right, and I want the speech to make that demand on the room rather than soothe it. But I also have to comfort his wife and his daughters, in a cathedral, in front of cameras, with a bishop speaking after me and a party that will read whatever I say as its position on the compensation bill we vote on in the autumn. He was not religious. Most of the room is. I am not.

Please write the eulogy, about eight hundred words, in a register that can be spoken slowly. It should not quote him, since the inquiry is contesting what he said and the family has asked that he not be made to speak from beyond the grave, and it should not quote scripture, since from me that would only be performance. Three audiences have to leave the cathedral feeling that they were addressed: the loyalists who want a statesman's funeral, the families from the north, and the journalists in the back row who will decide by Friday whether the speech was an apology, a whitewash, or an evasion. It has to work as a speech and not as an essay on the ethics of office.
