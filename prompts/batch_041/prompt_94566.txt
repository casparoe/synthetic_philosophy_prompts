I am writing the methodological preface to a book on climate, epidemic disease, and the early modern Atlantic world, and before I commit to it I want an audit of the position I have arrived at. Here it is, stated as flatly as I can manage and without hedging.

(1) Causal parity for non-human factors. Pathogens, volcanic aerosol forcing, and multi-decadal climate variability are full causal contributors to historical outcomes rather than background conditions against which human action takes place. The Tambora eruption of April 1815 and the harvest failures of the following year in Europe and New England belong in the causal account of the westward movement out of New England that followed, in the same way that a land policy or a shipping cost does; that the volcano had no intentions is beside the point. Nor do I take this parity to dilute responsibility, since nothing in it excuses what human agents did with the harvests they were given.

(2) Causation is not agency. To be an agent, an entity must be capable of responding to reasons, or at the very least to norms, and that is a substantive condition which no virus, eruption, or climate oscillation meets. I regard the current fashion for distributing agency across actants and vibrant matter as a category error, and an expensive one: it buys rhetorical force by flattening a distinction that does real explanatory work.

(3) Anti-determinism. Environmental determinism — the comparative thesis that environmental conditions explain the differences in development among human societies — is empirically thin and politically poisonous, and has served above all as a naturalizing idiom for colonial hierarchy. I want no part of it.

(4) Anthropocene realism. Humans are now a geological force. This is a description of the stratigraphic record and not a metaphor, and the concept is indispensable both scientifically and politically: we need a name for the fact that the species has become a driver of planetary process, and any historiography that refuses the name is unserious about the present.

(5) Contingency. Historical outcomes are not inevitable. Small perturbations can have very large downstream effects, and an explanation that narrates what happened as the only thing that could have happened has failed as an explanation.

(6) Causal selection is interest-relative. When we single out one cause and call it the cause, we are answering a question about what we want explained, not reporting a further fact about the world. There is no privileged cut in the causal graph marked out as the real cause.

(7) Explanation is differential. To explain something historically is to account for variation — why here and not there, why then and not later — rather than to narrate a sequence.

Does this hang together? I would like the tensions ordered by severity rather than presented as a list in which everything is equally bad. For each genuine conflict, say precisely what is inconsistent with what, what would have to be given up in order to resolve it, and what that concession costs; be explicit about which pairs only appear to conflict and why they do not; and finish with a verdict on which single revision would buy the most consistency for the least loss. I am not attached to any particular item, so do not assume that I know which of these I would sooner surrender — tell me which commitment is doing the work and which is along for the ride.
