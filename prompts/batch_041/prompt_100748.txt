I am assembling materials for a graduate metaphysics reading group on the special composition question, and I would like a one-page decision tree that carries a reader from a bare commitment about when some things make up a further thing to a named position, with every branching node phrased as a question the reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. Build the tree out of the logical space of answers rather than out of a list of -isms: begin with the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then place the actual positions in that space.

The distinctions I care about are these. First, whether composition is restricted at all: does anything ever compose a further thing, and is there any non-trivial condition on it? Second, if restricted, whether the condition is intrinsic to the candidate parts (for instance being in contact, fastened, or fused) or relational and external to them (for instance being caught up in a single causal process, or in a single life). Third, whether the answer makes composition a matter of degree or admits borderline cases, so that it can be indeterminate whether some things compose a whole. Fourth, whether composition is mind-dependent, that is, whether whether some things compose a whole can vary with how we carve up or count the world rather than being a further fact about it. Fifth, whether the question has one answer for every case or a different answer for different kinds of case, so that a single view can be, say, organicist about organisms and nihilist about artifacts.

The leaves should include at least: nihilism; universalism; organicism, on which the parts compose a whole only when they constitute a life; a contact view; a fastening or cohesion view; a causal-unity view; a brute or no-answer view; a deflationary or mind-dependent view; and a serial or case-by-case view. Where a leaf has a familiar name, use it; where it has none, mark it unnamed and state the combination of answers that generates it.

Use concrete cases to fix what the questions are asking, and make sure every node is answerable by someone who has not read the literature. The tree should at least handle the two bricks touching, a living cat, a pile of sand, a scattered collection such as the top half of a shoe together with a distant tower, a swarm of bees, a corpse, and a statue together with the clay it is made of. Where two leaves come apart only on cases the tree does not probe, say so rather than manufacturing a question that separates them by fiat.

Finally, diagnose the combinations of answers that no one occupies: for each empty region of the space, say briefly whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or merely because no one has bothered to defend it. Keep the whole instrument to a single page.
