I've been running a small reading group this term on pain and its discontents -- mostly grad students, plus a colleague from the pain clinic who keeps us honest about the neuroscience -- and we've gotten stuck on pain asymbolia in a way that's starting to feel less like a puzzle we haven't cracked than like two different conversations happening in the same room. The phenomenon itself nobody disputes. Patients with the relevant kind of lesion (our clinician says the localization is messier than the textbook stories make out, and keeps telling us we're sloppily conflating asymbolia with pain indifference and with congenital insensitivity, which she insists do quite different work for the argument) will report that the stimulus hurts, and some will still flinch or pull the limb away while others apparently don't; but none of them seem bothered. No distress, no suffering, sometimes they laugh it off, they'll let you repeat the stimulation over and over, they don't reach for analgesics. The philosophical fight is over what this tells us about the badness of pain. One side: here is pain without the badness, so the badness can't be an intrinsic feature of the sensation itself -- it has to be carried by the affective/motivational component, or by the subject's desires and aversions, or by an evaluative layer that the lesion stripped out. The other side: whatever those patients have, it isn't the pain we were theorizing about -- call it pain-like, call it a deficit in the capacity to be moved rather than a demonstration that pain's badness is detachable -- and the case shows us nothing except that we need to be much more careful about what the word is tracking. We go around this loop every few weeks and I can no longer tell wether it's an empirical disagreement we could resolve with better case data, a conceptual one about the extension of a term, or two groups answering seperate questions under one heading.

What I want from you is not a verdict on which side is right about the badness of pain, but a diagnosis of why the thing has stalled. My working hypothesis, and please treat it as a hypothesis to test rather than a frame to fill in, is that the real fault line isn't hedonism versus desire-satisfaction at all, but a disagreement about what kind of evidence a pathological case can be: one side treats the asymbolia patient as a natural experiment that isolates a variable inside a functional-role theory of pain, the other treats the first-person report as partly constitutive of the phenomenon, so a patient who reports pain while remaining unbothered is either misdescribing or describing something else -- and if that's the shape of it, no further case detail will ever settle it. If my hypothesis is roughly right, say what would actually count as progress here (a distinction, a better description of the cases, a formal constraint on the theory, or a principled decision to stop using pathology as a test case at all). If it's wrong, tell me what the disagreement actually is and what sort of thing could end it. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says -- use the standard positions and arguments, but don't bother with citations, I'll add references myself.

Finally, be upfront about how much of this is your own reconstruction: tell me where your diagnosis is most likely to fail, and name the specific consideration -- an argument, a piece of case evidence, a distinction about what asymbolia patients can still do -- that would flip you to a different diagnosis. I'd much rather have a diagnosis with a visible weak point than a tidy one that pretends the disagreement is cleaner than it is.
