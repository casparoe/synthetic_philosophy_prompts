I would like to check a line of reasoning about the leveling-down objection that I am fairly confident goes wrong somewhere, though I cannot see where. The objection, as I understand it, is aimed at egalitarianism: if equality is intrinsically valuable, then a world in which the better off are made worse off while no one is made better off is, in at least one respect, an improvement. That much I accept as a problem for egalitarianism. But it seems to me that prioritarianism must fare even worse. Prioritarianism gives extra weight to benefits received by the worse off; it takes the egalitarian concern with how people stand relative to one another and turns the dial up rather than down. So if leveling down is fatal to egalitarianism, is it not fatal to prioritarianism a fortiori?

Consider two outcomes. In A, the worse off are at 10 and the better off at 100. In B, the worse off are still at 10, but the better off are at 50. My understanding is that prioritarianism must rank B above A, since the distribution is more equal and prioritarianism is essentially a more intense version of the egalitarian concern. If that is right, which view actually escapes the objection? Is the only consistent option total utilitarianism, which would rank A above B? And if prioritarianism does somehow escape it, what distinguishes it from utilitarianism with a concave curve applied to well-being?
