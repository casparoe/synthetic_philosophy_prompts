I have been teaching Hofstadter's picture of creativity as variation on a theme — the model I have been calling, in seminar, the knob-twiddling model — and I would like to close the unit with a poem rather than a summary. The picture, as I have been presenting it, is this. A concept is not a definition but a region in a space whose axes are adjustable dimensions, and thinking a new thought is usually a matter of turning those knobs: producing a variant that is still recognizably of the same thing. What the concept is cannot be separated from the family of variations it tolerates, since to recognize a variant as a variant just is to perceive an invariant that survives the turning. The interesting cases sit at the edges. Sometimes a knob turns out to have belonged to the frame rather than the picture, and the change is not a variation but a change of subject; sometimes a feature that nobody would have called adjustable turns out to have been adjustable all along, and the thing is suddenly seen under a neighboring concept. That last move is the crux, and it is the hardest thing to describe from inside the space where it happens.

I want a poem that performs this rather than explains it, and I want to be able to hand it to the seminar and have them reconstruct the picture from the poem alone.

Write it to these constraints:

1. Six to eight stanzas, short lines, with one recurring line that appears once per stanza and never repeats exactly. Exactly one element changes from occurrence to occurrence, and successive occurrences should turn different kinds of knobs — addressee, tense, number, negation, register, scale, a noun swapped for a near neighbor, and so on. A reader should be able to list the knobs on a second reading.
2. Keep the recurring line short enough to hold in the head, so that the variation is audible.
3. One occurrence must be the crux: the change there should be one a reader would have sworn was not a knob at all, so that the line is suddenly about something else while remaining the same line. Do not flag it; let the surrounding stanza carry it.
4. The final occurrence must be the change of subject — not another value on an existing knob but a change in what the line is about, so that the whole sequence has to be re-read. Whether that move is genuinely discontinuous from the others, or only a larger twiddle, is the question I most want left standing. Do not resolve it.
5. No jargon in the body: not concept, variation, theme, dimension, parameter, space, frame, invariant, knob. The poem has to enact the idea, not gloss it.
6. Give it a one-word title that names the invariant the occurrences share, and keep that word out of the poem itself.

After the poem, a short note — one paragraph at most — listing, for each occurrence, which knob turns and what the invariant is; and saying where the poem leans on a contestable reading of the picture, for instance if it assumes the invariant is prior to the variations, or that the crux is a genuine discontinuity rather than a matter of degree.

If the picture as I have sketched it is muddled, or if the poem cannot do this — if an invariant that is supposed to be inferred from variations cannot be shown in a poem of this length without being named — say so plainly rather than writing something that only looks like compliance.
