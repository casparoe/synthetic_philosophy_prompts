I chair the grantmaking committee of the Halloran Foundation, a small family foundation that distributes about $6 million a year, mostly to well-evidenced global health charities. On our standard cost-effectiveness model those grants buy roughly 4,000 units of welfare per $1 million — a unit being our internal stand-in for a severity-weighted life-year — and the trustees have grown used to treating that figure as the benchmark against which every other application is judged. Our next meeting is a week from Thursday, and we must dispose of one application that has split the committee: a five-year, $3 million proposal from a two-university consortium to build and test an early-warning and mitigation system for a class of cascading infrastructure failures which, on the applicants' model, would otherwise cost something on the order of 10^14 units of welfare, essentially all of it falling on people who do not yet exist. The applicants put the probability that their system proves decisive at one in a million; our staff analyst, who spent three weeks inside their model, puts it at one in a billion. The figure I cannot get out of my head is that both numbers are preposterous by our usual lights: the analyst's estimate alone implies something like eight times our benchmark per dollar, and the applicants' implies several thousand times. The disagreement that has consumed three meetings turns out not to move the recommendation at all under plain expected-value reasoning, and it is precisely that which has unsettled everyone.

Two trustees have drawn opposite conclusions. One wants to decline, on the ground that no procedure which lets a one-in-a-billion chance dictate half of our annual budget can be a procedure we should adopt, and she has proposed amending the charter to compute expected value with every probability replaced by its square — a tempered rule under which this application would score under one percent of our benchmark and be dismissed out of hand. The other trustee insists this is probability neglect dressed up as prudence: we accept one-in-a-million risks with our own money and our own lives without a second thought, and if we had refused every intervention whose benefits accrued to people who did not yet exist, we would never have funded the vaccination work that is now the pride of the foundation. Neither has addressed the wrinkle that bothers me most: the applicants' own risk assessment concedes a probability of about one in a hundred thousand that the technology they develop is misused, and the harm they describe in that case is of the same order as the catastrophe they are trying to prevent — a risk they do not weigh anywhere in their headline number.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should land on a recommendation — fund in full, fund a scoping phase, decline, or decline with an invitation to resubmit — with a time frame that fits our meeting and the applicants' funding window, which closes at the end of next month, and with conditions concrete enough that the committee could adopt them by voice vote. The tempered rule has to be taken seriously enough that we say what is wrong with it, or concede that it should be adopted; the trustee who proposed it is not a crank and will not be fobbed off with platitudes about prudence. And be honest about what the losing side gets right, including what we would be saying to the trustee who fears we have just set a precedent by which any confident applicant with a spreadsheet can annex the endowment. Argue it in our own terms rather than by appeal to outside authority, keep it under two pages, and remember that the CFO wants any multi-year commitment booked in this fiscal year.
