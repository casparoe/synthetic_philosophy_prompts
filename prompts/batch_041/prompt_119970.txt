I'm the TA for a grad seminar on the foundations of mathematics, and I've been handed the job of writing the take-home question for our unit on whether arithmetic could turn out to be inconsistent. The discussion in class was kinda a mess — everyone kept lobbing "but Gödel" and "but Hilbert" at each other and nobody actually said anything about what a discovery of inconsistency would even be a discovery of — so I want a question they can't answer by reciting the incompleteness theorems. Here's the setup I want it built around (I made this scenario up, so it isn't something they can go look up, though the surrounding issues are the real ones): suppose a preprint appears claiming a derivation of 0=1 in a standard formalization of arithmetic; the derivation has been checked by a proof assistant, and a handfull of competent people have gone through it by hand without finding a flaw. The authors say this settles it — the basic axioms of arithmetic are inconsistent. In class I staged a role play with three positions: (1) this shows classical logic is the problem and we should go paraconsistent; (2) this shows the induction schema was the mistake, and we should retreat to a strictly finitary arithmetic; (3) the rational response is to keep doing mathematics as if nothing had happened, because the prior probability that a hand- and machine-checked derivation of a contradiction is actually correct is far lower than the prior probability of some shared subtle mistake — in the proof assistant's kernel, in the informal reading of the formalization, in the claim that the formalization really captures arithmetic, whatever.

Could you write me (a) the essay question itself — these are students who've had a semester of this stuff, it should be answerable in about 90 minutes with no notes, and a good answer has to do at least three things: say what's actually being claimed by "the axioms of arithmetic are inconsistent" as opposed to "here is a derivation of a contradiction that nobody has yet refuted", and why those come apart; take position (3) seriously rather than just calling it cope, including what would have to be true about our epistemic situation for that stance to be a defensible methodology rather than motivated reasoning; and say whether the retreat in position (2) can actually buy any safety, given that the safe base is itself a bit of mathematics that we accept on the same kind of grounds. I'd also like the question to leave room for the student to say something about whether "arithmetic is inconsistent" is even a well-formed thing to fear, depending on what you think mathematical statements are about — I don't want them to have to commit to a view, but an answer that never notices the issue shouldn't get full marks. Then (b) a model answer of around 800 words that genuinely does those things instead of name-dropping, and (c) a marking scheme: five must-hit points, 4 marks each for 20 total, with a one-line note for each on the characteristic way students fumble it.
