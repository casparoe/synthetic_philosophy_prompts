I'm putting together a session for a comparative literature reading group whose theme this term is literature and metaphysics, and the members are a mixed bag: two philosophers who will pounce on any loose handling of a concept, a couple of novelists who will object the moment a story is treated as a mere illustration of a thesis, and several general readers who know Borges's fiction but have never touched his essays. My chosen pairing is Borges's story 'The Garden of Forking Paths' and his essay 'A New Refutation of Time', and what I want from you is a reading of the story through the philosophy of time that Borges sets out in the essay, rather than a summary of either piece. The story, as I understand it, gives us a world of endlessly forking possibilities — the novel-within-the-story is a labyrinth in which every alternative is realised, and the sinologist who has finally decoded it presents it as a picture of time itself — whereas the essay appears to go the other way, arguing that the continuity of time is an illusion, that succession is not real, and that each moment is somehow self-contained, so that talk of a single flowing time is a mistake. I should say plainly that I am not asking you to settle which text is 'right'; I want a reading that takes both seriously and tells me how the tension between them is best resolved, and which way the resolution cuts.

Does the story dramatise the essay's denial of time, or does it quietly refute that denial by presupposing exactly the succession the essay rejects? Where in the story does the lens genuinely illuminate something — the deciphering of the labyrinthine novel, the long conversation between Yu Tsun and Stephen Albert, the final act in which the name Yu Tsun must transmit turns out to be both the town he is aiming at and the surname of the man he has just killed — and where does the story resist the reading, forcing me to admit that the fiction is doing something the argument cannot? I would also like you to say what the story adds to the philosophical debate that the essay alone does not, and to warn me about the readings I should head off during the discussion. In particular, I suspect at least one member will want to conclude that Borges simply 'believed in' parallel universes, and another will treat the whole thing as mystical hand-waving; I would rather not have the evening collapse into either.
