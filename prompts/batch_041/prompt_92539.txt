I'm a science journalist who writes mostly about AI, and this spring I'm guest-teaching a module in a graduate seminar on mind and machine — the kind of course where the students are fluent in both the engineering and the philosophy but keep collapsing one into the other. The stretch of the syllabus I care most about is the question of whether a trained network's internal states really are representations of anything, or whether "representation" is just the word we reach for once a probe or a sparse dictionary hands us a feature we happen to find interpretable. I want a final exam question that pushes them past the two stock moves — "of course it represents things, look how well it predicts them" and "it's all just weights, representation is a human overlay" — and makes them do the real work of saying what would have to be true for representational realism about a network to be warranted, and what a serious deflationary alternative would have to deny in order to be more than an attitude.

To keep them from writing a literature review, I've been sketching a criterion I want them to attack head-on. Call a candidate representational unit — a direction in activation space, a "feature" from a sparse dictionary, a small circuit — genuinely represented just in case it passes two tests: first, an intervention test, on which steering along that direction (or ablating it) produces the specific downstream behavioural changes the representation is supposed to underwrite; and second, a robustness test, on which the same unit turns up across random seeds, architectures, and training runs rather than being an artifact of one training trajectory. My students tend to treat passing both as settling the matter, and I suspect that is too quick — partly because the non-uniqueness of decompositions means two mutually incompatible feature sets can each pass, and partly because I am not convinced that "being causally used as a unit" is the same thing as "representing," rather than merely "being serviceable as a lever." I want the exam question to make them locate precisely where the criterion earns its keep and where it quietly begs the question.

Please write the exam question (a single essay question a strong student could answer in about 90 minutes), a model answer of roughly 800 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 marks. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make — including at least: distinguishing a first-order claim (the network represents X) from a second-order claim (our interpretability model represents the network as representing X); stating what notion of representation the criterion presupposes and what it leaves out; and working through a concrete case in which the two tests come apart — and it should name the specific mistakes that ought to cost marks, for instance sliding from "the probe decodes X" to "the network represents X," or treating robustness across seeds as evidence for realism when the deflationary view predicts it just as well. Please make the question answerable without access to any papers, and do not ask the students for citations.
