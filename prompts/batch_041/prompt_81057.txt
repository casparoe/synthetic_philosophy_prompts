I chair the research-integrity committee at a medical school, and Thursday's case has us split. A faculty member's paper states in its abstract that "in our cohort, the exposure was associated with roughly a halving of the risk of the outcome." The sentence is true as written: the unadjusted rate ratio was 0.51. What the abstract omits is that the association disappears once the model adjusts for the confounder that the paper's own discussion names as the leading one, and that the adjusted estimate sits in a supplementary table. A reader who tried to reproduce the number filed the complaint; she argues that no ordinary reader of that sentence would take it to mean the association survives adjustment. The author answers that he reported what the cohort showed, never used the word "adjusted," and put everything in the supplement. Our policy covers "falsification" and "misrepresentation of results," but defines both in terms of false statements and altered data, and says nothing about statements that are true yet leave a false impression. A colleague who teaches philosophy of language tells me the crux is the gap between what the sentence says and what the author meant by it, and that this gap bears on whether we can attribute intent at all.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate: a recommendation we can vote on, the reasoning behind it, and an honest account of the strongest argument for the other outcome. Two proposals for a standing rule are also before us, and I want your assessment of each, including what it would imply for this case and for the next one on our docket, in which a press release said a compound "has been tested in humans" when the only human study was a microdose safety run in eight healthy volunteers: (1) a statement misrepresents only if the author could not have added a clarifying clause, such as "though not after adjustment for X," without contradicting what he already wrote; (2) the question is instead whether the audience the statement actually reached, rather than the specialist reader, would reasonably take the false impression from it, whatever the author privately intended. Tell me which rule, if either, we should adopt, and what adopting it commits us to.
