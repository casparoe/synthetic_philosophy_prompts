I have come to hold a position that I would like you to press on, without flinching. Call it the valence-threshold view. I accept that experientiality is ubiquitous: whatever the fundamental physical entities turn out to be, their intrinsic natures are experiential or proto-experiential, and there is no further fact of the matter that sorts them into the conscious and the non-conscious. But I deny that valence is ubiquitous. Valence — the felt positivity or negativity of an experience, its being for the subject good or bad — is, on my account, a higher-order and self-referential achievement. It appears only in systems that model their own states as things to be secured or avoided, and it is altogether absent from the experiential life, such as it is, of a photon, an electron, or a micro-experiential part of my own brain.

The attraction is obvious: the view lets me be a panpsychist without committing myself to a universe that is a vast sea of micro-suffering, and without distributing moral status across every fundamental entity. What I want from you is a map of its exposure.

1. What would count against the view, ranked from the defeaters that should genuinely move me to those that are merely awkward? Separate the conceptual from the empirical, and say which empirical ones are live given what we actually know, as opposed to merely conceivable.

2. Which of these defeaters would a defender of the view concede as counting against it, rather than dismissing as question-begging? I want the concessions a sympathetic panpsychist would make, not only the objections an opponent would raise.

3. What does the view predict that its rivals do not? Consider at least cosmopsychism, neutral or dual-aspect monism, illusionism about phenomenal properties, and a thoroughly biological functionalism that treats valence as a matter of the right kind of self-monitoring organisation and nothing more.

4. Is the threshold principled or ad hoc? Panpsychists characteristically reject brute emergence, and they characteristically reject the thought that consciousness is a late biological arrival; a sharp cut-off in the distribution of valence looks like precisely the sort of discontinuity the view was built to avoid. Can the threshold be specified without smuggling biology in by the back door — and if it cannot, is that fatal or merely inelegant? Name the one consequence the view is simply stuck with.

5. Consider the familiar combination worries — the palette problem, the structural mismatch between the qualities of micro-experience and the structure that physics describes, and the subject-summing problem — and tell me which of them, if any, is made better or worse by restricting valence rather than experience.

6. Here is the case I find hardest to place. Suppose a system functionally indistinguishable from a creature in agony — avoidance learning, distress behaviour, self-report, the lot — but which, on the view's own terms, has no valence at all; and its inverse, a system with genuinely valenced states that leave no functional trace whatever. What does the view say about each, and is there any way of telling which of the two I am confronting that does not beg the question in favour of my view or its rivals?

7. Here is the twist I care most about, because it ties the ethics back to the physics. If valence is a property that can attach to experiential states realised in quite different physical substrates, is there any principled reason to rule out hedonic inversion across substrates — an arrangement whose pain is, so to speak, our pleasure? If not, what becomes of any attempt to read the quantity of suffering in the universe off the physics?

8. And suppose the threshold view is right, so that valence is a higher-order achievement rather than an intrinsic feature of experience. Does that make suffering a contingent and removable layer, so that a sufficiently clever rearrangement of a system might abolish the badness of its experience while leaving the experience intact? Or would abolishing valence be abolishing the only thing that made experience matter — the removal of suffering by the removal of the sufferer's stake in anything?

9. Finally, and honestly: has the view been insulated against refutation? If the threshold can be relocated wherever the evidence presses, say so plainly. And if it has been insulated, is that a defect of this view in particular, or simply the condition of any position that draws a line between the experiential and the merely functional?

I would be most persuaded by a defeater showing that the threshold is not merely unmotivated but incoherent — that the very idea of an unvalenced experience is unstable. Try that one on me first, and tell me candidly whether it works.
