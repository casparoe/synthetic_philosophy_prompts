Something that's bugged me for a while and I can't tell if it's a real tension or just institutions doing institution things. US perjury basically needs a false statement — Bronston walked after giving literally true but evasive answers about his Swiss bank accounts, and the Court's fix was "counsel should ask better follow-up questions." But in securities law, a statement that's true as far as it goes while omitting the thing that would make it not misleading *is* the fraud, and roughly the same goes for advertising and for medical informed consent, where not telling the patient something is treated like telling them something false. Now regulators are going after "dark patterns," which don't assert anything false at all but reliably leave you with a false belief. So the criminal rule looks like it's committed to a different theory of what's wrong with lying than the regulatory stuff is. (I think the deliberately-technically-true answer is called "paltering"?)

What theory does the perjury rule presuppose about the wrong of lying — that it's the breach of an assertion's truth-warrant, so that merely exploiting the hearer's inference isn't the same kind of act? Does the rest of the pattern presuppose a deception-based or autonomy-based theory instead, and are these actually inconsistent or just different domains with different stakes? Be careful to separate what the criminal practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with — I suspect a lot of it is really about proof problems, notice, and who gets to ask follow-ups rather than about moral philosophy, and I want to know if that's right or if there's a genuine commitment underneath. And tell me what would have to change in criminal practice if we adopted the deception-based view.
