I teach a second-year undergrad seminar on memory, history and identity -- 18 students, one 80-minute session, no reading assigned in advance, and I'd rather they do something than listen to me. Here's what I want them to feel: that when a community's memory and the documentary record disagree, "which one gives way?" isn't one question but at least three, because the two things aren't doing the same job. The record is supposed to track what happened. The memory is partly constitutive -- it's some of what makes the community that community. And then the bit I actually care about, which is that memory also has a grip on the record itself: communities remember what got left out of the archive, and that's still memory even when there's no document to check it against. So the archive has a shadow, and the shadow is remembered.

Design me a role-play or structured deliberation where they run into this themselves, before I say any of it out loud. My rough sketch is below -- gut it if you've got something better, but keep the shape.

Setting: a fictional town, and a centennial exhibit. Each table has to write a single museum plaque of about 120 words. Three tables of six, same packet for all three, and we compare the plaques at the end.

The conflict in the packet: the town's memory holds that a founding benefactor was complicit in driving several families off their land. The record in the packet -- company ledgers, county deeds, a survey map -- shows the sales were voluntary, paid at market rate, and the benefactor's name appears nowhere. The record is not fragmentary here; it's just contrary.

Roles, and the rules are what make it bite. The archivist may only assert what a document in the packet supports, and may not cite the oral accounts at all. The two memory-holders may only assert what the oral accounts say, and may not cite a document. The benefactor's descendant can veto one sentence. The school board member has to be able to defend the plaque to angry parents. The outsider historian is the only one allowed to use both sources, but every sentence they write has to be tagged "documented" or "remembered." The plaque has to be unanimous, and it has to answer three questions without hedging: what happened, who was responsible, what's owed. No "some say," no "allegedly."

Then the twist, about halfway through: a private letter surfaces that supports the memory -- but it was written by the benefactor's estranged daughter-in-law decades later, and it only reached the archive because a collector bought it at auction in the 90s. Now they have to fight about whether provenance or content governs admissibility, and whether the community's memory gets to set the agenda for what counts as evidence in the first place.

One more thing to build in: don't let the memory-holders agree with each other. The two oral accounts should contradict each other on the central fact, so nobody can treat "the community's memory" as a single block that either wins or loses against the record.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time -- I'm betting they converge instantly on something bland that names nobody, and I'd like a rule that makes that option unavailable rather than just discouraged. And be honest about what this can't reproduce: I already know it can't give them the time-depth, or the fact that real memory is passed between people who love each other, and that because I wrote the packet they'll treat the whole thing as a puzzle with a solution instead of a condition you live in.
