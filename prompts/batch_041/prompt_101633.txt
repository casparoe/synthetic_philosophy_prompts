I am assembling a position paper for a small workshop on procreative ethics, and before I circulate it I would like the view it defends put through a proper audit. Please take on the role of a philosopher who specializes in population ethics and the ethics of procreation — someone fluent in Benatar's asymmetry argument and in the objections it has attracted — and treat what follows not as an argument to be improved stylistically but as a set of commitments to be stress-tested for mutual coherence. Work carefully, and begin by restating each commitment in your own words, in a sentence or two, so that I can confirm you have read it as I intend it. Only after that should you turn to the question of whether the set hangs together.

My commitments are these:

(1) I accept the asymmetry that lies at the core of Benatar's case: the presence of pain is bad, the presence of pleasure is good; but the absence of pain counts as good even though no one enjoys that good, whereas the absence of pleasure counts as bad only where there is someone who is thereby deprived. It follows that the never-existent are not worse off for lacking pleasure, though they are better off for lacking pain.

(2) I am a value pluralist. Pleasure and pain are not the whole of what matters: achievement, understanding, love, and meaning are good in their own right and not merely as sources of agreeable experience, and the loss of such goods is not simply the loss of pleasant feeling.

(3) Harms require a subject. Where coming into existence harms someone, it harms the particular person who is created, and the harm consists in the bad things that befall that person; it is not an impersonal blemish on the state of the world.

(4) I do not accept that procreation is generally impermissible. Bringing a child into existence is not in itself a wrong done to that child, and parents who have children in ordinary circumstances have not done something for which they owe an apology.

(5) I hold that a life can be worth continuing even when it would not have been worth starting. My own life is worth living to me now, and yet I do not think I would have been wronged had I never been brought into existence at all.

(6) I think there is something seriously problematic about deliberately bringing into existence a child whose life will predictably be dominated by severe suffering, when one could instead have had a child whose life would go well.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey. Before you judge anything, take a moment to think through the strongest version of each commitment — the version its most capable defenders would recognize — so that you are not manufacturing conflicts out of loose formulations. Then sort the pairwise relationships among (1) through (6) into three groups:

First, pairs that are in genuine tension. For each, explain precisely what the conflict is, and rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious. For each, state what would have to be given up or revised to remove it, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere — what I would lose, what further commitments it would drag in its wake, and whether it would leave the rest of the set recognizable as the view I set out to defend.

Second, pairs that only appear to be in tension. For each, explain what makes them look incompatible and why the appearance dissolves once the commitments are read correctly. Be explicit about the reading that does the dissolving, and say whether that reading is a natural one or a rescue.

Third, pairs that are simply compatible, if any are worth mentioning.

I would rather you identify two or three real tensions and defend that selection than flag all fifteen pairs as somehow in tension; a good audit discriminates. Finally, tell me which single revision would clean up the most while costing the least, and flag any commitment above that is doing more work than its wording can bear — that is, any place where I seem to be relying on an unstated premise I would not want to defend in print.
