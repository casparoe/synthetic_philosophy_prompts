My closest friend and I are two wedding photographers who have spent the last several weeks arguing about whether to spend a substantial part of our savings on a booth at a large bridal expo, where we would offer a written money-back guarantee to any couple who books us and is unhappy with the photographs. We ended last night in a long, circular argument. His position is that the expense is not a cost but the whole point: a photographer who is not confident in their work could not afford to make that promise and risk the refunds, the lost time, and the reputational hit, so the promise itself proves something about us to couples and, more importantly, to the wedding planners whose recommendations drive most of our bookings. My position is that this proves less than he thinks. A bad photographer could make the same promise, take the bookings, and then either argue about what counts as unhappy or fold the business before paying; the guarantee only works if there is some mechanism that makes it hurt us in a way it would not hurt a bad photographer. He replied that this is just cynicism, and that in any market some things are believed because they are expensive, and that if we refuse to play the game we will be invisible. I think he is half right, and that is what worries me.

What I need is not a general pep talk about branding but a precise way to decide whether his plan is a genuine signal or a shared waste. I would like you to help me lay out the conditions under which a costly display actually conveys information to someone who cannot directly inspect our work, and the conditions under which it collapses into a tax that every competitor pays and no client trusts. Please pay particular attention to the asymmetry between us and a worse photographer: what has to be true about the cost, the refund, the time horizon, and the planner's incentives for the guarantee to separate good photographers from bad ones rather than merely raising the price of entry? I am also unsure whether the right move is to make the signal even more costly, to find a cheaper signal that is harder to fake, or to refuse the logic altogether and compete on something else. Finally, help me anticipate the counterargument he will make when I bring this up again: he will say that if the signal were not informative, the market would have stopped rewarding it, and that our reluctance is just fear. I want to know how to answer that without pretending that all costly displays are irrational, and what concrete proposal I should put on the table when we meet on Friday.
