I'm a teaching fellow for a 400-level moral philosophy seminar — something like "Wounds, Worth, and Standing" — and I've been handed the final this year. The unit I care most about is the one on honor, dignity, and victimhood as competing moral cultures: the idea that different societies put a person's worth in different places, which changes what counts as an injury, what counts as a remedy, and who gets to complain. I've grown alot allergic to questions the students can ace by reciting the standard three-way contrast (honor = reputation and dueling, dignity = inalienable worth and rights, victimhood = grievance and call-out). I want a question that makes them actually think, not remember.

Could you act like a seasoned instructor who's taught this material for years, and first spend a few sentences brainstorming what the real crux is — like, what's the thing a B student will miss — before you draft anything? Then give me the deliverables. Here's the twist I want built in. In lecture I've been pitching a metaphor I call the ledger of worth: every culture keeps some kind of account of what a person is owed, and the three types differ in who holds the account and which way it runs. On the honor picture your worth is a debt other people owe you, enforced by reputation, and an insult is a withdrawal — so the account is zero-sum and you have to defend the balance. On the dignity picture the account can't be touched at all: worth is inalienable, so insults are bookkeeping errors rather than withdrawals. On the victimhood picture — the one I can't get students to think about carefully — the account seems to run backwards: status accrues to whoever is owed the most, i.e. to the one with the largest outstanding balance of unredressed injury.

What I want the question to force is whether that backward ledger is genuinely a third moral culture or just honor culture with the sign flipped, and what follows for the question of whether any of the three is morally superior. So: one in-class essay question (75 minutes, closed book), a model answer around 700 words, and a marking scheme worth 25 points. The marking scheme should name the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost points — not vague stuff like "shows insight," but the actual moves, e.g. what an answer has to say to earn the points about whether the backward ledger is parasitic on honor's zero-sum structure, and seperately about whether the who-gets-to-complain feature of victimhood can be defended without collapsing into might-makes-right-in-reverse. Give each must-hit point a point value and a one-line note on how students typically botch it. And please make sure the question itself can't be answered by summarizing the three cultures — the crux has to be the ledger argument.
