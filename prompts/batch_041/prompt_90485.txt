I'm teaching a grad seminar on computability and the mind this term and the first session is a 9am Monday, so I want to open with a joke — partly to wake people up, partly because the whole seminar turns on something a bit funny that nobody in the field says out loud, namely that the thesis the course is named after isn't a theorem and can never be one. Here's the shape of what I want the joke to be about. We start with a hand-wavy informal notion: a method a clerk could grind through with paper and pencil, no insight or ingenuity required, and Church and Turing each hand us an exact replacement for it — abstract machines, lambda terms, recursive definitions. The replacements are provably equivalent to each other, which is a real theorem, but the further claim that they capture everything the informal notion was gesturing at is not provable; you just fail to find a counterexample for almost a century and eventually stop calling it a hypothesis and start using it as a definition, which is why we say thesis. Then there's the stronger empirical cousin that people in physics and philosophy of mind run with — every physically realisable process, whatever the underlying physics, can be simulated by one of these machines — which is where the exotic stuff comes in: worlds with infinitely many computational steps crammed into a finite stretch of time, oracle devices, etc. So the joke should turn on that epistemic situation: not ha ha Turing machines are slow, but something that only lands if you've noticed that the central claim of the field is a very well-supported definition, that its physical version is hostage to future physics, and that the naming is a small scandal everyone has tacitly agreed to ignore.

One joke, please, written out as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just the way you'd tell it out loud at a party full of people who've all read the 1936 paper and none of whom will laugh at a pun about halting. If it needs a sentence of setup or a sentence of afterword, keep those in the same plain prose. It doesn't have to be clean; I'd much rather it be a bit strange and actually about the thing than a tidy one-liner. And if you build it out of characters, don't make one of them a robot that wants to take over the world, thats the joke everyone has already heard.
