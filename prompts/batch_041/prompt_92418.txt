My friend and I spent most of last night arguing about what Rousseau would say about a proposal now before our city council, and I want a referee, not a survey of the options. The proposal: instead of building a conventional playhouse, the city would fund a permanent civic program in which ordinary residents — shopkeepers, students, retirees — are cast unpaid in plays they help write themselves, staged by professional directors in public squares, with every performance streamed live so that viewers at home can vote by phone on which of several endings the next night's performance will use. My friend thinks Rousseau would have been delighted, since the scheme erases the distinction between actors and spectators and turns an evening's entertainment back into something like a citizens' festival. I think he would have been appalled: it is still a spectacle, still professional illusion staged for an audience, and now paid for out of the public purse. Give us a verdict and defend it. Which features of the scheme actually bear on Rousseau's case against the theater and which are incidental — the amateur casting, the open-air setting, the public funding, the remote and voting audience? Is his objection to the stage as such, or only to the kind of stage a rich commercial capital breeds, so that a small republic could safely tolerate one? And if he rejects this scheme, what would he have the city fund instead? Take a definite side rather than laying out the possibilities. Begin with the verdict in a single sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
