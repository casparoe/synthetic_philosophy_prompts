I'm putting together the first week of a grad seminar on the numbers problem and I want to start with the spec rather than with anyone's answer to it. So before we read Taurek or any of the replies, tell me this: what does a theory of whom to save in a rescue case actually have to get right? I don't want a theory yet — I want the list of demands we should be making of one, and why each demand is on the list.

Give me the requirements, each with the case or consideration that forces it. The sorts of things I have in mind, though dont just take my list:

- the cases it has to handle: five strangers against one stranger; the same five against one who is my friend; five with serious but survivable injuries against one who will die without help; and the probabilistic version where the one is certain to die unless saved while each of the five has, say, a one-in-five chance of dying if left alone;
- the distinctions it must not be allowed to collapse: between the number of people helped and the total amount of good produced; between the strength of one person's claim and the weight of the numbers behind it; between what is better before the fact and what turns out better after it; between running a fair lottery among the claimants and simply maximising the number saved;
- constraints it can't contradict: the separateness of persons, the thought that people aren't just containers of welfare, and the assumption (if it really is one) that "better than" has to be transitive.

Then I want your view on two things in particular. First, which single requirement does the most work in ruling candidates out — the one that, if you dropped it, lets all the wrong theories back in? Second, is there a subset of these that can't be jointly satisfied? If aggregation and the separateness of persons, or ex ante fairness and ex post maximality, pull against each other, say so plainly and tell me which side the list ought to give up — that's the part I most want to see. And be straight with me about which requirements are genuine desiderata and which are just the prejudices of one camp dressed up as requirements.

One more thing: pitch the spec so it would be good enough to hand to someone designing an actual triage protocol for a disaster, not just for settling a philosophical puzzle — that's the standard I care about here. Keep it in British English, and seperate clearly what's genuinely settled from what's still contested among people who know this literature; I don't want a contested view presented as if it were the consensus.
