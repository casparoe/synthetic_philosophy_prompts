I belong to a reading group that has spent the term on Hellenistic ethics, and I have been given the opening slot next week to argue about the modern Stoic revival. My irritation is with the self-help wing of it, where the whole philosophy gets compressed into a single doctrine: some things are up to us and some are not, and the art of living consists in transferring your concern wholesale from the second category to the first. What I want is to test that doctrine against the case the revival itself treats as its showpiece, rather than against another abstract statement of it. Admiral James Stockdale was a US Navy pilot shot down over North Vietnam in September 1965; he spent the following seven and a half years as a prisoner in the Hanoi Hilton, was tortured repeatedly, was the senior naval officer in the camp and one of the organisers of the prisoners' resistance, and afterwards credited Stoic philosophy — Epictetus in particular — with getting him through. Part of his retirement went on studying the Stoics at a research institution at Stanford, which is one reason the modern revival has claimed him. The management literature has made a parable of him as well, the Stockdale paradox: the prisoners who did not survive were the optimists who kept expecting to be home by Christmas, while the survivors combined an unshakeable conviction that they would prevail in the end with a clear-eyed reckoning with how bad things currently were.

I would like the case analysed in both directions, and I want the doctrine stated precisely enough to be falsifiable before anything is mapped onto it: what exactly is supposed to be up to us, and what kind of relation is that (attribution, causal power, dependence on our own will?), and then what the strict doctrine says Stockdale faced in the camps — which of his responses, attitudes, projects and refusals fall on which side of the line, and what it permits him to care about. The half I care more about is where the case outruns the theory. I am suspicious of the word control, and I am aware that the Stoics had machinery the popular version mostly drops: the preferred indifferents; the practice of attaching a reservation to every plan, so that you commit to acting while accepting that you may be prevented; the division of the discipline into desire, action and assent; and the trichotomy (full control, partial influence, no control) that some modern writers quietly substitute for the dichotomy. Tell me whether that machinery does the work the popular doctrine is asked to do, whether any of it is genuinely available to a man in Stockdale's position, and whether the case is one the theory never anticipated — especially the hope the parable celebrates, given that the outcome was exactly the sort of thing the doctrine tells you not to stake yourself on. Then run it the other way: what does the episode show about the doctrine — a confirmation, a friendly amendment, or evidence that the revival's flagship teaching is a modern invention wearing ancient clothes?

Please write it in British English, and give me no reading list and no citations; I want the argument. Structure it as follows: "The doctrine, stated strictly" (about 200 words); "The episode under that description" (about 400 words); "Where the case outruns the doctrine" (about 450 words); "What the case shows about the doctrine" (about 350 words); and a closing "Bottom line" (about 100 words) telling me what I should actually say when my turn comes.
