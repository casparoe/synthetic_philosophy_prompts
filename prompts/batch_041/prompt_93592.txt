I teach a 300-level seminar on rational choice, 14 students, 75 minutes, whiteboard and a projector, no budget for anything. We've just finished expected utility theory and I want a session where they feel why a set of preferences that runs in a circle is supposed to be a defect and not just a quirk — the whole if-you-cycle-someone-can-take-your-money line. But I dont want to run the canned version where I hand them a non-transitive preference and then pump them. Two things have to be built in that the canned version skips.

First, their cycles should come out of something respectable, not out of sloppiness. So build the options as three small-stakes lotteries where the cycling is driven by how people handle very small and very large chances — the kind of setup where a normal, competent chooser will go A over B, B over C, and C over A without ever feeling confused or tricked. Stakes small and real (coffee money, or points redeemable for something at the end) so nobody has to sign anything, but they should care a little.

Second, and this is the part I actually care about: I want them to run into the escape hatch and have to decide whether it's an escape or a cheat. Before any choices get made, offer them the chance to lock in whatever bundle they end up holding and be untouchable for the rest of the session — no trades, no deals, whatever they're holding at the end is theirs. Some will take it, some won't. Then the trades happen as a series of seperate offers, each one individually a strict improvement (they swap into the option they prefer and get a little something extra on top), spread across the whole session so it never feels like a pump.

The debrief has to do the real work: is refusing to trade a legitimate way to keep cyclical preferences, or does it just mean you have to be the sort of agent who can stick to a plan? Does a series of trades, each of which is fine on its own, have to be fine taken together? And does the pump show that your preferences are incoherent, or only that you're exploitable?

Give me the rules, the materials, the three lotteries or a procedure for generating them, a 75-minute timeline, how the offers get administered without tipping them off, the debrief questions in order, and the stuff that will predictably go wrong the first time. My own worry is that half the room smells the trap and refuses everything, and the ones who took the lock-in just sit there smugly for an hour. Also be honest at the end about what the activity can't reproduce — my guess is that at these stakes it can't make the losses feel like real losses, and it can't make the question about whether trades compose into a package feel like anything other than bookkeeping.
