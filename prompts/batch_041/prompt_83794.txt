After thirty years in a physics laboratory I have spent the last three years teaching myself philosophy, mostly in a fortnightly reading group of fellow retirees, and we have arrived at a problem I cannot settle on my own. We have been working through the ontological argument: the Anselmian version, the Cartesian variant, and the modal versions, including the one attributed to Gödel, whose axioms concern which properties count as perfections and which license the inference that a being possessing all of them is at least possible. Our group has reached the unhappy state in which everyone holds an opinion and nobody can say which premise they actually reject. I would like you to build us a decision tree.

As we have reconstructed it, the argument needs at least four things: a definition of the being in question (nothing greater can be conceived, or a being that could not have failed to exist); a claim that such a being is possible, or at any rate not incoherent; a modal principle strong enough to convert that possibility into a necessity; and the claim that existing, or existing necessarily, is among the features that make a being greater. If our reconstruction is garbled, say so in a sentence or two at the outset and correct it, then proceed. Begin from that logical space rather than from a list of positions: fix the assumptions the argument requires, and let each branch node be the question that separates those who accept an assumption from those who reject it. Every node must be phrased so that a member of the group can answer it without knowing which position it leads to, and it should turn on a concrete case or a plain claim rather than on which label one finds congenial.

Two things must be built in, because they are what keep me up at night. First, my professional instinct is to ask what kind of quantity greatness is supposed to be: a scale with an upper bound, like a greatest integer, or something for which the question of a maximum is a category mistake, or a limiting case that need not be attained. Give that worry a node. Second, I keep meeting an objection I can only paraphrase: that the modal principles are so strong that the conclusion spreads, so that once the argument runs, nothing remains contingent, and this is offered as a reductio of the principles rather than of the conclusion. Give that a node too, and give the opposite node to those who think the word possible shifts meaning between the premise and the conclusion, being merely epistemic at the start and metaphysical at the end.

The tree should reach at least ten leaves, and it must include: acceptance of the argument; the denial that existence is a great-making feature at all; the parody strategy, with a node asking what disanalogy with the greatest conceivable island or pizza is principled rather than ad hoc; the charge that the possibility premise is credible only to someone already convinced; the concession that the argument establishes at most a necessary something, abstract or impersonal, rather than an object of worship; and the view that the argument at most yields a conditional theorem about a concept, never a fact about the world. Leaves must be positions, not names, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, and mark combinations nobody occupies, saying briefly whether they are empty because incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Use no citations and no reading lists; I cannot look them up, and the tree should stand on its own. Do not tell me which leaf is true. Present the result as a numbered, indented tree of roughly a dozen branch nodes, written for readers comfortable with possible-worlds talk but not with formal proof systems.
