I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, at work on a long-form piece about how Bayesian confirmation theory copes with evidence that was already in hand before the theory it supposedly supports came along. I've arrived at a position I now want to stress-test, and I'd like you to map its exposure — where it risks something, and where it's been quietly fortified against any risk at all.

The position is this. The old evidence problem is a genuine defect in the Bayesian account of confirmation, not a harmless technicality. On the standard story, a fact an agent already fully believes cannot confirm a hypothesis that predicts it, since conditioning on something whose probability is already one leaves credences exactly where they were — yet working scientists routinely describe long-known observations as confirming a new theory, and that gap is not a mere bookkeeping slip. My further, diagnostic claim is that no clever formal patch can close it. The trouble is that the framework has no principled way to distinguish merely possessing a fact from having that fact active or salient in the reasoning at hand, and the usual remedies — letting the agent come to know a logical relation it somehow already knew, re-weighting rather than conditionalizing, or appealing to a counterfactual credence in which the evidence had not been in hand — either change the subject or smuggle in exactly the psychology the formalism was meant to replace.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they actually are, not by how often they get recited. In particular: which of the standard repairs is a real solution rather than a restatement of the problem, and which escape routes for the Bayesian are live rather than merely logically available? Is my diagnosis parasitic on one particular formalization — evidence as a proposition, certainty as probability one — such that a better formalization dissolves the whole issue? Does the problem survive the parallel difficulty about new theories, and does that strengthen or undercut my case? Which of these points would a committed Bayesian concede as counting against them, as opposed to what only critics say on their behalf? And be honest about the last question: has my position been qualified into something that predicts nothing and forbids nothing, and if so, is that fatal for a claim of this kind or simply the price of doing philosophy of science? Close by telling me which single consideration you think should move me most, and why.
