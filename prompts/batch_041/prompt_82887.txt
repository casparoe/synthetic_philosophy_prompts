I'm a law student writing a seminar paper on the philosophy of political authority, and I keep running into a pattern in how states actually behave that I can't square, so I'd like you to help me reconstruct what the pattern presupposes. Take two cases. In the first, a group of officers and civilians forcibly moves against a government widely regarded as brutal and corrupt; the attempt fails, and the participants are tried for treason and imprisoned for decades. In the second, a nearly identical attempt succeeds: the old regime collapses, the new government is recognized within a year, and — this is the part that puzzles me — the courts of the new order, and eventually foreign courts too, treat its founding decrees as having been valid law from the moment they were issued, its pre-recognition acts are ratified retroactively, and the very people who would have been hanged in the first case are buried as founders in the second. So the same legal order, or its successor, condemns rebellion in general and honors it in the particular, and legitimacy seems to attach to the winning rather than to the justified. Compounding my confusion: many of these same states insist that soldiers and civil servants must refuse unlawful orders, and that oaths of office run to a constitution rather than to any person — which looks like it licenses precisely the private judgment that the treason statutes forbid.

Please act as a political philosopher with deep knowledge of both medieval Christian political thought and modern constitutional and legal theory, and think carefully before you commit to a verdict; I'd rather you spend your first effort laying out competing readings than rush to a tidy answer. What theory of political authority and political obligation does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view as precisely as you can, and be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with — I suspect it is compatible with more than one theory, and I want to know where it is genuinely underdetermined. Address at least these candidates: a purely de facto or effective-control account of authority, on which success itself constitutes the title; a common-good account on which a tyranny is not genuine authority at all, so that resisting it is not rebellion in the first place, but on which the remedy must come from public authority rather than from private presumption (Aquinas's discussion of sedition is the obvious classic here, and I'd like it engaged seriously, especially his worry that a tyrant removed by someone's private initiative may leave the people worse off than the tyrant did); and a legal-positivist account on which validity tracks only a system's rules of recognition, so that retroactive ratification is simply ordinary legal housekeeping with no philosophical remainder. Then tell me whether the retroactive validation of successful revolutions is actually required by any of these views or is instead a pragmatic device for preserving legal continuity, and whether the simultaneous demand that officials disobey unlawful orders can be made consistent with the condemnation of private attempts on a ruler, or whether the practice is simply incoherent on this point. Finish with the counterfactual: if we replaced the implicit view with a Lockean right of revolution, or with the strict positivist account, what concretely would have to change in the practice — in the treason statutes, in the doctrines of retroactivity and recognition, in the oath of office, in how the courts treat the founding acts of a regime they have just recognized? I don't need citations or a reading list; reason from the structure of the positions, and give me a clear bottom line about which reading you think the practice actually commits to and how confident you are in that diagnosis.
