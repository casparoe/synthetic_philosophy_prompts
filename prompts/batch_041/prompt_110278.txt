I am grading a take-home examination for a second-year undergraduate module in epistemology and the philosophy of language, and I would like your assessment of the script below.

**Question.** Let the *knowability thesis* be the claim that every truth is knowable: for any proposition p, if p is true then it is possible that p is known. Take for granted, in addition, (i) that knowledge entails truth — if p is known then p — and (ii) that knowledge distributes over conjunction: whoever knows a conjunction knows each of its conjuncts.

(a) Reconstruct the argument that the knowability thesis, so understood, together with (i) and (ii), entails that every truth is in fact known. (8 marks)

(b) State precisely what this result does and does not establish about the knowability thesis. (6 marks)

(c) Identify the two main families of responses and commit to one of them, giving reasons. (6 marks)

**Script.**

"Take any true proposition p and suppose, for reductio, that it is not known: p ∧ ¬Kp. Since this is a truth, the knowability thesis tells us that it is possible that it is known.

Consider what knowing it would come to. Knowledge distributes over conjunction, so a knower of p ∧ ¬Kp would know p and would also know ¬Kp. But knowledge is infallible: whatever is known is true in every possible world, and not merely in the world of the knower. So a knower of ¬Kp is in no position to be wrong about p's being unknown; that is, p is not known. The knower would therefore both know p and not know p. Contradiction. So nothing of the form p ∧ ¬Kp is true, and since p was arbitrary, no truth goes unknown: if all truths are knowable, then all truths are known.

It is important to see what this does not show. It does not show that knowledge is impossible, nor that the knowability thesis is incoherent on its own; it shows that the thesis, together with the two principles above, is incompatible with the existence of unknown truths. Since there plainly are unknown truths, the thesis must be given up unless one of the other ingredients can be questioned. It is also worth noticing that the argument trades on a strong, infallibilist conception of knowledge, since the knower of ¬Kp would have to be right about p in every possible world; a fallibilist, who allows that one can know something without its being true in every possible world, is not touched by the argument.

(c) Two families of response suggest themselves. The first restricts the knowability thesis so that it no longer applies to propositions whose being known is provably absurd; the witness p ∧ ¬Kp is excluded on the ground that no consistent knower could be in that position. This is unsatisfying, since the restriction looks tailor-made to exclude exactly the propositions that cause the trouble, and it is hard to motivate independently. The second family keeps the thesis unrestricted and revises the background logic. This seems to me the better option. The reductio assumes that the proposition p ∧ ¬Kp is either true or false; an intuitionist, who does not accept bivalence, need not grant that assumption and so need not run the reductio at all. On this view the paradox is an artefact of classical logic, and the knowability thesis is safe as long as one is prepared to give up classical reasoning about it. I therefore accept the second response."
