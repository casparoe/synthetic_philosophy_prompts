A colleague and I have been circling the same disagreement for months, and I would like to see it worked out as a dialogue rather than another round of us talking past each other. She is an empirical machine-learning researcher, someone who has spent years running experiments and watching how long it actually takes to discover whether an idea works; I work on optimization and self-reference, and my instincts are shaped by the thought that a system able to redesign its own cognition is playing a game whose returns resemble nothing in ordinary engineering. What we disagree about is not whether superhuman AI is possible — we both assume it is — but whether the transition from roughly human-level to vastly superhuman capability must be fast, on the order of days or weeks, or is more plausibly a grinding, multi-year, partially visible process in which each increment is won against resistance. I would like the dialogue to turn on a distinction I have been trying to get her to take seriously, and which I suspect cuts in her favour more than in mine: the difference between improvement that is insight-limited and improvement that is throughput-limited. If what an AI needs in order to get better is mainly better ideas, and ideas become cheap once it is sufficiently smart, then the loop closes on itself and growth is explosive. If instead what it needs is to consult the world — to run the experiment, wait, read off the result, run the next one, where every step costs wall-clock time that no amount of cleverness compresses below some floor — then even an arbitrarily intelligent system is climbing a staircase whose steepness is fixed by physics and logistics rather than by intelligence.

Give the two of them a concrete occasion for the argument, and let each be genuinely formidable. The theorist should have to answer the throughput objection instead of waving it away, and I want to see exactly where the escape route lies: is it that the system can simulate its own experiments and so skip the world entirely, or that it can redesign the apparatus — the instruments, the fabrication, the assay — and thereby shrink the floor itself, and if so, does that merely relocate the constraint one level down rather than removing it? The empiricist, for her part, should grant the strongest version of the self-improvement argument rather than denying that machines can do research, and should say precisely why the staircase still binds: which steps are irreducibly serial, which require contact with a world that does not answer faster on request, and what the observed shape of research productivity suggests about returns to cognitive investment. Let each of them concede something and revise a claim in the course of the exchange, and let the replies actually engage the previous point rather than alternate.

I do not want the dialogue to end in a handshake about the truth lying somewhere in the middle. Have it close by isolating the crux: the specific quantity or fact about the world whose value would settle the matter, and what each of them would predict we should observe if they are right — a shape of curve, a signature in the data, a pattern in how the field moves. No reading list and no citations; I want the argument itself, not a survey of who has said what.
