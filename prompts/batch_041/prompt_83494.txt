I have come to accept the unilateralist's curse as a genuine structural reason for restraint, and I want a candid account of what would count against it. Here is the position as I would state it; if my statement mischaracterizes it in a way that matters, say so and work with the corrected version instead.

Suppose a number of agents are each independently in a position to take some action, or to refrain from it. Each acts on her own fallible judgment about whether the action should be taken, and there is no coordination, no deference, and no way to learn what the others have decided before acting. Even if each agent is individually more likely than not to judge correctly, the probability that at least one agent acts on a mistaken judgment rises with the number of agents. So, other things equal, the more agents there are who might act in my place, the more reason I have for caution. Call that the structural claim. I also want to hold a stronger, responsibility-based claim: an agent who acts unilaterally in such a situation is open to a kind of criticism that does not reduce to the expected badness of her act. She is criticizable because her evidence could not distinguish her from the agents who would have acted wrongly, so her acting expresses a disregard for the possibility that others, equally placed, are mistaken.

What I want is a map of the position's exposure rather than a defense of it. Specifically:

- Which assumptions are actually doing the work, and what would the world have to be like for each to fail: the independence of the agents' judgments, their evidential symmetry, the absence of coordination or deference, the fixity of the action's value, the one-shot rather than repeated setting. For each, is the failure mode realistic or merely conceivable?
- Whether the inference from someone is likely to be wrong to I have more reason to refrain is sound. Each agent decides about her own act, while the rising probability attaches to the class of acts, not to hers. Is that a fallacy of division, or does the epistemic symmetry among the agents make the aggregate probability legitimately relevant to each of them? I am genuinely unsure which way this cuts.
- Whether the position is unstable. Restraint is itself a unilateral decision, so the reasoning seems to bear on refraining no less than on acting; and if every agent in the class reasons this way, no one acts. Does the argument silently require that inaction is the safe default? What happens when the action is required, when someone must do it and no mechanism selects who, or when delay has its own accumulating cost?
- Aggregation. If the agents' errors are independent, that is precisely the condition under which pooling their judgments would be informative. Why does the availability of even crude aggregation not dissolve the curse? And what becomes of the argument when errors are correlated, through shared evidence, shared models, or common bias, so that adding agents adds little risk?
- Cases where the value of acting depends on how many act (thresholds and network effects), where the action is reversible or partially corrigible, where the first actor creates an option for the others, or where the action is constitutive of a practice rather than merely instrumental to an outcome. Does the position get the right verdicts in these, or does it need to be restricted?
- Whether the responsibility-based claim is a distinct thesis or collapses into the structural one, whether it presupposes a control condition on blame that ordinary moral luck undermines, and whether it implies that the last agent standing, who knows all the others have refrained, occupies a different moral position from the first. If it does, is that acceptable or absurd?

Rank what you give me from fatal to merely awkward. Separate what a committed defender of the curse would concede as counting against it from what such a defender would simply dismiss, and say plainly if the position turns out to be so hedged that nothing could count against it, and whether that is a defect or a reasonable feature of a structural argument. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which single consideration, if it turned out the other way, would move you to a different verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
