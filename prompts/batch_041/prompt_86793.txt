I run the philosophy and ethics society at my high school. We meet once a week for eighty minutes, about sixteen students attend, mostly juniors and seniors, and nobody does any reading in advance — whatever happens has to be generated in the room. I have been asked to design and lead this term's session, and I want it to be about the authority of a person's earlier, competent self over the later self that replaces it, which is the authority an advance directive is meant to carry. I would like the group to run into the central difficulty themselves rather than hear me describe it, so please design an activity with rules that produce the problem, and have the vocabulary of directives, surrogates, and precedent introduced only in the debrief.

The difficulty I want them to collide with is this. A directive exists precisely in order to bind a later self who will want to do otherwise — that is its whole function. So when the later self objects and asks to be released, the objection cannot be offered as a reason against the directive without assuming the very point in dispute. And yet the later self's present preference is the only evidence anyone has about what that person is now like, and somebody has to decide on the basis of something. The other grounds available — that autonomy extends forward in time and may be exercised in advance; that a life is a whole whose shape its owner may settle ahead of time; that a person has interests in how their life goes overall, not only in how it feels from moment to moment — each purchase the authority at a price I want the group to feel rather than be told about.

Here is the shape I imagine, though please redesign it if you see something better. In the first phase, each participant receives an invented life card describing a specific person: their commitments, what they take to make their life worth living, and one thing they would refuse under any circumstances. Each then completes a directive form with three tiers — one instruction that must be carried out no matter what, one statement of the values that should guide whoever decides for them, and one matter deliberately left to that person's judgment. The forms are collected and returned by a draw, with the rule that nobody gets their own; each participant therefore becomes the later self of someone else, receiving a second card describing that person some years on, with changed circumstances and changed wants, and must argue to be released. The author of the directive is present and defends it. Before a release can even be considered, the later self must reconstruct, in the author's terms, the reasons behind the binding instruction, and the author alone judges whether the reconstruction is faithful. The room then divides into two chambers of eight; each chamber hears its four cases and must agree on a single rule, phrased as a completion of the sentence "A directive binds unless —", that decides all four cases, even if it decides some of them wrongly. The chambers then confront each other's rules, and the whole group must adopt one and apply it to a fresh case I supply at the end, in which a later self has adapted, reports being content, and asks to be released from a burdensome instruction. No real money: each pair stakes a token, the chamber's decision transfers the loser's token, and at the end the three students holding the most tokens choose the society's next three session topics and sit as the panel on the final case.

Please include the following. The eight life cards and their later-self counterparts, invented rather than recycled from well-known thought experiments, specific enough that different views about what makes a life go well will yield different verdicts on them. A materials list and a minute-by-minute timeline for the eighty minutes, with a note on what to cut if we run long. A debrief that maps particular moments in the activity onto the philosophical positions — at minimum the reconstruction test onto the claim that a directive's authority cannot rest on the later self's endorsement without becoming vacuous, the two chambers' rules onto the difference between a directive that instructs a surrogate and one that merely informs their judgment, and the contented later self onto the difference between an interest in endorsing one's life and an interest in experiencing it as satisfactory. I would also like a frank account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it: I expect most participants to hedge by writing directives that defer to their later selves, and I would rather let them do it and discuss it than forbid it; I expect the authors to argue that the instruction should stand simply because it is what they wanted, which is the question-begging move; I expect at least one participant to insist the later self is literally a different person, which the activity cannot test; I expect the chambers to deadlock over their rules; and I expect the most articulate later self to win, which will tilt the room toward present preference. Finally, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce. It stipulates that the same participant is the same person throughout, so the identity-based defence of the earlier self cannot be genuinely tried and the facilitator has to supply that metaphysical question. It gives the later self a voice, a memory, and a strategy, none of which a person in advanced dementia may have, so the activity will tilt toward the later self's autonomy and I should say so. And it manufactures conflict by handing out resentful later selves, whereas in life the later self is often content and the directive is invisible; the force of the earlier self's authority is hardest to feel exactly when nothing is at stake.
