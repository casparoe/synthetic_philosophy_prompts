You're a philosopher who works on both formal epistemology and the ethics of mental health care — think carefully before you answer, I'd rather have a slow, careful procedure than a tidy one.

I'm a consultant psychiatrist in an NHS trust and I've been asked to write the guidance our on-call doctors will use for what we've started calling 'binding requests': a patient, while well, asks us in writing to hold them to a decision they know they'll later want to reverse — don't let me refuse admission, keep me on the depot when I say stop, don't let me discharge myself. We get maybe a dozen a year. They're high stakes, and the call usually gets made by whoever's on at 3am, so it has to be something a tired junior doctor can actually run. Legal validity isn't my problem, the solicitors handle that; I need the ethical layer underneath it.

What's got me stuck is this. I've been reading about van Fraassen's reflection principle, which says roughly that your credence now ought to match what you expect your credence to be later, when the later you is better informed — its just a consistency constraint on belief. But the whole point of a binding request is to stop that happening: the patient wants the earlier belief to win. So when is it right to help someone bind rather than reflect, and when isn't it? I assume the principle only bites when the later judgement comes from a better-informed process rather than a distorted one — but then who decides which is which, and isn't that just the well self marking its own homework? And I notice the principle is about beliefs, not about what someone cares about, which I suspect is doing a lot of the work here.

Write me the procedure. In order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values and not facts. Mark clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on two cases — (a) someone with bipolar disorder who wrote, while well, that they want admitting against their will if they stop their lithium and go high, and is now saying no; (b) someone in the early stages of dementia who signed an advance statement refusing a treatment they are now asking for — and don't soften the answer if it's one I won't like. Start with a short brainstorm of the different things 'binding' could mean here and where the reflection idea runs out. British English please, and tell me at the end where the reasoning is weakest and what would change your mind about the approach.
