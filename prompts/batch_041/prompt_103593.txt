I teach a ninety-minute session for an upper-level undergraduate seminar — fourteen students, all of whom have had one term of symbolic logic — as the second meeting of a unit on formal systems and their limits. I want the session to be a hands-on activity rather than a lecture, and I want students to leave having felt, rather than merely been told about, a specific idea: a formal system can be specified completely and mechanically, and yet the most important fact about it — that some particular string cannot be derived in it — may be something you can only establish by stepping outside the system; and stepping outside is not an escape from systems altogether but a move into a further system that has limits of its own. Please design the activity in full: materials, roles, rules, a phase-by-phase timeline with minute budgets, and a debrief.

The system to use is the MIU system. The symbols are M, I, and U; the sole axiom is the string MI; and there are four rules, each applicable to any string you already have:

(a) if a string ends in I, you may append U;
(b) if a string begins with M, you may append to it everything that follows the M, so that MIU becomes MIUIU;
(c) you may replace any occurrence of III with U;
(d) you may delete any occurrence of UU.

The target string is MU. It cannot be reached, and the reason is that the number of I's in any derivable string is never a multiple of three — only rules (b) and (c) change that count, doubling it or reducing it by three, and neither move takes a non-multiple to a multiple — whereas MU contains no I. Put the axiom, the four rules, the target, and a blank ledger on the student handout and nothing else: no worked examples, no hints, no statement of what the session is really about. State the rules in your own words rather than as a quoted block.

Design requirements:

1. The students must hit the wall themselves. Phase one has the groups attempting the derivation under a rule that they may write down only strings licensed by exactly one application of one of the four rules, and that a string may not be used as a stepping stone unless it is in the ledger. Let them fail for a substantial stretch of time; the failure is the material the rest of the session works on.

2. The ledger is the hinge. Each group's ledger should have a column for the string, a column for the rule used, and a column for the number of I's. In the debrief the invariant must be read off their own accumulated data before it is stated as a theorem, so that it arrives as a pattern they produced rather than as a trick I announce.

3. Phase two asks each group to write a proof that MU is underivable, and then to classify its own proof: is it a derivation inside the system, or a claim about the system? Make the handout for this phase require them to say what kind of object their proof is and what it is a proof in.

4. Include an adversarial round: groups exchange proofs and try to break each other's, and I plant one deliberately bad argument whose conclusion is correct and whose premise is false. My suggestion is the claim that every derivable string contains an odd number of I's, which their own ledger refutes within the first minute or two, since MI can be doubled to MII in a single step; but pick whichever flawed argument you think is most instructive, and say in the debrief why a true conclusion does not rescue a false premise.

5. Include a level-raising round in which students are asked what system they were working in when they proved the invariant, and whether there might be a fact about that system which they cannot establish from inside it. They should generate the recursion themselves; the name of the phenomenon is not to be spoken until after this round. Only then should the debrief connect the exercise to the general point that every such jump lands you in a further system with its own blind spots, and that the arithmetic in which their invariant proof lives is itself a formal system.

6. For groups that finish early, add an extension in which they try to characterize the entire set of derivable strings rather than just ruling out one, and note in the debrief what is gained and what is risked by moving from a single negative result to a complete external characterization of a system.

7. The debrief must do at least these things: map specific moments in the activity onto the distinction between deriving a theorem and proving something about a system; explain that the invariant argument is an induction over applications of the rules, and therefore an ordinary mathematical proof carried out in a metatheory, not a piece of magic; and state plainly what the ledger cannot do, namely that no ledger of failed attempts, however long, is a proof of impossibility, so the group that first said "I bet it can't be done" was expressing a hunch and not yet a result.

8. Give me a section on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it. I expect to need help with at least: groups that fill the ledger with the same few strings over and over; a group that reports success on the strength of an illegal step, such as replacing a lone I with U or deleting a single U; groups that conflate "we have not found it" with "it cannot be found"; a group that stumbles on the invariant early and then has nothing to do; the prosecutor role being either ignored or used to wreck the group's morale; and the wide spread in how long groups take to give up.

9. Be honest at the end about what the activity cannot reproduce. I want a short, unsentimental note on the difference between this case and the cases that give the idea its real bite — in particular that the underivability of MU is itself provable, that nothing here is undecidable in the way an incompleteness result is, that the metatheory doing the work is uncontroversial arithmetic, and that the flash of insight which finds an invariant cannot be staged, only its aftermath can. Say which of these I have to supply myself, and how to do it without overclaiming on the exercise's behalf.
