I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in metaphysics. Sixteen students, one ninety-minute session, movable chairs, a whiteboard, index cards and a marker if I bring them, and no devices. I want a game they play that makes them feel the pull of the thought that some facts hold in virtue of other facts, and that this relation cannot run forever and cannot go in a circle — and then makes that confidence wobble. By the end they should have a felt sense of what is at stake in calling anything fundamental, not a definition of the word.

Assume the students have never encountered any of this, so the mechanics must carry the content rather than a lecture. The activity has to put the following ideas under their hands, in roughly this order, without naming any of them up front:

- Dependence as an explanatory, non-causal relation: one fact holding because another does, where the second is not merely a cause or a correlate of the first.
- Irreflexivity and asymmetry — nothing holds in virtue of itself, and if A holds in virtue of B, B does not hold in virtue of A — together with the fact that transitivity is a separate and contested matter, not a free gift of the other two.
- Full versus partial dependence: a fact that holds in virtue of a whole collection versus one that each member contributes to but none settles.
- Termination: whether every chain of dependence bottoms out in facts that hold in no further virtue of anything, and what it would mean if some chain did not — either descending forever, or closing into a mutual arrangement where each member supports the others and there is no floor at all.
- The status of the dependence facts themselves: if A's holding in virtue of B is itself a fact, does it too need something to hold in virtue of? What would a fact of that special kind be grounded in, and what follows if the answer is nothing?
- Fundamentality as a substantive status versus a place where we merely stopped asking — and whether it is absolute or relative to the kind of question being asked.

Deliverable: a runnable design, not a discussion plan. Give me the materials, the roles for all sixteen students, the rules, a win condition and a scoring scheme that does not use money (points, control of the board, early release, anything), the order of play, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes. The pressure must be structural: the students should be trying to win, and the metaphysics should happen to them as a by-product. At most three minutes of framing before the first rule is in play, and the name of the puzzle should not be spoken until the debrief. Build in at least one moment where the group has to choose between stopping and going on, and make the choice costly in points either way. Whatever rules you write must make at least one position in the game genuinely unwinnable, so that someone is stuck.

Then the debrief: map each stage of play onto a specific item from the list above, referring to moves the group is likely to have made rather than to the ideas in the abstract. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — at least two specific failure modes, each with the fix I should apply on the second run. And be frank about which item on the list the game cannot reproduce at all and that I will have to supply myself; if a short spoken paragraph at the end can do that work, write it out for me to read.

Finally, commit. One design, not a menu; where a choice is genuinely open, make it and defend it in a sentence. Take a definite position on this question and give your reasons: should the game be rigged so that the group ends up endorsing that there is a floor, or so that they end up accepting a structure with no floor — and which of those two is the better thing for them to leave with? I want your verdict, not the two sides. Then tell me which single moment in your design you would bet is the one that lands, and give me a rough number for how confident you are that it lands when a first-time facilitator runs the thing.
