Hey — I write a column and podcast about ideas for a general audience, and I'm doing a segment on Zhuangzi and death. The centerpiece is that scene everyone half-remembers: his wife dies, he's squatting on the ground drumming on a tub and singing, and Hui Shi turns up to tell him off for it. I want to end the segment with a poem, and I really don't want the greeting-card version where the wise man has no feelings and death turns out to be a big nothing.

Two things I need the poem to hold onto. First, the text has him say that when she first died he was definately cut up about it — the singing comes after he thinks the whole thing through, its not that he skipped the grief or was above it from the start. Second, he later weeps at Hui Shi's grave and says something like, now theres no one left to talk to. So the calm isn't the whole story, and the poem shouldn't pretend it is.

What I'd like: a sestina, with these six end-words, one per line-end, rotating the usual way — breath, form, change, season, chamber, wail. I chose those because that's basically the sequence he gives: she hadn't been alive, then there was breath, then a body, then a birth, then a death, and its just the next turn of the year. The form should do the arguing for me. The words keep coming back in new positions, nothing gets dropped, nothing gets added, and the envoi should land like the last turn of a cycle rather than a summary or a moral. Start the poem at the moment Hui Shi walks in, and let the speaker be caught between the wailing he's supposed to be doing and the singing he's actually doing — I want the poem to earn the singing, not open with it.

One more thing I'd like in there, not as a quote, just as a presence in the background: the skull he talks to out on the road, the one who says the dead have it better than kings do and turns down the offer to come back. That skull complicates the seasonal picture, since he isn't just turning with the seasons, he's refusing. If the poem can put that pressure on the ending without resolving it, thats the poem I want.

Don't use the word acceptance anywhere, thats the wrong word and it makes me nuts. Don't put any Chinese in it or tack a translation onto the end — it has to work as an English poem for people who've never read a line of the Zhuangzi. A strict sestina is 39 lines, so go with that if it works, but if the form is fighting the material, tell me what you'd do instead and why.
