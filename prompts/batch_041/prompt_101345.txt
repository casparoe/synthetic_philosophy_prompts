I've been defending a thoroughgoing accuracy-first epistemology: the only fundamental epistemic good is accuracy — graded closeness of one's credences to the truth — and the only fundamental epistemic norm is to maximize expected accuracy. I hold it in a strong form, namely that there is no distinctively epistemic reason that is not at bottom an accuracy reason. On this view probabilism, conditionalization, and the norms for disagreement and higher-order evidence are all consequences of the framework rather than independent principles, and once expected accuracy is fixed there is no residual question about what it is epistemically rational to believe or about what to do with one's evidence.

Tell me what would count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and say of each whether a committed defender would concede it or deflect it. I want to know in particular: whether the expected-accuracy arguments for conditionalization and the other diachronic norms are hostage to the choice of scoring rule, and what becomes of the view if they are; whether the framework can say anything credible about an agent who has reason to distrust her own reasoning; whether the extension from credences to outright belief and suspension is principled or an afterthought; and whether the complaints that accuracy is not all there is to epistemic value — understanding, explanation, knowledge, or the value of evidence that cannot change one's mind — do any real work or merely gesture at rival goods. Finally, tell me whether the position has been insulated by qualification to the point where nothing could count against it, and whether that is a defect in a view of this kind.
