I teach a graduate seminar on ethics and machine learning, and a colleague in the law school has handed me an argument that I can neither accept nor quite refute. He writes it out as follows.

(1) For a decision procedure to be fair to two groups, it must be calibrated within each group — among people assigned the same risk score, the observed outcome rate matches the score in both groups — and it must treat the groups equally with respect to errors, i.e. the same true-positive rate and the same false-positive rate in both.
(2) The group-fairness impossibility results establish that no nontrivial risk score can satisfy calibration together with both error-rate equalities whenever the two groups have different base rates.
(3) In the domains we care about — pretrial detention, lending, hiring, medical triage — base rates do differ between the groups at issue.
(4) Therefore no nontrivial decision procedure can be fair in any of these domains.
(5) Nothing in the results depends on the procedure being algorithmic. Every premise speaks only of a score, an outcome, and a threshold, all of which a human judge or loan officer supplies as readily as a model does.
(6) Hence no nontrivial human decision procedure can be fair in these domains either.
(7) Fairness, in these settings, is a mirage, and we should stop treating it as a constraint on either our models or our institutions.

I find (1) through (3) individually hard to resist and (7) intolerable, and I suspect (5) is not the weak link — the generalization to human deciders strikes me as exactly right, which is what makes the argument bite. Tell me which step to reject. For the step you choose, explain why it looked compelling in the first place, and then price the alternatives: if I keep the mathematics and give up part of (1), which part, and what do I owe someone who says I have merely redefined fairness as whatever happens to be achievable? If instead the fault lies in the slide to (4) or (6), say precisely what the result is a claim about and what it is not.

Two things I want kept straight along the way. First, distinguish the version that demands error-rate equality at every threshold from the version that demands it only at the operating point actually in use; the argument leans on the strong reading without flagging that it has. Second, (3) is a claim about the world rather than about any procedure, so if (4) follows, it may be a social fact rather than a theorem that is doing the work — tell me whether that shifts anything for (7).

Close by saying what would move you to a different verdict: which further argument, empirical finding, or reformulation of the fairness criteria would make you revise your diagnosis, and where in your own reasoning you would expect it to give way first.
