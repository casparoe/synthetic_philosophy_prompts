You are a philosopher with graduate-level expertise in value theory and in the epistemology of attitudes, and I would like your help building a diagnostic instrument. Please think the structure through carefully before you write a word of the answer — work out the logical space of positions first, privately — and give me only the finished product.

Here is why I am asking. Eight weeks ago my younger brother was diagnosed with a cancer whose five-year survival odds are a little better than even. Within days people began handing him reasons of a particular shape: that he had to believe he would recover, because believing is half the battle; that he should forgive our father, because carrying the resentment was driving up his cortisol; that he ought to feel grateful for the care he is getting, because gratitude is good for sleep. He is a literal-minded person, and he kept pressing me on whether those were reasons to believe, forgive and be grateful, or only reasons to arrange to have those attitudes, and whether the difference was real or a philosopher's invention. I gave him an answer, and it was a bluff. A friend who teaches ethics told me afterwards that I had stumbled into the problem of the wrong kind of reason — the problem you get when you analyse value as the fittingness of pro-attitudes and then notice that a demon's reward for admiring him is a reason to admire without making him admirable — and asked me which position I actually hold. I could not say, and worse, I could not reliably tell several of the positions apart. So what I want is an instrument that sorts people by what they say about cases rather than by which label they like.

What I need is a taxonomy of the positions on whether consequence-given considerations are genuine reasons for attitudes (admiration, gratitude, forgiveness and belief are the cases I care about; use whichever is sharpest for each fork), laid out as a decision tree whose branch points are questions that someone who has never read any of this could answer. I will be trying it out on a reading group of mixed background — two graduate students in philosophy and two clinicians who have read none of it — so no question may presuppose the jargon.

Requirements. Exactly five branch questions, ordered so that each one makes sense only against the answers to the ones before it, and each phrased as a concrete case or a plain claim rather than as, say, a question about accepting the object-given versus state-given distinction. At least one fork must turn on an attitude other than belief, and at least one on a consequence-given consideration that the person themselves welcomes rather than resents. The leaves must include the position that the fitting-attitude analysis should simply be restricted to object-given reasons (and should say what that restriction costs), the position that the consequence-given consideration is a reason for a different attitude rather than for the attitude in question, the position that it is a reason for the attitude and the value verdict must simply be swallowed, the position that the phrase wrong kind of reason names whatever the analysis cannot accommodate and therefore leaves the analysis circular, and the position that abandons the analysis of value in terms of fitting attitudes altogether. If a fork could be answered by reciting the standard moves in the literature, replace it with one that turns on a case. Where a pair of leaves cannot be separated by any of your five questions, say so rather than manufacturing a distinction. Where your five questions permit a combination of answers that nobody occupies, mark it and say whether you think it is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has bothered to explore it.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else — no introduction, no closing summary, no citations, and no names of authors or papers; say the position that so-and-so rather than naming anyone. Each item is one branch question: state it in a sentence, then in a sentence or two say what each answer commits the answerer to and which position it puts them in.
