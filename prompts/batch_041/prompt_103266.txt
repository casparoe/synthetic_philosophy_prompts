For the past several weeks I've been sitting in on a graduate seminar on collective decision-making for a piece I'm writing about how groups — juries, hiring committees, editorial boards — manage to be rational when no single member is in charge, and a puzzle one of the participants put to me has been rattling around my head ever since. Picture a three-member hiring committee that must decide whether to extend an offer. Rather than vote on the offer directly, the members are asked first to settle two sub-questions — whether the candidate is qualified, and whether the candidate fits the team — on the understanding that an offer is warranted just in case the candidate is both qualified and a good fit. The first member judges the candidate qualified and a good fit, and would hire. The second judges the candidate qualified but a poor fit, and would not hire. The third judges the candidate unqualified but a good fit, and would not hire. Each member's position is internally coherent. Yet when the committee tallies the sub-questions separately, a majority finds the candidate qualified and a majority finds the candidate a good fit, while a majority also votes against hiring — even though hiring is exactly the conjunction those first two majorities entail. The committee, in short, has contradicted itself, and it has done so while every one of its members reasoned impeccably.

A colleague who has been arguing with me about this insists the lesson is far more radical than a quirk of committee procedure, and he has laid it out as a chain I can't quite break. (1) A collective decision is legitimate only if it is fixed by the members' individual judgments and treats every member's judgment equally; there is no group mind that could judge on its own. (2) Legitimacy also requires that the group's verdict on any given question depend only on the members' verdicts on that question — the group's judgment about qualification should track the members' judgments about qualification, its judgment about fit should track their judgments about fit, and so on, rather than being smuggled in from some other quarter. (3) Any procedure meeting (1) and (2) is some form of issue-by-issue aggregation, and the case above shows that such a procedure can yield an inconsistent set of verdicts; indeed the general impossibility results show that no procedure can satisfy (1), (2), and consistency all at once. (4) An inconsistent set of verdicts cannot all be true, so it is not a judgment at all — the group has not actually decided anything; it has merely produced a contradiction. (5) Since (1) and (2) are just what we mean by legitimate collective judgment, and no procedure satisfies both together with consistency, it follows that a group facing interconnected questions — which is to say, any group worth convening — cannot form a legitimate judgment at all. Collective rationality, on this view, is a polite fiction, and every committee that believes it has reasoned its way to a decision is either incoherent or quietly authoritarian.

I'd like you to tell me where this argument goes wrong, if it does — which numbered step to reject, why it looked so plausible in the first place, and what rejecting it actually costs. I have a stake in the answer: I want to keep (1), and I'm willing to surrender either (2) or (4), but I suspect those two concessions are not the same price, and I'd like to know what each of them buys and what it forfeits. A second, related question, since a participant in the seminar floated it and I couldn't tell whether it was a genuine fix or a dodge: if the committee were to vote not on the three questions separately but on whole packages — accepting or rejecting the candidate together with a consistent set of reasons — does that truly escape the paradox, or does it merely relocate it? Please don't rest on anyone's famous theorem as a slogan; the seminar is full of people who can recite the results and none of whom can tell me what they cost. I want to see the machinery.
