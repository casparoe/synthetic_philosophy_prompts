I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, and next month I'm giving a paper to a joint departmental seminar that will be half analytic metaphysicians of causation — a couple of committed Humeans about laws, two or three dispositionalists who hold that causal powers are real and irreducible, the rest agnostic — and half Islamic philosophy specialists who read Arabic and know the Ashʿarite kalām literature firsthand. My thesis is that al-Ghazālī's denial of a necessary connection between cause and effect, in his discussion of causality in the Tahāfut, is not a proto-Humean regularity theory but a rival to it, and a stronger one: he denies necessity in the world without denying causal production, which he relocates in an agent's will, whereas Hume pays for his parsimony by having no place left to put the production. I also argue that the standard textbook line that al-Ghazālī anticipated Hume has the comparison backwards in the respects that matter. The Islamicists will additionally have heard me say that calling his position "occasionalism" is a label of European provenance retrofitted onto a kalām dispute whose real stakes were divine freedom and the denial of created secondary causality, and that the argument is aimed at the philosophers' necessary emanation rather than at induction.
