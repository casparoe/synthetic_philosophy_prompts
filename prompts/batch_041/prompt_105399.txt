I am a retired experimental geophysicist — thirty-odd years of instrument work and data reduction — and in the last few years of my retirement I have begun reading the philosophy of science seriously for the first time. I have become preoccupied with Otto Neurath's proposal that science might be carried on, and above all taught, in a pictorial language rather than a verbal one: a disciplined system of signs governed by explicit compositional rules, learnable by people who share no common tongue, and intended for the working public as much as for specialists.

As I understand him — and I may well be bending him — the pictorial language was never merely a scheme for making statistics legible. It was bound up with his broader commitments: that scientific statements answer only to other scientific statements and never to some pre-linguistic given; that our concepts are not sharp-edged but travel in loose clusters whose boundaries shift with use; that knowledge is a ship we rebuild plank by plank while still at sea, not a building with foundations; and that the fitting form of the whole is an encyclopedia of overlapping entries rather than a single deductive system. I would like you to set out the most important implications of this cluster of views for a contemporary ambition of my own: to construct one standardized visual language — a fixed vocabulary of pictograms plus strict rules of combination — in which scientific findings and their uncertainties could be stated so that any reader, whatever his or her native language or training, would recover the same content. I am chiefly interested in the implications that a casual admirer of infographics would not foresee: the places where Neurath's own commitments cut against the dream of a neutral, transparent visual language, and the places where they underwrite it. Please separate the implications that genuinely follow from the views as I have stated them from those that are only attractive extensions I might be smuggling in, and rank the former by how much they matter.

At the end, tell me how confident you are in the central claims you have made, and single out the one point you are least sure of — I would prefer it to be a point where someone who knows Neurath's writings better than I do might think you have quietly fitted him to a modern agenda. Kindly write in American English.
