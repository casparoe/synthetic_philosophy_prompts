I recently took part in a seminar where a senior colleague of mine — a philosopher who has spent years thinking about testimony and the ethics of communication, and who is quick to pronounce on whether someone's assertion was "in order" — assessed a series of cases we discussed, and I found myself on the receiving end of one of her verdicts, which has made me want to reverse-engineer her implicit standard. Her verdicts, as best I recorded them, were these. (1) A colleague checks a clock she knows to be working and tells me the time: she said the assertion was fully in order. (2) Someone forms a justified true belief by ordinary means but, through an unusual stroke of luck, fails to know — the classic Gettier shape — and asserts it anyway: she said the assertion was still in order. (3) Someone asserts something he knows to be false, intending to mislead: she said this was out of order. (4) Someone asserts a proposition he has no evidence for at all and does not even believe, and it happens to be false: out of order. (5) Someone asserts a proposition that is false and that he believes only out of wishful thinking, on no evidential basis whatever: out of order. I pressed her once about case (2) and she insisted the lack of knowledge made no difference so long as the person was doing everything epistemically right.

Next week's case is one I care about personally, because it is close to something that happened to me. A researcher asserts a proposition that is in fact false, but she holds it on the basis of overwhelming, undefeated evidence — her belief is thoroughly reasonable and she is entirely sincere and confident; it simply turns out that the evidence was misleading. Predict my colleague's verdict, tell me which single standard she appears to be running and which other standards fit her track record just as well, and identify the further case that would tell those standards apart if her verdict on next week's case turns out as I expect.
