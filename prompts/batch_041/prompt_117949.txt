I teach a 300-level seminar on autonomy and the ethics of influence — eighteen students, mostly philosophy majors with a few from psychology and law, one hundred minutes in a room with movable chairs. I am trying to stop lecturing on the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, because when I do, they nod along at every criterion I offer and then, minutes later, call the same case obviously manipulation and obviously not. I want an activity instead: runnable, with roles, moves and a scoring scheme that needs no money, in which they have to commit to verdicts before they know how the verdicts will collide, and in which the collision comes out of the mechanics rather than out of me. They should come away having felt one specific thing — that they can apply any single clean criterion of manipulation perfectly consistently and land on answers they themselves will not accept, so that the disagreement among theorists starts to look like the residue of three or four different questions sharing one word rather than like sloppiness. I do not want any of the criteria named to them before they have been burned by one.

Here is the material I want them to run into, in my own framing, since I do not want the design to lean on anyone's published taxonomy. Three candidate tests we keep circling: the Route Test, on which influence is manipulative when it works by bypassing the target's capacities for weighing reasons rather than by supplying her with something she can weigh; the Daylight Test, on which the mark is concealment, of the means or of the intent, so that transparent influence is legitimate whatever it does; and the Motive Test, on which neither the mechanism nor the visibility matters, only what the influence is for and whether it respects the target's standing to decide for herself. And a fourth that students always reach for, whose cost I would rather they discover than be told — that influence counts as manipulation when the target, fully informed of how it worked, would repudiate it. Build the hard cases in: a covert intervention that plainly serves the target's own stated ends; a wholly transparent intervention that works on mood rather than on reasons; a true statement disclosed at a moment chosen to produce despair rather than belief-revision; a move that succeeds only because the target trusts the influencer and would fail the moment she suspected it was a move. I also want the group to stumble into the belief/desire asymmetry — moves that change what someone believes can present themselves as reasons, whereas moves that change what she wants, or how she feels, have no obvious analogue, so if that asymmetry is the criterion then a great deal of ordinary moral upbringing, and much of friendship, is manipulation, which is either a reductio or the truth.

Constraints on the design. The concept must be enacted, never announced: whatever they are asked to do should be intelligible to them as a game with a goal, and the criterion they are applying should arrive as a rubric on a card, not as a thesis. Each participant must render a verdict under one assigned rubric before any comparison across rubrics happens, and the assignment must be such that players cannot see one another's rubrics during play. No real money; tokens or credibility chips only. It has to work with eighteen people in a hundred minutes with no preparation beyond what I can print, it has to survive one group going off-script or one player refusing to play, and it must not reward anyone for guessing which test I favour, because in the debrief I will not be endorsing any of the four.

Give me the design in the following sections and order, with roughly these lengths. Setup and materials (about 200 words): room arrangement, group size, timing, what I print and hand out, and what I may and may not say before we begin. Rules of play (about 500 words): roles, the moves available, turn structure, how a verdict is rendered, the token economy, and how a round terminates. Payoffs and scoring (about 250 words): what winning amounts to under each rubric, and why the rubrics have to be distributed as they are for the collision to be honest. Timeline for the hundred minutes (about 200 words). Debrief (about 500 words): the mapping from particular moments in the run onto the Route, Daylight, Motive and Endorsement tests, including the moment at which each test delivers a verdict the room will reject, and the questions that open the belief/desire asymmetry without settling it. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (about 250 words), with the fix for each failure. What the activity cannot reproduce (about 150 words): be frank about the feature of the problem I will still have to supply myself from the front of the room.
