I'm putting together a session for my upper-level philosophy of physics seminar — fourteen students, most of them comfortable with the basic formalism of quantum mechanics but whose only prior exposure to measurement is the textbook collapse postulate — and I want a single 90-minute activity that makes the Born rule problem for the many-worlds interpretation something they feel in their hands rather than something they hear described. The arc I'm after is this: first the students should accept without fuss that a branching process is fully specified by a deterministic rule for how one situation splits into others, with every branch continuing to exist; then they should be made to act — to place a bet, stake a payoff, choose between wagers — in a situation governed only by such a rule; and then they should discover that nothing in the rule, and nothing in the bare fact that all branches survive, tells them how much any branch is worth to them, even though they were forced to decide. The last third should convert that discomfort into the actual shape of the problem: quantum mechanics predicts definite frequencies (a spin prepared one way comes up 'up' about nine times in ten), whereas a bare branching picture with no measure over branches predicts nothing of the sort, and supplying the measure — weighting branches by the squared modulus rather than the modulus, or counting branches, or imposing some decision-theoretic constraint on rational betting, or treating the agent as merely uncertain which branch she is in the way one is uncertain which of several indistinguishable rooms one has woken up in — is where all the work and all the controversy lies. The activity should presuppose no named derivation; I want them to have wanted the missing piece before I tell them what philosophers have tried to do about it.

Some seeds for the mechanics, which you should redesign if you can sharpen the pressure. I picture a branching 'rulebook' — a card or small booklet stating, for each round, that the current situation splits into two branches bearing certain marks (say one branch marked 9 and the other marked 3, the marks presented as bare numbers carrying no stated interpretation) — plus a physical device (a coin, a die, a deck; whatever keeps it honest) that realizes the split, with the crucial constraint that both branches are always kept and no participant is ever eliminated, so that each round every student's set of worlds doubles. Before each split a student must commit to one of two wagers that pay off differently in the two branches, and the points accrue to the worlds rather than to the student, so that at the end the question of what a student's score even is collapses into the question of how worlds are to be weighed. I want the arithmetic of the trap to be visible on the board: someone who ignores the marks and counts branches will price a 9-and-3 split at even money, someone who weights branches linearly by the marks will price it at three-to-one, and someone who weights by the marks squared will price it at nine-to-one — and I want the activity arranged so that all three answers surface as live options, with defenders, before anyone says which one quantum mechanics actually requires or why.

Give me the full runnable package: the setup and any roles, the materials and how to prepare them, the round-by-round rules and the payoff scheme (no real money — points and a trivial prize are fine, and I'd rather the scoring be transparent than clever), a realistic timeline for the 90 minutes including how long to let each phase breathe, and the debrief, which should map specific moments in the game onto specific parts of the problem — the moment branch-counting feels natural and why it fails against real quantum statistics, the moment linear weighting feels natural and why it is not what the theory delivers, the pull of the thought that an agent who cares about all of her worlds should be indifferent to the marks, and why that cannot be right either. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: where students will muddle whether they are tracking worlds or themselves, which ones will try to escape the pressure by declaring the whole setup meaningless, and how I should respond without tipping the answer. And be honest at the end about what the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect it can stage the demand for a measure but not the physical fact that fixes it — and say plainly where the game is stipulative and which piece of the actual argument the facilitator has to supply from the front of the room.
