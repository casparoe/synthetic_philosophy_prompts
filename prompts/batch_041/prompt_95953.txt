I'm a third-year PhD student in social psych — my undergrad was in philosophy, so I have just enough of that background to get myself into trouble — and I just sat through a two-day workshop on the replication crisis. I left with a headache and a nagging feeling that the whole way our field handles evidence is alot weirder, philosophically, than anyone in the room was willing to admit.

Here's the practice I want you to poke at. In our lab, a study gets preregistered with a directional hypothesis and a power analysis for the smallest effect we actually care about. When it comes back, we report the p-value, the effect size, and a 95% confidence interval. If p < .05 we call it a finding, it goes in the paper, and it gets cited. If it doesn't, we call it a null, it goes in the file drawer, and — this is the part that really bugs me — when we later run a meta-analysis or just tally how many findings have replicated, a study that came back at p = .09 in the right direction gets logged as a failed replication, while a fresh study that came back at p = .04 gets logged as a confirmation, even though the two effect sizes are basically the same. Then when someone presses us on any of this at a conference, we all nod along to the standard line: a p-value doesn't tell you the probability that your hypothesis is true, non-significance isn't the same as no effect, and we should really be thinking in terms of effect sizes and uncertainty instead.

So what does this practice actually presuppose? What theory of evidence, and what picture of what a scientific claim is, does the workflow only make sense under? Is it the error-rate view, where a claim is licensed by a procedure that controls long-run false positives, or the evidential view, where a p-value is supposed to be a graded measure of what the data say, or is the practice quietly running both at once and hoping nobody notices the seam? Which rival views does it rule out — and in particular, does treating a non-significant result as a failed replication commit us to something stronger than the official line about absence of evidence? I'd like you to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits nicely with, since I suspect a lot of this is underdetermined and that we're smuggling in extra commitments. Then tell me what would have to change if we swapped the presupposed picture for a Bayesian one — not the toy version, but one where the prior is doing real work.

Please give me a verdict rather than a survey, so actually commit to a reconstruction and defend it. And be explicit about where your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong, and what you'd have to see to change your mind — like, what piece of the practice, if I described it differently, would flip your answer.
