I would like to know whether the following configuration has an established name, and — more than the name — how it should be carved: what the phenomenon actually is, which neighbouring ideas it gets confused with, and where its edges fall.

I have been trying to take seriously the suffering of wild animals, and I keep meeting a feature of my own moral perception for which I have no term. The quantities are certain and enormous. Almost every egg in a clutch, almost every tadpole in a pond, almost every nestling in a tree is gone before maturity, and most are not killed quickly. I believe this is bad, and I believe it with the same seriousness with which I believe that a human being dying of a disease is bad. But the two beliefs behave nothing alike. A stranger dying of a stroke commands my attention. A hundred thousand tadpoles drying in a pond that dries up does not. Nor is the difference simply the species line: one animal trapped and drowning in a flood moves me more than the whole pond.

What I notice in myself is that my attention appears to run on a question I would have said was irrelevant: who did this, and is this the way things are meant to go? Where there is an author to be indignant at, the suffering registers as an event. Where the suffering is the ordinary output of a system doing what systems do — predation, parasitism, starvation, the arithmetic of a species that throws thousands of offspring at the world so that a handful survive — it registers instead as background, as the way things are, and the absence of anyone answerable does not present itself to me as a feature of the suffering at all.

A single deer lets me separate the variables, and the separation is what I cannot finish. When a hunter kills the deer, there is an author, and a moral agent, and the death is abnormal. When a wolf kills the deer, there is an author but no moral agent, and the death is normal. When the deer starves in an ordinary winter, there is no author at all and the death is normal. My responses to these three differ, and I cannot tell which variable is doing the work — authorship, moral agency, or normality. Nor is it plainly authorship, since an earthquake killing a person grips me, and no one is answerable for an earthquake.

So: is there an established concept for this? I know the theological category of natural evil and the psychological work on how large numbers numb rather than move, but neither is quite it. The first is organised around a possible author and the question of his responsibility; the second does not care whether anyone did anything. What I want is not a label to file this under but a carving. Is the important line between harm that has an author and harm that does not, or between harm that is the normal operation of a system and harm that interrupts one? Are these one distinction in two costumes, or two that come apart, and which of them, if either, does the explanatory work? Is this one phenomenon, or several that I have fused by choosing examples where they coincide?

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Which case, or which pattern of cases, would show that I have misidentified the mechanism, and where is your own reasoning most likely to fail — in the psychology, in the metaphysics of harm, or in the assumption that there is a single phenomenon here to name?
