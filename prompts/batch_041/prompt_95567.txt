ok so a friend from my decision theory reading group has this argument and it feels like it proves way too much, but every time I try to poke a hole he patches it. Here it is cleaned up:

(1) A rational agent's credence in P is just her prior for P conditionalized on her total evidence.
(2) Aumann's agreement theorem: if two agents share a common prior and their posterior credences in P are common knowledge between them, then those posteriors are equal.
(3) If you and I each trust the other to be a rational updater and we've each said our number out loud ("I'm at 70"), then our posteriors are common knowledge -- I know yours, you know mine, I know that you know mine, and so on up.
(4) Any two rational agents share a common prior, because a prior is what you have before any evidence arrives; there's nothing to conditionalize on that could justify one prior over another, so a difference in priors is arbitrary, and arbitrary starting points aren't rational.
(5) So any two rational agents who have each stated their credence must already agree.
(6) And it's stable: agreeing doesn't change anything, so any disagreement that survives the exchange means at least one of us isn't rational (or isn't being straight with the other, or never really shared a prior).

(7) But this is nuts. People who are clearly rational and clearly understand each other disagree about tax policy, about whether a defendant is guilty, about how to read quantum mechanics, and they don't converge just because they've both said "I'm at 70" and "I'm at 30." So one of (1)-(6) has to go.

Wich one, and why did it look so good in the first place? I obviously want to keep (7), and I'd also like to keep the idea that it can be perfectly rational for me to hold a prior you don't share. Tell me what giving up whichever premise I should drop actually costs, and whether Aumann's theorem really has the teeth my friend thinks it does. Also, is there a reading where the argument is basically fine and only the conclusion sounds worse than it is?
