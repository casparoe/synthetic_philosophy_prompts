I have spent much of the past year trying to read a box of notebooks that belonged to my late grandmother. They are written in a private shorthand that no one in the family can decipher, and the attempt has pushed me toward a view about meaning that I now hold fairly firmly — but that I want tested rather than confirmed.

The view, which I first encountered in Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and have since restated in my own terms, concerns where meaning is located. It says that the meaning of a message is not a property sitting inside the message but a relation among three things: the message's structure, the world that structure corresponds to, and a decoder able to recognize the correspondence. On this account every message can be sorted into three layers. There is the frame message — the signal that something here is a message at all, in Hofstadter's phrase "I am a message; decode me if you can!" There is the outer message — the information, carried implicitly by the pattern and its surroundings, that tells a decoder how to proceed. And there is the inner message — the content that comes into view once the outer message has done its work. The claim I actually care about is the strong one: that the inner message has no decoder-independent existence, that there is no fact of the matter about what a message means apart from some possible decoder and decoding scheme, and that the inner message is not cargo the artifact carries but an achievement of a competent reader.

What would count against this? I want the observations, arguments, intuitions, and results that should move someone who holds it, ranked from what would be fatal to what would be merely awkward, and I want to know which of them a committed defender would concede as a genuine cost and which a defender would wave away as misdirected. Some cases seem to cut deeper than others. An undeciphered script that no living person can read: must the view say it has no content, or can it say the content is there for any possible decoder? A pattern produced by accident — erosion that happens to spell a sentence, a random text that coincides with a real one — does the view count it as a message, and if not, on what principled basis does it exclude it? Natural signs that carry information with no sender at all — tree rings, footprints, a rash, the genetic code — are they messages, and if they are not, has the view quietly narrowed itself into a special case rather than a theory of meaning? And my grandmother's notebooks, which I would swear mean something even though no one can read them.

I also want the structural pressures. Does the view survive the regress it invites, every frame message needing its own frame and every outer message its own outer, so that understanding never bottoms out? Can it distinguish a correct decoding from a wrong one, or does it license me to read whatever I like into the notebooks? Does it collapse into a general theory of context and relevance, so that the three layers are just pragmatics under another name? Does it sit uneasily with the thought that the inner message is the meaning the sender intended — since if intention fixes the content, the content is not merely decoder-relative, and if it is merely decoder-relative, the sender's intention seems to lose its authority? And what does the view predict that its rivals do not: the view that content is intrinsic to the message, the view that it is fixed by the sender's intention, the view that it is fixed by use? Finally, tell me honestly whether the position has been hedged into something nothing could count against, and if so, whether that is a defect or simply the shape a view of this kind has to take.
