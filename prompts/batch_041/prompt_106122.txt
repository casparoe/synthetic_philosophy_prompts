I've read the standard ravens-paradox explainers like a dozen times and they all stop exactly where it gets interesting. Fine: the hypothesis that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the claim that all non-black things are non-ravens, and by the equivalence condition a green apple is supposed to confirm the ravens hypothesis, which seems nuts. I get that part. What I actually want is the conceptual crux everyone skips.

Here's my guess, tell me if it's right. A green apple only confirms the hypothesis if its less likely when the hypothesis is false than when it's true -- confirmation is a likelihood-ratio thing -- and that ratio depends entirely on how you went looking for evidence. So the paradox isn't really about the logical relation between evidence and hypothesis, it's about the sampling procedure that the logical formulation quietly assumes and never states. If that's right, cash it out:

Take three ways of gathering evidence: (i) you inspect ravens and check their color; (ii) you inspect non-black things and check whether they're ravens; (iii) you sample arbitrary objects and record both properties. In which of these does finding a green apple or a white shoe raise the probability of the ravens hypothesis, by roughly how much, and why do the three come apart? I want the sampling model to be explicit in each case.

Then the bit that really bugs me. If confirmation just means the probability went up, and logically equivalent hypotheses always have identical probabilities, then the equivalence condition is trivially satisfied and does no work at all -- so the paradox can't even be stated in that framework. Which would mean the ravens paradox is only a paradox for someone who isn't a Bayesian, i.e. someone who holds a positive-instance or relevance notion of confirmation. Is that right? If so, say precisely which notion of confirmation you have to hold for there to be anything to resolve, and what the resolution actually has to give up.

No citations or reading list, just reason it out. I want the answer to make clear that the interesting question is how you looked, not what you found.
