I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy at work on a paper about the semantics of evaluative language, and I would like you to draft an essay that adjudicates a controversy I have been circling for some time. The controversy concerns whether expressivism about moral discourse — the view that a sentence such as "lying is wrong" does not describe a fact but expresses a non-cognitive attitude of approval or disapproval — can be made consistent with the way moral sentences behave when they are not asserted. The problem is often called the Frege–Geach problem.

The difficulty, in outline: moral sentences embed. They appear under negation ("lying is not wrong"), in the antecedents of conditionals ("if lying is wrong, then getting your sibling to lie is wrong"), in disjunctions, and in the scope of attitude verbs ("she believes that lying is wrong"). In such positions the sentence does not appear to express the speaker's attitude at all; it seems instead to contribute a truth-evaluable content, and that is what makes the surrounding arguments valid. A moral instance of modus ponens looks valid in exactly the way a non-moral instance does. Yet if the meaning of a moral sentence is the attitude it expresses, then an unasserted occurrence — which expresses no attitude — must mean something different from the asserted occurrence, and the argument becomes an equivocation; if the meanings are the same, the sentence appears to have a truth-conditional content after all, and expressivism looks superfluous. The difficulty has a second face: if "lying is wrong" expresses disapproval, what does "lying is not wrong" express? Not the mere absence of disapproval, since denying that lying is wrong is itself a substantive evaluative commitment, and the attitude it expresses must be distinguishable from the attitude expressed by asserting some unrelated moral claim.

I want the essay to assess a specific repair and then come down on one side. The repair — call it the inferential-role strategy — has three commitments. First, the meaning of a moral predicate is exhausted by its inferential role: to grasp "wrong" is to be disposed to make certain transitions in practical reasoning, from the acceptance of a moral claim together with a description of an action to the formation of an attitude toward that action, and to reject certain other transitions. Second, the logical constants are likewise characterized by their inferential roles rather than by their contributions to truth conditions, so that validity is a matter of licensed transitions among sentences rather than the preservation of truth. Third, an embedded moral sentence is therefore neither a disguised attitude report nor a truth-apt content awaiting expressivist reconstruction; it is a licensing node whose inferential role constrains which transitions the containing sentence licenses, and the attitude expressed by the asserted sentence is the endorsement of those transitions.

The essay should defend a verdict on whether this strategy dissolves the embedding problem, merely relocates it, or succeeds only within a restricted range of cases. It should test the strategy against at least the following: the validity of moral instances of modus ponens and the invalidity of nearby arguments that ought not to come out valid; the negation case described above, including what distinguishes the attitude expressed by "not wrong" from a mere withholding of disapproval; embeddings in conditionals whose antecedents are never asserted; ascriptions of moral judgments to other people; and the requirement that the moral predicate make the same contribution in every occurrence, asserted or unasserted. Where the strategy fails, the essay should say precisely what fails and why, and whether the failure is fatal to expressivism as such or only to this version of it. Please end with a definite verdict and defend it against the strongest objection you can formulate; do not conclude that the matter is undecidable or that both sides have a point. No citations or bibliography are needed — argue from the concepts themselves.
