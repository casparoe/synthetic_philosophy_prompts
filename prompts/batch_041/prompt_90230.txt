I am a novelist — twenty years of writing, a reasonably precise command of craft vocabulary, and no formal training in law, politics or moral philosophy. I have been reading around transitional justice: the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission and its conditional amnesty, the Argentine prosecutions of the juntas and their later revival, the gacaca courts in Rwanda. What I want from you is a systematic translation of the central dispute — amnesty exchanged for truth, together with the case against it — into the vocabulary I actually think in: scene and summary, story and plot, focalisation, free indirect discourse, unreliable narration, character arc, denouement, and the difference between a resolution that is earned and one that merely arrives.

Be honest about the translation. Tell me which parts of the dispute map cleanly onto craft terms, which can be carried across only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in the way a novelist thinks about an ending. I am especially interested in three things.

First, whether the distinction between testimony and confession — a witness establishing a fact before an authority, a character implicating himself before a reader — survives the move, or whether a commission that asks for both at once is asking for something my vocabulary cannot represent.

Second, whether the state's decision not to punish has any real analogue in a narrative that simply declines to show a punishment. If it does not, is that a gap in my framework, or a sign that amnesty is a performative act of a kind fiction never performs?

Third, whether the reader's appetite for a villain, and for arcs brought to completion, is doing any genuine work in the retributive case, or is merely an aesthetic preference dressed up as a moral one.

Close with a residue report: what did not survive the crossing, and is the loss a defect of the framework I am translating into, or evidence that the original claim was less clear than it looked?

Please answer in British English.
