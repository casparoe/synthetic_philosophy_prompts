I'm a science journalist — fifteen years on the beat, mostly in the territory where decision theory, medicine, and behavioral economics overlap — and the dean of the journalism school where I did my master's has asked me to give this year's commencement address. The audience will be about eight hundred graduating students and their families under a tent, plus a small contingent of faculty from the joint program the school runs with the philosophy department; one of those faculty is a decision theorist who has published on the value of information, and he will be sitting in the second row. I mention him because I want the philosophy right even though almost nobody else in the tent will have heard of it. The address needs to be warm, concrete, and deliverable — ten to twelve minutes spoken, with a story or two, some humor, no lectern-thumping — but I want its spine to be a single technical result rather than the usual platitude about the pursuit of truth. The result is the one usually called Good's theorem, and the plain version I care about goes like this: if your preferences are over outcomes rather than over what you happen to know, if the information is free to you, if you can ignore it once you have it, and if you are an expected-utility maximizer who never forgets anything, then acquiring the information cannot lower your expected utility — the value of information is never negative. Journalists treat something like this as an article of faith. I want the address to state the theorem honestly, in a couple of sentences of plain English that will not make the man in the second row wince, and then spend most of its length on the places where its conditions come apart in the lives my graduates are actually about to lead.

So please write that address. Build the body around three or four concrete ways the conditions fail, and make at least two of them scenes I can narrate from the stage. Some material to work with, which you should feel free to sharpen, cut, or replace: a woman who declines a genetic test because a positive result would set off obligations she does not want to have — to her sister, to an insurer, to a version of herself she would then have to become; a poker player who would rather not learn the tell that would tell her she is beaten, because the knowledge would show on her face; a doctor who could order one more scan and doesn't, because the incidental findings would create duties he cannot discharge; a reporter who can still choose not to read the replies. These are not the same failure dressed four ways, and I want the speech to make the differences audible: sometimes knowing costs something; sometimes you cannot un-know; sometimes knowing changes what you want; sometimes other people can read your knowing; sometimes the information arrives as an obligation rather than as a fact. If you add a fifth case of your own, make sure it is doing new work rather than restating one of these.

I don't want a literature review, a reading list, or any citations, and I don't want you to quote anyone, living or dead — if you need an aphorism, coin it and let me deliver it as my own. The theorem should not be presented as false, and the ending should not be that knowledge is a trap. I want something more defensible than knowledge is power, and warmer than a caveat: something like the thought that whether to know is itself a decision, that it was always a decision, and that from here on it is theirs to make deliberately rather than by default.
