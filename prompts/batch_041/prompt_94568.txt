I'm a transactional lawyer, about fifteen years in, mostly M&A and commercial contracts. I know the formation doctrines cold — offer and acceptance, the objective theory of assent, duress and undue influence, fraudulent and negligent misrepresentation, the duty to disclose, unconscionability, capacity, mistake, all of it. My moral philosophy, on the other hand, is basicly one jurisprudence elective I took in law school plus a running argument with a friend who does applied ethics. And that argument never goes anywhere, which has made me suspect that her vocabulary and mine only look like the same vocabulary.

So here's what I'd like from you. Take the main philosophical accounts of what makes consent valid and restate each of them, one by one, in my vocabulary. The ones I know enough to name: the three-condition story where valid consent needs competence, adequate information and voluntariness; the coercion literature, which I gather turns on a fight about baselines — when is a threat a threat rather than just a hard bargain, and a threat against what; the deception literature, where I'm told the interesting case is non-disclosure rather than an outright lie; and the deeper views about what consent actually is — a mental state, a performative act, the transfer of a permission — and why acting without it is wrong. Put all of that into offer and acceptance, objective assent, duress, misrepresentation and disclosure duties, unconscionability, capacity, mistake, and the line between a bargain and a gift. Be as faithful as you can, and dont smooth over the places where the fit is bad.

Then the part I actually want, which is the residue. Which of the philosophical claims come through cleanly? Which ones only survive because you quietly import something my vocabulary doesn't have — a baseline of moral entitlement rather than legal entitlement, say, or an interest in the quality of someone's will that the objective theory of assent deliberately refuses to look at? And which ones have no counterpart in contract law at all? I have a hunch the mental-state views do badly, and maybe the whole idea that consent is about what's going on inside the person rather than about whether a transaction can be enforced.

One complication I want you to deal with rather than dodge. Most of the ethics cases are about acts, not terms — surgery, sex — and if I want the legal analogue of those I have to leave contracts entirely and go to battery and the medical informed-consent doctrine, where the test is usually what a reasonable patient would want disclosed and the wrong is a failure of the doctor's duty rather than proof that the patient didn't consent. Tell me whether that switch of legal vocabulary changes the translation, because I suspect it does, and I suspect that informed consent in that sense isn't consent at all.

Finally, tell me which side the residue is a problem for. When a philosophical claim has no home in my vocabulary, is that because contract law is missing something real — because it's built for institutional purposes and has no way to talk about what went wrong between two people — or because the philosophical claim was vaguer than it sounded and the law's precision is exposing that? My suspicion is mostly the second, with one exception: the baseline problem looks to me like the one place where the lawyers and the ethicists are doing exactly the same thing and neither side notices. Tell me if I'm wrong about that.

I'm not going to read anything you recommend, so skip the reading list. Just the translation and the residue.
