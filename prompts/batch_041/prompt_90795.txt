I am a junior in high school and, mostly by accident, the person who runs our game theory club's Tuesday sessions. Next week I have the floor for about twenty minutes on the slogan "throw out the options that are never a good idea," and I want to open with a worked example that is mine rather than the one everybody has already seen. I have sat through the two-by-two illustrations so many times that they no longer surprise anyone, and I would like an example that a room of fifteen-year-olds will find genuinely counterintuitive and that I can defend line by line if someone challenges me. Please construct it for me rather than describing an existing one.

The example I want is a two-player game in which both players move at the same time and the payoffs are laid out in a table, and in which one of the first player's options is removed by that "never a good idea" reasoning only once we allow randomising. Concretely: I want an option that is not strictly beaten across the board by any single one of the player's other options, but that is strictly beaten, against every one of the opponent's options, by a genuine weighted mixture of the other two. Make the game as small as it can possibly be, tell me how many options each player has, and give me a short argument — not an assertion — that no smaller game could exhibit this, because I expect to be asked. Use small whole numbers for the payoffs, give the mixture weights exactly as fractions and make them unequal rather than fifty-fifty, so that the mixing itself is visibly doing the work, and dress the whole thing in a concrete everyday setting (two rivals, each with a short list of options) so that it reads as a scenario, with the payoff table printed plainly underneath. Then walk me through it: the eliminations round by round to the end, the set of options that survives, and whether that surviving set would change if the eliminations were done in a different order. I would also like three short explanations. First, why being beaten by a mixture means the option cannot be a best reply to any belief about the opponent — argued from scratch, without naming a theorem at me. Second, why that same fact means the option is played with probability zero in every equilibrium of the game, and what those equilibria are. Third, which single feature of the table is doing the real work: exactly what I would have to change to make the option undominated, and why giving the opponent a third option would ordinarily undermine the example unless I chose that option's payoffs with care. Finally, one line of housekeeping — would anything change if I read the same numbers as weakly beaten rather than strictly beaten?
