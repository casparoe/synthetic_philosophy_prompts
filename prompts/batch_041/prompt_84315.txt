I am the senior trade-policy adviser in the ministry of trade of a mid-sized country that still administers one distant dependency, and three or four times a year my team is handed a proposal that would create, renew, or dissolve an exclusive commercial arrangement over a territory: a chartered company with the sole right to trade in a region, a thirty-year concession to a single consortium, or, in the other direction, a plan to give up an unprofitable possession while keeping access to its market. The proposals arrive as investment cases with revenue projections attached; the minister decides on my recommendation, usually within a month, and my team has no one who can build a general-equilibrium model.

I want a decision procedure we can actually run, built out of Adam Smith's treatment of empire and colonies in the long chapter on colonies in Book IV of An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776), rather than out of the two things we use now, which are the projected fiscal return and the strategic argument that we must not lose ground to our neighbours. The parts of that treatment I take to matter, in whatever words you prefer, are: that the trade with a colony and the monopoly of that trade are two different benefits, and only the first is generally worth having; that an exclusive privilege raises profits in the privileged branch, diverts capital from employments that would have used it better, and is paid for by consumers at home and by the people of the territory, who buy dearer and sell cheaper; that a chartered company which is at once merchant and sovereign over the people it trades with has an interest opposed to theirs and no third party to check it, which is why Smith expected such companies to govern badly; that the defence and administration of a distant possession is a charge on the revenue of the home country, so that an empire may be a net expense even when its trade is large; that Smith's measure of a policy is the annual produce of the land and labour, not the volume of trade or the bullion in the treasury; and that his own remedy, where a possession was to be kept at all, was to make the arrangement reciprocal — the territory contributing to the common burden and represented in proportion to what it contributes — and where it could not be, to part with the authority and keep the trade on equal terms.

Then run the procedure on two live proposals. The first is a thirty-year exclusive concession over the trade of a remote coastal province of ours that currently trades on open terms with everyone: the consortium offers roads, a port, and a share of the revenue in exchange for the right to exclude others. The second is the reverse case: an island dependency that costs us a naval subsidy and a resident administration, and takes about a tenth of our exports; one faction wants to relinquish it outright, another says that relinquishing it means losing the market altogether.

Give me the procedure as exactly five numbered items, in order, with no introduction and no conclusion, each item no longer than three sentences. Each item should do three things: state the step as a question my team puts to the proposal; say what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether that answer can stop the proposal on its own; and say what the step returns on each of the two proposals. Put in the fifth item the point at which the procedure stops being a calculation and hands the decision to the minister as a question of politics or of values, and the ways in which the procedure will misfire — including the proposal on which it gives an answer I will not want to hear.
