I have been arguing for weeks with a colleague in my lab about whether adversarial debate can serve as a method of scalable oversight. The proposal, as I understand it, is this: rather than have a weak judge — a human without the relevant expertise, or a smaller model — evaluate a complicated claim directly, you have two instances of a stronger model argue the claim's truth and falsity in alternating, length-limited turns, and then let the weak judge pick the side it found more convincing, on the theory that the truthful side has an easier job than the liar. My colleague insists the scheme is dead on arrival because of what he calls the obfuscated-argument problem: a dishonest debater can hide the flaw in an argument behind enough complexity that the honest debater cannot dig it out within the turn limit, and the judge, who by hypothesis cannot evaluate the arguments on their own merits, is then left choosing between two things it cannot check. I think he is too quick, and that the very asymmetry that is supposed to make debate work ought to cut against the obfuscator as well, but I cannot make the point stick and I suspect I am smuggling in an assumption about how competent the judge really is.

I would like you to write a dialogue between two researchers who are both fully competent — one defending the promise of debate, the other pressing the obfuscated-argument objection — and I want it run against a specific case rather than in the abstract. Take a debate over whether a single-line change to a large, tangled codebase introduces a subtle security vulnerability: the sort of claim where the honest side might offer a genuine demonstration of exploitability, the dishonest side an argument that merely has the shape of one, and the judge can neither read the codebase nor follow the argument. Have the defender lean on the complexity-theoretic intuition that a debate between honest agents can in principle settle questions that direct judging cannot, and have the critic attack the disanalogy — that this intuition assumes a judge that is merely limited in time, not one that arrives with priors and biases that an obfuscator can exploit. At least once, one of them should concede a real point and adjust rather than simply restate; they should each anticipate and answer the other's strongest move rather than talk past each other; and they should finish by locating precisely where they still disagree, saying whether the remaining disagreement is conceptual or empirical, and naming the experiment or case that would settle it. As a last twist, I have been toying with a repair and want it tested in the dialogue: require each debater to pre-register a short plain-language summary before arguing, and show the judge only that summary plus the rebuttals. Have one character raise it seriously and the other argue that it merely relocates the obfuscation upstream — and let the exchange decide whether it helps. Do not write a survey of the published debate; make the two of them reason their way through it.
