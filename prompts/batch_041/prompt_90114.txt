Write an essay on the limits of the principle of procreative beneficence.

As the principle is usually stated, parents who have decided to have a child should choose, from among the children they could have, the one whose life is expected to go best — where best means the greatest expected well-being, and not the best life for any particular individual. Its defenders present it as a modest extension of ordinary parental solicitude. Its critics say it is either empty, because possible lives cannot be ranked on a common scale, or else unacceptable, because it licenses a eugenic attitude towards children and expresses a disparaging view of the lives of those born with impairments.

Consider now what the principle implies in a setting where the menu of possible children is not fixed in advance. Suppose the parents may decide how many children to have; may create further embryos in order to widen the pool; may postpone conception in the hope of a better candidate; and must accept that the family structure they choose — how many siblings the selected child will have, and how thinly the parents' attention will be spread — is itself a determinant of how well that child's life is expected to go. Call this unbounded beneficence.

Under these conditions, does the principle still tell parents what to do? Three worries are worth testing. First, that it sets off a maximising logic with no natural stopping point, since there is always another draw to wait for; and that this becomes self-reinforcing if some of the traits it is worth selecting for are also the traits that make a person a good selector, so that the gains compound across generations. Second, that once family size is itself open to selection, the principle begins to reproduce the population-ethics difficulty of preferring a larger number of lives that are barely worth living to a smaller number of flourishing ones. Third, that the only way to arrest these consequences is to add a threshold or a side-constraint, which alters the principle rather than applying it.

Weigh these worries against the strongest replies available to a defender — for example, that the principle is comparative only within a fixed set of alternatives; that the costs of waiting, of further selection and of family size are themselves part of the calculation; and that a principle may be extremely demanding without being false.

Reach a verdict. Either the principle should be retained in its unrestricted form, or it should be restricted in some specific way (state the restriction and show what it excludes), or it should be abandoned. Defend your verdict against the most serious objection to it. You need not name any authors or works; what matters is whether the argument holds.
