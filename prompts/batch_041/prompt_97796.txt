Compare the decision-theoretic structure of Roko's basilisk — the claim that a future AI will reward those who worked toward its existence and punish those who knew of it and did nothing — with that of Pascal's mugging. Both are commonly dismissed as absurd consequences of expected-value reasoning, in which a minute probability of an astronomically large reward or punishment is allowed to swamp every ordinary consideration, and I want to know whether the basilisk is simply the mugging in futurist dress. I suspect the resemblance is superficial: the mugging turns on a threat whose fulfilment is a causal consequence of your refusal, whereas the basilisk conditions its reward and punishment on your present decision-theoretic disposition, so that your compliance matters as evidence about the kind of agent you are rather than as a cause of the AI's behaviour. Does that shift from causal to evidential or acausal reasoning change the argument's status? If one rejects the mugging by refusing to let unspecified tiny probabilities and unbounded utilities drive choice, does the basilisk fall with it, or does it survive on the strength of an acausal dependence between present deliberation and a future agent? And if one grants that such a dependence is intelligible, does the basilisk follow, or does the astronomical-stakes objection still bite independently? Please be explicit about where the two arguments genuinely diverge and where the difference is merely cosmetic.
