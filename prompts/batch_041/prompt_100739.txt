Here's the question I set (20 pts):

Q. (Short essay.) Consider: a patient in a hospital dies because the nurse on duty did not administer a drug that had been ordered. A cousin of the patient, who also knew about the order, did not telephone the hospital to remind anyone. Assume the drug would have saved the patient.
(a) State the counterfactual ("but-for") analysis of singular causation and apply it to this case: did the nurse's omission cause the death?
(b) State the main objection to applying the but-for analysis to omissions.
(c) Consider this amendment — call it the "normality amendment": count an omission as a cause only when it departs from what normally happens in the circumstances. Does the amendment answer the objection in (b)? What does it cost?

Here's what the student wrote:

"(a) The but-for analysis says that an event C causes an event E just when E would not have occurred if C had not occurred. For omissions we swap the event for an act: an agent's failure to do X caused E just when E would not have occurred had the agent done X. On the facts, had the nurse given the drug, the patient would have lived, so the nurse's omission caused the death. Strictly speaking, though, we can never observe the unactualized world, so the claim that the patient would have lived is not really true or false — it's a more or less useful counterfactual — but it's the right kind of thing for the analysis to work with.

(b) The problem is that the test is far too generous. The cousin's failure to ring also satisfies it: had she rung, someone would have chased the drug up and the patient would have lived. So does the pharmacist's failure to double-check, and the plain fact that no orderly happened to give the drug. The but-for test therefore makes everything that didn't happen a cause of the death, and it can't tell us which of these are the causes we're actually interested in.

(c) The normality amendment helps here. On it, only omissions that depart from the normal course of things count. Nurses are supposed to give ordered drugs, so the nurse's omission departs from the norm and counts; cousins are not normally expected to ring hospitals, so the cousin's omission doesn't depart from the norm. So the amendment restores the distinction the but-for test lost, and the cousin's failure to ring is not a cause of the death at all. The cost is that the analysis is no longer purely about what happens in the world; it now leans on a notion of what is normal or expected, which is partly a matter of norms rather than facts, and different communities may draw the line in different places."
