I chair the admissions and standards committee of a state licensing board, and every third year we have to re-justify each of our entry requirements to the legislature. We do it badly every time: the members who went through the requirements defend them, the newer members point out what they cost applicants in income and years, and we end up voting on whether a requirement feels relevant. Six of the nine of us hold the credential, which does not help. I would like a procedure we can actually run in a committee meeting, on one requirement at a time, that tells us whether a costly entry requirement is doing the work we claim for it.

Take the following as given. Our staff analyst wrote a memo setting out what has to be true for an expensive requirement to be a credible signal of the professional competence we are charged with certifying, rather than a toll on the people who want to enter the field. The memo is a translation of the handicap principle and of the economics of education-as-signal into our terms, and the committee has accepted it as our working position. Its conditions:

1. Differential cost. The requirement must be cheaper for applicants who already have the trait we care about than for applicants who lack it. A cost that falls equally on everyone, or that scales with family money, spare time, prior connections, or willingness to absorb mistreatment, is not screening for competence; it is screening for those other things.

2. A sanction on the wrong type. A signal stays honest only if applicants who lack the trait and acquire the credential anyway end up worse off for having done it. So we must ask what the credential actually buys, and whether that payoff penalizes the poor-fit holder. If the credential is simply required in order to work at all, then the only penalty the wrong type suffers is the cost itself, and the requirement is a tax rather than a test.

3. Separation rather than pooling. If nearly everyone can eventually satisfy the requirement, or if it ratchets upward as applicants compete, it stops separating anyone from anyone. The memo distinguishes requirements that are absorbing — met once and held forever, like a degree — from requirements that recur — met again and again, like hours or periodic exams — and says the two need different treatment.

4. A direct index beats a costly ritual. Where a cheap test measures the trait itself rather than standing in for it, a costly proxy is dominated even if the proxy also correlates with competence.

5. Revealing versus producing. If the requirement creates the competence rather than revealing it, the signaling story does not apply, though the fairness questions about who can afford to acquire it do.

6. Who pays. Costs borne by the applicant, by their family, by clients, or by the public are not interchangeable, and we should record which is which before we vote.

We have also been warned about two failure modes to design around: the strongest applicants may be the ones who skip the costly requirement, so its presence among successful licensees proves less than it appears to; and a requirement that was informative when it was rare may be worthless once it is universal.

What I want from you is a procedure, in the order we should run it, for reviewing a single entry requirement. For each step: the question we ask, what evidence counts as an answer to it, what we do when we cannot get that evidence — we have almost no outcome data linking entry requirements to later performance, and we are not going to get any — and whether the step ends in a verdict (keep, keep with modification, replace, abolish) or stops the review with a no, or refers the matter to the full board because the question has become a value judgment about who should bear the cost of entering the profession rather than a factual one. Include a short form for the fifteen minutes we will actually have per requirement, and mark where the short form is unsound.

Then run the procedure on three of our requirements:

- 1,200 hours of supervised practice, unpaid, which applicants typically complete over a year while holding another job;
- a written entry examination with a 45 percent first-attempt pass rate, retakeable for a fee;
- 40 hours of in-person continuing education every two years, in a state where the nearest approved provider is a five-hour drive from two of our three population centers.

At least one of the three should come out badly for us. Be explicit about which one, what it means, and what we would have to give up.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which questions about our requirements it will not settle, which of the six conditions are doing real work in it and which we could drop without changing any verdict, where it is a value judgment wearing the clothes of an audit, and which of the six conditions you would amend and how.
