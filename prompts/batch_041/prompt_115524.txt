Last night a friend and I argued past midnight about whether Nietzsche's scattered remarks on diet, climate, and regimen amount to anything more than the self-report of a chronically ill man with strong tastes, and I would like to settle the question in writing rather than in another round of shouting.

I am a nutritional epidemiologist by training. I design and analyze n-of-1 trials, I spend my working hours with causal graphs, target-trial emulation, effect modification, dose–response curves, and confounding by indication, and I am reasonably fluent on the physiology side: energy balance, glycemic variability, circadian entrainment, altitude acclimatization, heat and cold exposure, allostatic load, the gut–brain axis, and the difference between a mechanism and a mere marker. I have read Nietzsche only in translation, mostly the late works. I know the section of Ecce Homo called Why I Am So Clever, where he discusses nourishment, place, climate, and recreation, and the passage in the fifth book of The Gay Science where he writes about needing a new and stronger health than any previous one. What I do not have is any grip on how to state his position in my own vocabulary without either flattening it into wellness advice or mystifying it into something untestable.

So please restate his views on diet and health as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of nutritional epidemiology and physiology. Include his avoidance of alcohol and coffee, his preference for small and simple meals, his insistence that place and climate matter more than people admit, his practice of lying still when ill instead of forcing himself through it, his treatment of reading and walking as forms of recovery, and his larger claim that health is something one must invent for oneself and that a philosopher's physiology is legible in his philosophy. Where his claims can be turned into hypotheses, turn them into hypotheses: name the exposure, the outcome, the plausible mechanism, the confounders he could not have seen, and the places where his evidence amounts to a single unblinded subject who was already committed to the conclusion.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which of his claims translate cleanly into testable hypotheses? Which translate only with distortion — for instance, does his insistence that dietary rules are personal and non-generalizable map onto precision nutrition and n-of-1 methodology, or does the mapping break because he treats non-generalizability as a virtue rather than a limitation? And which of his claims have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary: the idea that a regimen is answerable to a task or a destiny rather than to health outcomes, that illness can be a form of strength, that the question of what one eats is subordinate to the question of what one is for?

Finally, since my friend and I are still arguing, give me a verdict rather than a survey. Is the residue evidence that my framework is missing something real — the way a regimen functions inside a life, as an act of self-legislation rather than a bundle of exposures — or is it evidence that his claims were normative and unfalsifiable from the start, dressed up in the language of physiology? Do not split the difference.
