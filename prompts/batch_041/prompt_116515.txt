I am preparing briefing material for a researcher who will spend three weeks doing fieldwork in a region whose everyday conventions differ sharply from her own, and she has asked for a moral audit of the conventions she is likely to run into. Assume throughout that she knows what each convention is, that she can comply with any of them at modest cost to herself, and that her compliance or non-compliance will be legible to the people around her. Treat the conventions as stipulated; nothing about the region beyond what is stated here is relevant.

The conventions:

1. Restaurant tipping. At sit-down restaurants, diners are expected to leave a gratuity of roughly a fifth of the bill, and servers are paid on the assumption that they will receive it. At counter-service places, no gratuity is expected.

2. Escalator usage. Riders stand on the right and leave the left side clear for people walking up. Compliance is near-universal; a single rider standing on the left harms no one, though walkers are delayed.

3. A street-crossing rule that is universally flouted. Crossing mid-block on an empty road at three in the morning carries a fine on paper, but residents cross whenever it is convenient and enforcement is effectively nonexistent.

4. Greeting. In the smaller towns, strangers are expected to greet one another in passing. A visitor who walks by in silence is not fined or harmed, but is read as cold, and locals may later be less helpful to her.

5. Footwear indoors. Guests remove their shoes at the door. A guest who keeps them on is understood to have shown contempt for the household; the hosts are visibly stung, though no one is harmed.

6. A communal meal. The hosts seat guests at a table separate from the family, and the women of the household serve without sitting. Compliance means the visitor takes part in the arrangement without protest. She finds it demeaning.

7. Rotating payment. Among residents, whoever proposes an outing pays for everyone, on the understanding that the favour will be returned later. The visitor is present for one such outing and will not return.

Please answer the following.

(a) Give each convention two scores between 0 and 1: the strength of the moral obligation on a lifelong resident, and the strength of the moral obligation on the three-week visitor, where 0 means no moral reason whatever to comply and 1 means an obligation as stringent as any she has. Present this as a table, then order the seven conventions by the visitor score, breaking ties explicitly and saying what separates the tied items.

(b) For each convention, classify any obligation you found as either a conduct obligation or a meaning obligation. A conduct obligation is one that survives her announcing in advance, sincerely and clearly, that she is acting on her own customs and means no disrespect; a meaning obligation is one that her announcement would dissolve or substantially weaken. Where an item is neither, say so.

(c) Identify the two conventions whose ordering is most sensitive to how heavily one weighs a single consideration, and name that consideration.

(d) State the strongest single principle under which the conventions receiving the highest visitor scores are binding on her, and then say whether that principle also binds her at item 6. If it does not, say precisely what distinguishes item 6 from the highest scorers; if it does, say what she would be doing wrong by refusing.
