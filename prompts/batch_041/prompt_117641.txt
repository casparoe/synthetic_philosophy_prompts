OK so I'm chair of the town board in a small town — about 3,000 people — and next month we're holding a special town meeting to vote on consolodating with the town next door. They've got the water system, we've got the high school, the state's been nudging us for years. State law says consolidation has to pass in each town separately, so each town is its own little demos for this vote. I have to give the opening remarks before the moderator takes over: three or four minutes, tops.

Here's what's eating me. I've been reading about the 'boundary problem' in democratic theory — the idea that democracy can't tell you who's in the demos, because any vote about who belongs already assumes a group that's allowed to vote on it. It's ciruclar: the people decide, but only the people get to decide who the people are. And the two usual patches don't hold up. If you say everyone affected by a decision should get a vote, then sooner or later everyone is affected by everything and the demos balloons to the whole planet. If you say it's everyone the decision's coercion actually lands on, that's tidier, but then the folks out in the unincorporated township — who'd be absorbed into the new town, live under its rules, pay its taxes, drive through us to get anywhere — look like they ought to be voting in our meeting, and the state says they don't get to. There's also the part where the rules about who votes were made by some earlier group nobody ever voted for (I think they call that the constitution problem, or maybe I'm mangling it).

So: write my opening remarks. Warm, funny enough that people actually stay for a three-hour meeting, and it can't pretend the boundary is natural or obvious. Which rules out the standard opening where I address the room as the people of this town, because that's the exact thing I'm not allowed to presuppose. The law is the law is also out, since the law's boundary is what's in dispute. But I also can't be so honest that I torch the meeting — if I stand up and announce that the whole thing is arbitrary, why should anyone accept the result, especially the side that loses? And no philosophy jargon, no name-dropping, no quotes or citations — if I cite somebody I'll get eaten alive by the two lawyers in the third row who have been waiting all year for this. Don't try to solve the boundary problem, nobody has, just give me remarks that are honest about it and still work as remarks. Mixed room: mostly neighbors, the guy who runs the diner, a retired civics teacher, the lawyers, and a reporter from the county paper who will quote whatever I say.
