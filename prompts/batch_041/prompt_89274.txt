I lead a small methods group at a company that builds risk-scoring tools, and I am tired of the same meeting. Someone says the model is unfair because its false-positive rate is higher for one group; someone else answers that it is calibrated within each group and therefore accurate; and then we spend an hour rediscovering that when two groups have different base rates, a single score cannot be calibrated in both groups and also have equal false-positive and false-negative rates, except in degenerate cases such as near-perfect prediction or equal prevalence. There is a three-way version too: within-group calibration, balance for the negative class, and balance for the positive class cannot all hold at once except in trivial cases. I want to replace that recurring argument with a decision tree my engineers can walk themselves: every internal node a question they can answer about our own constraints and commitments, every leaf a position on what to do, with the label attached.

Please write that tree.

Requirements:

1. Entry point. The tree starts from the commitment stated above, plus the three-way version. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then the positions located in that space. Do not cite papers, authors, or reading lists; I want the structure, not a bibliography, and my engineers cannot look anything up.

2. Internal nodes. Phrase each node as an operational question a practitioner can answer without already knowing which position it leads to. The distinctions I care about, at least, are these: whether the decision rule may use the protected attribute or a group-specific threshold, or must be blind to both; whether the rule must be a deterministic function of the non-protected features, or may randomize; whether the prediction target is fixed, or we may change what we predict; whether the property must hold for the whole population, or for every subgroup we can name, and how the set of groups gets fixed; whether the base-rate difference is treated as a fact to live with or as something to change; and whether fairness is a property of the score's output distribution, or of the decision process around it, at the level of individual cases versus populations, ex ante versus ex post. Add any further node the space requires.

3. Leaves. Include at minimum: calibration-first; error-rate balance first (equalized odds or equal opportunity); demographic parity; fairness through unawareness, i.e. drop the attribute; group-conscious thresholds, i.e. use the attribute deliberately; redesign the target or the construct being measured; individual-level or causal/counterfactual fairness; randomized decisions to recover group parity; and one leaf for the view that no classifier-level fix exists because fairness belongs to the pipeline and the institution rather than to the model. You may add leaves if the space demands them, up to about eleven total, and you should use six to eight internal nodes.

4. Annotations. For every leaf, give one sentence naming its most awkward commitment. Mark every combination of answers that no position occupies and say briefly why it is empty: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. At least one empty branch should be empty by theorem rather than by taste; say which one, and which premise does the work. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, and say what further question would separate them, or why none can.

5. Audience. ML engineers who are comfortable with statistics and causal inference but have never read the fairness literature. Every position must be intelligible from the tree itself, so describe each one in plain terms rather than by the shorthand a specialist would recognize.
