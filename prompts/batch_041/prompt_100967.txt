Having spent the better part of forty years as a field ornithologist, I have lately been reading my way into the philosophy of mind, and I have run up against a scheme that sorts creatures into four grades according to how they cope with their surroundings. The first sort is born with its repertoire fixed: it lives or dies by its reflexes, and nothing it does in its lifetime alters the pattern. The second sort can learn from the consequences of its own behaviour, adjusting what it does by trial and error. The third can, so to speak, run the trial inside its head, discarding hypotheses before they cost it anything in the world. The fourth performs that same trick with hypotheses that were never its own, thinking with implements it inherited from other minds — notations, diagrams, arithmetic, the accumulated apparatus of a culture. What puzzles me is that the last two grades appear to be distinguished only by where the hypotheses come from, and I cannot persuade myself that this is a difference in kind rather than a difference in bookkeeping.

I should therefore like you to argue the case that my suspicion is misplaced. Argue, that is, that the fourth grade marks a genuine discontinuity in the evolution of mind, and not merely an unusually generous supply of pre-tested ideas handed down to creatures that are otherwise of the third grade. Make the strongest version of that claim you can sustain, and defend it on its merits rather than by appeal to authority; I have no interest in a survey of what various authors have written, and I do not want a reading list. Be explicit about the machinery of selection and of information that you take to be doing the work. Tell me, above all, what a third-grade creature cannot do that a fourth-grade creature can, and why that difference could not be reached by a third-grade creature that merely happened to be very thoroughly schooled by its elders — since it seems to me that a sufficiently well-taught simulator would be indistinguishable from your fourth grade, and if that is so, the fourth grade earns its place only by an equivocation.
