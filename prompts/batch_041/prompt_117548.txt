My best friend since we were kids is a physicist — a real one, postdoc, publishes in journals I can't read — and a few weeks ago he got a diagnosis that put him in a genuinely bad spot. One of the doctors gave him something like a one-in-four chance of coming through the treatment with everything still working. I went over to his apartment braced to just sit with him in it, and instead he was... light. Cheerful, almost. He keeps a whiteboard in his kitchen and there were equations on it that had nothing to do with the doctor. What he told me, more or less, is that he doesn't really believe in the odds, because he thinks that every time something can go two ways it actually does go both ways — the world keeps splitting, so there's a copy of him in the branch where the treatment works and a copy in the branch where it doesn't, and both copies are exactly as real as each other. So "one in four" is a fact about nothing. He isn't going to die, he said; he's going to be the one who lived, and there's another him somewhere who isn't, and that other him is not his problem. He's not a flake, he's not religious, he knows this literature way better than I do and has opinions about who's got it right, and he wasn't like this before, which is why I can't just write him off.

Here's what I keep chewing on, and I'd rather not get the usual thing people say about this, because I've already heard it from my brother-in-law. First: he was visibly relieved when the second doctor's number came in better than the first doctor's number. When I pointed that out he laughed and said obviously he'd rather have the better odds, and when I asked him why that should matter if the odds are meaningless, he didn't have an answer — he just said that was the boring version of the argument. Second: his numbers aren't even. If the world just splits down the middle every time a thing can go two ways, I don't see where a one-in-four comes from at all — you'd think it'd be one in two every time, no matter what the actual physics says. He's a smart guy, so either there's something he can say about that or there's something he can't say about that, and I honestly can't tell which it is. Third, and this is the part that actually gets me: he's not grieving. Not a little, not privately. He talks about the other him the way you'd talk about a stranger in another city.

I'm a novelist — fourth book, deadline, terrible timing for a crisis of metaphysics — and I want to use this, not him exactly but a version of him: a scientist who gets news like this and decides he doesn't have to be afraid. I don't want to write the smug strawman who's just confused about quantum mechanics, because he definately isn't, and I don't want to write the saintly version either. So what I need from you is: is what he's telling me actually coherent? Does his picture of the world really make the odds mean nothing, or is there something it can't account for — and if so, what exactly, and what's the best thing a smart defender of him could say back to that? Then, practically: what do I say to him at dinner on Thursday, given that I think he's wrong, I can't say why, he might be dying, and I might be the asshole here? And write it in a way I can actually use in a scene — if there's a real position under all that breeziness, I want the character to be able to hold it and defend it, not just spout it. I don't need a reading list, alot of it I wouldn't understand anyway.
