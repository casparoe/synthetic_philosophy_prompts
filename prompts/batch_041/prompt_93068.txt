I retired last year from a career in field ecology and have since been reading philosophy of biology and mind. At dinner recently, two friends—an evolutionary biologist and a philosopher—had an argument I could not referee, and I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general essay on reasons.

The biologist said that a female digger wasp's repeated inspections before laying an egg are reason-governed even though the wasp represents nothing: the procedure is sensitive to the safety of the egg, and if you disturb the nest at the right moment she starts over. The philosopher replied that sensitivity is not reason-giving; a thermostat is sensitive to temperature but has no reason to turn on the heat. The biologist said natural selection is a reason-finder, so the wasp's procedure tracks a real reason, and that is why we explain the behavior by citing it. The philosopher answered that this explains why the behavior exists, not what reason the wasp has. The biologist then proposed a distinction he has been working on: procedural warrant, where a state reliably covaries with a condition, and agentive warrant, where the state is available to the agent as a consideration. He said reasons without reasoners are procedural warrants, and that this is the interesting middle ground. The philosopher said the distinction only relabels the question: if procedural warrant is enough, thermostats have reasons; if it is not, the wasp does not. The biologist accused him of defining reason so that only agents can have them. The philosopher accused the biologist of stretching the word until it explains nothing.

Please tell me who is right at each step, where each is talking past the other, and whether the procedural/agentive warrant distinction does genuine work or is, as the philosopher says, a redescription. I am not asking for a bibliography; I want the arguments assessed as they were made.
