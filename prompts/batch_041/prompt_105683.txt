I have been keeping track of my verdicts about disagreement and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Here are the cases and my reactions. When a colleague and I have read the same paper, share the same background, and disagree about whether it contains a statistical error, I think I should shift part of the way towards her. When we have read the same poem and disagree about whether it is any good, I do not think I should shift at all. When we share the same evidence but she stands to profit if I concede, I hold firm. When we share the same evidence but I am exhausted and she is fresh, I think I should shift more than halfway towards her. When we share the same evidence and she reaches her conclusion by a route I cannot reconstruct but that I know has served her well before, I shift a little. When we agree on every premise and differ only in how we weight the considerations, arriving at opposite overall verdicts, I feel I should stay put, and yet I can point to no mistake on either side; this is the case that nags at me. And when many colleagues, each with the same evidence as I have and each reaching their view independently, all disagree with me, I find that I hold firm, which sits worst of all with the first case. My own tentative guess is that I should move towards a disagreeing peer in proportion to how much of my confidence rests on my own first-order reasoning rather than on the evidence we share, but I cannot make that fit the crowd case, where the shared evidence is the same and yet I do not budge.

Please tell me what principle, or small set of candidate principles, captures this pattern. For each, say which of my verdicts it preserves and which it forces me to abandon, and what it predicts for a case I have not listed: my disagreement with a future version of myself who has thought about the question for another ten years and reached the opposite conclusion.

Write in British English, and give me exactly five numbered items and nothing else — no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should state one candidate principle and then, in no more than a sentence or two, which of my verdicts it saves and which it sacrifices, together with its prediction for the case I have not listed.
