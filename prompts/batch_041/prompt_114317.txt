A colleague who works in finance defends the epistemic authority of markets with a picture I keep encountering: a price is like a thermometer. It does not make the weather, she says; it reports it. On her account the price of a bond, a share or a prediction-market contract is a reading of how things stand — the pooled private information of everyone who trades — and the rational response to a reading is deference: if the market says thirty per cent, believe thirty per cent, and treat any pundit or deliberating committee that says otherwise as a worse instrument. She uses the picture to argue that we should prefer market verdicts to expert commentary, to our own hunches, and to slower and more parochial procedures.

I should like an audit of the picture rather than a verdict for or against markets. Set out the correspondence explicitly — what stands in for the temperature, for the mercury, for the calibration, for the observer, for the units — and then identify where the mapping breaks down. Two breakdowns interest me in particular. First, the reading is also an input: the traders who generate the price are the same agents the price is meant to inform, so the instrument is part of the system it purports merely to measure. Second, the no-trade results: if a price genuinely carried the private information of everyone in the market, there would be nothing left for anyone to trade on, so the informative reading seems to depend on precisely the noise, hedging and liquidity motives that a clean measuring instrument would exclude. I am also struck by the related puzzle that an instrument nobody is paid to read will not be read accurately.

Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the deference conclusion from those that are merely picturesque, and say plainly whether the conclusion survives, and if so in what weakened form. If a different analogy — a jury, a poll, an auction — would do the work better, name it and say what it buys.

Please write the answer in British English.
