I host a small game-theory podcast, and next month's guest is a theorist I've been trying to book for ages. His whole line is that the iterated elimination of dominated strategies -- not Nash equilibrium -- should be the backbone of the subject: players discard dominated strategies, you let mixed strategies do some of the eliminating, and you iterate until nothing else can go; on his view Nash is downstream of this and we way overrate it. I want to press him hard and not just let him re-deliver the standard lecture, so can you draft me 8-10 interview questions? I need at least one each on the following, and for each of those a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give:

- The fact that a mixed strategy can strictly dominate a pure strategy even when no pure strategy dominates it. I want to put a concrete matrix in front of him and make him say why the player "can't" play the dominated pure strategy. The one I'm planning to use (player 1's payoffs only, choosing A, B, or C against the opponent's two columns): A = (3, -1), B = (-1, 3), C = (0, 0). So a 50-50 mix of A and B yields 1 against either column, strictly beating C's 0 against either column, while neither A nor B beats C on its own. Make him defend why C is out of bounds.

- The order-independence theorem -- what exactly it says, and whether it still holds once mixed strategies are allowed to do the dominating. Then hit him with the flip side: what happens when weakly dominated strategies enter the picture, and the fact that the order of elimination can change the outcome and can even knock out a Nash equilibrium. I want the follow-up to be about whether refusing to use weak dominance costs him most of the procedure's teeth.

- The epistemic story. Does common knowledge of rationality actually get you the iteration, or only the first round? I think it buys only the first round in general and he'll need something extra -- make him name the extra assumption, defend it, then follow up on whether that assumption is doing illegitimate work.

- How all this relates to rationalizability, and whether the correspondence between the two survives once there are three or more players rather than two.

Then a couple more of your own that make him earn the claim that this is a genuine solution concept and not just a preprocessing step -- something about what it predicts, where it predicts anything different from Nash, and what would count as evidence against the whole program.

Keep them pointed and technical, no "so tell us about your journey" fluff, and dont let him hide behind "that's a modeling choice" without paying for it.
