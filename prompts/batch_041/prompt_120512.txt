I run a monthly discussion evening at my local library: about eighteen people, mostly retired, mixed political sympathies, one long table, ninety minutes. I have no academic background in this; what I have is a folder of newspaper clippings and a growing irritation. Every time the subject of a country "backsliding" comes up, the argument never gets past two questions. Was it ever really democratic to begin with? And who gets to say it isn't now?

I have built my own tool for the evening and I would like the session built around it. I call it the five doors: elections, courts, newspapers, the civil service, and town-level government. My rule of thumb is that a country is backsliding when the people in charge close doors that were once open, one at a time, using the rules as they go — erosion rather than a single rupture, and never a coup. I want the group to find out for themselves why that rule of thumb keeps failing, without my standing up and explaining it to them.

Please design the session, with these pieces:

- A fictional country and a dated timeline of roughly a dozen events, with no real names in it, each event defensible on its own and none of them dramatic. I do not want the evening to become an argument about Hungary, Poland, Turkey or the United States, though I expect it will drift there in the last ten minutes, so tell me how to handle that.
- Rules and materials — cards, scoring sheets, whatever you think works — that carry the group through at least three rounds, with something deliberately changed between them: first, scoring the timeline against my five doors and declaring a verdict; second, the same timeline with each table given a different starting year to measure from; third, each table arguing the verdict from the position of somebody with something at stake, after which the room has to settle who has the standing to make the call at all.
- A moment near the end where the group must throw my five doors away and write its own list, and then tries to agree on it.
- A debrief mapping each round onto a part of the problem: what counts, measured from when, and decided by whom — including the awkward case of a country that was never fully open in the first place, and therefore may not be able to slide at all. End the debrief on the question I actually care about: whether the word does any work, or is just a polite way of picking sides.
- What will go wrong the first time: which round collapses, which role nobody will play, what to do when someone refuses to argue the government's side, and how to stop the group treating my scoring sheet as an answer key.
- An honest account of what the evening cannot do — which part has to be explained from the front, and what the game will make look more arbitrary than it really is.

Finish with a last line in exactly this form: Core moment: followed by one sentence naming the single moment the whole session is built to produce.
