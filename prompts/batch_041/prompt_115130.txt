I've spent the last several months in a long-running argument about behavioral genetics with a friend who is a good deal more hereditarian than I am, and I've started to suspect that what I've been defending is less a position than a set of slogans, each of which does useful work against him in isolation. Before I write any of it up where other people can see it, I want an audit. Here is what I've actually been claiming. First, that heritability is a variance ratio — the share of phenotypic variance in some population at some time that traces to genetic differences among its members — and that as such it is a statistic about a population and carries no individual-level reading at all. Second, that the standard designs (twins, adoptions, and now polygenic scores) nevertheless deliver real causal knowledge about the sources of individual differences, and that I'm willing to accept the usual finding that adult cognitive ability is substantially heritable in the populations that have been studied. Third, that it is a fallacy to move from a high heritability estimate to the conclusion that environmental interventions on the trait will fail, since a trait can be near-fully heritable and still move a great deal when the environment changes for everyone at once. Fourth, that gene-environment interaction and gene-environment correlation are pervasive enough that the assumptions behind the standard variance decomposition are probably false for most traits anyone cares about, and that the gap between twin-based estimates and estimates built from measured genotypes is evidence that the twin numbers are inflated. Fifth, that the genetic share of variance in cognitive ability comes out larger, not smaller, in better-off families. Sixth, that a heritability estimate is a genuinely useful predictive parameter — that the response to selection can be forecast from it, as animal breeding demonstrates. And seventh, that nothing whatever follows about group differences from any within-group heritability estimate, so the usual hereditarian inference about achievement gaps is a non sequitur.

I don't think I'm contradicting myself, but I also don't think I've checked. What I want is an audit rather than a lecture: which of these commitments actually pull against which, ordered by how much damage the tension does, and for each one, what the cheapest repair is and what it costs me to make it. Where a pair that looks like a conflict is really an artifact of running two different senses of heritability together, say so and say which sense is doing the work in each of the claims. And if there is a single revision — one commitment dropped, weakened, or restated — that would clean up more of the mess than any other, tell me which one it is and what I would be giving up by making it.
