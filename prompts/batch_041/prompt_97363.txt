I am drafting a position piece on the naturalism–normativism debate about health and disease, and I want to know how much of that disagreement is substantive rather than terminological. Here is the proposition I would like a credence on:

(P) There is a single, non-arbitrary reference class, specifiable by biological theory alone and without evaluative input, relative to which the naturalist's statistical-and-design analysis of function yields determinate verdicts about health and disease; consequently the naturalist can state conditions for disease that are fully value-free.

Your task is not to tell me what the standard positions are, but to give me your credence that (P) is true and to decompose it. In particular, give me a number and a one-line justification for each of the following, then show how they combine:

- the probability that a biological account of function (whether selected-effect, goal-directed, or some other) can be made precise enough to classify individual traits as functional or dysfunctional without smuggling in a norm;
- the probability that biology itself fixes a unique reference class (species, sex, age, ecological niche, individual optimum) rather than leaving the choice open, and that fixing it does not already embed an evaluative judgment;
- the probability that the cases where the reference class actually flips the verdict are ones where there is independent pressure toward a single correct answer, rather than cases where reasonable people are simply using the word differently;
- the probability that, even granting a value-free analysis of function, the residual disagreement between naturalists and normativists is merely verbal — that the two sides are answering different questions and could both be right.

To make the reference-class question concrete, I have been worrying about a constructed case: an isolated population whose members lack a trait that the rest of the species possesses, so that by species-wide statistics they are abnormal but by local statistics they are typical, with no suffering and no impairment in either framing. Whether this is a disease seems to turn entirely on which reference class you pick. Tell me whether you think that case is decisive against (P) or merely a technical embarrassment for it, and whether the answer changes if you add that the trait loss is recent and environmentally induced rather than long-standing.

Then tell me which term your overall number is most sensitive to, and what argument or kind of evidence would move it most — for instance, a demonstration that evolutionary theory forces one particular reference class, or a finding that ordinary disease judgments track dysfunction rather than harm.

For calibration: my own credence in (P) is about 0.3, and a colleague who works on the philosophy of medicine puts it near 0.75. Say whether your number sits nearer mine or hers, and, more usefully, which of the sub-questions above you think accounts for most of the gap between us.

Do not worry about citations or a literature review; I will supply those. Please finish with a final line of exactly the form \answer{X}, where X is your credence in (P) to two decimal places.
