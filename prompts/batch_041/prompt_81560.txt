I am a novelist at work on a book whose cast includes a philosopher of science, and I have reached the scene where she tells a joke at a dinner party — one meant to land with the two other philosophers at the table and to leave everyone else quietly uneasy. I would like you to write that joke. Its subject is the long quarrel over what a law of nature is: whether laws are nothing over and above the patterns in what happens, so that the true statements of law are simply the axioms of the best systematization of the world's entire history, the system that strikes the best bargain between simplicity and strength; or whether laws are something further, genuine governing or necessitating relations in virtue of which the regularities hold, rather than a tidy summary of them.

A few constraints. The joke must turn on the actual content of the disagreement rather than on the word law doing double duty between the legal and the natural, and it must not be funny merely because philosophers are funny. It should be intelligible to any competent reader of the debate: someone hearing it ought to be able to tell which position is being needled and to feel that the needle is fair. Keep in mind, as material, that for the regularity-minded the world is a mosaic of local matters of particular fact with no necessary connections among distinct existences, so that a statement of law has whatever authority it has from its place in the best system and not from anything in the world that compels it; that such a philosopher will suspect the governing law of being a piece of theology, or of grammar, smuggled back into metaphysics; and that the opposite camp will retort that a summary is a description dressed up as an explanation, and that a world whose laws do not necessitate anything has lost precisely what we wanted laws for. One promising seam, if you want it: what it would mean for a law to be broken, and whether the regularity-minded view can even make sense of the question. You may take aim at either side, or at both, so long as the result stays coherent.

It should be short enough to say aloud across a table — a few sentences of setup and a punchline, no more — and it must survive being spoken, so no footnotes, no parentheticals longer than a breath, and no dependence on knowing anyone's name. If you wish to add a few sentences of prose explaining why the joke works, put them after it and keep them brief; do not explain what the joke already makes plain. Write the whole of it as continuous prose, the way one writes in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
