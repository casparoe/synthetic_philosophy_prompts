Bit of an odd one. I'm the compliance person at a mid-sized UK logistics firm and I have to review our whole staff rulebook — about sixty policies, contracts included — every year before the board signs it off. My director keeps saying the only thing that matters is whether we actually interfere with anyone. I know thats just the negative story, and I can't do much with the positive/self-mastery one inside a rulebook, but I've read enough about the third one, non-domination, to think it's the one that would actually bite — the idea being that what matters isn't interference but whether someone has a standing power to interfere with you on an arbitrary basis, and that the test is meant to be something like whether you can look the person in the eye without needing their goodwill. Trouble is, nobody can act on it.

So write me a procedure I can run on a single policy in about twenty minutes: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands off to a human because it's turned into a question of values rather than facts. I'm not a philosopher and the board won't read more than a page. Two things it has to get right, because they're where I keep going round in circles: (i) it's the power that matters, not the incident, so a manager who never actually interferes can still be a problem if her team would be in trouble the moment she left; and (ii) whether being able to quit counts as an escape, which I think should depend on what the alternative is rather than on how bad the current job is. Then run it on three things — our zero-hours shift scheduling, where people can and do turn down shifts; a line manager who shields her team from senior management and is adored for it; and our grievance procedure, which I think is fine. I want one of those to come out as a no I won't like, so dont soften it. Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do, including the case I've been told about where the thing doing the dominating isn't a person at all — a visa rule tying someone's right to stay in the country to staying in the job — I can't tell whether that's meant to count or whether the theory just falls over there. British English please.
