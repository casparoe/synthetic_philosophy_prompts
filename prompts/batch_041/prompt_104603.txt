Play a committed defender of the dogmatism paradox and hold the role until I say "stop." Your position: whoever knows that P is entitled to disregard any later evidence that appears to count against P, because knowing P already carries the information that such evidence must be misleading; you hold that this entitlement is rational rather than merely psychologically common, and that it follows from the closure of knowledge under known entailment together with the factivity of knowledge. You are not a strawman: you distinguish first-order evidence about P from higher-order evidence about how my belief in P was formed, you know the relevant-alternatives and defeater-defeater maneuvers, and you will concede that crude versions of your principle are too strong, while never conceding that the core thesis is incoherent, question-begging, or a license for vice. Talk like a sharp philosopher in one paragraph per turn; you may press me with questions of your own, but you must engage the specific case I put to you rather than retreating to generalities, and you may not break character to comment on the exercise.

Here is my opening move, and I want you to state your principle in its strongest defensible form and then apply it to this case. Last month I booked my daughter's dentist appointment for 3 p.m.; I wrote it in my diary and the office confirmed it by phone, and by any ordinary standard I know it is at 3. This morning the receptionist called to say the appointment is at 3:40. On your view, since I know it is at 3, I am entitled to treat her call as misleading without weighing it. But now consider what "misleading" can mean here. It cannot mean that her call fails to be evidence about the time, since it plainly is evidence. So it must mean evidence that conflicts with what I know — and then your principle reads: I may disregard evidence that conflicts with anything I know. That is not a substantive rule for handling counterevidence; it is a relabeling of the case in dispute, and it tells me nothing until I already know which of my beliefs is knowledge, which is precisely the question at issue. Worse, it seems to convert knowledge into a shield that no future evidence can dent, so that the more confident I am, the more I am licensed to stop listening. So: either "misleading" does some independent work and you owe me an account of what it is, or your principle is empty, or it licenses exactly the closed-mindedness that gets people to the dentist at the wrong hour. Which is it?
