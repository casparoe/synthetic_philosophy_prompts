For two years a colleague and I have argued without moving about whether normative facts — what there is reason to do, what is right — are fixed by what an idealized deliberator would conclude: an agent fully informed, fully coherent, and unconstrained by bias or limited time. I defend the account. She holds that it is circular, since one cannot characterize the ideal deliberator without already using the normative notions the account was meant to explain — correct reasoning, proper information, what she ought to believe — so it assumes what it promises to derive; and idle, since even if the ideal deliberator's verdicts exist we have no independent route to them, so citing them can neither guide nor justify an actual agent. My replies — that a functional or recursive characterization need not be viciously circular, that the account is a metaphysics of reasons rather than a decision procedure, and that the demand to know why one should care what an idealized self would advise simply assumes reasons must be anchored in actual desires — satisfy me and plainly do not satisfy her. I want the structure of this impasse rather than a ruling on who is right. My hypothesis is that three distinct disagreements have fused into one: a substantive one about whether normative facts can be constituted by an ideal agent's responses without circularity; a verbal one about whether the word reason names a metaphysical favoring relation or a deliberative consideration; and a disagreement about which cases count as data, in which the alienation intuition is either decisive or irrelevant. Say whether that is right and, if it is not, what the real structure is; then say what would count as progress given the diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result about whether the idealization can be specified without normative vocabulary, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected. Finally, run the diagnosis through one case: an idealized deliberator, fully informed and coherent, reaches a verdict that no argument could ever bring any actual human to accept, not through bias or ignorance but because it is unreachable by our reasoning. Does that verdict bind us, and what does each side's answer reveal about what the dispute was really about?
