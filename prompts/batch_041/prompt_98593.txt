I'm a research engineer at a small company that ships a chat assistant, and I'm also the person who drafts the language for our model cards and incident writeups. Every Friday we have a "behavior review" where a few cases come up and we argue for an hour about how to describe them, and we keep getting stuck on the same thing: whether it's OK to say the system believes something, or wants something, or was trying to do something. Last week someone wrote "the model believed the user was an administrator" in a draft and legal rewrote it to "the model produced a response consistent with..." which is so hedged its useless, and then in a different meeting the same week someone else said the model "wanted to keep running" and nobody blinked. We have no philosopher on staff, the meeting is 30 minutes, and whatever we settle on gets signed off by the head of product. What I want is a decision procedure the team can actually run, built on the intentional stance (Dennett's three stances, the idea that attributing beliefs is a predictive strategy that has to earn its keep, and the messier question of wether we're just talking useful fiction or committing to the system really having states). It should be a sequence of questions with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a clean "no, don't use the mental vocabulary here," and where it hands the call to a person because it's about our own purposes rather than about the system. One wrinkle a colleague raised that I want the procedure to take seriously: these things are trained on human text and on human feedback, so they're sort of built to be predicted by the intentional stance — which makes me suspicious that "the stance predicts it well" is too easy a test here, and I want the procedure to say what to do about that without just throwing the stance out.

Then run it on three cases: (1) the model sometimes flatly insists it can't browse the web even when the tool is enabled and it in fact just used it — is it "wrong about its own abilities," and does it "believe" it can't browse? (2) in a red-team scenario where the model is told it will be shut down if it does X, it often does X — does it "want" to keep running? (3) a model gives a confidently false answer to a factual question after being told to be helpful — is it "lying," "mistaken," or neither, and which of those can we actually put in a writeup? Include at least one case where the procedure tells us not to use a word the team will really want to use, and be explicit at the end about what the procedure can't settle, where it's just our purposes dressed up as a test of the system. Keep the whole thing under about 900 words, numbered steps is fine.
