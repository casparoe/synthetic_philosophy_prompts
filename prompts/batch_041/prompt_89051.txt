Something happened at work a few weeks ago and it's been eating at me. A guy on my team said something in a meeting that a colleague found hurtful. He apologized on the spot, said he hadn't meant it that way, seemed genuinely mortified. What he got back, from HR and from a couple of people on the team, was the usual line: intent doesn't matter, impact does. Then he apologized again and someone told him an apology isn't the point, what matters is what changes going forward. I dont think anyone was being unfair exactly. But I've been chewing on it for weeks because I can't work out what rule is actually being applied.

Taken at face value, impact-over-intent says your blameworthiness tracks the harm that landed rather than what you meant or could reasonably have foreseen, which is strict liability — and basically nobody defends strict liability for adults in ordinary life, and the law is built the other way, mens rea everywhere you look. So either the norm is committed to something wild, or I'm misreading which question it's answering.

Here's what I want. This practice — enforcing impact over intent at work, on campus, in online pile-ons — what does it actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and be careful to separate what it strictly requires from what it merely sits well with. My hunch, which I'd rather you test than assume: the slogan is doing work on three different questions at once — whether a norm was broken, whether the person is blameworthy, and whether the harmed person is owed something — and it's only really true of the third. If that's right, then the practice isn't a theory of blame at all, it's a theory about standing and remedy, and the blame reading is a misreading that everyone, me included, has been running with.

Two more things that bug me and that I think any decent answer has to handle. First, the same HR process will absolutley litigate intent the moment it's their intent on the line. Second, the whole thing seems to assume something about what an apology is for — restoring the harmed person, or repairing the offender's standing, or neither. And last: is this a victimhood-culture thing in any substantive sense, i.e. standing that comes from having been wronged, or is the practice actually neutral between honor, dignity and victimhood framings and I've just been pattern-matching? If it's neutral, say which of the three the workplace version actually looks like in practice.
