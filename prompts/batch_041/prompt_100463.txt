I'm building a small anthology of poems about logic puzzles for a reading group, and I need one on the MU-puzzle — the MI → MU thing from GEB with the four rewriting rules — together with the "jootsing" idea that goes with it: jumping out of the system, i.e. the move you need can't be derived from inside the rules, you only get it by standing back and taking in the whole system at once, and from in there it feels like a violation.

Heres what I want. A poem, about 24 lines, in five numbered stanzas of four lines each plus a short unnumbered coda of three or four lines. Loose rhyme or none at all, your call, but each stanza has to end with a refrain that is a literal string of M's, I's and U's — MI, MIIU, MIIIU, that sort of thing — set off on its own line or tacked onto the end of the last line.

The constraint: each stanza's string has to be reachable from the previous stanza's string by exactly one of the four MIU rules, and I want you to tag each stanza in brackets with which rule you used, like [rule 3: xIIIy → xUy]. Nothing else about the poem is constrained — it can be about being stuck inside a rulebook, about the wall you hit, whatever you like — but the strings have to actually check out, so that someone who knows the puzzle can verify each step and confirm the body of the poem never breaks its own invariant. And it must not break it: whatever the number of I's in the refrain is, it must never be divisible by three, and I'd like the running count noted somewhere, maybe as a little figure at the end of each stanza.

The last thing, and this is the bit that matters: the poem has to try to arrive at MU and fail. It should get as close as it can, show the reader the wall rather than just announcing that one exists — let the invariant do the work — and then, in the coda, joot. So the final refrain is allowed to be MU even though no sequence of the four rules gets you there, but only if the coda openly admits that it just stepped outside, and the last line says plainly what the jump was and why it couldn't have been earned from in there. I dont want it to read like a cheat or a riddle solved — the whole point is the difference between proving something inside a system and understanding the system from outside it.

After the poem give me one section called "Why the form had to break", about 200 words, telling me which rules you actually applied in which order, why that particular route was the one that puts the wall in front of the reader, and what exactly the coda does that the rules forbid.

British English please (colour, realise, that sort of thing).
