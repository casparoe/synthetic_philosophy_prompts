I teach moral and political philosophy at a mid-sized state university, and for the past couple of years I've been having a running argument with my partner, who manages programs for an international development NGO. She's been posted to a country office where most of the local partner organizations she oversees are, frankly, family businesses: a board member's nephew holds the vehicle-maintenance contract, the logistics officer hired her own cousin, the district coordinator put his brother-in-law on the payroll as a 'community liaison' with no real duties. Her agency's auditors flagged three of these as procurement fraud and two people were fired. Nobody local denies any of it. Their line is: of course you take care of your people, that's what the money is for; and they point out that her own agency hired the regional director's old roommate into a senior post last spring without so much as a posting. There's also a rule in her staff handbook requiring her to declare any gift over about fifty dollars, which nobody has ever complied with, because the gifts that actually get given here are at weddings and funerals and are handed over in front of two hundred people.

My colleague in anthropology, who has done fieldwork in the region, tells me I'm asking the wrong question. She says 'corruption' is a travellers' word — it names other people's obligations while leaving our own networks unexamined — and that the famous line between a gift and a bribe is drawn by trivia: who hands it over, whether the envelope changes hands before or after the favor, whether there's a receipt. She also says the ideal of the impartial, personally disinterested official isn't a moral truth but a fairly recent piece of state-building machinery, and that once you take that away there's no fact of the matter left about whether those two people should have been fired. I'm definately sympathetic to about 70% of this, and the other 30% is where I get stuck, because I can hear how the same move could be made about things I'm not willing to give up.

So here's what I want from you. Tell me whether my partner was right to think the firings were defensible, and don't just tell me 'context matters.' I want the actual philosophical structure: what work the word 'corruption' is doing, whether there's a defensible non-parochial standard that would condemn the nephew's contract while letting the regional director's roommate slide (or whether they're really the same act, in which case she should be consistent about it either way), and what survives of the anthropologist's argument once you notice how much it proves. If the honest answer is that there's no neutral ground here, say that too, and tell me what it implies for how she should actually do her job — she still has to sign off on the audit findings.
