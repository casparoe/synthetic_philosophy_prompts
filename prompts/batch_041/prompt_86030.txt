I'm putting together a graduate seminar on decision theory, and I want to pin down where I actually stand on a claim that I think is under-argued. The puzzle is the absent-minded driver. A driver must pass through two intersections; at each she may exit or continue. Exiting at the first pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, and passing both without exiting pays 1. Her memory is wiped after each intersection, so at the moment of decision she cannot tell whether she is at the first intersection or the second; the two decision points are indistinguishable to her. The familiar evidential treatment has her continue with probability 2/3 at each point and exit with probability 1/3, for an expected payoff of 4/3. The familiar complaint is that a causal treatment cannot return that recommendation: analyzing each decision point on its own, the causal reasoner says continue at the first intersection (since continuing leads to the second, where exiting pays 4) and exit at the second (4 beats 1), but an agent who cannot tell the two points apart cannot carry out both recommendations, and if she is restricted to a single unconditional action she can do no better than 1 (always continue) or 0 (always exit).

The claim I want a credence in: that the puzzle is not evidence for evidential decision theory over causal decision theory, but is really a puzzle about act-individuation. Because the two decision points are indistinguishable to the agent, "continue at the first" and "continue at the second" are not two acts but one act under two descriptions. Individuate acts by the agent's epistemic situation, and a causal analysis of the resulting single act — the policy of continuing with probability 2/3 — yields the 4/3 recommendation, so the causal/evidential dispute never gets joined.

Give me your credence in that claim, decomposed. At minimum, price these: (a) that "one act under two descriptions" is the right description of the case rather than a stipulation that assumes what it needs; (b) that a causal analysis of the coarse-grained act yields 2/3 rather than quietly conditioning on the agent's own future behavior, which is evidential reasoning under another name; (c) that choosing a probability is itself an act a causal theory can evaluate, rather than a reformulation that changes the subject; (d) that the coarse-individuation move does not also deliver evidential verdicts in the cases that are supposed to separate the theories (predictor problems, the toxin puzzle), in which case the conciliatory reading collapses back into evidentialism by another route; (e) that anything is left of the puzzle afterward — for instance, whether a correct theory must make the randomization rational or may merely permit it. Add or drop sub-questions as the arithmetic requires.

My own credence is about 0.65 and a colleague's is about 0.15. Tell me which sub-question that disagreement most likely lives in, which sub-question your number is most sensitive to, and what single argument or example would move you most.
