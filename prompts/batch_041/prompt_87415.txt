A position I've drifted into and would like stress-tested rather than defended: that experiential, or at least proto-experiential, properties are among the fundamental features of the world, and that if so, the class of things whose states could matter morally extends well past creatures with nervous systems — plausibly down to whatever the fundamental constituents of physics turn out to be, so that the destruction or rearrangement of physical structure is in principle a morally loaded act. Give me the case against it, as a map of its exposure rather than a survey of the literature. Which arguments, observations, or formal results count against it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? Which would a defender have to concede as a genuine cost, and which would they be entitled to dismiss as question-begging? What, if anything, does the position predict that its rivals don't? And once it has been qualified in the usual ways, does it retain any risk at all, or has it become unfalsifiable by construction — and if the latter, is that a defect in a view of this kind or simply the price of doing metaphysics?

Two places I suspect the real work lies, and where I'd like you to press hardest. First, the slide from "there is something it is like to be an X" to "X has a welfare that can be set back": is that inference independently motivated, or is it smuggled in? What would have to be true of a micro-subject for it to have interests at all — valence, goals, a temporally extended standpoint, something that can go wrong for it? Second, the internal pressure: if micro-experiences don't compose into unified subjects, does the view make the ethical situation better or dramatically worse, and is there a non-question-begging way to tell? What would move me most is an argument that the ethical upshot is not merely speculative but incoherent — that there is no possible fact of the matter about whether we have helped or harmed anything at the fundamental level.
