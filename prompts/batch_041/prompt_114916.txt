I sit on my university's animal ethics committee, and over the past year our remit has quietly expanded from mice and zebrafish to whatever anyone happens to be working on — black soldier fly larvae in a waste-to-feed pilot, a shrimp disease study, a lab growing cultured neural tissue for a drug screen, and one memorable application involving an engineered yeast that a student argued might suffer. We are not philosophers, we meet once a month, and each novel case gets about twenty minutes, so what we need out of this is a procedure rather than a seminar. I have drafted a rough scoring scheme of my own and I suspect it is broken but I cannot see where. It scores an entity on four axes: whether it has internal states that function to track its own bodily condition; whether those states are integrated with other information rather than wired straight to a fixed response; whether its behaviour shows genuine trade-offs, in the sense that it will tolerate damage or pain to pursue something else; and whether what it undergoes can matter to it over time, shaping what it seeks later. The axes pull apart and I have no principled way to aggregate them. Worse, when I tried the scheme on calibration cases, a household thermostat and a colony of chemotaxing bacteria scored respectably on the first two axes while a scallop scored near zero, which either shows that my axes are the wrong ones or that I am asking the wrong question. And I cannot shake the suspicion that treating sentience as a matter of degree has smuggled in an assumption I do not actually hold — that moral standing comes in the same degrees, so that a halfway-sentient creature gets halfway-standing.

What I want from you is a written decision procedure, one page plus a worked appendix, that my committee could actually run on a submitted protocol. It should be a sequence of questions in the order we should ask them, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure returns a verdict, and the points at which it stops and hands the matter to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. It must terminate in one of four outcomes — exempt, light-touch (the researcher proceeds under conditions we set), full committee review, or escalation to the university's research governance office — and it has to be candid about which steps are really just my four axes in disguise and which of them should be discarded outright. Then run it, showing your work at each step, on three cases: the black soldier fly larvae reared and killed in bulk for the waste-processing trial, where I suspect the procedure will tell us we have to review a facility the entomology department has run for years without ever filing a protocol; a study on cultured human neural tissue that has begun showing spontaneous activity; and the thermostat, which I am using as a floor test, to see whether the procedure refuses moral status to anything with a feedback loop. Tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do, and whether it can survive being used by a committee that includes two members who think the whole exercise is sentimental nonsense — if it cannot, I would rather know that before I put it in front of them. Positions and arguments can be referred to in your own words; do not bother with references, I will source those myself.
