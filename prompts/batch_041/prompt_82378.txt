I've been going round in circles about whether two things can be exactly alike, so here are my gut verdicts on some cases and I'd like the principle behind them, if there is one. Two iron spheres, same size, same mass, two miles apart, nothing else in the universe: I say there are two spheres, not one. Two identical twins: obviously two people, and the only thing doing any work there is that one is here and one is there, which doesn't feel like cheating to me at all. But when I say the same thing about the spheres -- that their positions are what make them two -- it suddenly does feel like cheating, and I can't say why. A perfectly uniform grey patch that I mentally divide down the middle: not two things, I made the halves up. Two things that are exactly alike and in exactly the same place at the same time: thats one thing, not two, I can't even get a grip on the alternative. A single uniform sphere alone in an empty universe: one thing, and I don't think the fact that the whole world is symmetric under reflection through its centre gives me any reason to say there are two, or none. And the one I keep going back and forth on: slide that entire two-sphere universe two miles to the left and I want to say it's the same universe described differently, not a different universe -- though I'm far less sure of that than of the rest. So is there a principle that gets all of this, or close? State it precisely, tell me what it says about the shifted universe, and be honest about which of my verdicts it can't save. Also say what would change your mind: which consideration would push you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to be wrong. Answer in American English.
