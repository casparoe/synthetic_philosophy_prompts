You are a philosopher of social science and a metaphysician of social kinds, with expertise in the ontology of classification and in cohort analysis. I want you to help me think through a puzzle about a practice that has become common in government, human resources, and marketing: the generational equity audit. A city, for example, now requires every proposed housing, transit, or tax policy to include a generational impact statement. The statement must estimate how the policy will affect Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z, and must flag any policy that imposes disproportionate burdens on one generation. The categories are treated as constituencies with interests, not merely as age brackets. Yet the agencies that produce these statements do not agree on the birth-year boundaries; some shift the cutoff by a year or two, and some add a Gen Alpha category. The same person can be classified differently depending on which agency’s form is used. Meanwhile, the audits sometimes attribute a political or economic outlook to a generation as a whole—saying that Gen Z values stability or that Boomers resist density—and sometimes treat the category as a mere label for people born in a window. I am not asking whether the policy is wise. I want to know what philosophical commitments the practice presupposes.

Please start by brainstorming the candidate ontological and explanatory views that could underlie such a practice: for example, generational realism, social-kind realism, nominalism about generations, generational consciousness, methodological individualism, and the view that generations are useful fictions or administrative categories. Then write a polished analysis in American English. In it:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of generations that the generational equity audit presupposes, if it presupposes one. What must be true of generations for the audit to make sense as more than a rough proxy for age?
2. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Could a nominalist or a methodological individualist run the same audit without inconsistency? Could a realist about generations run it while denying that generations have interests or causal powers?
3. Explain the tension between treating generations as real kinds and treating their boundaries as revisable, inconsistent across agencies, and partly stipulated. Does the practice presuppose that the boundaries are discovered or that they are constructed? Can it be both?
4. Say how the practice relates to the age-period-cohort identification problem. If the audit attributes an effect to generation, what must it assume about period effects and age effects? What would change if we replaced generational categories with continuous birth-year or age variables?
5. Tell me what would have to change in the practice if we adopted each of the rival views you identified. For each, say whether the audit would survive, need revision, or collapse into something else.
6. End with your best judgment: is the generational equity audit philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories license it, or does it commit its users to a substantive ontology of generations? Defend your answer.

Do not provide citations, bibliographies, or reading lists; reason from the concepts themselves. Avoid jargon where plain language will do, but do not simplify away the philosophical distinctions. Write in American English.
