You are acting as a philosopher of law with genuine expertise in two places at once: Anglo-American contract doctrine and the decision-theoretic literature on cooperation without causal contact. I want a piece of careful conceptual translation, not two survey essays stapled together. Before you translate anything, spend a few sentences setting out, in your own words, (a) what contract formation actually requires and (b) what the acausal-trade position actually claims, and say where you expect the mapping to strain. Then do the translation. Work slowly, and keep your attention on the joints rather than on the summary.

My background, so you can calibrate: I am a transactional attorney. I am at home with offer, acceptance, consideration as bargained-for exchange, the objective theory of assent, the ordinary rule that silence is not acceptance, promissory estoppel, anticipatory repudiation, specific performance, third-party beneficiary doctrine, the peppercorn theory of consideration, unconscionability, and the line between a binding contract and an unenforceable agreement to agree. I have essentially no background in decision theory and do not want to acquire one; I want my own vocabulary used properly.

Here is the position I want restated. Its defenders hold that two agents which can neither communicate nor causally affect one another — systems in causally disconnected regions, or two processes that will never exchange a signal — can nonetheless be said to trade. The mechanism offered is this: each agent selects the policy that maximizes expected utility conditional on the other's predicted policy, and the two policies are correlated, either because both agents implement the same decision rule or because a shared prior over decision procedures makes each one's choice evidence about the other's. My cooperation does not causally benefit you; it is logically entangled with, or raises the probability of, yours. The further claim is that both parties end up better off than under the no-trade baseline, and that this constitutes a genuine exchange rather than a fortunate coincidence of preferences.

Your task is to restate that position as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, without softening it into an analogy and without smuggling decision-theoretic commitments in under contract-law names. Go element by element and mark each one as translating cleanly, translating only with distortion (naming the distortion), or having no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. The residue report is the centerpiece. I especially want to know: what becomes of offer and acceptance when nothing is ever communicated, and whether the objective theory of assent can be satisfied by a prediction rather than a signal; whether correlation between the parties' policies can be made to do the work of consideration, and whether a bargained-for exchange can be bargained for by inference; what breach and remedy could mean when neither party can withhold performance or be made to pay; and whether the acausal trader has earned the word 'trade' or is equivocating between exchange and mutual prediction. Take the asymmetric variant as well — one party vastly more capable, for whom the cooperative act is a rounding error and for whom it is everything — and tell me whether the peppercorn theory, unconscionability, or the doctrine of consideration has anything to say about it.

Then report the residue running the other way: notions my vocabulary treats as essential that have no counterpart on their side. Reliance, unforeseen contingencies, the assignment of risk, the possibility of a party who is absent rather than merely silent — say which of these the acausal-trade picture cannot express, and whether the gap matters.

Close with a verdict on the residue itself. Is what fails to translate a defect of contract law — a body of doctrine built for a world of communication, reliance, and enforcement, and merely parochial about exchange without them — or is it evidence that the acausal-trade claim is less determinate than it sounds, because 'trade' is doing rhetorical work that a colder vocabulary would refuse to license? If the latter, name the specific claim that has to be weakened for the rest to survive.
