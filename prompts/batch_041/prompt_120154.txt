So my friend Maya and I just burned two and a half hours on this and now I actually have to write it up, so congrats, you're the tiebreaker. I sit on our state's ballot review board — we're the ones who rule on whether a measure is legally fit to go to the voters, and whatever we decide has to be out before the printers start. What's in front of us is Proposition 9, which is a bundle. Half of it is stuff nobody in their right mind votes against: caps insulin copays, free school lunches, that kind of thing. The other half hands the redistricting maps back to the legislature and guts the independent commission, which polls around 35% on its own and would definately lose as a standalone question. Glued together it might actually win, and the drafters obviously knew that. They also slipped in that if any single piece ever gets struck down, the whole thing is void, and that it can only be amended by a two-thirds vote — so if voters wanted to fix one part later they'd basically have to start from scratch.

We've got three options: certify it as one yes/no question, split it into two questions that get voted seperately, or reject it — and rejecting it needs a legal hook, and the only hook we have is the single-subject rule, which is exactly the fight we're having.

Maya thinks splitting is paternalistic garbage. Voters are adults, they can read, and if you start splitting things you walk into her favorite objection: vote on the pieces separately and you can end up with a set of results that no majority of voters actually endorsed — a majority of people can be against a majority of the things that pass, because each piece has its own different majority behind it. So "just let them vote on each piece" isn't obviously the neutral option either. She also half-jokingly said that if we're so scared of majorities rolling over people, we should require that anything passed by initiative also win a majority of counties, and I said that's a minority veto in a costume, and she said so is your single-subject rule.
