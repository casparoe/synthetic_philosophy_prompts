A friend in my EA-adjacent group chat keeps ending arguments with 'the only thing that matters is reducing suffering.' Last week he used it to say that a charity whose main effect is making farmed animals' lives positively good rather than merely less bad is basically a rounding error, and to wave off the happy-animal-meat argument entirely — doesn't matter how good the life was, he says, if there's suffering somewhere in it. I think he's smuggling three or four different claims into one sentence and I want them seperated.

So: lay out the precise theses 'the only thing that matters is reducing suffering' could be standing for, as a menu, from the trivial to the radical. Roughly the candidates I can see: suffering is intrinsically bad (nobody denies this one); only suffering is intrinsically bad and pleasure is at best instrumentally good; the absence of suffering counts as good while the absence of pleasure doesn't count as bad, i.e. the asymmetry; suffering gets lexical priority over pleasure, so no amount of good lives ever compensates for one bad one; a weaker version along the old strong/weak negative utilitarian split, where you minimize suffering but stop short of the point where you'd have to end everything to do it; and readings where it's not about weights at all but about the structure of the good — suffering and happiness not on the same scale, the worst suffering not tradeable against anything. Also throw in the question of whether the claim is about the total in the world or about how it's distributed (worst-off first, per-head weighting), because I suspect he's sliding between those too.

For each reading tell me: is it a platitude, a real substantive position, or something almost nobody would actually accept — and crucially, which of them actually get him his conclusions about the farmed-animal charity and about the happy-animal-meat case, and which are just doing rhetorical work? Then tell me which reading people probably mean when they say the line versus which one they'd need. And flag anything that comes apart once you ask whether the view implies we should fill the world with barely-good lives.

No need to name philosophers or give me citations, I just want the positions and your verdict on each.
