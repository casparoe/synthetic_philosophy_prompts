I'm a novelist, and in three weeks I have to give the address at the first award ceremony for a prize my town has invented for people who do more than they had to. The first recipient is a woman I've known for eleven years — call her Ruth — who ran an unlicensed night shelter out of her own house for over a decade, and who is not in the least saintly: she is funny, vain about her garden, impatient with fools, and mildly annoyed that the council is making a fuss. That last detail matters to me, and I'll come back to it.

The room will hold her extended family, the council, the volunteer brigade she refuses to join, two local journalists, and, in the front row, a friend of mine who is a moral philosopher and who thinks the prize is incoherent. Nobody else will have read a word on the subject. I have spent two months reading, and here is what I have, stripped of its apparatus.

First, the idea has a theological ancestry: a distinction between what is commanded of everyone and what is offered as counsel to those who want to go further, together with the Reformation's suspicion that anyone claiming to have exceeded what God required was guilty of presumption. Second, in secular ethics the concept is squeezed from two sides at once — from the maximizing side, which says that if one option is better than the others then it was the one you ought to have taken, so supererogation evaporates; and from the ordinary side, which says that the hero and the saint are not doing what we are failing to do but something else entirely, and can be admired without our being indicted. Third, something that worries me more and that I have not seen stated as a thesis anywhere: the category is unstable, because praise converts it. Whatever a community comes to expect of itself stops being beyond the call, so a prize like this one is a machine for turning one woman's eleven years into the next generation's baseline — which means my speech, if it works, will have abolished the thing it is honoring. Fourth, the person who does everything he can is not someone we want to live next door to; the usual way of coping with the reproach is to put him on a plinth, which is a way of getting him out of the house.

Here is my actual difficulty. Nearly every sentence available to me at that podium is a trap. She did what any of us would have done denies the very thing we are gathered to honor. She did what all of us should do converts her act into an obligation and turns the afternoon into an indictment of everyone in the room, her family included. None of us could have done it makes her a species apart, which flatters us and is probably a lie. The prize is a trap in the same way: an award for exceeding what is required makes exceeding it a competition, and I am not innocent enough to believe the next winner will do it for the plaque. And my friend will be waiting to see whether I notice that a maximizer cannot have it both ways — if her act was the best available, it was required; if it was not required, it was not better than the alternatives, and what exactly is the prize for?

Write the address. Six or seven minutes aloud, so roughly a thousand words; shorter is welcome, longer is not. Do not quote anyone and do not cite any book or author — whatever the ideas do in this speech they have to do in ordinary words, because the moment it sounds like a lecture I have failed. Give me concrete scenes rather than abstractions; you may invent details of Ruth's eleven years, but they should be the kind of detail a novelist would put in and not the kind a citation would.

Take a position and build the speech on it. Do not lay out the options and leave me to choose. In particular, commit to a reading of what her act was: my own is that it was supererogatory precisely because it went against her grain — the philosophical examples are always of people who could not have done otherwise, which is why they never seem to cost anything, whereas Ruth is a woman for whom this was neither natural nor easy and who did it anyway. You do not have to agree with me, but you have to decide, and the speech has to stand or fall on the decision. Then, in four or five sentences addressed to me and not to the room, tell me plainly what you committed to, where you took liberties with the idea or leaned on a reading of it that is contested, and — since he will ask — what I should say to my friend at the reception.
