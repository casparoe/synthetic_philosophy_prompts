I am a doctoral student in political theory, and I am putting together a session for a reading group on the closing chapters of The Prince — the stretch where Machiavelli turns to fortune and to the quality he calls virtù. Rather than run the usual exegetical conversation, I decided to make the session concrete by building a small coding scheme. I have taken about twenty historical episodes (Cesare Borgia's campaigns in the Romagna, the collapse of the Florentine republic in 1512, Pope Julius II's move on Bologna, and so on) and I want to assign each of them two numbers: the share of the outcome attributable to fortune and the share attributable to virtù, with the two constrained to sum to one hundred per cent. My anchor is the sentence in chapter twenty-five where Machiavelli writes that "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less", which I read as a quantitative claim about a reasonably stable proportion, licensing an expected value of roughly fifty per cent fortune across a large sample so that individual episodes can be ranked as above or below average in their dependence on luck. I am treating virtù as competence or effectiveness — the prince's skill, energy, and capacity to act — as distinct from moral virtue, which I take it is not what the term means here, and fortune as the residue of chance that no amount of preparation can remove.

Two coding problems have defeated me. The first is the leader whose own fixed temperament is what destroys him: the impetuous man who is ruined when the times turn cautious. His nature is inside him, so the share looks like virtù; but his nature is also precisely what he cannot alter, so the share looks like fortune, and I cannot tell which way the ledger should fall. The second is other people's deliberate choices: when a prince's designs are wrecked by a pope's election or an ally's defection, the proximate cause is a human decision, which is the sort of thing virtù is supposed to master, and yet the prince had no control over it, which is the sort of thing fortune is supposed to name. My provisional fix was to count as fortune anything the prince could not have prevented at reasonable cost, but that makes every coding depend on a counterfactual I cannot observe. I have also noticed that the river-and-dikes image seems to make the two axes dependent rather than independent — fortune's share appears to shrink as virtù's preparation grows — and if they are dependent in that way, I do not see how summing them to a hundred per cent can be right, though I cannot see what else to do either. For what it is worth, a colleague in the political science department told me that leadership scholars do something similar when they try to separate skill from luck in executive performance, so I assumed the conceptual groundwork was already settled and that I merely needed to fix my rules.
