I am writing a novel whose protagonist is a philosopher of cosmology who has convinced herself that the Boltzmann-brain argument refutes itself, and I want to know whether a careful reasoner would agree with her. Give me your own credence — not a survey of the literature — that the following is true: if an eternally expanding de Sitter universe makes isolated, self-aware fluctuations vastly more numerous than ordinary observers, then the cosmological reasoning that supports this conclusion is itself overwhelmingly likely to be the product of such a fluctuation, and the argument therefore cannot rationally raise our credence that we are Boltzmann brains. Decompose that credence into its parts — that such a cosmology obtains; that the measure problem should be resolved by counting observer-moments uniformly rather than by a measure that suppresses brief fluctuations; that a Boltzmann brain's apparent physics carries no evidential weight; and that this reflexive collapse is a genuine defeater rather than a disguised prior — give a rough probability for each, show how they combine, and name the term your number is most sensitive to. Then give me your credence that this very reply of yours is a Boltzmann brain's, and tell me whether the two figures are consistent. Take a definite position and defend it, and write in British English.
