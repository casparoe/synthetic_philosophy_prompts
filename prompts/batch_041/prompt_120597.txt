Our instructor records her verdicts on every case in two columns -- one for aesthetic value (how good the thing is to look at) and one for artistic value (how good a work of art it is) -- and she insists that the two columns move independently. Her verdicts so far:

- A painting made in the 1930s in the manner of a seventeenth-century master, sold as a rediscovered masterwork, exhibited for four decades, and exposed when the forger's papers came to light: aesthetic value unchanged by the exposure; artistic value close to zero.
- A modern replica of a famous canvas, painted by a museum's own technician in period materials and openly labeled a replica: aesthetic value equal to the original; artistic value zero.
- The authentic painting by that same old master, half its surface destroyed in a fire and left unreconstructed: aesthetic value much reduced; artistic value undiminished.
- A version of one of his compositions executed in his workshop by an assistant, from his design but not by his hand: aesthetic value equal; artistic value zero.
- A fresh impression struck by the estate of a twentieth-century painter from her own copper plate, decades after her death: aesthetic value equal to her lifetime impressions; artistic value zero.
- A group of paintings that the same twentieth-century painter made at the very end of her life, reworking motifs from her early work in a loose late manner, all by her own hand: aesthetic value below the early versions; artistic value full.

Next week's case is the following. The twentieth-century painter's single most famous early canvas is the centrepiece of a museum's collection.
