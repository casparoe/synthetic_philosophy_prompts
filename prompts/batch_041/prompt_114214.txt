I'm in a weird cross-listed seminar (half philosophy, half African studies) and two people in it got into an arguement last week that we're all still litigating over coffee. The reading was about ubuntu and relational personhood — the idea that you don't come into the world a person, you become one through your relations with others, so personhood is something you attain, and someone can grow into it or fail to. Callum said it's a lovely idea and a trap: if the community is what makes you a person, then the community decides who counts, and anyone it refuses to recognize isn't just disliked, they're not fully a person — which is the exact shape of the worst abuses, and it also means the framework has nothing to say to a government that claims to speak for the community against an individual. Nadia said he's conflating two claims: one about what a person *is* (that our capacities are formed in relations, that there's no self sitting around prior to them) and one about what a person ought to be (that we should be oriented toward others). She said the first is metaphysics, the second is ethics, and the exclusion worry only gets off the ground if you slide them together; on the achievement reading, failing to be a person is failing a standard, not losing your standing. Callum said that's the slide itself, not a fix — if you're a person to the extent that you meet the standard, you've still got degrees of personhood, and if the community sets the standard, the dissenters and the awkward are the ones who get graded down. Nadia said the standard doesn't have to be the community's; it can be a general ethical standard the community is answerable to, which is how the community can be wrong about who counts. Callum said fine, but then the relational part isn't doing any work — if the standard can be stated without mentioning relations at all, you're a liberal with extra steps. Nadia said no, because the capacities you'd need in order to meet any such standard are themselves developed through relations, so relation is built into how you become a moral agent in the first place, not just into what you're told to do.

Nobody could settle it and I'd genuinely like a ruling.
