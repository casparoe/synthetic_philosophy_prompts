I am in a graduate seminar on anthropic reasoning and I have to write a short comparative essay for it; I would rather think the thing through with you than have you rehearse the standard positions. The comparison I have to draw is between two rules for reasoning about your own place among observers. The first says that you should reason as though you had been drawn at random from the set of all observers who actually do or will exist, so that the size of a world enters your reasoning only through your rank within it: finding yourself, say, the sixty-billionth human is a less remarkable coincidence if the total number of observers is small than if it is enormous, and the rule therefore tends to favour hypotheses that imply fewer observers. The second says that you should reason as though you had been drawn at random from the set of all observers who could possibly have existed, so that the mere fact of your existing is itself evidence: a hypothesis under which a trillion people exist is, on that account alone, a trillion times more likely to be the one you find yourself in than a hypothesis under which a single person does, and the rule therefore tends to favour vast worlds over cramped ones. I have watched the first do the work in the doomsday argument and the second invoked to defuse it, and I have watched the second made to look absurd by the case of a philosopher who, on the strength of nothing but the arithmetic of observer-counts, becomes nearly certain that a huge cosmos rather than a tiny one is real. What I want from you is a genuine comparison rather than a survey of who said what: what each rule is really a principle about (a sampling procedure, a principle of indifference, a licence to conditionalize on your own existence), what each buys that the other cannot, and whether they are true rivals that return conflicting verdicts on the same question or two answers to different questions that have been forced into a competition they were never meant to enter.

To keep the comparison honest, please test both rules against a case of my own devising that my seminar could not settle. A demon proposes an experiment: it will flip a fair coin, and on heads it will create a single room containing a single occupant while on tails it will create a trillion rooms each containing a single occupant, and it will then remove from every occupant any knowledge of which way the coin fell. Before doing any of this, however, the demon flips a second, independent fair coin, and only if that one lands heads does it carry out the experiment at all; if it lands tails, nothing and no one is ever created. You are an occupant and you know the entire set-up. Tell me what each rule implies about the first coin, whether the bare possibility that no one at all might have existed changes either verdict, and whether either rule can even be stated without quietly presupposing a reference class of exactly the kind it is supposed to help you choose. Finally, take seriously the objection that the second rule is not a separate assumption at all but merely the first one with an inflated reference class, and tell me whether that reduction succeeds or whether something essential is lost in it.
