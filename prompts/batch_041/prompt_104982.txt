I'm drafting a short piece on Nozick's treatment of animals and I want you to tear it apart before I show it to anyone. Take the role of someone who's taught Anarchy, State, and Utopia at the graduate level for years and knows the libertarian rights literature cold — I need a real philosopher's eye on this, not a summary. And please actually think before you write; I'd rather have four sharp objections than twelve shallow ones.

Here's the gist of my draft. Nozick's whole apparatus of side-constraints is built to rule out the aggregative logic of utilitarianism among persons: persons are to be treated as ends and not merely as means, so we can't sacrifice one for the greater good. But then he turns around and, in whats really a passing remark, more or less floats a split view — aggregative welfarism for animals, Kantian constraint for people. My claim is that this isn't just an oversight or an offhand concession. It falls out of the structure. Side-constraints on his account are grounded in something like the capacity to be a rational agent who can shape a life in light of a conception of it, and animals don't have that. So the moral community gets divided at the joint of rational agency, and animals land on the outside, where the only constraint left is a welfarist one.

The interesting part is that I think this is unstable in a few ways. First, the very argument Nozick uses to rule out interpersonal aggregation — that it fails to take seriously the separateness of persons — seems to have a sentience-shaped analogue: if what's doing the work is that each subject has a life that can go better or worse from the inside, then a cow's life also goes better or worse from the inside, and the separateness point doesn't obviously stop at the species line. Second, if animals are outside the rights framework entirely, then his own minimal state has nothing to say about them, and a lot of what libertarians want to say about cruelty to animals — that it's a rights violation, not just a bad outcome — becomes unavailable. Third, I suspect there's a quiet circularity: rational agency is doing double duty as both the ground of inviolability and the criterion of membership in the community of the inviolable, and the argument needs those two to come apart. Fourth, he never seems to ask what a Nozickian entitlement theory would even look like with animals included — could they hold claims at all, or is the whole apparatus of acquisition and transfer just silent about them?

I should say I'm not leaning on any particular passage or page here. I'm reconstructing the shape of the view, not attributing exact sentences. If you think the reconstruction distorts something he actually says, say so, and say how.
