The World Anti-Doping Agency's therapeutic use exemption system allows athletes to use otherwise prohibited substances when they are medically necessary to treat a diagnosed condition, on the understanding that the treatment restores the athlete to a normal state of health rather than enhancing performance beyond it. Yet the same system permits athletes to sleep in altitude tents, to train in hypoxic chambers, to take creatine and caffeine, and to undergo surgery and rehabilitation that may leave them stronger than before; it also permits athletes who carry rare genetic variants that raise hemoglobin or muscle mass to compete without penalty. What philosophical commitments does this pattern presuppose about the distinction between therapy and enhancement, about the nature of health and disease, and about fairness in sport? Reconstruct the implicit theory. In particular, say what conception of a normal baseline the exemption system requires, whether that baseline is statistical, functional, or normative, and whether it is the athlete's own untreated state or a population norm. Then identify which rival views the practice rules out: for example, that the relevant question is whether an intervention is safe, or whether it is available to all, or whether it respects the athlete's autonomy, rather than whether it is therapeutic. Consider whether the practice is internally consistent, given that it treats some interventions that raise a trait above the athlete's natural level as legitimate—altitude training, surgery, nutritional supplementation—while treating others that do the same as cheating—erythropoietin, anabolic steroids, gene editing. Is the distinction between restoring and enhancing doing the real work, or is the practice better explained by a different commitment, such as a rule-based conception of sport as a contest of natural talent plus effort, or a luck-egalitarian view about which advantages should be compensable? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several theories of health, fairness, or the meaning of sport would license the same rules. Finally, tell me what would have to change in anti-doping practice if we replaced the therapy/enhancement distinction with a straightforwardly safety-based or availability-based criterion, and whether the resulting practice would still be recognizably sport.
