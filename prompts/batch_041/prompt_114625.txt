I'm leading one fifty-minute session of a cross-disciplinary reading group — half economists who have never opened an early-modern philosopher, half philosophers who have never thought about monetary policy — on Berkeley's writings on money from the 1730s. There is no pre-reading and no handout, so the whole view has to be assembled in the room. Don't explain it to me; give me the teaching plan.

The substance, in my own words, so that you are not reconstructing it from memory: for Berkeley money is a ticket or a counter, useful so far as it stirs up and circulates industry rather than because of what it is made of; the true source of wealth lies in the elements and in human labour worked upon them, with gold and silver serving only as instruments for moving that wealth about; less money circulating quickly does the work of more money circulating slowly; and if the denominations are retained, trade could go on "although the bullion were gone." He states the core claim as a query — whether "the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter" — and pairs it with proposals for a national bank and a circulating credit. He also denies that general terms need abstract ideas to answer to, which is why there is no essence of money to be discovered, only uses to be compared.

What I need from you:

- The order in which the components must go in, and what depends on what — including whether this audience needs any of his theory of meaning at all, and if so where it can be slipped in rather than announced.
- The one carrying example to build the session on, and what it must have and lack. My instinct is cigarettes in a prisoner-of-war camp, or the stone money of Yap; I suspect both are traps, and I want to be told which, and why.
- The misconceptions this particular mix of people will bring, in the order they will surface, and exactly where in the sequence I head each one off. My guess is that the big one is "then money is imaginary and prices are groundless," with a secondary "this is just modern credit theory in older spelling." Tell me the ones I have not anticipated.
- The single closing question that would show me whether the idea landed.
- What I should cut, said out loud as cut. The candidates I have in mind are the pamphlet war over the Irish copper coinage, the detail of his bank proposal, his later metaphysical work, and the whole question of whether he counts as a forerunner of modern monetary thought.

Assume I cannot hand out texts or cite anything, and do not recommend readings. Under 700 words: open with one sentence giving the order in a single breath, then the plan.
