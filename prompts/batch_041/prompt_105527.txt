I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I run a reading group on cognition for colleagues who are also engineers rather than philosophers. We have spent several sessions arguing about the thesis, associated with Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander, that analogy is the core of cognition, and we keep talking past one another because we do not agree on what the word "analogy" is meant to cover or on what kind of claim "core" is. Please build us a decision tree that takes a reader from a starting commitment to a named position on that thesis.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of famous names: first the questions that genuinely divide the positions, then the positions that the resulting combinations of answers carve out. Every branch node must be a question a reader can answer without already knowing which position it leads to, phrased in terms of concrete cases or plain commitments rather than labels.

For our purposes, the distinctions that matter are these. Scope: does "analogy" cover only deliberate cross-domain mappings, or does it also cover ordinary categorization and perception? Dependence: does analogy operate on concepts that already exist, or is the analogical process what constitutes the concepts in the first place? Automaticity: is analogical processing automatic and pervasive, or a deliberate, effortful process reserved for hard problems? Unification: does a single mechanism underlie all analogy, or does the word name a heterogeneous collection of processes? Foundational status: is analogy the core to which other capacities reduce, one capacity among several, or itself derivative of something more basic? Finally, treat the question "is analogy the core of cognition?" as having both a mechanism reading and a redescription reading, and make sure the tree can tell apart a position that takes analogy to be an implementable mechanism from one that thinks the thesis only redescribes cognition in analogical vocabulary.

I need the following positions to appear as leaves, though you may add others you think belong: the view that analogy is ubiquitous, automatic, and constitutive of concepts, so that everything from seeing to reasoning is analogy; the view that analogy is a distinct, deliberate, high-level faculty that maps between pre-existing representations and is important but not foundational; the view that what we call analogy is derivative of similarity and association and is no special faculty; the view that cognition is a toolbox in which analogy is one tool among deduction, induction, and simulation, with no primacy among them; and the view that analogy is best understood as compression or prediction, so that the real core is compressibility and analogy is a symptom of it.

Use each of these cases at least once, as a branch node or an illustration: a barista who reaches for a customer's usual order on sight; a programmer who recognizes that a new bug is the same as a bug seen in an unrelated codebase; a child who calls a zebra a horse; a jazz musician who quotes a lick from one tune inside another; and someone seeing a face in a cloud. Also include a case in which a language model completes an analogy item correctly, with the branch question being whether that settles anything about the thesis.

Please deliver at most seven branch questions and between six and nine leaves; for each leaf give the position's name (or "unnamed") and one sentence on its most awkward commitment; and add a short note on which combinations of answers are unoccupied, saying for each whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If any two positions cannot be separated by your questions, say so explicitly rather than forcing a distinction. Keep it to about a page, in plain language, with no citations or reading lists.
