I write on moral responsibility, and I have been trying to locate my own view of standing to blame from the inside out — from verdicts on cases rather than from a favored theory. What I want from you is the principle, or the smallest family of principles, that captures the pattern in the verdicts below, stated precisely enough that I can test it against new cases; and, where no principle can save every verdict, an honest account of which verdict I should surrender and why. I would rather have a principle that saves all of my verdicts at the cost of an unlovely disjunction than a clean principle that tramples two of them — but tell me plainly if the clean principle is the one I ought to want.

I should flag at the outset that I keep four things apart that are often run together: (i) the standing to resent, or to take up a blaming attitude privately; (ii) the standing to address the wrongdoer, to demand an answer or an apology; (iii) the standing to condemn publicly; and (iv) the standing to sanction or punish. In several of the cases my verdicts come apart across these modes, and I treat that as data rather than noise.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A senior colleague who routinely cuts methodological corners publicly accuses a junior colleague of doing the same. I hold that the junior colleague may rightly disregard the accusation, and that the senior colleague lacks the standing to address or publicly condemn — though the charge may be true, and others may take it up. Private criticism, I think, is still available to him.

2. A passerby with no relation to either party loudly condemns a man for being a cold and distant father. My verdict: he oversteps — not because of hypocrisy, since he may be a devoted father, but because the matter is not his business. Yet I also think that if the conduct were cruelty rather than coldness, the passerby would have standing to protest, since some wrongs are anyone's to condemn.

3. A woman who was a neglectful parent twenty years ago, and who has genuinely changed, blames her neighbor for neglecting his child. My verdict: full standing, in every mode. Change, not a clean record, is what matters.

4. The same woman, now reformed in conduct but still insisting she did nothing wrong and having made no amends to her own children, blames the neighbor. My verdict: her standing is impaired. She retains the standing to hold the belief and to lament, and perhaps to address the neighbor, but not to condemn or demand.

5. A state with a documented recent record of torture condemns another state for torture. My verdict: it lacks the standing to lead or to sanction, and its condemnation is compromised; but this does nothing to weaken the case against the other state, and third parties should act regardless.

6. A man who betrayed his own brother years ago now blames that same brother for betraying a third party. My verdict: he has no standing to blame — and I feel this more strongly, and for what seems a different reason, than in case 1. Here the hypocrisy is not merely that he has done the like; it is that he wronged the very person he now proposes to call to account.

7. A man who betrayed a business partner years ago, and never made it right, blames a stranger for betraying a third party. My verdict: full standing — even though, by the measure of case 6, he is just as much a hypocrite, having done the same kind of thing and left it unrepaired.

8. A close friend of a woman who has been wronged blames the wrongdoer. My verdict: full standing to blame, third-party blame being perfectly in order. But if the friend were to announce that he forgives the wrongdoer, that would be out of order — forgiveness is the wronged party's to give. So the standing to blame and the standing to forgive come apart, and in opposite directions from the third-party case.

What I cannot get to sit still is the pair formed by 6 and 7. If hypocrisy is a defect in the blamer's own record, then the identity of the wronged party should be irrelevant, and 6 and 7 should come out alike; if the identity of the wronged party matters, then I need an account of why, and that account must not quietly turn standing into something relational all the way down and so wreck case 3. I am also unsure how much of cases 3 and 4 is doing independent work, or whether 4 is just case 1 under another description.

Please give me the principle or principles, stated precisely, with the case that motivates each clause. Say what the account predicts for cases I have not listed — construct two or three of your own that would separate the rival principles — and tell me honestly which of my eight verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I should revise. Close by stating how confident you are in the account you have offered, and singling out the single point in it of which you are least sure.
