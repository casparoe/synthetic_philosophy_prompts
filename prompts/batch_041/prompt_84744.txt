I'm in a grad seminar that's been grinding through Scanlon's contractualism and I keep going back and forth on the aggregation thing. Here's the shape of it as I understand it, tell me if I've got it wrong. The view says an action is wrong when it's ruled out by any set of principles that nobody could reasonably reject, and the standard is supposed to be individual: when you compare candidate principles you look at the strongest objection any single person could raise, not at the total pile of objections. You don't get to say well, this principle upsets a million people slightly so it's out. That's the whole separateness of persons thing, and it's meant to be the selling point over utilitarianism. But then the problem, which I think is the real one: it looks like contractualism can't justify the everyday stuff that obviously needs aggregating. My go-to example is a rule that lets hospitals run a drug trial where a thousand participants each face a tiny chance of a bad side effect so a huge future population gets a cure, where no single participant has a super serious complaint but the numbers still seem to be doing the work. Or even simpler, a speed limit that shaves a tiny bit off everyone's convenience and saves a bunch of lives. A friend in the seminar says this shows contractualism is dead on arrival as a moral theory. Another friend thinks there are patches, like a tie-breaker when complaints are equally strong, or a threshold below which complaints just don't count, or letting the numbers in only when every individual complaint is trivial. I honestly cant tell whether those patches are principled or just utilitarianism in a trench coat.

Write me an essay on whether contractualism can accommodate aggregation without giving up what made it attractive in the first place. Lay out the strongest version of the problem and the best responses you can, and then dont punt, reach a verdict and defend it. If you think it survives, say what the fix actually costs and why it isn't a betrayal of the core idea; if you think it can't, say which premise has to go. Bonus if you tell me what the whole aggregation fight reveals about whether the separateness of persons is even a coherent thing to care about, or whether it's doing any work once you press on it. I don't need citations or a reading list, just the argument.
