You are a philosopher working in bioethics and the philosophy of disability, with a strong background in population ethics and the metaphysics of harm. I am preparing a position statement for a workshop and I want an adversarial consistency audit of my views before I present them. Please think carefully and work through the package slowly rather than reacting to it impressionistically. Before you commit to a verdict, brainstorm: generate the candidate tensions you can see, including ones that may turn out to be merely apparent, and only then decide which are genuine. Do not flag every pair as a tension, and do not hedge by calling everything "in some tension." I want a discriminating audit, not a list. I do not need citations or references; engage with the substance.

Here is my position as I would state it:

1. I accept a principle of procreative beneficence, but in a deliberately weak form: prospective parents have a genuine reason — a reason, not an obligation — to select, among the children they could have, the one whose life can be expected to go best.

2. I hold the social model of disability. The disadvantage that attaches to impairment is produced mostly by social arrangements — inaccessible environments, stigma, exclusion, the organization of work — rather than by impairment as such. In the same spirit, I think having a disability is a mere difference rather than a bad difference: it does not by itself make a life go worse.

3. I am committed to not treating the products of injustice as natural constraints. Where a disadvantage exists only because of an unjust arrangement, I do not want my practical reasoning to take that disadvantage as a fixed fact about what kind of child to have, since reasoning that way is part of what keeps the arrangement in place.

4. I accept a person-affecting view of harm: an action harms someone only if it makes that person worse off than they otherwise would have been, and no one is made worse off by the fact that a different possible child was brought into existence instead of them. I also think that the wrongness of an action in this domain tracks the harm it does.

5. I hold a strong commitment to reproductive autonomy: no one should be coerced or pressured about these choices, and the state should stay out of them.

6. A commitment that may or may not be load-bearing: the expected welfare of a child with a given condition depends substantially on how many other people have it. Accommodation, community, and familiarity are functions of prevalence, so the disadvantage associated with a condition tends to shrink as the condition becomes more common.

7. And a verdict I keep arriving at: I think a parent who chooses to implant an embryo that will be deaf, when a hearing embryo is available, is doing something wrong — though I do not think it should be illegal, and I do not think such parents should be pressured out of the choice.

What I want from you:

First, a one-paragraph restatement of the package in your own words, to show that you have it.

Second, the audit. Identify the genuine tensions. For each, say which commitments are pulling against each other, explain the mechanism of the conflict rather than just asserting that two views are opposed, rank the tensions by severity, and give the cheapest repair together with an honest statement of what that repair costs. Where a tension is merely apparent, say so explicitly and explain what makes it dissolve.

Third, tell me which single revision would resolve the most, and whether that revision is one I should actually make or whether it would cost me something I care about more than consistency.

Finally, flag any commitment that is doing no work in the package, or that you suspect I hold mainly because of the company I keep.

Write in American English, and do not soften the audit to be polite. If the position is incoherent, say where.
