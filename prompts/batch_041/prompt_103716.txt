right, I'm putting together my views on rational choice for a decision theory reading group and I want a sanity check before I stick my neck out. here's roughly where I've landed:

(1) I'm a Bayesian expected-utility maximiser: the rational thing to do is maximise the expected value of a utility function over final outcomes, and beliefs should be your probabilities.

(2) I genuinely have the Allais preferences. In the first pair I'd take the certain £1m over the gamble that gives 89% chance of £1m, 10% chance of £5m and 1% chance of nothing. In the second pair — 11% chance of £1m versus 89% chance of nothing, against 10% chance of £5m versus 90% chance of nothing — I'd take the £5m gamble. (I think these two are what people call the common consequence and common ratio effects, if the labels matter.)

(3) I think the independence axiom, or sure-thing principle, is a genuine requirement of rationality: if two options give the same outcome in some event, that shared part shouldn't affect which one you prefer.

(4) My defence of the first choice is diminishing marginal utility of money — the first million is worth far more to me than each of the next four, so being risk-averse over gains is perfectly rational.

(5) I'm a consequentialist about choice in this sense: only the probability distribution over final outcomes should matter. How a decision is framed, dressed up, or split into stages shouldn't change what it's rational to do.

(6) I trust my considered judgements about particular cases over any axiom that tells me I'm being incoherent. The axioms are meant to codify good choice, not to overrule it.
