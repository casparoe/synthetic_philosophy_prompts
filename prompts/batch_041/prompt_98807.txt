I'm in a graduate metaphysics seminar that has just finished a unit on the necessity of origins — the thesis, usually credited to Kripke, that a person's origin is essential to her: that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg, and that any individual differing in origin would have been a different individual. I understand the contemporary arguments well enough, but the assignment is to write a short paper reconstructing how a pre-Kripkean metaphysician would have received the thesis, and I've chosen Leibniz. So imagine putting the question to him directly: here is a child who in fact arose from one particular sperm and one particular egg — or, in the contemporary version, from one embryo among several created by IVF and selected for implantation. Could that child have come from a different sperm and egg? Could a different embryo have developed into that very person? I want you to predict his answer and, more than that, his reasoning, and to be explicit throughout about where you are extrapolating from his known commitments rather than reporting something he actually said.

Two things I'd especially like you to press on. First, would his verdict coincide with the modern one, or would it arrive there by such a different route that the agreement is superficial? My suspicion — and I'd like you to test it — is that a Leibnizian metaphysics of individual substances and possible worlds makes the result come out more strongly than the Kripkean one: that he would hold far more to be essential to you than merely your biological origin, and that this threatens the modern claim that origin is specially or distinctively essential. Second, I'd like your view on whether he would regard the question as well-posed at all, or whether he would say the modern debate is trading on a notion of 'the same individual across possibilities' that his framework either sharpens into something unrecognizable or dissolves altogether. Please argue from his actual machinery — complete individual concepts, possible worlds, the identity of indiscernibles — rather than gesturing at 'rationalism' in general, and flag any point at which the prediction is genuinely underdetermined by what he wrote.
