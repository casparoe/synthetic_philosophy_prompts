Explain the nonidentity problem, and be precise about which premise of the ordinary harm-based account of wrongdoing it pressures: the assumption that an act can be wrong only if it makes some particular person worse off than that person would otherwise have been. Then consider a pair of cases. In the first, a woman may conceive a child now, knowing that the child will have a moderate but livable impairment, or take a medication for a month and conceive a different, unimpaired child; she conceives now. In the second, she may conceive now and have an impaired child, or wait a month and conceive the very same child — same egg, same sperm — who will then be unimpaired; again she conceives now. Explain why most people judge the second choice clearly wrong while hesitating over the first, and whether that difference survives scrutiny. Finally, say whether the problem is best read as showing that the counterfactual comparative account of harm is false, that the person-affecting restriction is false, or only that our intuitions in different-people cases are unreliable, and say whether a revised, less strict version of the restriction — one on which an act can be wrong for how it affects people in the aggregate even though no one is made worse off — escapes the problem without collapsing into a purely impersonal view. Defend the reading you favor. Please answer in American English.
