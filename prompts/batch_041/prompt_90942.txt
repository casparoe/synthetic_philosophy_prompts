I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of mind — my main project is on panpsychism and the combination problem — and I've ben reading around in the history of the idea for a side chapter. I keep stalling on a passage in William James's Principles of Psychology (1890) and I'd love some help actually understanding it. Here's the bit:

"The reader who found himself swamped with too much metaphysics in the last chapter will have a still worse time of it in this one, which is exclusively metaphysical. Metaphysics means nothing but an unusually obstinate effort to think clearly. ... One of the obscurest of the assumptions of which I speak is the assumption that our mental states are composite in structure, made up of smaller states conjoined. This hypothesis has outward advantages which make it almost irresistibly attractive to the intellect, and yet it is inwardly quite unintelligible. Of its unintelligibility, however, half the writers on psychology seem unaware. ... The theory of 'mind-stuff' is the theory that our mental states are compounds, expressed in its most radical form."

And then a little later in the same chapter:

"But with the dawn of consciousness an entirely new nature seems to slip in, something whereof the potency was not given in the mere outward atoms of the original chaos."

Three things I'm stuck on:

1. When James calls the "composite" hypothesis "inwardly quite unintelligible," how strong is that claim supposed to be? Is it a hard incoherence claim — the thing can't even be coherently conceived — or the softer claim that we just can't see how the compounding would work, i.e. that it's inexplicable? I have a hunch he's running some kind of dilemma against the composition view, but I can't tell exactly what the dilemma is, or whether it's aimed at the composition of consciousness as such versus one particular version of it.

2. What is the "mind-stuff" theory he names at the end? Is that the same as what we'd now call panpsychism / micro-experientialism, or is it a distinct (stronger or weaker) view? I've also run into the phrase "mind-dust" and I honestly don't know whether that's James's own label, a later commentator's coinage, or something else again.

3. How, if at all, does his framing here map onto the contemporary combination problem? Is his worry the same as the worry that micro-subjects couldn't add up into one subject, or is it closer to the qualitative/structural versions of it? And is the "dawn of consciousness" discontinuity in the second quote actually part of the combination issue, or is it really an evolution/emergence worry that I'm conflating with it?

No reading lists or citations needed — I just want the ideas laid out in plain terms.
