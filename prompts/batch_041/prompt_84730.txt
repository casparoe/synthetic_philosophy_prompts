You are a philosopher of science who works on the demarcation problem, with a specialization in the epistemology of history and archaeology. I am going to give you a set of verdicts of mine about what is and is not pseudoscience, and I want you to help me find the principle behind them — reflective equilibrium done on request. Two constraints on your answer. First, no reading lists, no citations, no bibliography; I want your reasoning, applied to my cases. If you want to invoke a familiar position — Popperian falsifiability, Lakatosian research programmes, Kuhn, Laudan's argument that the demarcation problem should be abandoned — state it in your own words and show me what work it does on a specific case of mine; if it does no work, leave it out. Second, do not paper over mismatches with a disjunctive clause that saves every verdict by fiat. If a simple principle forces me to give up one of my verdicts, say which one and argue for the sacrifice.

Please work in stages, and show the stages. Begin by brainstorming: lay my cases out in a table of features — who is making the claim, what kind of training they have, what observation would count against them, what they do when it is contradicted, whether they claim the authority of a discipline, whether they publish their data and methods, and how the surrounding community responds. Then look for the pattern, then test candidate principles against the cases, then give me your verdict. Think carefully and at length; a short answer that quietly drops the awkward cases is worse than a long one.

Here are my verdicts.

1. The Rebuilt Chronology. A working mathematician applies statistical tests to the surviving corpus of ancient narrative texts, finds what he takes to be suspicious regularities, and concludes that most of what we call antiquity is a duplicate of the Middle Ages — that the standard chronology was manufactured by early modern chronologers and that the manuscripts were reworked to fit it. The argument is presented as a mathematical result about texts. My verdict: pseudoscience, and pseudohistory specifically. I notice, though, that the thesis is in principle testable, and has in fact been tested and come out badly.

2. The Phantom Centuries. Someone claims that a stretch of the early Middle Ages never happened and that its most famous emperor is a fiction, arguing from gaps and contradictions in the documentary record. Dendrochronology, eclipse records, coinage, and Chinese and Arabic sources all tell against it. The reply is that all of those were produced or adjusted by the same fabricators. My verdict: pseudoscience.

3. The Rearranged Planets. A mid-century writer claims that the orbits of the planets shifted within human history and that ancient records preserve the catastrophe. The mechanics are impossible and are shown to be impossible. His followers reply that the testimony of the ancients outweighs the calculations, and that the rejection is sociological. My verdict: pseudoscience, even though I cannot point to one decisive test that the defenders themselves accept.

4. The Ancient Astronauts. The claim that human civilization was seeded or repeatedly visited by extraterrestrials, argued from iconography and from alleged technological anachronisms. My verdict: pseudoscience. When I ask what would count against it, I never get a specific observation; I get a redescription of the evidence I already gave.

5. The Machine. A hobbyist with no historical training fine-tunes a language model on a corpus of ancient texts, finds that the resulting embeddings cluster in a way that, he says, proves the texts are later forgeries, posts his code and data to a blog, calls the method a new science of history, and declines to say what clustering result would have disconfirmed him. My verdict: pseudoscience. This one makes me uneasy, because his error may be nothing worse than statistical naivety, and naivety is not the same as fraud.

6. The Untestable Physics. A research program in fundamental physics whose critics say it cannot be tested within our lifetimes, but which produces rigorous mathematics, trains students, openly concedes that it currently makes no contact with experiment, and proposes what sorts of evidence would tell against it. My verdict: plainly science, not pseudoscience. This is in tension with 1 through 5.

7. The Contamination Loop. A group that does genuine fieldwork, genuine radiocarbon dating, and publishes in its own journal, whose editorial board is drawn from its own members, and whose central claim about a lost civilization is protected by a standing rule that any anomalous date indicates contamination of the sample. My verdict: pseudoscience, even though procedurally they do everything a scientific community does.

8. The Honest Maverick. A linguist argues that a famous corpus is the work of three authors rather than one, on stylometric grounds. The field rejects her conclusion. She publishes her code, answers every objection in public, states what evidence would change her mind, and when a colleague finds a coding error she corrects it and revises her numbers. My verdict: not pseudoscience, though she is probably wrong and definitely outside the consensus.

9. The Vindicated Outsider. The historical case where a minority view is mocked for decades and then absorbed into the mainstream once the evidence catches up. My verdict: not pseudoscience, and the establishment's bad treatment of it is not retroactively a mark against it.

10. The Two Vehicles. The same revisionist thesis about antiquity, once argued by the mathematician in case 1 and once argued by a credentialed historian with careful philology and footnotes, who is simply wrong. My verdict: pseudoscience in the first case, ordinary error in the second. This makes me worry that my verdicts are tracking people and rhetoric rather than claims.

11. The Disclaiming Hobbyist. This is the one I keep going back and forth on. Someone propagates roughly the picture in case 1, but frames it explicitly as a reading, an imaginative reconstruction, not an empirical claim about the past, and does not claim to be doing history at all. His audience nonetheless treats him as an authority on what actually happened, and he never corrects them. Half of me says: speculative fiction, badly labeled, not pseudoscience. The other half says his disclaimer is doing exactly the work that the contamination rule does in case 7, and the effect on his audience is the same.

My tentative principle, which I would like you to test and repair rather than accept: a claim is pseudoscientific when it is presented as empirical knowledge about the world while the community maintaining it operates a repair mechanism that guarantees no observation could count against it, and the repair mechanism is not itself offered as a testable hypothesis. Tell me whether that gets 1 through 5 and 7 right, whether it wrongly condemns 6, 8, and 9, and what it does with 10 and 11.

Then give me the following. The principle, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that someone could apply them to a new case and get a determinate answer, with the clause that each case motivates named. An honest statement of which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would rather overturn the verdict or complicate the principle. Your predictions for at least two cases I have not listed — please invent them so that they are genuinely diagnostic, one that your principle classifies as pseudoscience and that I might have called mere error, and one the reverse. And finally a conceptual question that I think my cases are circling: is pseudoscience a property of claims, of practices, or of people, and is it a matter of degree or all-or-nothing? If the answer is that the label attaches mostly to communities and their habits of evidence-handling, say so plainly, even if it means my verdicts in 1 and 10 cannot both stand.
