Ok, something about the church-and-state stuff has been bugging me and I can't get a straight answer. In Germany the state collects your church tax for you -- it's a surcharge on your income tax, your employer withholds it from your paycheck, and the government itself keeps the database of who belongs to which church. If you want out, you have to file a formal declaration with a government office. And people still call Germany a secular state. Meanwhile in the US the government can't collect a dime for any church, but churches pay no property or income tax, my donations to one are deductible, and we've got a religious motto on the money and presidents sworn in on a Bible. A friend insists America is the truly secular one and Germany is the fake, and I think thats backwards in some ways and right in others.

What I actually want is the philosophy, not the history tour. What does each setup presuppose about what separation of church and state even means, and about what religion is? The German one seems to treat religious membership as a public legal status you formally enter and exit -- closer to citizenship than to a hobby -- which suggests the state thinks religion is a public thing it has a legitimate hand in. The American one treats religion as private and off-limits, except it's definately not off-limits: it's exempted and subsidized and woven into civic ritual. Is either committed to a coherent theory of neutrality, or is each just a pile of leftovers -- an establishment that never got dismantled vs. a disestablishment that never reached the tax code? Which parts are real philosophical commitments and which are just inertia? And here's the part I can't shake: if religion is just one preference among many, why does it get tax breaks the chess club doesn't -- does that presuppose religion is specially valuable, or specially fragile, and is either one actually compatible with neutrality? Give me the theory each system needs, versus the theory it says it has.
