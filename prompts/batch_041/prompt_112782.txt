I am a teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate course titled "Knowledge in the Age of Artificial Intelligence." The midterm exam, which I wrote with the instructor, included the following question: "Dr. Elara Voss, a visiting philosopher of mind, recently proposed the 'No Free Understanding' thesis. According to this thesis, any belief that is formed by relying on an external cognitive artifact—such as an AI assistant, a calculator, or a search engine—cannot count as understanding, even if the belief is true, justified, and reliably produced. Dr. Voss argues that understanding requires the subject to grasp the explanatory connections among the relevant propositions, and that this grasping must be the subject's own cognitive achievement, not merely the output of a tool. Evaluate Dr. Voss's thesis. In your answer, distinguish between knowledge and understanding, and consider whether the thesis is defensible. Does reliance on an external cognitive artifact necessarily preclude understanding?"

One student's answer, reproduced verbatim, reads as follows: "Dr. Voss's thesis is intriguing but ultimately too strong. To evaluate it, we must first distinguish knowledge from understanding. Knowledge is typically defined as justified true belief, though Gettier cases show that this definition is incomplete. Understanding, by contrast, is not merely a collection of true beliefs; it involves grasping the relations of dependence and explanation among those beliefs. For example, I can know that the Pythagorean theorem is true, but I understand it only when I see why the squares of the legs sum to the square of the hypotenuse. Given this distinction, Dr. Voss is right that merely receiving a correct answer from an AI does not automatically give one understanding. If I ask an AI for the proof of the Pythagorean theorem and copy it down without following the steps, I have knowledge (assuming the AI is reliable) but not understanding. However, Dr. Voss's thesis goes further: it claims that any belief formed by relying on an external cognitive artifact cannot count as understanding. This is too strong. Suppose I use an AI to check my proof of the Pythagorean theorem. I work through the proof myself, grasp the connections, and then use the AI to verify that I haven't made a mistake. Here my understanding is my own cognitive achievement; the AI is just a tool for error correction. Dr. Voss might reply that the belief is still partly formed by the artifact, but that seems to conflate the context of discovery with the context of justification. The artifact helps me justify my belief, but it does not replace my grasping of the explanatory relations. Furthermore, Dr. Voss's thesis seems to be a form of skepticism about testimony. Since testimony is a legitimate source of knowledge, and we often rely on others for our beliefs, it would be odd to say that reliance on an AI is always incompatible with understanding. If I can understand a proof that I learned from a textbook, why can't I understand a proof that I learned from an AI? The source of the belief does not determine whether I understand it; what matters is whether I can grasp the connections myself. That said, Dr. Voss has a point that reliance on AI can erode understanding if it becomes a habit of outsourcing all cognitive work. But that is a psychological or educational claim, not a conceptual one. The thesis as stated is false because it rules out cases where the artifact is integrated into the subject's own cognitive process. A more defensible thesis would be that understanding requires the subject to be able to reconstruct the relevant explanatory connections without the artifact, at least in principle. But that is not what Dr. Voss says. In conclusion, Dr. Voss's thesis is too strong. Reliance on an external cognitive artifact does not necessarily preclude understanding. The thesis should be rejected in its current form."
