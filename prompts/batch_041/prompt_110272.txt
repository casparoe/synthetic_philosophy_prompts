Quick setup in case it helps: expressivists say a sentence like lying is wrong doesn't describe a fact, it expresses a non-cognitive attitude (disapproval, roughly). Frege-Geach points out the same sentence shows up unasserted inside compounds — if lying is wrong then getting your kid to lie is wrong — and inside valid arguments like modus ponens, so its meaning cant just be the attitude you happen to have when you assert it, on pain of equivocation. Then there's the nastier sub-problem I've seen called the negation problem: grant the expressivist a compositional semantic value for the sentence; what does lying is not wrong express? The mere absence of disapproval? A distinct attitude of tolerance or approval? If the value of the sentence is an attitude, negation has to do something determinate to it, and it's not obvious there's a principled answer that isnt just smuggling descriptive content back in.

My vocabulary, since I mostly think in PL: pure values vs effects, evaluation contexts, thunks and forcing, denotational vs operational semantics, monads / algebraic effects, referential transparency, quoting vs evaluating. My hunch is content = the pure value, force or assertion = an effect that fires when you run the thing, an embedded occurrence = a subterm whose effect doesn't fire, and expressivism = the claim that the value of a moral sentence is itself an effectful computation.
