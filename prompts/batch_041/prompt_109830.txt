I am enrolled in a graduate seminar on the history of decision theory, and for my final paper I want to write a speculative chapter on how John Maynard Keynes would have received a contemporary family of decision theories that travel under the name risk-weighted expected utility. The distinctive move in these theories is to deny that the value of a risky prospect is simply the probability-weighted sum of the utilities of its possible outcomes. Instead, the outcomes available to an agent are first ranked from worst to best, and her attitude toward risk is represented by a separate function — a risk function — that reweights the cumulative probabilities of reaching each successive rank; a risk-averse agent is one whose risk function is convex, so that worse outcomes loom larger in her deliberation than their objective probabilities would warrant. The point of the manoeuvre is to make room for the thought that aversion to risk is a basic and rationally permissible attitude in its own right, on a par with the agent's utility function, rather than a mere artefact of diminishing marginal utility; and to secure that room, the framework abandons the independence axiom — the sure-thing principle — that the older expected-utility tradition treats as constitutive of practical rationality. Choices of the Allais type, on this view, need not be mistakes.

What I want from you is not a survey of the secondary literature but a reasoned prediction, in roughly eight hundred words, of Keynes's verdict. Keynes built his philosophical reputation on a theory of probability as a logical relation of partial implication between evidence and hypothesis, on which many probabilities cannot be assigned numerical values at all, and on which a probability has, over and above its degree, a weight: the balance of relevant evidence and relevant ignorance standing behind it. I keep oscillating between two readings of how he would react, and I would like you to adjudicate between them. On the first, the risk function is a late and formal vindication of the notion of weight, since both make the agent's response to a probability depend on something other than the probability itself. On the second, it is a travesty of that notion, since weight is a matter of how much evidence one has, whereas the risk function is a free psychological parameter hung on the agent rather than on her evidence. Please come down firmly on one side rather than hedging, defend your reading with attention to the technical details of the framework — the rank-dependent weighting, the treatment of risk attitude as an independent primitive, the abandonment of independence — and say what, if anything, Keynes would have wanted to salvage from it. You need not cite anything; I care only about the argument.
