I'm preparing a handout for a reading group on free will and moral responsibility, and I keep stalling on the position one of our members defends with tiresome consistency. His claim is that no one is ever morally responsible for anything in the deepest sense, because responsibility of that kind would require a person to be responsible for the very traits of character that produced her action; being responsible for those would require her to be responsible for whatever produced them; and so on without end, until the chain runs off the edge of her biography, since she cannot have authored herself out of nothing. I am told the position has a lineage and a name, but the label is not what I need. What I need is a case, and the cases people usually reach for strike me as question-begging: they either install somebody else doing the manipulating, or they quietly assume that the universe is deterministic, and in either case the audience's verdict is carried by the intrusion rather than by the regress itself.

So please construct a single thought experiment, written as one continuous narrative of a few hundred words: one person, one action, no science fiction, no brainwashing or neuro-intervention, no external intervener of any kind, and no clinical condition used as a device. The case has to satisfy four conditions at once. First, every condition a thoughtful compatibilist would place on ordinary responsibility must be met in the details of the story rather than by assertion — she wants to do what she does, she endorses the desires that move her rather than feeling alienated from them, she would have acted differently had there been reason to, she is neither coerced nor deceived nor ignorant of what she is doing, and the action is an expression of her character rather than an uncharacteristic lapse, so that nobody can deflect the regress by saying the deed was not really hers. Second, the story must be neutral about determinism: write it so that a determinist and an indeterminist could tell the same events without altering a word, and then show explicitly why the regress survives the indeterminism, both when the indeterminism falls at the moment of decision and when it falls at an earlier moment of inner struggle that shapes the character she later acts from. Third, the narrative must contain at least one episode in which she deliberately works on her own character — resolving to become more patient, or less afraid, or more generous — so that a defender has something to point to when he says she is responsible for who she is; I want to see whether the regress simply restarts at that episode or whether anything in it blocks the restart. Fourth, the action should be of middling moral weight, and the case must not lean on its being wrong: if the same structure could be run on a small act of kindness with equal force, say so and sketch how.

After the narrative, work through the following in order. Lay out the regress step by step as it applies to this person and this action, saying at each step what would have to be true for the chain to terminate. Identify the one feature of the case that is doing the work, and say whether removing it — for instance, by making the episode of self-fashioning the product of an earlier adult choice of hers, with no outside influence at all — collapses the case into ordinary compatibilism or merely moves the bump one step further back. State which premise of the argument the case makes vivid and which premise a defender must reject if the case is to be defused. Then give the strongest reply available to someone who thinks the argument proves too much, namely that it shows only that a person cannot be the ultimate author of herself, which no compatibilist ever claimed, and say what the defender of the argument should concede if that reply fails. Finally, tell me honestly whether the case does any work that a bare statement of the three steps would not have done, or whether I have merely dressed up the same steps in a biography.
