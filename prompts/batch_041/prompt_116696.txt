I have no academic training — I studied accounting, and I now run a small repertory cinema — but for about two years I have been keeping a notebook on a question that keeps embarrassing me, and I would like help deciding where to spend my attention on it. The question is why I pay money to be made miserable. I can account for the easy cases: suspense in a thriller, information in a documentary, the relief at the end of a hard story. The ones that bother me are the ones where the sadness itself seems to be the point. I have sat through a film about a marriage coming apart four times and come away each time grateful for a grief that has nothing to do with my own life; I have also watched a documentary about a famine that happened a century before I was born, and enjoyed it, which troubles me more than the fiction does; and meanwhile the same story told badly leaves me cold. Everyone I raise this with reaches for the same three or four moves — that it is catharsis, that I am really enjoying the craft, that the pain is only make-believe, that I am enjoying the relief of its being over — and none of them has ever explained the badly-made-film case to me, or why I feel cheated rather than moved when I can tell a film is engineering my tears. My own guess, which I would like tested rather than flattered, is that two separate things are doing the work: first, that being moved at all is pleasant as evidence that I am not numb, and second, that what separates the good film from the bad one is that the sadness feels earned, or fitting, and that what I enjoy is the fittingness rather than the sadness. I can already see that these two guesses may be pulling in different directions, and that the famine documentary sits awkwardly with both.

I have roughly a year of evenings, a film club of about a dozen people who will humour me, and no intention of writing anything academic. If I want to finish the year with a real grip on this problem instead of a pile of half-read essays, which open questions in this area should I spend the year on, and in what order? I am asking for a prioritisation rather than a reading list, so please do not send me to titles or authors — I will not chase them, and I would rather have your reasoning than your references. For each question, I want to know why it is upstream of the others — what gets easier, or simply dissolves, once it is settled — how far a determined amateur with no training and no laboratory can actually push it, and what would move it up or down my list. I would also like to know which of the moves I keep running into are, in your view, dead ends I should stop spending evenings on, and why they keep surviving anyway. Finally, I want an honest verdict on my two guesses: whether they are live positions, restatements of something older that I have simply not read, or whether they collapse under a case I have not thought of.

Please structure your answer as follows. 'Summary': one short paragraph restating what I am asking for and the target you are aiming at, so I can check that you have understood it. 'The questions, ranked': the main body, working down my list in order, with a paragraph or two on each explaining its position, the criteria you are using, and the trade-offs between those criteria. 'Dead ends': a short section, a paragraph or two, on what I should stop spending evenings on. 'Where my guesses stand': about a page, taking each of my two guesses in turn and saying what would have to be true for it to work. 'What would change the ranking': a brief closing section on what I would have to learn or observe for the order to shift.
