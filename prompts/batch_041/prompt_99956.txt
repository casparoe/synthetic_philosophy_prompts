Student's answer: Strawson's main distinction is between the participant attitude and the objective attitude. When we take up the participant attitude toward someone, we're engaged with them in an interpersonal relationship, and this involves what Strawson calls the reactive attitudes — resentment when they wrong us, gratitude when they help us, and also things like guilt, indignation, and forgiveness. These attitudes are responses to the quality of the other person's will toward us. The objective attitude, by contrast, is the stance we take when we view a person as something to be managed, treated, or studied rather than as a participant in a relationship; Strawson's examples are the very young, the mentally ill, and people suffering from severe mental derangement. In that mode we might feel pity or mere curiosity, but we don't resent the person in the ordinary way. Strawson's point is that the reactive attitudes aren't optional extras we could just drop — they're woven into the fabric of ordinary human life, so any theory that told us to abandon them would be asking us to do something we can't really do. Now the pessimist says that if determinism is true then people aren't really responsible, so our practices of blaming and resenting are unjustified. Strawson agrees with the pessimist that determinism, if true, would make resentment irrational, but he argues we can't help having these attitudes anyway, since they're so deeply rooted in our psychology. So his conclusion is that determinism must be false, because we clearly do hold each other responsible and we can't give that up. The optimist, by contrast, thinks we can keep blaming people because it's useful for social control, and Strawson thinks that's a cold and inadequate way to defend our practices.
