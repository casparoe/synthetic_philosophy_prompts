I have spent the last decade alternating between two beats, criminal justice and insurance, and the combination has left me with a question I cannot settle on my own. When I hear the retributive case for capital punishment made — the claim that a killer has forfeited something and that a proportionate death is what justice owes, quite apart from whether it frightens anyone off — I notice that I am involuntarily translating it into the idiom of my other beat: underwriting, loss reserving, expected severity, tail risk, the machinery by which a carrier prices an event it devoutly hopes never to pay out on. The translation comes out disturbingly fluent. That fluency is what worries me.

So I would like you to do it deliberately. Take the three strands of the death-penalty debate as they are actually argued — retribution, general deterrence, and the acknowledged risk of executing someone innocent — and restate each of them, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of insurance and risk management. I am reasonably fluent in that vocabulary: I have sat through enough rate filings to handle moral hazard, adverse selection, experience rating, correlated risk, aggregate versus per-occurrence limits, the ex ante/ex post distinction, frequency versus severity, and the practice of excluding a class of loss from a policy rather than pricing it. I am not an actuary, so keep the arithmetic out of it and stay at the level of concepts.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which claims come across cleanly, without loss? Where does the translation require distortion, and what kind of distortion? What has no counterpart in the target vocabulary at all? And, finally, the judgment I cannot make for myself: where something fails to survive, is that a structural defect of the insurance vocabulary — something it is constitutionally unable to say — or is it evidence that the original claim was doing less work than it sounded like it was?

A few things I would particularly like you to take up, though not as a checklist:

Desert. Does the notion that a punishment is owed, rather than merely useful, have any counterpart in the vocabulary of a premium, a reserve, or a schedule of rates? Or does the target vocabulary contain only prices and never debts?

Moral hazard names the case in which the insured, knowing he is covered, behaves worse. The retributive case seems to want something close to the reverse: a person made to bear a loss precisely because he caused it. Is that the same concept inverted, a different concept entirely, or an incoherence that the insurance vocabulary is right to have no room for?

The executed innocent. A carrier can price a wrongful payout and hold a reserve against it. But can the framework represent a loss that cannot be indemnified — where no payment restores the position of the party who bore it? I am interested in whether the honest answer is that such a loss is not expensive but uninsurable, and therefore not a number in the model at all but an exclusion.

Standpoint. The underwriter is deliberately indifferent to which policyholder ends up bearing a given loss; that indifference is what makes pooling possible. Is that indifference a technical convenience or a substantive moral commitment? And does the retributive case deny it?

Proportionality. Does it survive as anything more than a rate table — a mapping from offence to sanction that could in principle be rescaled without changing what is asserted?

Close by telling me which of the three strands is most damaged in transit, and whether I should conclude that the retributive case is a genuine claim that my second vocabulary simply cannot hear, or a claim that sounds more substantial in the language of moral desert than it does when stated in the plainest terms available.

One ground rule: do not let the actuarial idiom quietly import a consequentialist premise and then present the result as a faithful translation. If the target vocabulary forces an assumption that the retributivist would reject, say where it forces it.

No citations, bibliography, or reading list, please — I want the argument, not the sources.
