I am preparing a graduate seminar that reads Nyāya epistemology alongside contemporary reliabilism and the epistemology of testimony, and for the opening session I want a verdict rather than a survey. Take the four sources of knowledge that Nyāya recognises — perception, inference, comparison or analogy, and verbal testimony — and rank them, in descending order, by how much evidential weight a careful knower ought to give to a single unconfirmed deliverance of each: one isolated perceptual episode, one inferential step, one accepted comparison, one report from a speaker of unknown reliability. Give me a number for each, expressed as a share of a fixed total, and justify each placement by naming the epistemic property that is doing the work — causal contact with the object, the reliability of the process, the availability of independent checks, or the presence or absence of defeaters. Do not average the tradition's rhetoric into a tie; if you think two of them come out equal, say so and say why that is not a cop-out.

Then rank the same four a second time under a different background assumption: that a cognition is valid unless and until something undermines it, so that its validity is not established by any further means, such as the success of the action it prompts. I take the tradition to label that position svataḥ-prāmāṇya and the first, externally certified position parataḥ-prāmāṇya, but I would rather you correct my labels than defer to them. Say which ranking Nyāya itself would endorse, which ranking the schools holding the rival view would endorse, and, more interesting to me, whether the ordering of the four actually changes between the two rankings or whether only the weight of each shifts. If the ordering is stable, explain what that shows about the relation between a source's reliability and the separate question of how its validity is known.

The tradition's list of candidate sources is longer than Nyāya's four. Postulation or implication, and non-apprehension — the means by which one is supposedly able to know an absence — are admitted as independent sources by some schools and refused by Nyāya. Tell me where each would land if inserted into your ranking, and whether Nyāya's refusal of them is a substantive epistemological decision or merely a consequence of how it draws the boundary around perception and inference.

Finally, be explicit about the status of your own answer: mark which parts are settled among specialists in classical Indian epistemology, which are genuinely contested, and which are your own construction. I do not want a contested reading presented as the consensus, and I would rather have a shorter answer that keeps these registers separate than a longer one that blurs them.
