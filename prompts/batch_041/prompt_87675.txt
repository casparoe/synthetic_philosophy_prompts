Suppose Hume came back and got himself up to speed on the commons literature that grew up after he died: Ostrom's field studies of irrigation systems, forests and fisheries that ran for centuries without either privatisation or a central state, the institutions she found doing the work (clear boundaries, cheap conflict resolution, graduated sanctions, rule-making nested at several levels), Hardin's tragedy-of-the-commons essay and the whole framing that came out of it, and the experimental work showing that communities will punish free-riders but that the same sanctioning machinery can get turned on people who merely refuse to go along with the local norm. His own line was that two neighbours can agree between them to drain a meadow, whereas a thousand never could, and that's why he thought we need government and magistrates to make justice pay at scale. So — does he read Ostrom as vindicating him or refuting him? I want to know where he holds his ground and where he has to give something up. What does his account of justice as an artificial virtue, of conventions that don't need promises behind them, and of the sensible knave, actually predict about a commons governed by design principals rather than by a sovereign? And what would he make of commons he couldn't have imagined — open-source software, Wikipedia, or the atmosphere, where the resource has no real boundary and some of the free-riders are states? Be specific about which bits of his position survive contact with the evidence and which don't.
