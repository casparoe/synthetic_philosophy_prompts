So I've been roped into an advisory role for a small outfit that does subsurface life detection, and they keep hitting the same wall: an instrument returns something ambiguous, and whoever is on duty has to decide on the spot whether we're calling it life and which protocol tier that triggers. Right now the de facto procedure is "ask the most senior person in the room," which is not a procedure, and last season that cost them part of a sample and a lot of goodwill.

What I want from you is a decision procedure they can actually run. The person running it is a grad student on a duty shift, not a philosopher -- chemistry degree, read the manual once. They've got maybe four hours, and the answer has to be defensible later to a review board, because the tiers are not cheap: Tier 2 destroys part of the sample, Tier 3 means quarantine, a moratorium on the site, and a phone call to people outside the institution. It comes up a couple times a season, basicly, but they run practice drills constantly.

You'll have to pick your definitions first, and this is the part I actually care about. The field has a few live candidates: life as a chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution; life as a self-maintaining, far-from-equilibrium metabolic system; life as something with intrinsic purposiveness; life as a property of a lineage rather than of an individual. Don't give me a survey of the debate -- no reading lists, no citations, I don't need them and neither does the student. What I need is the translation: which distinctions survive contact with a checklist, and which have to be dropped because someone with four hours can't operationalize them. Say out loud which definition the procedure is locked to, and what changes if you lock it to a different one, because I'm fairly sure the answer flips.

Then the hard part. Run it on three cases and tell me where it breaks.

First, a brine sample with a strong chiral excess in amino acids, real organic complexity, and a redox gradient that looks sustained, but no cells, no genetic polymer, nothing dividing. Second, something in a returned sample that looks like a virus: a protein coat around a nucleic acid, able to replicate only inside a host's machinery, no metabolism of its own. Third, a digital organism in their origin-of-life simulation that replicates with heritable variation and has been evolving open-endedly for a year.

One of those should come out the wrong way. I want to see which verdict the procedure forces that you or I wouldn't want, and what the student is supposed to do about it.

Give me the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, where indeterminate is the honest output and what that triggers (I bet that's the most common result, and a procedure that can't say I don't know is going to get gamed), and where it hands off to a human because the question is values and not facts. Be honest about which steps are seperate judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure can't do at all. Plain American English, no jargon the student wouldn't already have, and keep it short enough that they could actually keep it at the bench.
