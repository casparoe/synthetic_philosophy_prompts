I read an essay recently that summed up Dennett's thing about qualia, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I go repeating it. Here's the gist as I remember it.

The essay says Dennett's paper is the classic eliminativist attack on consciousness. Qualia are the raw feels — the ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible qualities of experience, like the smell of coffee or the ache of a sore tooth — and Dennett's whole point is that they don't exist. The centerpiece is his coffee-taster example: two guys, Chase and Sanborn, have been drinking the same brand for years. One day Chase says the coffee tastes exactly the same as always but he's stopped liking it; Sanborn says the coffee tastes different now but he likes it just as much. Since neither man can be wrong about his own experience — first-person reports about qualia are incorrigible — theres no fact of the matter about whether the taste changed. No fact of the matter means the taste-as-experienced isn't a real property, so qualia are a fiction. Dennett then lists the four properties qualia are traditionally supposed to have — ineffable, intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible — and shows each one is incoherent. The upshot is that consciousness is an illusion and a mature neuroscience will eventually just replace talk of subjective experience with talk of brain states. The title comes from W.V.O. Quine, who coined the verb 'to quine,' meaning to stubbornly deny that something everyone else takes for granted is real.

How fair is this? Go through it claim by claim. What would someone who actually knows this material accept, what would they wince at, and what's just wrong? I especially want to know whether the Chase-and-Sanborn example is being used the way Dennett actually uses it, whether the summary has his burden-of-proof move right, and whether the conclusion about consciousness being an illusion actually follows from anything he argues. I dont need citations, I just want the audit.
