I edit a small in-house newsletter for the philosophy of religion reading group in my department, and every issue ends with one document written entirely from inside a position — deadpan, no narrator stepping outside to explain the joke. The next issue is on the fideist reading of Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief: the reading on which religious convictions are not hypotheses about the world, on which the believer and the atheist who denies God are not contradicting one another, and on which the whole matter belongs to a form of life rather than to evidence. What I'd like from you is a mock pastoral document from a parish that has swallowed this reading whole — a letter to the congregation from the vicar, followed by a short numbered section of questions the parish is often asked — around 800 to 900 words in total, with a dated heading, written in earnest by a man who is entirely at ease with the view and mildly pained by how often he has to explain it. Please use the following specifics rather than inventing your own: the parish is St. Aldwin's, Marrowfield; the vicar is the Rev. Martin Ashby; the letter is being circulated because the diocese has demanded that the parish say plainly where it stands on the clause in its statement of faith which asserts that something happened to a body on the third day; the council has adopted a rule, which it calls the translation test, that any doctrinal sentence which can be restated as a claim about what did or will happen in the world is to be rejected as a category mistake; a small committee now vets hymn texts for the same fault; one parishioner, Mrs. Ada Boyce, has written in to ask whether she will see her late husband again, in any sense she would recognize; and a faction on the council, whom the vicar privately thinks of as the plain-sense party, wants the old wording restored.

The humour has to come from getting the position right rather than from inventing absurdities, so that someone who has taught these lectures would find the piece fair and a little uncomfortable. The vicar's reassurances should always resolve into grammar; his reply to Mrs. Boyce should be exactly as warm as the view permits and not a degree warmer; and he must not quietly relapse into ordinary realism halfway through — if the temptation to do so is there, it should register as a visible cost of the position rather than as a change of mind. The standard objections need to land without anyone in the document ever stating them: that a belief nothing could contradict has stopped being a belief about anything; that this move renders the disagreement with the atheist, and the disagreement with the believer of another faith, equally non-substantive, which sits oddly with a parish that still remits money to a mission society; that what one believes now looks indistinguishable from what one was brought up to do; and that the same leniency, applied consistently, will eventually have to be extended to the astrologer and the flat-earther down the road. A reader who knows nothing of the secondary literature should still be able to tell what is being ironized, and a reader who knows it well should be able to separate the view itself from its consequences.

Finish with a short note, clearly set apart from the document, of three or four sentences: how confident you are that this is a fair picture of Wittgenstein's own position as opposed to the fideist reading of it — the lectures on religious belief survive only as notes taken by his students in the late 1930s and published decades later, so this is contested ground — where the piece takes liberties with the material, and which single claim in that note you are least sure of.
