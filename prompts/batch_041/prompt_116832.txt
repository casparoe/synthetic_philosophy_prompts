I'm TA-ing the philosophy of medicine unit this term and I've got a stack of short-answer responses to get through tonight, so I'm farming one out to you. The question I set asked them to (a) state Boorse's biostatistical theory of health and disease and explain in what sense it's supposed to be value-free, (b) give the strongest normativist objection to it, and (c) say how the account is meant to deal with the reference-class problem and with the disease/illness distinction. Here's the answer I just recieved:

Boorse's biostatistical theory, or BST, is a naturalist account: it tries to define health and disease without appealing to values, so that "X is a disease" is a descriptive claim, on a par with "X is a broken bone." The core idea is that organisms have a species design, and the parts of an organism have natural functions that contribute to survival and reproduction. Health is the normal functional efficiency of those parts relative to a reference class, and disease is an internal state that interferes with normal functional efficiency. So disease is a theoretical, scientific concept, and medicine can be a science of disease rather than a branch of ethics.

One immediate worry is the reference-class problem: normal for whom? A seventy-year-old's lung capacity is not the norm for a twenty-year-old, so BST has to relativize "normal" to a comparison class. Boorse's answer is that the comparison class is fixed by the species design, since you compare an organ to the same organ in the same species at the same stage of life, so this is a technical problem rather than a conceptual one.

The strongest normativist objection is that BST smuggles values in anyway. Judging that a part is "functioning normally" already presupposes a goal for the organism, and "species design" is just whatever the theorist has decided to treat as the standard. The history of medicine backs this up: homosexuality was in the DSM until the 1970s and was dropped largely under social pressure, and it's hard to read that as a discovery about functional efficiency. Normativists like Wakefield argue that you can't say what counts as a disorder without bringing in harm, which is an evaluative notion.

Boorse's response is to distinguish the theoretical concept of illness, which is value-free, from the everyday concept of disease, which is value-laden: illness is what doctors treat, disease is what the layperson worries about. That lets him say that the concept science uses is clean even though ordinary talk isn't. He also accepts that BST has the consequence that any statistically abnormal trait counts as a disease, and he bites the bullet: on his view an unusually high IQ or an unusually long Achilles tendon would be a disease state, since each is a departure from the statistical norm. That's counterintuitive, but it's the price of a value-free account.

The further consequence is that medicine as a practice should be value-free too: if disease is a factual matter, then decisions about what to treat should just follow the facts, and Boorse thinks doctors shouldn't be making judgments about what's worth treating.
