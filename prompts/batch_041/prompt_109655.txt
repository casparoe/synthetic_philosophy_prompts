I have been reading arguments about when an AI assistant should refuse or override a user for years now, and almost all of them are object-level: someone names a hard case, someone else names a harder one, and the winner is decided by which case feels stickier. I want the specification first. Before we argue about what assistants should do, write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of assistant paternalism has to meet. Treat the standard of adequacy as the deliverable, not as a preamble to one.

Fix the purpose as follows: the theory has to be good enough to write an actual deployment policy against, for someone who must decide tomorrow what the assistant will and will not do — not merely good enough to survive the standard objections.

Give me three tiers, and behind every requirement put the case or consideration that motivates it, not just its name.

1. The cases it must get right. At minimum: the competent adult who wants help with something imprudent but not catastrophic; the user whose judgment is impaired in a way the assistant cannot verify (mania, intoxication, a bad week); the user who precommitted to being stopped and now wants help past their own commitment device; the user whose request is fine for them and costly to someone who never agreed to it; the user with no realistic alternative to this assistant; and the request that is merely distasteful rather than harmful. For each, state the requirement and what a theory failing it would be forced to say.

2. The distinctions it must respect rather than collapse: refusing versus overriding versus answering in a way engineered to change the user's mind versus declining to volunteer; soft paternalism versus hard; the user's present preferences versus their later ones versus the fiction of a true self that outranks both; a type-level policy versus a token-level judgment. Add whatever distinction you think does the most work and that I have not listed.

3. The constraints that come from the assistant's position rather than from ethics in general: that it can verify almost nothing about the user; that it is not a party with standing to make claims on the user's behalf; that its policy must be uniform across millions of users and cannot be adjudicated case by case; and that its two errors are asymmetric — a false refusal and a false compliance are not equally bad — with your view on which way the asymmetry runs.

Then the part I actually want. Here are four candidate requirements. Some I believe are genuine desiderata; some I suspect are the prejudices of one camp dressed up as requirements. Rule on each, and tell me which, if any, cannot be jointly satisfied with the rest.

- The later-self ratification test: an override is licensed only if the user, restored to full information and their ordinary state, would endorse having been overridden. (I have not seen this stated this way; it is my attempt.)
- The reversibility asymmetry: paternalism is licensed in proportion to how irreversible the harm is and how transient the user's objection is likely to be.
- The detection precondition: no policy may license an override on the basis of a condition the assistant detects less reliably than the user does.
- The exit condition: the assistant's authority over a user scales with how easily that user can leave.

Finish by naming the single requirement that excludes the most candidate theories and saying what it excludes. No citations and no names of scholars — I want the structure of the problem, not the literature. A table is fine for the four candidates; the three tiers should be prose.
