I'm a third-year student in a course on explanation in the social sciences, and we've spent several weeks on the claim that a religious ethic can help explain the emergence of a certain economic mentality — the idea that a culture's values can be a genuine cause of economic behavior rather than a decoration on top of material interests. I keep running into the same objection from a friend in the economics department: any case of this kind can be redescribed so that the values do no causal work, either because the material conditions selected for the values, or because the values are just a rationalization of the interests they appear to explain. I'd like to see whether that objection can be answered by design rather than by argument, so I want you to build a case for me.

Please act as a philosopher of the social sciences with a specialty in the methodology of ideational explanation — someone comfortable both with causal-inference debates and with interpretive social science. Think carefully before you write. Begin with a brief brainstorming pass in which you state the design constraints in your own words, say where you expect the difficulties to lie, and note any constraint that may be jointly unsatisfiable; then give me the finished case and the analysis.

Here is the distinction I want the case to turn on. Call values a selector when material conditions have already generated a set of live options and the values merely settle which option a group takes. Call values a generator when they bring about a course of action that the prevailing material conditions would not have generated on their own. Your case must be one in which the values are generators, not selectors.

Constraints on the case:

- Invent it. No real religions, regions, or historical episodes, and no names of scholars or works anywhere in your answer — I want a constructed case, not a recalled one.
- Small scale: one polity, at most two generations (roughly fifty to seventy years), no external shocks such as plague, conquest, or discovery, since those give the materialist an easy exit.
- The value system must not be a work ethic or a thrift ethic, and must not be Protestant in flavor. Its economic effect should be a by-product that its own adherents would not have endorsed as an economic goal.
- Specify the material conditions, and include a neighboring comparison group matched on climate, soil, trade access, and legal status that lacks the doctrine.
- Cross-boundary test: the doctrine must arise under one set of material conditions and then be adopted by a group living under materially different conditions, producing the same behavioral shift there.
- Reverse-causation block: the doctrine's founders must have been materially harmed by the very behavior it later produced.
- Name the outcome variable and give one measurable indicator of it.

Then do the analysis. State the generator explanation, the selectionist explanation, and the rationalization explanation, and for each say what it predicts and which specified feature of your case it cannot accommodate. Identify the single feature doing the work, then delete that feature and show precisely how the case collapses back into an ordinary selection story. Give the strongest materialist counterattack you can and say whether the case survives it. Finish with an honest paragraph on whether the case establishes values as causes or only as enabling background conditions, and be willing to conclude that the design fails if it does. Flag every stipulation as a stipulation, and don't pad the answer with hedging.
