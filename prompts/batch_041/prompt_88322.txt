Ok so I need help with something thats been bugging me for about a year now. I'm in a small reading group on long-run development — mostly econ PhD students plus a couple of economic historians — and we've spent the last several months going around in circles on the big question: why are some countries rich and others poor, and is it institutions, geography, or culture? The weird part is that nobody in the group actually disagrees about the facts. We all accept that the settler-mortality instrument does what it does, that the reversal-of-fortune result is sitting there in the data, that tropical disease burden and distance from the equator track income, that the deep-roots people can push things back through language trees and genetic distance, that the Weber-ish culture stories have some cross-country support. Everyone reads the same tables. And yet every session ends with the institutions people saying 'yeah but yours is endogenous' and the geography people saying 'so is yours' and the historians saying 'you're all asking a dumb question,' and nobody has moved an inch in a year. There's alot of eye rolling.

My hypothesis, and I want you to tell me if its wrong, is that this isn't mainly an empirical dispute at all. Two things I keep noticing. First, the two econometric camps seem to want different things from an explanation: one side wants a variance decomposition across a cross-section (how much of the spread in income does X account for, holding the rest fixed) and the other wants a mechanism story about a particular transition (why did England diverge after 1500, why did the tropics stall). Those aren't the same question and no coefficient answers both, but everyone argues as if they were. Second, the things being named as causes don't look like they're at the same level to me. 'Institutions' as it actually gets measured is often something like expert perceptions of expropriation risk or constraints on the executive — which is at least partly downstream of prosperity, so maybe we're regressing income on a proxy for income. And when you push on it, 'institutions' turns out to be shorthand for a grab bag that's partly constituted by the culture and the geography you're trying to control for. It's like asking whether a car's speed is caused by the engine or the gearbox.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey — I know the main papers, so please no reading list and no citations, I couldn't check them anyway and I don't need them. Is this stall:

(a) a hidden empirical question just waiting on a better natural experiment or a cleverer instrument;
(b) a disagreement about standards of evidence — the credibility-revolution crowd versus people who think historical explanation is a different kind of thing entirely, so each side thinks the other's evidence doesn't count;
(c) a disagreement about what an explanation even is here — decomposition vs. mechanism vs. narrative;
(d) the question being ill-posed, because institutions, geography and culture are mutually constituting and there's no fact of the matter about 'how much' each contributes;

or some combination — and if it's a combination, which parts of the debate fall under which.

Be concrete about which moves by which side are genuine mistakes and which are just different projects being run under one name. Then tell me what progress would even look like here: a new experiment, a formal result about when a decomposition like this is well-defined at all, or just admitting the question should be replaced by something narrower (mechanisms in specific episodes, say). If you think nothing will ever settle it, say that and say why, and tell me whether the debate is still worth having in that case. And tell me what I could actually say next week that would move the group rather than just being the guy who keeps muttering 'it's all endogenous.'
