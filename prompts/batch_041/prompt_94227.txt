I've been going down a rabbit hole on formal agents that reason about themselves, and I've ended up convinced of something I can't tell if it's deep or just a clever restatement. Here's the position: there is no such thing as an agent that fully and uniformly trusts its own reasoning. Not "it's hard," not "it's risky" -- impossible, for anything sound enough for the trust to mean anything. The engine is Löb's theorem. Set it up like this: take a theory T strong enough for arithmetic, with a provability predicate (call it a box) that behaves itself -- provability of provability, distribution over implication, that sort of thing. The popular statement of Löb goes: if T proves that A's provability implies A, for a given A, then T already proves A. And then the kicker: if you just add the whole schema, box A implies A for every A, as new axioms, then taking A to be falsum hands you "T is consistent" inside the theory, and since T sits inside the extended theory, the extended theory proves its own consistency -- and by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem that makes it inconsistent. So self-trust taken as an axiom isn't merely unprovable, it's fatal. The most I can see anyone getting is stratified or partial self-trust -- trust in a weaker subsystem, or in the proofs you've actually run -- and the untrusted remainder just moves up a level every time you try to close the gap. So any AI design that assumes the agent can rely on its own future conclusions is built on sand. (I'm supposed to be writing a term paper on Kant's third antinomy and this has eaten my entire week, so forgive the fixation.)

What I want is the other side of it: what would actually count against the position? Not "here are some papers," I mean the arguments and constructions that should move me, ranked from the ones that would kill it to the ones that are merely awkward. Some things I've half-thought of and don't trust myself on. Does the whole thing depend on the agent using an *unbounded* provability predicate? A real agent searches for proofs up to some length, and I have the impression that bounded proof predicates famously fail some of the box conditions, so Löb may simply not apply -- and the same agent can supposedly prove, for each n, that proofs of length at most n are sound, which sounds like it buys back something usable. Is that a real escape or a consolation prize? Then the probabilistic route: if the agent represents its beliefs as probabilities rather than provable sentences, is the reflection principle even the same beast, or does self-trust come for free and the Löbian obstacle turn out to be an artifact of insisting on proof? And what about the idealization itself -- the theorem needs a recursively axiomatized theory, so if you allow an infinitary rule or an oracle the reflection schema becomes provable, which makes me wonder whether the obstacle is really about self-trust or just about finitary resources.

My own hunch, and I'd like you to attack it rather than be polite about it: I think the real culprit is that the agent has to use one symbol to say both "I will conclude A" and "A," and that if you keep the agent's model of itself permanently one layer down -- a hierarchy of self-models where each level only ever speaks about the level beneath it -- you get a coherent, stable picture of a self-trusting agent. A friend says that just relocates the untrusted level and changes nothing. Who's right, and what would actually settle it? And please tell me what a defender of the position would themselves concede counts against it, not just what the critics say -- and whether the position has quietly become unfalsifiable by being stated as "no agent can fully trust itself" rather than as a claim about any particular architecture. If it has, say so, and say whether that's a fatal problem for a thesis this general or just the price of the generality.
