I am a novelist, and for the past few years, while writing a book about a family that the criminal law comes to define, I have been reading around the mid-century English argument over whether the law may enforce morality as such — what I have seen called the Hart–Devlin debate: the judge's view that a shared morality is part of what holds a society together, and the legal philosopher's answer that the law may restrain a person only to prevent harm to others. I am not asking for a summary of that debate or for reading recommendations; I could not use a bibliography. What I want is a principle that fits the verdicts I actually hold, which are less tidy than either side's.

Here is where my gut lands, case by case.

- A brother and sister, both adults, living together and sleeping together in private, no one else involved: not the law's business, however much it unsettles me.
- A competent adult with a terminal illness who asks a doctor to end his life: this should be lawful.
- Two competent men who agree to settle a quarrel with pistols and one kills the other: the survivor should be prosecuted for murder.
- A man who has sex with a farm animal: this should be a crime, though I cannot name a person it harms.
- A healthy adult who sells one of his kidneys to a stranger for cash: this should be illegal.
- A small religious community whose adult members, freely and repeatedly, consent to ritual scarification as worship: this should be lawful.
- Sexual acts between adults in a public park, in view of people who cannot reasonably avoid passing: a crime.
- Burning a flag, or staging a play that mocks a religion: must be lawful.

I keep stumbling over two pairs. The first is the incest and the kidney sale: both are private, consensual, and touch no third party, yet I want one left alone and the other forbidden. The second is the duel and the assisted death: both are competent adults consenting to be killed, yet I want one punished and the other permitted. The kidney and the ritual scarification trouble me in the same way — both are consensual alterations of the body, and I treat them differently.

My tentative principle is this: the law may intervene only where an act harms a person who has not consented, or is done to a being incapable of consent, or is inflicted on the senses of a public that cannot avoid it; and consent counts only when the person is competent and the act does not destroy or permanently surrender the very thing that makes consent worth having. Please test that against the eight cases and repair it where it breaks. Tell me which clause each verdict actually needs, and be honest about which of my verdicts the best version of the principle cannot keep.

Then turn it loose on cases I have not considered: a market in human eggs; a sanctioned boxing match that leaves one fighter permanently brain-damaged; a private, consensual beating that causes lasting injury; a museum that displays a corpse over the family's objection. I want the principle stated precisely enough that I can see what it forbids and why, not a survey of what others have said.
