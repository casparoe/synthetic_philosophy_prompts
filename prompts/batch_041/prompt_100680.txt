I have spent my career as an actuary at a midsize property-casualty insurer, so I am comfortable with loss distributions, premiums, reserves, retentions, capital charges, ruin probabilities, and, when a single number is demanded, value-at-risk and expected shortfall. A recent diagnosis in my family forced me to make a set of high-stakes decisions about treatment, savings, and insurance coverage, and I noticed that my own choices did not look like expected-utility maximization. A colleague then pointed me toward risk-weighted expected utility, and I have been trying to understand it ever since. I would like you to restate that theory, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use.

Treat the agent as an insurer or reinsurer choosing among contracts, and map the theory's primitives onto the objects I already work with: loss distributions, deductibles, retentions, premium loadings, capital requirements, and risk measures. Say explicitly what corresponds to the risk function, what corresponds to the utility function, and how the ranking of prospects is constructed from them. Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly into actuarial language, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all? In particular, is the theory's distinction between risk aversion and diminishing marginal utility something my risk measures can capture, or does it require a separate object that actuarial practice does not currently have? Finally, tell me whether the residue is a defect of actuarial vocabulary, something it should be able to express but cannot, or a sign that the original theory is less clear than it looks. I want the translation to be honest: do not quietly replace the theory with expected utility or with a standard coherent risk measure and call that a translation.
