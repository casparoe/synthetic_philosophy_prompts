I would like your help adjudicating a disagreement that arose in my department's reading group. I observed it but did not take part, and I am genuinely unsure who had the better of it. The subject was the long-running dispute over just deserts and punishment — the divide between compatibilists such as Dennett, who hold that wrongdoers can deserve blame and hard treatment, and hard incompatibilists such as Caruso, who deny basic desert on the ground that no one is the ultimate author of their own character. My two colleagues, whom I will call A and B, were pressing on whether the retributive intuition survives once one takes seriously how much of any offender's conduct is downstream of unchosen circumstance. Let me reconstruct their exchange as faithfully as I can.

A opened by granting the hard incompatibilist premise: basic desert would require a kind of ultimate self-authorship that no one possesses, and any retributive justification smuggles that authorship back in. But A drew an unusual conclusion. Rather than abolishing the response to wrongdoing, A proposed replacing punishment with what A called moral quarantine — restraint, containment, and reform justified the way public-health quarantine is justified, entirely without appeal to what anyone deserves. A insisted this was not merely a pragmatic substitute: on A's view, once you see that the retributive justification depends on an impossible authorship, the only honest remaining grounds for interfering with an offender are forward-looking.

B replied that the demand for ultimate self-authorship is incoherent and self-defeating — it asks for a self that caused itself, and such a self would be less responsible, not more. Responsibility, B said, tracks the control we actually have: responsiveness to reasons, absence of coercion, the capacity to have acted otherwise in the ordinary sense. B then argued that the quarantine analogy proves too much. Quarantine does not require the quarantined person to have done anything wrong, so it cannot explain why we single out offenders at all, why we ask for apology and repair, why the offender's own attitude toward what they did matters, or why two equally dangerous people should be treated differently according to what they did.

A conceded the point about a self-caused self but pressed the attack from a different direction. A introduced what A called desert laundering: compatibilism, on this view, takes the retributive intuition and simply relabels its trigger, substituting reasons-responsiveness for ultimate authorship, and then treats the relabelling as a justification. But the newly cited property is itself unchosen, and if an unchosen property cannot ground deserved suffering, neither can anything downstream of it. A called this the transitivity objection: you cannot earn desert from a source that was never yours to begin with, no matter how many steps removed you are from it.

B answered that the transitivity objection proves far too much. By that reasoning no one ever deserves anything — not praise, not gratitude, not love, not even the respect A wants extended to offenders. B then offered a positive proposal: desert is not a metaphysical relation to an ultimate source but a status internal to a practice. Within a practice that already presupposes reasons-responsiveness, to say an offender deserves something is just to say the norms of that practice license it. B called this immanent desert and suggested that A's objection demands a grounding the practice never claimed, like complaining that chess pieces are not really bound by the rules because the rules are only conventions.

A closed by objecting that immanent desert makes desert hostage to whatever practice we happen to have, so that a sufficiently cruel practice would make cruelty deserved. B replied that practices are answerable to further norms, and added that A's own quarantine scheme must also single out offenders on the basis of what they did, and so must make desert-like distinctions after all.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each of the five rounds, tell me who has the better of the argument and why, distinguishing the points on which one of them is clearly right from those where both are talking past each other. Then say who is more nearly right overall, and — most importantly — what both of them are missing, whether that is a distinction neither has drawn, a position between theirs, or a consequence each is avoiding. I am not asking for a reading list or for appeals to authority; I want the arguments assessed on their merits, and I would rather have a carefully defended verdict on the specifics than a general survey of the debate.
