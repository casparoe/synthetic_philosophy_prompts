I write novels for a living, but for the past several years I have also taught a small advanced seminar in the philosophy department, a course I built around the question of what it means to say that someone has a reason to do something, since that question keeps surfacing in the fiction I care about. I am now setting the final essay for this term's cohort, and I want to build it around two characters from a manuscript I am revising, because I have found that my students argue far more sharply about invented people than about the stock examples in the literature. The first is Nadia, a lucid, well-informed, and entirely non-akratic woman who has thought carefully and repeatedly about the suffering of animals in industrial farming, is under no illusion about the facts, and yet, when she looks honestly inward, finds nothing in her makeup — no desire, no project, no commitment, no evaluative attitude she would own — that would move her to change what she eats; her friends and her wider culture insist that she nonetheless has a reason to change. The second is Tom, who wakes one morning with a powerful and persistent urge to help a stranger he will never meet, an urge that (let us stipulate) was implanted overnight by a neurosurgical intervention he did not consent to, does not endorse, and would disown if asked; the urge is nevertheless his, in the thin sense that it moves him. I want the essay to force my students to say what makes some consideration a reason for this particular agent rather than merely a consideration that the rest of us wish applied to her, and I want the two cases held together, since I suspect they pull in opposite directions and I want to see who notices.

Please write the examination question itself — one essay, answerable in about seventy-five minutes of writing — together with a model answer of roughly seven hundred words and a marking scheme. The question must be unanswerable by reciting the received distinction between reasons that depend on an agent's existing motivations and reasons that do not; I want it to land on the crux, which as I see it is whether the first camp can extend what counts as an agent's existing motivations far enough to capture Nadia without thereby swallowing Tom, and whether the second camp can earn its verdict on Nadia without simply stipulating a standard that floats free of her. The model answer should show a first-class student negotiating that crux rather than summarizing positions, and it should not presuppose any particular reading or require citations or reading lists, since the examination is closed-book. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make — I would like roughly five such moves, with marks summing to twenty-five, and a one-line note on how candidates typically get each of them wrong, so that the difference between a strong pass and a distinction is legible to my co-marker, who is a good philosopher but a merciless grader.
