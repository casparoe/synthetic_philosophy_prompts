I am a third-year undergraduate currently taking a seminar on Davidson and the philosophy of language, and I am trying to write a term paper on a puzzle about radical interpretation that my classmates and I cannot settle. The setup we have been given is familiar enough: the radical interpreter has nothing to work with but the bare facts about which sentences a speaker holds true, under which observable circumstances, and must arrive at both a theory of what those sentences mean and a theory of what the speaker believes, even though the two seem to be fixed only together — one cannot read belief off the behaviour without already knowing what the sentences mean, and one cannot fix the meanings without already knowing what the speaker believes. What I cannot get straight is the role of the logical vocabulary in this process. Charity bids me to maximize agreement, but agreement about what? If I assume that the speaker's connectives, quantifiers, negation and identity work as mine do, I appear to be presupposing part of the language rather than discovering it; if I do not assume that, I cannot see how the interpreter gets from patterns of assent and dissent to a compositional, Tarski-style truth theory for the language at all, since the construction seems to need the logical apparatus in place before it can be tested against anything.

A classmate has proposed a distinction that I find tempting but suspicious, and I would like you to adjudicate it. She distinguishes what she calls content charity, a matter of attributing to the speaker a body of largely true beliefs, from what she calls form charity, a matter of attributing to the speaker our standards of inference and consistency. Her claim is that the arguments establish only the first, and that the second is smuggled in: charity about beliefs, she says, is a defensible methodological constraint, whereas charity about logic is a substantive and possibly false assumption that a speaker reasons as we do. She illustrates the point with a speaker who assents to a sentence, assents to a conditional whose antecedent is that sentence, and then dissents from the conditional's consequent, and asks why we should not conclude that we have mistranslated rather than that the speaker is muddled — or, conversely, why we should not conclude that the speaker is muddled rather than that our translation is wrong. Another classmate insists that the distinction collapses, on the grounds that having beliefs at all just is having a largely coherent body of them, so that there is no gap between the two sorts of charity for the gap to open up in. I have read the essays twice and still cannot tell which of them is closer to the truth.

I would be grateful if you, taking the role of an expert in the philosophy of language and in the literature on interpretation, irrationality, and the constitutive status of logic, would think this through carefully — considering the strongest version of each reading before committing to any of them — and then explain what is actually at stake and which of these positions, if either, the arguments can support. Please give your answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one a sentence or two, ordered from the initial setup through to the resolution, with no introductory framing and no concluding summary.
