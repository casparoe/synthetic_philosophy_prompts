I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have agreed to help grade a batch of written assignments for an introductory online course on practical reasoning. Before I return the grades, I want an assessment I can stand behind, so I would like you to evaluate one submission in detail.

The assignment question given to the students was this:

"Explain the structure of the argument commonly called Pascal's mugging. Suppose a stranger claims that, with some small but positive probability, he can deliver an enormous benefit — say, ten to the power of one hundred years of bliss — provided you hand over your wallet. Explain how an expected-value calculation is supposed to make handing over the wallet the rational choice, and evaluate the following objection: 'The argument fails because the probability that the stranger can deliver is effectively zero, so the expected value is zero.' Finally, consider whether the argument's force depends on the promised benefit being infinite; if the stranger promises only a finite, though astronomically large, benefit, does the argument still go through?"

The student's submission was this:

"The scenario called Pascal's mugging is essentially a modern restatement of Pascal's wager. In Pascal's wager you choose between believing and not believing, the payoff for belief is eternal bliss, and because any positive probability multiplied by an infinite payoff is infinite, belief wins. Pascal's mugging moves the same reasoning into the street: a stranger offers you an enormous reward — a hundred years of bliss, or vastly more — if you hand over your wallet.

The expected value of handing over the wallet is the probability that the stranger delivers multiplied by the size of the payoff. Even if that probability is tiny — one in a trillion, say — the payoff can be set so large that the product still exceeds the value of the wallet. Since the stranger is free to name any payoff he likes, there is no upper bound on the expected value he can offer, and expected-value maximization therefore tells you to hand over the wallet.

The standard objection is that the probability the stranger can actually deliver is zero, so the expected value is zero as well and the argument collapses. This is a decisive reply: if the payoff is impossible, no amount of multiplication will rescue it.

As for whether the argument depends on the payoff being infinite: it does. If the benefit is merely finite, however large, then multiplying it by a very small probability yields a very small number, so handing over the wallet is plainly irrational. The mugging only bites when the payoff is literally infinite, which is why it is really Pascal's wager in disguise.

The overall lesson, then, is that expected-value maximization can be made to recommend an absurd action, so it cannot be the whole story about practical rationality."
