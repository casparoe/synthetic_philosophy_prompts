I'm a philosophy professor (philosophy of mind, mostly) and for the past year I've been crashing the interpretability reading group that the ML people down the hall run. I went in thinking the question "do these networks have representations?" was either trivially yes or obviously confused, and I've come out genuinely unsure, which is annoying. The two people I keep arguing with are Nadia, a postdoc who does circuit-level work on small transformers and is a realist about representation, and Tom, who is a deflationist about basically everything and thinks "the model represents X" is just shorthand for "X-talk reliably predicts the model's behaviour," and that asking which of the two of them is *right* is a category mistake. I want to see them go at it properly instead of me paraphrasing them badly to my grad students.

Here's the concrete thing I want them to fight over, because it's the case that actually made me wobble. Nadia's group trained two small transformers on the same synthetic task: strings from a little formal grammar with two hidden variables, one controlling subject-verb agreement and one controlling bracket depth. Both models hit essentially identical accuracy. In model A there's a single direction in the residual stream whose ablation selectively kills agreement while leaving bracket depth untouched, it also comes out as the top principal component, and you can steer the behaviour by adding it. In model B, nothing like that: a linear probe decodes the agreement variable perfectly from a random 50-dimensional subspace, there are something like 3000 different directions that, when ablated, degrade agreement by exactly the same amount, none of them privileged, and nothing steers. Nadia says A is what having a representation looks like and B merely *encodes* the same information in a superposition-heavy mess without representing it; Tom says the contrast is an artifact of the tools, since A's tidiness is a fact about the basis training happened to settle in and the probe found a linear direction only because we built it to look for one. I flip-flop daily.

Write me that dialogue -- Tom and Nadia, over coffee, both sharp, neither one a strawman. Nadia should press the ablation-and-steering asymmetry and the fact that the grammar variable was sitting there in the data-generating process all along; Tom should press that a direction which is one of 3000 equally effective directions can't be the real vehicle of anything, and that "represents" is smuggling in normative work the causal facts can't underwrite. They have to actually answer each other -- if Tom lands a blow, Nadia has to deal with it, and vice versa. Give me exactly five exchanges, numbered, no intro and no closing summary, a couple of sentences per speaker each time.
