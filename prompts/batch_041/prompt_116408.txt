Alright, so I did 35 years of bench physics before I retired and only now am I reading any philosophy of science, so bear with me if I dont have the vocabulary right. Something's been nagging at me.

In my old department everybody leaned on idealizations constantly -- frictionless surfaces, point masses, ideal gases with zero intermolecular forces, infinite populations, steady-state everything. Nobody batted an eye. And there's that old line (Friedman, I think) that a theory's assumptions can be as unrealistic as you like as long as it predicts well. Great.

But the very same people would insist a theory is only worth anything if its assumptions are at least roughly true, that if you get the mechanism wrong you dont really understand anything even when the numbers come out right. We'd happily say the ideal gas law explains why heating a sealed tank raises the pressure, then in the next breath admit that gases obviously do have intermolecular forces and the law is just a simplification. And in the lab we'd put careful error bars on our scatter, but never an error bar on the idealization itself, as if neglecting friction were somehow free.

Someone recently told me there's supposed to be a real difference between idealization, which deliberately distorts, and abstraction, which just leaves things out. I'd never once thought about that in all those years.

So: what does the working practice actually presuppose about what a scientific model is? Does using a knowingly false assumption commit you to models being mere prediction devices where truth is beside the point? Or does the practice presuppose the opposite, that any decent idealization is a limiting case of a truer model we could in principle de-idealize, so it's approximately true of something real? Could it be both, depending on which move you're making? Which rival pictures of models does the practice quietly rule out? Is it even self-consistent, or are we instrumentalists on Monday and realists on Tuesday? And if you swapped the presupposed view for a clean alternative, what would actually have to change in how we teach and use these models?
