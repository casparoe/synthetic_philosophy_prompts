I cover foundations of physics for a general-interest science magazine, and I have spent the better part of a year reporting on a dispute that keeps resurfacing in seminar rooms and conference hallways without ever resolving: what, exactly, the violation of the Bell inequalities tells us about locality. I would like you to draft the joint statement that the two camps in this dispute could actually put their names to. The first camp holds that the experimental violations reveal a genuine nonlocal connection between separated systems — that the failure of factorizability is a failure of locality in a substantive, world-describing sense, whatever we eventually say about signaling. The second camp holds that what fails is only outcome independence, that parameter independence — equivalently, the no-signaling condition — survives both in the quantum formalism and in every experiment, and that since no-signaling is the only operationally meaningful content we can give to the denial of nonlocal causation, the experiments establish the failure of a certain explanatory demand rather than the presence of any nonlocal influence. Please produce the document these two would sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration, organized as follows.

First, a numbered section of agreed claims: what both accept about the assumptions the theorem actually uses and the class of theories it excludes; what both accept has been established experimentally, including what the closing of the standard loopholes does and does not settle; what both accept follows about signaling, about the status of relativity, and about the impossibility of using entanglement for faster-than-light communication; and what both accept about the structure of their remaining disagreement.

Second, a disagreements table with one row per disputed point, giving each side's position in wording that side would accept as its own, and naming the crux for that row. I expect the cruxes to include whether the decomposition into parameter and outcome independence is physically significant or an artifact of how we write the joint distribution; whether a violation of outcome independence alone can count as a nonlocal causal relation or only as a nonlocal correlation; whether no-signaling is the right operationalization of the denial of nonlocal causation or merely a practical constraint; and whether the superdeterministic escape — denying that the measurement settings are independent of the hidden state — is a live option, whether it deserves to be called local at all, and what each side predicts about its prospects.

For each crux, give the case, argument, or thought experiment that both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction about how it would come out. Where you judge that part of the dispute is merely verbal — for instance, that the two sides are using the word nonlocal in different senses, one causal and one purely correlational — say so explicitly and identify which part. The statement should be constructive and evenhanded; neither side should be able to read it as a concession by the other.
