I am a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar I am co-teaching on progress, providence, and the philosophy of history. My dissertation concerns how the arc-of-history image does argumentative work in human rights advocacy and in longtermist writing, and the reason I want a classification instrument rather than an essay is that almost everyone I interview says some version of: of course things have gotten better, and of course we should keep pushing. Press them, though, and they turn out to be committed to structurally different claims. So please build me a decision tree that sorts the positions on whether history bends toward justice. Sort by structure rather than by mood: the root must not be an optimism-versus-pessimism question, and no node may be answerable merely by naming a label one already favors. Aim for twelve to sixteen leaves; branch two ways only where the underlying question is genuinely binary, and allow three or four answers where the logical space does. The readers are graduate students who will fill the tree in themselves and then argue about their own results, so each node has to be a question they can answer from the inside, in their own voice, without knowing where it leads. What I most need the tree to expose is which positions make the direction of history a reason for patience and which make it a reason for exertion — the arc claim gets deployed in public argument on both sides, and I want to see whether the quietism follows from the position or is smuggled in.

Two pieces of scaffolding you should build on, since they are the reason I cannot simply assign this from a textbook. First, treat the claim that history bends toward justice as decomposable into three separable sub-claims: a trend claim (the record shows net moral improvement), a vector claim (some mechanism or force pushes outcomes in a moral direction), and a terminus claim (there is an end state toward which the whole thing converges). These come apart cleanly — a lucky-record view takes the trend with neither vector nor terminus, a defeated-mechanism view takes the vector while denying the trend, a terminus can be posited with no mechanism at all — and I want the tree to exploit those gaps rather than collapse them. Second, distinguish three senses in which the direction might be guaranteed: nomological (a law of social development), providential (a purpose that cannot fail), and practical (nothing guarantees it, but the commitment is warranted because acting on it is what makes it likely, or because a practice of the kind we have cannot be carried on without it). Now locate the following in that space, glossing each only as far as the tree requires: Augustinian and Bossuet-style providence; Hegel's cunning of reason; Kant's idea of a universal history as a regulative rather than constitutive idea; Smith's invisible hand; Marx's historical materialism; William James's meliorism, on which the world's salvation is neither certain nor impossible but a live probability that our efforts bear on; Löwith's thesis that modern philosophies of progress are secularized eschatology, and so incoherent once the providence they dropped is gone; Popper's anti-historicism; Berlin-style value pluralism; Benjamin's messianic interruption, on which progress is the storm and redemption is a break rather than a culmination; a genealogical position on which talk of direction is an effect of power rather than a description of it; Nietzsche's eternal recurrence and the older cyclical views; Whig history together with the standard critique of it; and the longtermist reconstruction of the arc as a probabilistic claim about a very large future. Add any leaf you think is missing, and if one of my items is not really a position on this question at all, put it outside the tree and tell me why.

Deliver it as an indented tree: every node labeled with the question that splits it, every leaf carrying the position's name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then two short sections. First, the combinations of answers the tree leaves empty, each with a diagnosis — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, along with the extra question that would separate them, if there is one. I suspect, for instance, that my tree will fail to distinguish Kant's regulative-idea reading from the longtermist practical wager, and I would rather be told so than have it papered over. No citations and no reading list; I will add those myself. American English, please, and plain prose — the handout has to survive being read aloud in a seminar.
