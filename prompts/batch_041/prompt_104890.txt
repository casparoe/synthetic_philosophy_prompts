For the last few weeks a friend and I have been going back and forth about the Platonia-style dilemmas — the family of puzzles in which some large number of people are each privately invited to submit a bid or a number, and a single uniquely chosen submission takes the whole prize, so that if nobody submits, or if two people submit the same thing, everyone walks away with nothing. I've been trying to construct my own version of it: a thousand strangers are told that each may send in one positive integer, that the sender of the lowest integer that no one else sends will receive a fortune, and that anyone who sends an integer that someone else also sends gets nothing; nobody can communicate, and nobody knows who else has been invited. My friend thinks the whole thing is a trap and that the only sensible move is to fold, since any bid has a vanishing chance of being unique. I've come around to the opposite position, and I want you to try to knock it down.

I hold that there is a distinct and correct mode of practical reasoning — the one Hofstadter called superrationality, and which I've also seen called renormalized rationality — according to which a player who knows that everyone in the situation is symmetric, and knows that the others are as capable of this same reasoning as they are, should not treat the other players' choices as independent facts to be predicted. Instead they should reason from the premise that everyone who reasons correctly will arrive at the same strategy, and then ask which shared strategy is best. On that basis I think the answer here is not to abstain but to randomize — to submit with some small probability calibrated to the number of players, on the order of one over the number of participants, so that the group's shared reasoning has its best shot at producing a unique winner. I take this to be more than a heuristic: I think it is the correct theory of choice for symmetric games, and I think it is what makes cooperation in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma defensible rather than merely sentimental.

What I want from you is a map of what would count against this position. Not a general survey of decision theory, but the particular arguments, formal results, intuitions, or experimental findings that should move someone who actually holds the view. Sort them by how decisive they would be, and be honest about which of them a committed superrationalist would themselves concede as counting against them rather than dismissing as a misunderstanding. I am especially interested in three pressure points. First, symmetry: what happens to the theory when the players are not identical — when they have different beliefs about how many others will play, different degrees of confidence in one another's rationality, or when it is common knowledge that some participants are simply not superrational? Second, existence: does the superrational prescription even pick out a determinate strategy in a genuine many-player lottery, or does the reasoning that works cleanly for two players fail to converge once the prize is contested by a crowd? Third, insulation: if the theory only yields verdicts in perfectly symmetric, idealized setups, tell me plainly whether that narrowing is fatal or simply the ordinary price of a normative theory, and give me your own verdict on whether I should keep the view. I'll add that what would move me most is a case where the superrational move and the intuitively right move come apart — a scenario in which following the symmetry constraint makes everyone demonstrably worse off than some other rule they could all have followed — so if such a case exists, lead with it.
