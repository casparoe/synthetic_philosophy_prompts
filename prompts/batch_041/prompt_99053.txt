Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about Keynes, and neither of us could settle it, so I want an outside reconstruction rather than an opinion.

The occasion was that my friend has been reading about Kelly betting and the family of ideas that goes with it: the claim that when you face a series of multiplicative bets you should size each one to maximize the expected logarithm of your wealth rather than its arithmetic expectation, that this growth-optimal rule is somehow forced on you by any long sequence of bets, that the reason has to do with time-averages and ensemble-averages coming apart, and that the practical upshot is that avoiding ruin matters more than maximizing expectation. He calls the whole package geometric rationality and treats it as a late-arriving vindication of much older practical wisdom.

His claim was that Keynes would have recognized the Kelly package immediately as his own view. His Keynes is the one who ran a Cambridge college's endowment, who traded currencies and commodities on his own account, who was as much a working speculator as a theorist, and who at least once watched his own book go nearly to nothing and then rebuilt it by learning to size positions and to think about surviving rather than about arithmetic edge. Kelly betting, he said, is just Keynes's investing with the mathematics finally written down, and Keynes would have signed it on sight.

My claim was the opposite. I said that the thing that makes Keynes worth reading on probability is precisely his insistence that the interesting cases are the ones where you do not have the numbers: where probability is not a single measurable quantity, where you must weigh how much evidence stands behind an estimate and not merely the estimate, and where the assumption of a long run of identical, independent repetitions is exactly the fiction he spent his career dismantling. Kelly, on my reading, needs the very things Keynes said we rarely have — a known probability, a known payoff, and a world that will let you play the same game over and over. He would have seen growth-optimality as one more over-mathematized calculus of the sort he distrusted, and said so.

We could not agree because we were each quoting a different Keynes, the practical one and the epistemological one, and neither of us could say how the two fit together.

What I want from you is a prediction of Keynes's actual views on this cluster of questions, argued from what he actually held and did rather than from what would be flattering to either of us. Commit on each of the following separately, because my friend and I kept conflating them: (1) whether Keynes would accept the growth-optimal or log-wealth rule as a normative prescription for someone placing a series of bets; (2) whether he would regard the logarithm as arbitrary or as privileged, given what he held about the measurement and comparability of probability and utility; (3) what he would make of the long-run or repeated-bet justification for the rule, given his treatment of long-run arguments elsewhere; (4) whether his notion of the weight of evidence gives him a reason to reject the rule or to refine it; (5) whether the ruin-avoidance motive — survival first — is something he would endorse independently of any expected-value calculation; and (6) whether his own investing practice is evidence for the growth-optimal picture, evidence against it, or neither.

Keep it concrete. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or references; I have no internet access and I do not want them — I want the reconstruction and the reasoning. Where you are unsure what Keynes would have said, say so explicitly rather than papering over it.

Please organize the answer under these headings, in this order:

- Summary (about 100 words): your bottom line on what Keynes would have thought, stated up front.
- What Keynes brought to the table (about 250 words): the parts of his views on probability, uncertainty, and evidence that the prediction has to rest on, set out plainly.
- Verdicts, point by point (about 500 words): work through items (1)–(6) above, one short paragraph each, and end each with a one-line verdict.
- Where the two framings collide (about 200 words): the places where the Kelly/geometric picture and Keynes's picture are genuinely incompatible, as opposed to merely different in vocabulary.
- Which of us was closer (about 150 words): say where my friend's Keynes and my Keynes each turn out to be right, and where we both missed something.
- Confidence (about 100 words): what you would expect a well-informed Keynes scholar to dispute in your reconstruction.
