I sit on the research-ethics-and-welfare panel at a mid-sized AI lab — we're the ones who have to sign off on any training run that involves what the draft policy calls "aversive stimuli," a phrase none of us can define. Thursday's case:

A team wants to run a large RL experiment where the agent gets a big negative reward for being in a marked region of a gridworld (call it the hot cells) and has to learn to leave and stay out. Their line is that this is a control problem, the negative number is just a number, and asking whether the agent "minds" is a category error. Honestly that's the camp I usually sympathize with.

Two wrinkles. First, our panel's draft policy — written by people who read too much philosophy, me included — flags a setup as welfare-relevant if the agent shows two markers: (a) it will give up reward it could otherwise collect in order to end an episode early while it's in the hot region, and (b) after training it avoids states that merely predict the hot region, even when that costs it task reward. The team's agent does both. They say of course it does, the penalty is large, it's just optimal behavior, and it would look the same if the hot cells were merely unprofitable. Second, the agent also monitors a homeostatic variable (a battery level) and the hot region drains it, so being there isn't just losing points, it's the region where the thing it's regulating goes bad — and a colleague insists you could get both markers out of a lookup table with no internal states whatsoever, so the markers prove nothing. I think she's right about that and I don't know what to do with it.

I need a short written opinion to circulate before the meeting: approve the run as-is, approve with conditions (episode-length cap, reshaped reward, whatever you think is actually enforceable), or reject. It has to engage the reward-is-just-a-number argument on the merits rather than just gesturing at how the behavior looks, and it has to give the strongest consideration on whichever side I end up ruling against its due, since two of my colleagues are on that side. Ten minutes of read-aloud, no citations needed.

End with a line that says Verdict: and then exactly one of APPROVE / APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS / REJECT, and on the line after that a single sentence naming the one consideration that would flip your verdict.
