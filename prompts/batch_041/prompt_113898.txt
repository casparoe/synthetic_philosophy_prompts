I've been reporting for a while now on the Victorian philosophers who founded the Society for Psychical Research back in the 1880s — Sidgwick and his circle — and the thing I can't get past is how completely the argument they started has frozen. The way I understand it, Sidgwick really thought you could just go gather the evidence and settle, one way or the other, whether consciousness survives death; that it was an empirical question like any other, and that if the answer was yes it would change alot. A century and a half later both sides are standing exactly where they started.

The survival side points to a pile of cases — mediums coming out with names, dates and small details about the dead that nobody in the room could plausibly have known, sometimes several mediums producing fragments that only make sense when you line them up together — and says the only explanation left standing is that the dead are talking. The skeptics say fine, grant every case at face value, but that same data is exactly what you'd expect if living people had huge uncontrolled psychic ability, telepathy and clairvoyance with no known ceiling, so you can never tell a dead person did it from a living person doing it without knowing how. Some of them call that the super-psi move. And then a third group says the whole thing is unfalsifiable, or that it's fraud and coincidence all the way down, and that continuing to ask the question is a category mistake.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict. My hunch — tell me if I'm wrong — is that this was never really an empirical dispute, even though everyone on every side talks like it is, and that the super-psi move is doing something strange: it seems to make survival untestable without anyone ever having to say a single piece of evidence is bad. But maybe it's simpler than that. Maybe it's a fight about priors and burden of proof — how weird does the world have to get before survival beats a living super-psychic, and who's supposed to prove what. Or maybe the two sides are answering different questions and don't realize it: one about what the evidence actually shows, the other about what it's reasonable to believe given everything else we know. Or maybe Sidgwick's own thing was never just survival — I've read people argue his ethics ends in a standoff he couldnt close and that the psychical research was him hunting for a way out, though I can't tell how much of that is people retrofitting a story onto him.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is a hidden factual or statistical question doing the work, is it bedrock priors, are they talking past each other, or is the question just ill-posed? And then be honest about what would move it — would a genuinely better experiment help, or a conceptual distinction, or changing the question, or nothing at all and why? I'd especially like to know what each side would have to give up for it to resolve, because I want the piece to end somewhere real instead of with the usual welp, nobody knows.
