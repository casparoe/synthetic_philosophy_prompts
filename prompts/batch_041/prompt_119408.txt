I'm a philosopher who has been co-teaching a cross-listed graduate seminar with a computer scientist — half the room comes from the machine-learning side, half from philosophy, and neither half has much of the other's background — and the session that has fallen to me is the one on low-impact AI: the cluster of proposals that try to penalize an agent for the side effects of its actions without first handing it a full specification of what we value. I don't want you to write the lecture; I want the plan for it. Begin with what has to be in place before the question even makes sense to this room, in order, and say why each piece depends on the one before it. My own view, which I'd like you to test rather than assume, is that they first have to see that a side effect is not a descriptive category at all but is relative to a task and to a baseline, and that any baseline — doing nothing, following some safe default policy, the counterfactual world in which the agent never existed — is already a substantive choice about what counts as a change worth caring about, so that the ambition of defining side effects without values is in trouble before the technical work starts.

Then tell me how to sequence the two families of proposals that the ML half will have heard of and the philosophy half won't. The first family measures impact as the degree to which an action reduces the reachability of future states — how much harder it makes it to get to states the world could otherwise have reached — measured against a baseline such as inaction, on the thought that keeping the world's options open is a good you can name without knowing what anyone wants. The second measures impact as the degree to which an action reduces the agent's ability to achieve a range of other goals it might have been given, on the thought that you need not know the true objective so long as you protect a spread of possible ones. I want the sequence to make plain that each is an attempt to buy value-agnosticism cheaply, and then to locate where each one gets gamed: a penalty on lost reachability can be satisfied by an agent that first arranges the world so that inaction is just as costly, and a penalty on lost goal-attainment is only as good as the set of goals it happens to protect. I had planned to carry the whole session on the coffee-fetching robot that smashes a vase on its way to the kitchen, but I suspect that example smuggles in the value judgment — the vase is worth preserving — exactly where I want the students to catch the smuggling; so either tell me how to use it without that, or give me a better case, and tell me what the carrying example must have and must lack.

I'm expecting at least these misconceptions, and I want each mapped to the point in the sequence where it predictably forms and the point where I should head it off: that a side effect just means an unintended effect; that impact is the physical magnitude of the change; that a better measure would fix everything, so the difficulty is merely engineering; that low impact means doing nothing; and the sophisticated-sounding one, that this is all just specification gaming, the measures are fine, and the optimizer is at fault. For each, say whether it is a confusion or a live position I shouldn't score cheap points against. Finish with one question I can put to the room in the last ten minutes that would separate a student who has understood the problem from one who has merely learned that side effects are hard, and with what I should say out loud that I am refusing to cover at this level — no formalisms and no literature, obviously, but tell me what else — and what I should send them away to think about instead.
