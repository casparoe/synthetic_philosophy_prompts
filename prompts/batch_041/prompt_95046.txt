I'm a novelist, not an academic, but I've somehow ended up running a 75-minute session at a small writers' festival — about 18 people, mixed bag, a few will have done some philosophy, most won't. My current book is about a group of friends who sign a pact to keep each other honest, and the whole second half is them trying to get out of it, so I keep circling this idea of binding your future self and whether the later you has any standing to complain about the earlier you. Design me an activity that makes the room feel the pull of it first and the trap of it second, in that order. I want them to genuinely bind themselves to something early on — small stakes, no money, but real enough that breaking it stings — and then, a while later, hand them a perfectly legitimate-looking way to unbind, and see what they do. The bit I care about most: at the signing I want two or three people in the room who never agreed to the pact, latecomers or someone who stepped out, and the pact applies to them anyway, and I want the group to work that out for themselves rather than me announcing it. Then a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the two versions of this — one person tying their own hands, and a whole polity doing it to itself and to everyone who comes after. Give me rules, timings, what to do if it flops halfway, and be honest about what the game can't do. I assume it can't reproduce the case where the thing you bound yourself to turns out, in hindsight, to have been monstrous — which is honestly half of what I'm writing about.
