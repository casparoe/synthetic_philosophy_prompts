So I'm in this reading group on democracy and we keep going round in circles, because half the room thinks more competent people should have more say is obviously right and the other half thinks it's obviously a non-starter, and nobody can actually say what the disagreement is. I want to settle it with a decision tree instead of more arguing.

Can you build me a flowchart that starts from the thing almost everyone in the room already excepts -- that voters are often ignorant, that some people understand policy much better than others, and that it would be better if our collective decisions were more often correct -- and then branches on the questions that actually split the positions, ending in named leaves.

A few branch points I specifically want in there:
- whether there's a fact of the matter about the right policy independent of what people want, or whether correct just means whatever comes out of a fair procedure
- whether the right answer is reachable by ordinary people under good conditions (deliberation, decent info) or genuinely needs expertise
- whether expertise is measurable and stable enough to license giving anyone formal extra power, as opposed to just tinkering with institutions so they track truth better
- whether the relevant competence is individual or a property of groups (crowds, deliberation, diverse problem-solvers)
- where legitimacy is supposed to come from once equal votes are off the table: correctness, consent, or contestability

Leaves I want to see at minimum: restricted suffrage (a competence test for the franchise), plural voting (extra votes for the competent), an epistocratic veto (a competent chamber that can block legislation), lottocracy / rule by lot, deliberative mini-publics with real power, technocracy via insulated agencies, futarchy-style outcome-betting, liquid / delegable voting, and the pure proceduralist option that says the whole truth-tracking framing is a mistake. If some leaf needs a name nobody's coined, just call it unnamed and say so.

Then the part I actually care about. I have a half-baked proposal of my own and I want to know where it lands: a bicameral setup where one house is elected normally and the other is drawn by lot from everyone who passes a short exam on basic stats and civics, and the lot-house gets a suspensive veto over any bill that fails a pre-registered cost-benefit test. Where does that sit, which existing leaf is it closest to, and what would have to change about it to push it into a different leaf?

Also: show me the combinations of answers that don't line up with any real position, and for each say whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any two leaves your tree can't actually seperate with these questions -- don't paper over it.

Keep it to like 6-8 branch questions and 10ish leaves. It's for a reading group of grad students, not for publication, so be blunt about which positions look unserious.
