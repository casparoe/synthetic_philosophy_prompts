So my friend and I got into it for like two hours last night about Moore's paradox and I still can't tell who came off worse. He's a smart guy, reads a lot of pop philosophy and pop psych, and here's his take, delivered with total confidence:

"Moore's paradox is that weird thing where you say 'it's raining but I don't believe it's raining.' The reason it's weird is that it's a contradiction -- the two halves can't both be true at once. If I don't believe it's raining then as far as I'm concerned it isn't raining, so the moment I say the first half I've already contradicted the second half. Moore's whole point was that this is the one place you can catch someone being inconsistent purely from the shape of their sentence, without knowing anything about the weather or the person. It happens because we've got guaranteed access to our own minds: you literally can't be wrong about what you believe, that's the one thing you can't be mistaken about, and that's why the second half acts like a veto on the first. The other version, 'it's raining but I believe it isn't raining', is the same sentence with the negation just moved around, and it's contradictory for the same reason. And watch what happens when you switch out the 'I': 'it's raining but Dave doesn't believe it' is completely fine, nobody would blink. Which shows the whole thing isn't really about belief or truth at all, it's about the first person -- a quirk of English and the word 'I'. Also, the weirdness is only in the saying, not the thinking. I can sit here and think 'it's raining and I don't believe it's raining' all day and nothing goes wrong, it's only out loud that it gets weird. So it's a fun puzzle, but it doesn't show anything deep about knowledge or the mind. Philosophers just love a sentence that sounds funny."

I pushed back and he just kept saying "same thing" and changed the subject, which made me think he was bluffing, but I'm definately not enough of a philosopher to say where he's off, and honestly some of it sounded right to me. Can you go through his paragraph clause by clause and tell me what's accurate, what someone who actually works on this would wince at, and what's flatly wrong? Especially the contradiction claim, the bit about not being able to be wrong about your own beliefs, and whether those two versions of the sentence really are "the same thing." Then the bigger question: does his conclusion survive once you fix the mistakes? And one more thing I want to know, because I think it might decide it -- does this weirdness show up for other mental states too, like knowing something or intending to do something or hoping for something? If the version with 'know' is fine but the version with 'believe' isn't, that seems like it would say something huge about belief, and if it's the same for everything then I don't see how he gets to call it a quirk of English.
