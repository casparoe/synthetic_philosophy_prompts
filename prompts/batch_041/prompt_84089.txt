So I'm a backend engineer — no philosophy or linguistics degree, I just read alot — and next Thursday I'm giving a 20-minute talk to a local language-and-mind reading group, followed by Q&A. The Q&A is the part I'm dreading.

Here's my thesis, roughly. The poverty of the stimulus argument gets run as one argument but its really two. (1) The data a kid hears doesn't pin down the grammar — lots of grammars fit it. (2) No general-purpose learner could get from that data to the grammar without language-specific machinery. The LLM stuff only ever attacks (2), and it doesn't even win there — it just relocates the innateness into the architecture and the training setup. Which means the real question isn't "nativism, yes or no," it's "which biases, and how would we tell," and that's an empirical question, not something you settle by pointing at a corpus. Meanwhile (1) is untouched, and I think (1) is the interesting half. The one concrete thing I lean on is the old question-formation example — the rule that would move the first auxiliary word, which kids supposedly never use even though it fits the data they hear.

The room: about a dozen people, mostly generative syntacticians who think Chomsky basically won, two developmental people who run infant looking-time studies, and one NLP person who works on scaling laws and will not sit still for "stochastic parrot" talk. Nobody there cares about my code.

What I want: the objections I'm actually going to get from those people, in their vocabulary, ordered by how much damage they do — not the generic internet ones. For each, a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is thin. Plus the one question I'm praying nobody asks.

And be straight with me about which objections are settled — where everyone who knows the area agrees and I'm simply wrong — versus which are live fights where my answer is as good as theirs. Don't hand me one camp's position and call it the consensus; I'd rather know I'm out on a limb. Also don't tell me to go read something, I'm not going to.
