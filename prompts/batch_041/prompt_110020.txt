So there's a pattern I keep running into at work and I can't find a word for it. We had established that our job runner processes each message exactly once -- tested it, traced the code, the queue supposedly guarantees it. Then someone finds a duplicate charge in the ledger. And the instant reaction, mine included, is: that can't be a real duplicate, the ledger query must be double-counting, or someone replayed the message by hand. The scary part is the reasoning feels airtight rather than lazy: since we know it's exactly-once, whatever produced that row is by definition not a counterexample to exactly-once, it's an artifact, so the right move is to go fix the artifact and move on. Same shape showed up with a sensor: we knew the calibration was good, so every weird reading got filed as "sensor's flaky," and eventually we had no idea whether the sensor was broken or the world was weird, and no way to find out, because the rule said there was nothing there to check.

Is there a name for this? I know confirmation bias and motivated reasoning, but those are about wanting the answer to come out a certain way, and this isn't that -- the logic seems to check out on its own. I also don't just mean plain stubbornness, because you can be stubborn about something you don't actually know, and this only kicks in once you're sure. The bit that really gets me is that the same reasoning would have told me to ignore the very evidence that made me sure, if I'd happened to see it earlier.

If there's an established name, tell me, and if there isn't, name it and tell me where the edges are -- when it's actually fine to write off a contrary reading as a broken instrument or a flaky test, and when it's this thing instead, and how it differs from confirmation bias and from just having a justified belief your not going to give up. A bare label won't help me, I want to know what exactly the move is and what it isn't.
