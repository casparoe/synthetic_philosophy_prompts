I have been reading a good deal about how artificial intelligence should be built and governed, and I keep encountering a move that I would like to be able to name with some precision. The move runs as follows. Someone proposes a safeguard — a slower rollout, a costly independent evaluation, a commitment to hold back a particular capability until it is better understood. A respondent objects that the safeguard is pointless unless everyone adopts it: if we adopt it while others do not, we have simply handed the advantage to the less careful, and the outcome is worse than if we had never bothered. The conclusion is that we ought not to adopt the safeguard unilaterally, and that until universal adoption can somehow be arranged, the prudent course is to keep pace.

What interests me is the shape of the argument rather than any single instance of it. It is applied with something like a one-way valve: the same logic would seem to tell equally against racing ahead on capability, yet I almost never see it turned in that direction. It treats a failure of coordination as a reason for inaction rather than as the very thing that needs repairing, and because each party reasons this way, the failure of coordination becomes self-fulfilling. And it converts a strategic situation into something spoken of as though it were weather, a constraint imposed from outside rather than something constituted by the choices of the parties themselves.

I have met relatives of this move elsewhere. In arguments about climate policy and about labor standards one hears something similar, usually under the heading of a race to the bottom, but there the coordination problem is at least treated as a problem to be solved; in the setting I am describing it tends to function as a conversation-stopper, as if it had settled the matter. I have also heard the phrase alignment tax used for the cost of a safeguard, and I suspect one of the concepts in this neighborhood is close to what I am after.

Is there an established name for this specific move, or for the more general phenomenon of a good practice being withheld because others might not adopt it? I do not want a bare label. What I want is a carving: what exactly the phenomenon is and is not, which of the nearby concepts — the prisoner's dilemma, the race to the bottom, the tragedy of the commons, the alignment tax, or whatever else you think is nearer — fits it best, and where each of the others falls short. If nothing established covers it, I would like a term with a definition and a few boundary cases, including cases that look similar but should be excluded. And please say whether the one-way application I described belongs to the phenomenon itself or is merely a rhetorical habit that often accompanies it.
