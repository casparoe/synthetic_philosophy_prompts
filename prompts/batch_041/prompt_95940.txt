You are a philosopher of history and archival theorist with a working knowledge of how record-keeping systems have served states, churches, and employers. I would like your help naming something.

I have spent two years in parish registers, quarter-session files, and hearth-tax rolls for a rural community, and a pattern keeps recurring for which I cannot find the right word. Everything I know about these people reaches me through an institution that had business with them: baptisms, fines, assessments, hiring contracts, removal orders, the notice a labourer had to give before leaving. Their own arrangements — the feast, the boundary dispute settled without lawyers, the craft, the song — surface only at the point where they crossed a ledger. Two consequences trouble me. First, the documentation is thickest exactly where the community was most closely governed, so anyone who weighs evidence by volume will find these people most numerous, most devout, and most disorderly in the very decades when they were most watched. Second, the only slots the paperwork offers me are the institution's: I can describe them as pauper, vagrant, recusant, or servant, but not in their own terms. And where they did leave writing of their own, it was almost always composed for an outsider — a deposition, a petition, a statement taken down by a clerk, a mark made in place of a signature.

Is there a name for this? I know about silences in the archive, but that seems to concern what is missing; what I am describing is a systematic distortion of what is present, produced by the fact that appearing in the record at all was something one did on an institution's terms. I also know about survivorship bias, but that concerns what lasts rather than the conditions on which a thing was admitted to the record in the first place. So: what are the nearest established concepts, how exactly does each fall short of the thing I have described, and does any of them fit it exactly? If none does, coin a term for it, define it, and mark its edges — say what would have to be true of a source for the pattern to be absent, whether it persists when the record-keeper belongs to the community or the institution is a benign one, and how it relates to the familiar advice to read the archive against the grain. If what I have described is really two or three distinct phenomena bundled together, separate them and name each.
