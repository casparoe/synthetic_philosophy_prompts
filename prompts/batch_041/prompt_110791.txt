I'm a second-year PhD student, and this term I'm the teaching assistant for a mid-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of computation. The instructor is away at a conference this week and has handed me the short-essay section of the midterm to mark; I've read this one twice and would like a careful second opinion before I put a grade on it, not least because the student has already emailed to ask what the marking standard is. The essay prompt was: "Explain the epistemic status of the Church–Turing thesis. Is it a theorem, a definition, an empirical hypothesis, or something else? Choose one of the three readings we distinguished in week 4 and defend it against the strongest objection you can find to it." The three readings, as the students were given them, were these: the *stipulative* reading, on which "effectively calculable" is simply defined as "Turing computable," so that the thesis records a terminological decision rather than a discovery; the *empirical* reading, on which the thesis is a substantive, contingent claim about what can in fact be computed, answerable to evidence and in principle refutable by a counterexample; and the *explication* reading, on which the thesis asserts that a particular formal notion adequately captures an informal one, and draws its support from the fact that several formally different explications all picked out the same class of functions. Students had 45 minutes and a 600-word limit.

Here is the answer, in full:

"The Church–Turing thesis states that every effectively calculable function — one that a person could compute by following a finite set of rules, without insight or ingenuity — is computable by a Turing machine. I will argue for the empirical reading, with the explication reading as a close second, and against the stipulative reading.

The strongest evidence for the thesis is that three formalisms arrived at independently — Turing machines, the lambda calculus, and the general recursive functions — all define exactly the same class of functions. Turing machines were built to model what a human calculator does; the lambda calculus came out of the foundations of logic; the recursive functions came out of work on the formalisation of arithmetic. Each was designed for a different purpose, and each turned out to draw the same boundary. If the informal notion of effective calculability were vague or indeterminate, this agreement would be a coincidence, so the convergence of three independent approaches on one class is best explained by the thesis being true. This is the sort of support we give a scientific hypothesis, and it is why the thesis should be read as an empirical claim.

The thesis is not a theorem. Its left-hand side is informal, and no proof can connect an informal notion to a formal one; the equivalence results are theorems about formal systems, and they presuppose the thesis rather than establish it. Nor is it a stipulation. If 'effectively calculable' just meant 'Turing computable', the thesis would be true by convention and would tell us nothing we could discover to be false, and it would be odd to call Turing's analysis a major intellectual achievement if all he had done was legislate a use of words. Besides, the fact that no counterexample has ever been produced, and that every candidate has turned out to be either non-effective or programmable, shows that we are dealing with a substantive claim about the limits of computation.

The obvious objection to the empirical reading is that we could never recognise a counterexample: to refute the thesis we would need a function that a human can compute and no machine can, and we cannot tell a genuine limit of machines from our own failure to write the right program. I think this objection is weaker than it looks. If someone were trained well enough to decide the halting problem for arbitrary machines, that would refute the thesis on the spot, and there is nothing in principle to stop us from discovering that we have that capacity. The thesis is therefore a contingent claim about human cognitive powers, and it is at least coherent to suppose that it is false."
