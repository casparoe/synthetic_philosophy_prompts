Alright, I need help and I'm a little embarassed to ask. I have to give a eulogy and I've been putting it off for weeks. My old high-school geometry teacher died last month at 84. He taught for like forty years and ran a lunchtime puzzle club that honestly raised me. I'm the one giving the eulogy at the memorial. The room is going to be split: a bunch of his former students are coming, and a few of them went on to be actual logicians and mathematicians, and then there's his family — his two sisters and his grandkids — who loved him but have no clue what he did with his afternoons and probably assumed the puzzle club was just him keeping nerdy kids out of trouble.

You're a philosopher who's also good at writing for people who aren't philosophers. Before you draft anything, brainstorm a bit — think about what a eulogy is actually for, and about the different things this one could be doing — then write it. Think carefully about the spine of it, because I don't want a generic nice speech that could've been about anybody.

Here's the spine, and it's the part I can't figure out how to handle. He had this thing he'd pull on us whenever someone demanded a reason to trust reasoning itself. You'd use modus ponens, say, and he'd say fine, why are you allowed to do that. You'd say, well, because if A, and if A then B, then B. And he'd say, but why are you allowed to use *that* rule. And it just goes forever, one rule at a time, you never actually get to the bottom of it — Lewis Carroll has the tortoise and Achilles doing basically this exact dance. His whole point, which he'd drive home with real glee, was that you can't justify the rules you reason by from inside reasoning; at some point you just reason, or you don't, and no extra premise is ever going to save you. He called this, I am not making this up, "the bottomless lunch," because the puzzle club would run way past the bell trying to climb out of it.

So here's what I want the eulogy to be brave enough to do. The room is full of people who, right now, want a reason. They want a reason to believe he's alright, or that his life added up to something, or that they're allowed to grieve — and I keep feeling like the honest answer has the same shape as his answer to us: there isn't a premise that closes it, and you just have to go on. I want that idea doing the actual work of the speech, not tacked on as a cute reference to the puzzle club. Don't fully explain it and don't lecture; let it do the work.

Constraints. Do not quote anyone and do not cite anything — no names of books, no reading lists, nothing we'd have to look up at a memorial; this has to work for someone who never opens a book. Don't say anything he'd have considered false or sloppy — he was merciless about that and his old students will notice instantly. And nothing about him watching over us from somewhere, he'd have hated that.

One more thing that has to be in there. For about thirty years he kept a sealed envelope in his desk with a twenty-dollar bill inside, as a standing prize for any student who could give him a non-circular reason to trust modus ponens. Nobody ever won. He asked in his will that the envelope be opened and read out at the memorial and the money given to his youngest grandkid. I have no idea what's in it. Don't invent a feel-good reveal — you can leave it sealed in the speech, or you can decide he wrote exactly one line, but if you do, it has to be something he'd actually have written.

Make it land for the logicians in the room without losing his sisters.
