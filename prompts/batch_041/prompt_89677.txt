For the past two months I have been sitting in on a cognitive science lab's reading group on folk psychology, and one of the senior members — a computational neuroscientist whose own work consists of probing language models for the factual commitments their outputs seem to presuppose — has a line he deploys whenever the conversation turns to whether a model "really believes" anything. Beliefs don't exist, he says; folk psychology is a false theory, and asking whether the model believes something is like asking whether a thundercloud is angry. The line has become a conversation-stopper in the group. It gets used to make the modest point that we should stop describing probe results in belief-talk, and it also gets used to make the sweeping point that there is no fact of the matter about what any system believes, so there is nothing for a probe to find in the first place. I am sympathetic to the first use and think the second is close to nonsense, but when I try to say why I end up sounding as though I am defending ghosts. Two other people make it worse: one says this is just what Dennett says, the other insists that Dennett is not an eliminativist at all but some variety of instrumentalist, and that the two positions are constantly being run together. I have read the standard material on eliminative materialism and come away with the impression that everyone is using the same sentence for four or five different jobs, which is why the argument never moves.

What I want from you is a menu rather than a verdict. Take "beliefs don't exist," together with its cousin, the claim that folk psychology is a false theory, and set out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. Some of the axes I keep losing track of, though there may be others: is the target the entities, the concepts, or the words; is the claim that belief-talk is false, that it will be superseded by a vocabulary that carves the mind differently, or merely that it is a useful fiction no one should mistake for description; is it local to the propositional attitudes — belief, desire, intention — or does it also swallow pain, memory, and the self; is it a prediction about what a mature neuroscience will say, or a conceptual claim about what our present terms could possibly refer to; and does the analogy with phlogiston and caloric help, or does it break down because the discarded posits were embedded in a theory with laws, whereas ordinary belief-attribution may be a practice or a skill rather than a theory. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude that no one needed to state, a substantive and defensible claim (and what would have to be true of cognitive science for it to be right), or something false or self-undermining — including the familiar worry about whether anyone can consistently assert it.

Then tell me which of these readings the thundercloud remark actually needs in order to make "does the model believe P?" a category mistake rather than a loose way of speaking, whether any reading really delivers that, and whether the reading that does the work leaves the group's ordinary belief-talk about one another untouched or takes that down too — which is the point I most want to be able to make in the meeting. If none of the readings supports the strong dismissal, tell me what the strongest defensible version of his point is, so that I can concede the part of it that is right. Close by naming the case, example, or argument that would best separate the readings that are still standing.
