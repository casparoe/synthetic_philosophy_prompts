I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who is finishing a master's in economics and has been reading his way through decision theory, and I came away unable to tell how much of what he told me was the position and how much was his own gloss. What he said, as best I can reconstruct it: expected utility theory is committed to risk-neutrality, because once the agent's utilities are fixed, rationality just consists in weighting each outcome's extra utility by its probability; Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility, in Risk and Rationality (2013), relaxes this by adding a third ingredient alongside beliefs and desires, a risk function that maps probabilities to weights and encodes the agent's global attitude toward risk, with the identity function recovering ordinary expected utility. He drew the risk function on the back of a receipt and wrote something like REU(L) = Σ r(p_i)u(x_i) — I may have copied it down badly — and said this is why the Allais chooser can prefer a sure OK to a gamble offering a 10% chance of something better at the price of a 1% chance of something bad: she gives the 1% chance more weight than its probability warrants.

He then drew three conclusions that I am not sure follow from any of that. First, that the risk function is really just a distortion of the agent's probabilities — she behaves as if the bad outcome were likelier than it is — so REU is a cousin of prospect theory and in fact the same mathematics as the Choquet-style models in which people's beliefs are non-additive; on his telling, a distorted probability measure and a risk function are two descriptions of one thing. Second, that since the risk function is a free parameter that can take any shape, REU abandons Savage's sure-thing principle and thereby certifies any preference pattern as rational — it cannot criticize anyone — which is why economists treat it as a descriptive model of how people choose rather than a theory of how they ought to, and why Buchak's real contribution is empirical, a better predictor of Allais-type behaviour than expected utility. Third, and this is the one that struck me as odd, that her risk aversion turns out to be diminishing marginal utility under another name, so there is less here than the book's reputation suggests.

I would like an audit rather than a summary. Take his claims one at a time — including the formula I wrote down, which I suspect is not what she actually says — and tell me which a decision theorist would simply accept, which they would wince at as a simplification that loses something, and which are wrong; then tell me how someone defending Buchak would reply to the parts that are wrong, and whether his three conclusions survive once the rest is corrected. The thing I most want sorted out is the interpretive question in his first conclusion: whether reading the risk function as a distortion of belief is a legitimate way of taking the formalism — the sort of thing you would say if you were reconstructing her theory in the vocabulary of Choquet expected utility — or whether it gets the agent's own epistemic state wrong in a way she would reject. I am inclined to think he is right that it is ultimately descriptive, since someone I know who works in behavioural economics told me much the same thing, but I would rather not accept that on two people's say-so. I am not going to chase down the literature, so do not bother with citations.
