I coordinate the mediation panel at a legal-aid clinic in a mid-sized city. We take about forty two-party cases a year — two-person businesses dissolving, cohabiting couples separating with shared property, siblings fighting over a parent's estate — and each case gets a single two-hour session with a volunteer mediator who has no economics or game-theory training. The stakes are small but concrete: a van, a client list, a piano, a care obligation, a few thousand dollars. Our intake form asks each party to spread 100 points across the disputed items and across the alternatives they would fall back on if we fail, and it records what actually happens if there is no agreement — small claims court, the status quo, whatever is already in that party's own name. A colleague who used to teach economics has proposed that we stop haggling item by item and instead compute a single recommended split from those forms using the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution: the point at which the straight line from the disagreement point to the ideal point — what each side would receive if it got the most it could conceivably receive — crosses the frontier of what is jointly possible. Her argument is that the recommendation would then be invariant to how we scale the points, would not depend on which party we happen to call player one, and, unlike the Nash solution, would give a side a better share whenever that side's opportunities improve, at the price of abandoning the requirement that options nobody chooses can be ignored. I am half-convinced, and I want to know whether the thing survives contact with our actual cases.

Please write the procedure my volunteers would run, and make it something they could execute in twenty minutes with the two intake forms in front of them. I need the questions asked in order, what counts as an answer to each, where the procedure must stop and hand the decision back to the parties because the question is one of values rather than arithmetic, and where the numbers are judgment calls dressed up as measurement. Then run it on two cases. The first is a design studio being dissolved by its two founders: cash on hand, a client list, the studio's name, and a two-year non-compete to be allocated between them, with one founder holding a much stronger fallback because a former client has already offered her work. The second is three siblings dividing a house, a car, and the obligation to pay for a parent's care, where the house cannot be divided and one sibling has been living in it rent-free.

I am prepared to be told the proposal is a mistake. If the recommendation will shift when we add an option nobody would ever take, or will systematically hand the party with the better fallback the larger absolute share, or will fail outright once a third party is at the table, say so plainly rather than working around it, and tell me what to do instead. Keep it under 900 words: the procedure first, then the two worked cases, then a short list of what the procedure cannot do.
