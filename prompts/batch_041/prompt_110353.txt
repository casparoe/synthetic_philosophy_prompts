I run a 90-minute session for our undergrad philosophy of philanthropy reading group — about 18 students, mixed econ and philosophy, and it's the one meeting where I want them doing something with their hands rather than arguing about a paper. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel the force of the importance / tractability / neglectedness framework, the one they've all absorbed from the effective-altruism blogosphere and can recite in their sleep, and specifically the moment where it stops being a decision rule and turns into a machine for laundering whatever they already wanted to fund. I want the mechanics doing the work: give them a fixed pot with a little real sting to it (say $200 of the group's own budget, or if that's a hassle, a pot of points that decides who picks the pizza place every week for the semester — I want them to care a non-trivial amount), a menu of maybe six causes that I invent myself so they can't just look up the answer, and rules that force them to score each cause on the three criteria and combine the scores into a single number. Build the trap in: two of the causes should come out near-identical on the stated criteria but differ on a fourth thing they aren't told about at first — one of them can't absorb more money past a low ceiling (the people running it would more or less sit on the cash), and the other is neglected for the specific reason that a well-informed attempt at it already failed and the failure is on the record. And I want at least one cause where the three criteria pull apart so that the combined score recommends something nobody in the room actually believes in, so they have to fight about whether the number or their gut is the thing that's wrong.

The sequence should let them walk into the problems themselves before I name anything: that neglectedness is as often a reason for suspicion as for excitement (why has nobody done this? sometimes the answer is because the people who knew most judged it hopeless), that the three criteria aren't independent (a cause can look intractable precisely because it's neglected, so scoring it low on tractability double-counts the neglect), and that importance is hiding an enormous amount of contested moral weight — if one of the causes is farmed animal welfare and another is a scholarship fund for kids in our own town, the framework hands them no way to compare the units, and I want them to hit that wall live, in the activity, rather than nodding at it during my summary. Include roles or rules, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and the debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the real structure: room for more funding, the counterfactual question of what would have happened with this money anyway, and the objection that we can't know the long-run effects of anything, so the whole scoring exercise may be guesswork dressed up as arithmetic (someone in my department calls that cluelessness, if that helps you locate it). Then tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — my guess is that one student refuses to play on moral grounds, another games the point system, and the room burns twenty minutes relitigating a single cause. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce and that I'll definately have to supply myself: I don't think any 90-minute game can convey the real magnitude of long-run effects, or the genuine uncertainty about whether the framework's inputs are even estimable, and I suspect the moral-weight problem is something the exercise can only dramatize rather than resolve. I'd rather the writeup say so than pretend the game covers it.
