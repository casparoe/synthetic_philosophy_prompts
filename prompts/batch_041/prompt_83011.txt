I'm taking a module on interpretation and meaning this term, and I'm writing a seminar paper on a point the standard treatment slides past. The standard treatment runs roughly like this: the radical interpreter has only behaviour to work with, the principle of charity constrains which beliefs and meanings may be attributed, and indeterminacy is then declared harmless, on the grounds that two rival manuals compatible with all the evidence are either the same manual in a different notation or a difference that makes no difference. I want to argue that this resolution depends on a feature of the examples everyone reaches for, and that once that feature is gone the indeterminacy is genuine. I need a case built to order to carry the argument, and I would rather not use the rabbit.

Please construct one to the following specifications.

The setup. One ordinary adult speaker, in an ordinary setting, whose language is being interpreted from scratch by someone with no bilingual informant, no shared history and no prior access. No exotic framing of the speaker, no aliens, no feral children, no brains in vats, no telepathy, and no stipulation about the speaker's inner life. The situation should be describable in a few sentences.

The manuals. Supply two complete interpretive manuals for this speaker, each assigning meanings to expressions and attributing beliefs and desires, and each answerable to exactly the same evidence: every disposition the speaker has to assent or dissent, under every condition you care to specify. Both manuals must make the speaker come out largely rational and largely right by our lights, and you must argue that neither is more charitable than the other rather than simply asserting it.

The divergence. The manuals must disagree about what the speaker's utterances mean, and the disagreement must reach the level of truth conditions, so that it cannot be discharged as a change of notation. Three constructions are excluded because they are the easy cases: manuals related by a systematic permutation of the objects the speaker is talking about; manuals that differ only in the reference assigned to sub-sentential expressions while agreeing sentence by sentence; and manuals that express the same content in a different vocabulary.

The persistence condition. The divergence must survive any extension of the evidence. It must not be the sort of disagreement that more observation, more speakers or a longer corpus would settle. Explain why, for your case, no possible evidence distinguishes the manuals.

Then do the following.

1. Say what each manual commits its interpreter to, and locate the difference precisely — in the meanings assigned to particular expressions, in the logical form attributed to particular sentences, in the beliefs attributed, or in some combination. Be explicit about the trade-off that makes the divergence possible.

2. State what a Davidsonian must say about the case. If the reply is that one manual fails charity or is not really a theory of meaning, say exactly which constraint it fails and why; if the reply is that the manuals are after all intertranslatable, exhibit the translation. Then state what a Quinean would say, and what difference the two replies make to whether there is a fact of the matter about what the speaker means.

3. Identify the one feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens when it is removed — in particular whether removing it collapses the case back into the harmless kind of indeterminacy, and why.

4. Say how the position that comes out worse would attack your case: what the strongest objection is, and whether a version of the case survives it.
