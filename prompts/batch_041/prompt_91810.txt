I've been teaching Lakatos to CS grad students for a few years and I keep catching myself explaining him in machine learning terms, which I half know — I can read an ML paper, I've run my share of ablations, I understand what a scaling law is, but I'm a philosopher, not a practitioner. So do it properly for me: take Lakatos on research programmes — hard core, protective belt, positive and negative heuristic, progressive vs degenerating problem shifts, novel facts, no instant rationality — and restate the whole thing in the vocabulary an ML researcher actually uses: model families and their inductive biases, architecture commitments, training pipelines, auxiliary engineering assumptions, benchmark suites and their saturation, ablations, the difference between patching a system after it fails and making a change that predicts something you didn't build it for. Then the residue report. What maps cleanly, what only maps with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all. I'm especially interested in the normative bit: Lakatos is telling us how to appraise a programme as rational or not, and I want to know whether that survives as a question an ML person would recognize, or whether it just evaporates into whether the thing worked on the benchmark. If it evaporates, is that ML's vocabulary missing something, or is it Lakatos dressing up hindsight as a criterion? And don't let my own mappings through on the nod — if the hard core isn't really the inductive bias of an architecture family, or the protective belt isn't really the eval harness you quietly re-tune, say so. Same for the historiographical side: does Lakatos's distinction between rational reconstruction and actual history have any analogue in how ML papers rewrite their own lineage, or is that where the translation definately breaks down?
