I am an undergraduate representative on my university's Committee on Ceremonies and Symbols, which meets next week to decide the fate of the Lantern Walk, and I am responsible for drafting and defending the written opinion I will submit to the committee. Please draft it.

The committee's materials establish the following. The Lantern Walk is a candlelit procession held each October and described in every alumni brochure and on the university's website as "our oldest tradition, observed without interruption since the college's founding in 1874." Two months ago a graduate student in history produced archival evidence that the Walk was in fact invented in 1967 by a dean of development, who scripted the route, designed the lanterns, and wrote the "ancient" invocation recited at the close; the ceremony was launched as part of a fundraising drive, and there is no trace of it before that year. The same report observes that the invocation borrows imagery from a regional Indigenous harvest ceremony. Since then a major donor has written to the president stating that his pledged gift is contingent on the Walk being preserved "as the tradition that connects us to our founding," while the alumni association has circulated a memo arguing that the Walk's value lies in what it means to people now, not in how old it is.

Our chair has circulated a draft "Continuity Standard" for judging such cases, with three tests: transmission (has the practice been handed on by its participants, or merely maintained by administrators?), continuity (is there an unbroken chain of observance?), and function (does it still perform the work for which it was created?). I should apply this standard explicitly, though I am not bound by it.

In my course on history and memory we have been reading Hobsbawm and Ranger on invented tradition and Anderson on imagined communities, and a seminar discussion left me with a proposal I would like you to take seriously: what a classmate called "reparative authenticity" — the thought that a fabricated tradition can become genuinely ours if it is openly re-founded on acknowledged invention rather than on a false claim of antiquity.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend one concrete course of action (preserving the Walk as currently described, re-founding it under an honest account of its origin, retiring it, or some other option you judge best), specify what the committee should actually do at the meeting, and give the reasoning. It should engage seriously the strongest case for the position you reject — whether that is the alumni association's present-attachment argument or the donor's continuity argument — and say what, if anything, the committee owes the Indigenous community whose ceremony the invocation echoes. Do not include citations or a reading list; argue from the materials above.
