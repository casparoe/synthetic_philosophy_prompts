I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on coherent extrapolated volition — the idea that what's really good for someone is what they'd want if they knew a lot more, thought much faster, were wiser, and had their preferences made consistent with one another. I want to open the session with a dialogue that gets people past the hand waving, so please write one for me.

Two characters, both sharp, neither a patsy. One is a committed CEV person: the extrapolated preference is the authoritative one, so a lot of our hand-wringing about paternalism dissolves — if the extrapolated you wants X, X is what's really good for you, and your present resistance is just noise that the procedure is designed to correct for. The other thinks it's a shell game: the procedure doesn't discover a hidden preference, it manufactures one, and it can only ever launder the values of whoever sets up the idealization.

Two things I want them to actually fight about rather than gesture at:

1. The revelatory-vs-transformative problem. Give them a concrete case: a guy who's spent 25 years hand-copying a text he half suspects is a forgery, and whose extrapolated self — fully informed, fully coherent — would drop it tomorrow and do something else entirely. The CEV person says the extrapolated preference wins, so we shouldn't, eg, fund his copying. The critic says the extrapolated self is a different person wearing his name, and that the point of his life is bound up with the very incoherence being corrected for — so idealization doesn't reveal what he cares about, it deletes it. Make them press each other on what's actually doing the work here: is the idealization a discovery procedure or a construction, and if it's a construction, can it be authoritative at all?

2. The aggregation mess in the collective version. Suppose you extrapolate two people's preferences and they still conflict — in fact the idealization sharpens the conflict, since now neither of them can plead ignorance or confusion. So what was extrapolation supposed to buy us politically? Does CEV make interpersonal conflict easier to resolve, or does it just relabel it? Let the CEV person have a real reply (maybe convergence is supposed to happen at the level of what to want rather than which of two incompatible wants gets satisfied, or maybe it's a decision procedure for a designer and not a theory of right), then let the critic show why that reply either collapses back into ordinary politics or makes CEV vacuous.

Also have them hit the regress at least once: to extrapolate you need a criterion of improvement — coherence? information? which information? — and any such criterion is itself a value, so the procedure looks like it presupposes the very thing it was meant to ground. Don't let either of them just assert that this is or isn't fatal; make them trade actual arguements about whether the regress is vicious or benign.

No strawmen. The critic has to state the CEV view in a way the CEV person accepts before attacking it, and the CEV person should land at least one point the critic has to concede. Around 1200 words, no citations, no names of philosophers — just the argument.
