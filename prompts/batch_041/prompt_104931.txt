I sit on the research ethics committee at our medical school and I have to write the decision memo for a protocol we're voting on next week, and I'd rather it not be another "approved with conditions, see attached."

The study is on a hereditary metabolic disorder — treatable if it's caught at birth, usually fatal in the first year or so if it isn't — that clusters in an extended family network in a rural district a few hours from here. The team did the community engagement properly, and the traditional council has effectively said yes on behalf of the community. But they've attached a condition of their own: they want the council's consent to count as the consent, meaning no individual opt-out. The PI relayed their reasoning roughly as: a person here is a person through their relations, your standing comes from belonging, and you owe your kin. If one branch of the family refuses to be counted, the pedigree has holes, carriers in that branch go undetected, and those children die. So refusing isn't just a private choice on their view — it's a failure of the very thing that makes you a person. The PI is sympathetic and keeps calling this the respectful, non-colonial way to handle consent.

The complication is that not everyone agrees the council speaks for them. A group of younger people, and some of the women, wrote to us saying that if carrier status gets returned to the family it will wreck their marriage prospects, and that the council doesn't get to trade that away on their behalf. So now there's a communal claim and an individual claim, and both are being made in the name of basically the same idea about persons being constituted by their relations.

I'm torn and so is the committee. One reading: rejecting the communal model here is just imposing a Western individualist picture of the person and pretending it's neutral. The other: a view that persons are made by their relations is exactly the view that should protect the person the group is about to override — if my personhood lives in my relations, then a community treating me as a means is failing at the thing it claims to be. Also, the council's position seems to slide between describing how things are done and claiming how persons ought to be treated, and I can't tell if those come apart here.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. I want a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with specific conditions — with a realistic time frame, and I want the strongest version of whatever I'd be rejecting dealt with honestly rather than waved at. Tell me what the recommendation commits us to the next time a protocol like this comes through, because I don't want to write something we can only apply once. And please actually engage the idea that personhood is relational rather than assuming it's either sacred or disposable — the deadline is Thursday and I need to send this round to the committee tonight.
