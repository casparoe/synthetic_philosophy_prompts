I am a high school student preparing a position paper for my school's mathematics and philosophy seminar, and I have chosen to write on the strategy-stealing assumption. My understanding of it is this: in a game whose two players begin with the same options, we suppose that the second player has a guaranteed winning strategy, let the first player make an arbitrary opening move, and then have the first player follow that very strategy; because the extra opening move cannot be a disadvantage, the first player is now guaranteed to win as well, which is absurd, so the second player never had a winning strategy to begin with. In Hex — the game in which each player tries to connect their two opposite sides of a rhombus of hexagonal cells, and in which a draw is impossible — this is taken to prove that the first player wins, even though it exhibits no strategy.

I would like you to argue for the following position. The strategy-stealing assumption is not, despite appearances, an assumption about symmetry, and the argument that rests on it is not a symmetry argument. The premise doing the real work is the separate claim that a player's own extra move can never be a disadvantage; a second premise, that the game cannot end in a draw, is needed to convert the conclusion that the second player cannot win into the conclusion that the first player does win; symmetry itself does no more than let the two players exchange roles. It follows that the name is a misnomer, and that the argument's conclusion should be read narrowly, as a result about a restricted class of games rather than about games as such.

In making the case, please do all of the following.

1. State the premises of the argument and identify which step of the reasoning depends on which premise, showing where the reasoning collapses if a premise is dropped.

2. Invent a small game, state its rules precisely enough that a reader could play it, and use it to show the strategy-stealing reasoning failing because the first player's extra move is genuinely harmful. An artificial game is perfectly acceptable; a misère or zugzwang-flavoured one is likely to be easiest to construct.

3. State the strongest objection to this position that you can, and either answer it or concede the part of it that succeeds. One candidate worth weighing: both premises are necessary and neither can be said to do the work on its own, so the position reduces to the uninteresting observation that an argument needs all of its premises.

4. Close with a verdict on what the strategy-stealing argument is: a theorem about symmetric games, a theorem about games that are monotone in a player's own moves, or a non-constructive existence proof whose content is exhausted by the fact that some winning strategy exists.

5. Add one paragraph on whether the same assumption is at work in the non-game settings where people say that whoever moves first can simply appropriate whatever a later competitor would have done, and on where that analogy strains.

There is no need for citations, references, or suggested reading; I care about the argument rather than its provenance. Please write in continuous prose rather than bullet points.
