I'm slowly working my way through William James's "The Moral Equivalent of War" (1910) and I keep getting hung up on the opening. He writes: "The war against war is going to be no holiday excursion or camping party. The military feelings are too deeply grounded to abdicate their place among our ideals until better substitutes are offered than the glory and shame that come to nations as well as to individuals from the ups and downs of politics and the vicissitudes of trade." Then a little further down: "Showing war's irrationality and horror is of no effect upon him. The horrors make the fascination."

What I can't work out is what he's actually argueing. On the face of it the peace advocate seems to be conceding the whole case to the war party -- that war is where our ideals of heroism come from, and that you can't shame or reason people out of it. So is "better substitutes" doing all the work, and a substitute for what, exactly? The part about glory and shame coming from "politics and the vicissitudes of trade" is what confuses me most: is he saying those already give us a substitute and it's just not good enough, or that they're a pale shadow of what war supplies? And is "the horrors make the fascination" a claim about human psychology or is it more of a rhetorical move? I'm not after sources or a reading list, just help understanding the passage. Please answer in American English.
