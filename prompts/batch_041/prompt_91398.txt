Okay so I'm definately writing the final for my upper-level seminar on epistemic injustice — mostly juniors and seniors, a couple of MA students sitting in — and I've got a problem. Two of my three questions are basically "explain the testimonial/hermeneutical distinction," and every year alot of the class just recites the definitions back at me and coasts to a B+. So for the third question I want something that actually punishes that. Can you draft it, along with a model answer and a marking scheme?

I want it built on a case I made up, so nobody can look it up, and nobody needs to cite anything — so the model answer shouldn't lean on anyones name except Fricker's, and shouldn't need dates. Here's the case:

For about fifteen years, people with a certain chronic illness had no word for what was happening to them. Their fatigue and sensory crashes didn't show up on any test, so they got shuffled between neurology and psychiatry and mostly landed on an anxiety diagnosis. Then a clinician who had the condition herself but hadn't disclosed it published a paper naming it and offering a mechanism. She called it a disorder of "attention and arousal amplification": the symptoms are real, but they're produced by the patient's own hypervigilance. Meanwhile patients had been calling it "the drag" among themselves and describing it as an exertion-triggered collapse that fluctuates day to day. The clinical term won. Insurers use it, most patients now use it too, and the patients' own description gets filed as the lay version of the expert one. So they finally have a name — but the name comes with a story about their attention being the problem, and that story is what decides who gets disability payments.

The question should force three moves: (1) whether this counts as hermeneutical injustice on Fricker's own definition, and where that definition strains — in particular whether a wrong can live in the *content* of the concept that fills a gap rather than in the gap itself; (2) keeping testimonial injustice distinct from hermeneutical injustice while saying what the case shows about how the two feed each other; and (3) evaluating a fix one of my students proposed last term — that we should call something hermeneutical injustice whenever a group's own concepts get overridden, "no matter who is prejudiced against whom." Is that too broad? If it is, what's the narrowest amendment that still catches this case?

Give me the question as the students would see it (75 minutes, closed book, essay), a model answer of about 700 words that would actually earn an A, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points worth 25 marks total — and for each point, one line on how students typically miss it. American English throughout, and write it like I talk; I'm pretty informal in handouts.
