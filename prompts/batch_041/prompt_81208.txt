I'm the research-integrity and dual-use person at a mid-sized university — basically a one person office. I get maybe six to ten cases a year where a lab wants to publish a method or run a study that plenty of other labs could also have done, and the committee wants my read in about two weeks, with no staff and no power to actually stop anyone. Almost everything I see lately has the same shape and it's the unilateralist's curse, which I half understand and half dont. As I've got it: when a bunch of people could each independently do a thing, whether it gets done is settled by whoever is most optimistic or least cautious, not by the average or the majority — so the more people who could do it, and the less certain anyone is about the payoff, the closer to certain it becomes that it gets done, good or bad. Great as a diagnosis, useless at my desk. "Someone will probably do it anyway" seems to cut both ways: it's an argument for going ahead (if it's going to happen, better here, where I can insist on the safeguards) and an argument for holding off (I'd be the trigger, and my doing it also shows everyone else that it's doable and respectable). I also can't tell when the curse is actually a problem — if twenty groups could each publish the same finding that a widely used product is unsafe, I want one of them to, and the fact that it'll probably happen isn't a reason to stop. And I don't trust my own estimate, since the person asking me is the person who thought of it and I'm the one being asked, which selects for enthusiasm. Someone once told me the fix is a rule like "go ahead only if you'd be fine with everyone in your position going ahead too," but that just seems to restate the problem — obviously fine for some cases, obviously not for others.

So: you're someone who actually works on collective action and decision theory and has advised real people rather than just written papers about it, and I want a procedure I can run. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a clear no or a clear yes, and where it hands the thing back to a human because the call is about risk appetite or values rather than facts. It has to be usable by someone who's never read anything about this, runnable in an afternoon, and honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure. It also has to cope with the two cases I'm actually sitting on: a lab that wants to publish a method making a dangerous thing much easier to make, whose main argument is "someone else will, and we'd be more careful"; and a researcher who has evidence that a widely used product is unsafe and could either sit on it or go public, where the usual worry about acting unilaterally seems to point the other way. My guess is the first comes out "hold" and the second "publish" — if your procedure lands differently, don't soften it to match me. No citations, no reading lists, no "it depends."

Before you write, think it through: who's acting, how many others could, whether it's reversible, whether the value of the action goes up or down as more people do it, whether you'd be setting a precedent or lowering a barrier for everyone else, and who eats the cost if you're wrong. Then give me exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion, each a short paragraph: 1) the features that have to be present for the curse to even apply, i.e. how I tell a curse-shaped decision from an ordinary one; 2) the ordered questions themselves, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; 3) the stop rules, the points where it hands off to a human, and what to do when I can't get a number; 4) the two cases run through it end to end, including whichever one gives me an answer I won't like; 5) where this misfires and what it can't do.
