I am a student in a seminar on Kierkegaard and nineteenth-century Danish thought, and I am writing a term paper on the Corsair affair, which I would like to treat as a case study rather than as biography. The episode, as I understand it: in December 1845 Kierkegaard, writing under one of his pseudonyms in the newspaper Fædrelandet, publicly asked to be taken up and abused by the Copenhagen satirical weekly Corsaren, the paper published by Meïr Aron Goldschmidt, whose pieces were unsigned and whose editorial identity was deliberately slippery. The paper obliged, and through 1846 it ran a campaign of caricature and mockery aimed at Kierkegaard's person, his figure, his clothing, and his daily walks through the city, so that he became a figure any passer-by could point out. He did not retreat or answer in kind; he kept publishing throughout, and he later remarked that he could divide his life into the time before Corsaren and the time after it. What interests me is that the theory of the press and the public through which we now read this episode was itself being written while the campaign was running: the review of the present age that he brought out in March 1846.

I want the affair analyzed through that theory, in both directions. The elements I have are these: levelling, understood as an abstract process that no individual performs, so that it is impossible to point to a leveller; the public, described as a phantom, an abstraction that is never present anywhere, never acts, and therefore can never be held responsible for anything; a distinction between the public and the crowd, since a crowd is at least a concrete gathering that can act; the claim that the press is the organ through which the public is conjured up and levelling gets done; and the rule that a writer who means to do anyone any good must address the single individual, since the public cannot be addressed at all.

Three questions, in order. First, the mapping: who or what is doing the levelling here, Goldschmidt, the paper's anonymity, or the reading public that consumed the caricatures? Does the crowd/public distinction survive contact with the case, or does the case collapse it? Second, the responsibility claim: if the public is a phantom incapable of responsibility, what is the status of a harm that was perfectly concrete, delivered by identifiable if unsigned hands, to a man who could be pointed at in the street? Does anonymity dissolve responsibility, or does it redistribute it? Third, and hardest: Kierkegaard solicited the attack. He asked in print to be scolded, and the single individual who insisted that one address only the single individual went out of his way to be processed by the machine of the public. Is that a contradiction the theory cannot absorb, or is it precisely what the theory predicts, that in an age of levelling becoming a single individual may require passing through the very abstraction one condemns? Say where the theory is silent rather than making it speak, and close with a verdict: what does the affair show about the theory, a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated? Argue it; I do not need a reading list.
