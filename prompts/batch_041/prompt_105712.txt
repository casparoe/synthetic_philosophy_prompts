My office is finally retiring the vending machine in the break room — the big ugly one that's been there since before anyone can remember, the one that ate everyones quarters for twenty years and gave out the wrong snack so reliably that people started leaving it little notes. I'm doing the farewell speech at the goodbye party on Friday. I want it warm and funny for the whole floor, most of whom just love the thing as a piece of junk and don't want a philosophy lecture.

The complication is two people in the room have been arguing about that machine for a decade. One is a Searle type: whatever the machine seems to "want" is derived, borrowed from us, there's nothing actually going on inside it. The other is a Dennett type who says the two-bitser case — the machine that's an exact duplicate of a real one so you can't tell from its behavior — shows there's no principled, in-kind line between the machine's apparent intentions and ours, only a difference in complexity. They'll both be there. I don't want either of them to wince, and I don't want to say anything false just to be nice.

So help me write the speech: about three minutes spoken, warm enough for the non-philosophers, but doing the real work of saying something affectionate and true about a machine without either crediting it with original intentionality or pretending there's a clean way to deny it. Where's the line?
