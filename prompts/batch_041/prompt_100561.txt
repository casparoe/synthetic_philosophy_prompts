For the last few months a colleague and I have been circling the same disagreement in our philosophy of mind reading group, and I'd like to see it written out as an actual dialogue so that I can tell whether we're making contact or merely talking past each other. The shared background is the realist reading of real patterns: roughly, that a pattern is real when it earns its explanatory keep, when describing a system in those terms compresses and predicts its behaviour better than a description that refuses to invoke the pattern, and that the physical, design, and intentional stances are three such compressing vocabularies aimed at one and the same system. My colleague's contribution — which she is rather proud of, and which I cannot decide is a genuine refinement or a quiet abandonment of the position — is that the three stances are not flat alternatives but form a strict hierarchy of compression. The physical stance, on her telling, is the ground floor and the only description answerable to nothing but the system itself; each stance above it buys additional predictive leverage at the price of robustness, since the higher patterns are the easier ones to defeat by intervening on the system. She concludes that patterns are real all the way up, but that they come in degrees of reality: intentional patterns are real only derivatively, the way a shadow is real, resting on design-level facts, which in turn rest on physical ones. I think this inverts the whole point. The reason the intentional stance is worth taking is precisely that it is not reducible in practice to the level below; the fact that one could in principle predict the same behaviour from the physical stance given unlimited computation seems to me to show nothing about which patterns are real, and if anything the higher stance is the more real one, because it is the one that captures what the system is doing rather than merely what its parts are doing.

I'd like a dialogue between two philosophers who hold these two positions — her graded, hierarchical realism, and my flat, or perhaps inverted, realism — staged around a concrete case rather than in the abstract. Let them argue over a corporation: it can be described physically as the motions of its employees and equipment, at the design stance as an organization with a structure and functions, and at the intentional stance as a single agent with beliefs and intentions, and her view seems to entail that whatever we say about it as an agent is real only in a weaker sense than what we say about the molecules. Neither should be a strawman; both should be competent and should actually engage the other's points, conceding where a concession is warranted and pressing where the other has really committed to something. In particular, I want them to fight over the dispensability-in-principle argument, over whether compression can ever license ontological commitment or is merely a convenience, and over whether talk of degrees of reality is a coherent notion at all or just a way of smuggling the physical stance back in as the arbiter.

Please write it as continuous prose in the form of a real conversation, the way one might write up a seminar exchange in a letter to a friend. No headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables — just the dialogue and whatever minimal connective tissue it needs.
