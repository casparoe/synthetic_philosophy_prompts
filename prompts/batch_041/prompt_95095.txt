A colleague in my philosophy of mind seminar has reconstructed an argument for the conclusion that dreams are not experiences, and I cannot locate its error even though I am convinced it proves too much. The argument runs as follows. (1) Nothing about a dream is available to the dreamer, or to anyone else, except through the dreamer's waking report or waking memory. (2) If the only access we have to some alleged episode is a later report of it, then the report is the criterion for the episode: what makes it true that the episode occurred is that a report of the appropriate kind is sincerely given under normal conditions. (3) So what makes it true that someone dreamed is that she sincerely reports, on waking, having dreamed. (4) If what makes it true that someone dreamed is a waking report, then the dream is not an episode occurring during sleep; it is constituted by, or entirely consists in, the waking report and the memory it expresses. (5) Therefore dreams are not experiences that occur during sleep. (6) The same reasoning extends to any alleged mentation in sleep, since all of it is known only through waking reports; so there are no experiences during sleep at all. (7) But subjects in lucid dreams report continuous awareness and deliberate control during the episode, and can signal with prearranged eye movements while it is going on; if those reports are taken at face value, some experiences do occur during sleep. (8) Hence either lucid dreams are not what they seem, or the argument fails somewhere.

My colleague adds, as motivation, that dreams are sometimes reported as incorporating a stimulus that occurred at or just before the moment of waking, with a long narrative leading up to the incorporation, and he says this makes it economical to suppose the whole narrative is assembled at waking. He accepts the first disjunct of (8) and takes the argument to show that the received view of dreams is confused. Dennett's suggestion that the dream narrative may be assembled at the moment of waking, rather than undergone in real time, is the kind of view that makes (5) seem less absurd to him. I would rather keep the reality of at least some sleep experience than keep (2) or (4), but I want to know what I am buying and what I am giving up. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the trouble is a slide between two senses of criterion, or between knowing and being, say exactly where the slide occurs and what work each premise is doing. If instead the argument is valid as it stands, tell me which of (1), (2), (4), or (7) is least costly to abandon and what the rest of the position looks like afterward. I am also interested in whether the real-time evidence from lucid dreaming can do independent work here, or whether it begs the question by relying on the very reports whose status is in dispute. Treat the argument as the object of diagnosis rather than as an occasion to survey the literature.
