I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology — fourteen students, one hundred minutes — and I want to run an activity that makes them feel, from the inside, why self-locating belief is a genuine problem rather than a puzzle about words. By the end they should be able to state precisely what the halfer and the thirder each claim about a Sleeping Beauty protocol, and, more importantly, they should have felt their own credence move when the framing changed. They should not hear the name of the problem, or the labels, until the debrief.

Design the activity so that the self-locating question arises out of what they do rather than out of what I say. I want you to work out the mechanics and the payoffs around the following structure.

Run the experiment for real, with a physical coin and real but trivial stakes — tokens that buy a small prize at the end, nothing anyone would mind losing, but something. Cast some students as the sleeper and some as the bookmaker. Follow the protocol faithfully: the sleeper is woken once if the coin lands heads, and twice, with the memory of the earlier awakening erased, if it lands tails; at each awakening the sleeper must stake tokens on how the coin landed, at odds the bookmaker posts beforehand.

Then run a second, non-standard condition: the same awakening protocol, but with a coin biased two-to-one in favour of heads. I want the group to have to compute the fair odds rather than recall them, and to discover that the halfers and the thirders among them disagree about the second case exactly as they did about the first — and that their intuitive staking does not match the odds that would make them break even.

Give me the rules and roles, the materials, the payoff scheme and how the tokens are settled, a round-by-round timeline for the hundred minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the formal distinction between self-locating and ordinary credence. Include a control condition of your own devising in which the number of awakenings is the same whichever way the coin lands, so that they can see what self-location is actually contributing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — I expect at least one bookmaker to post odds that make both sides lose, and at least one sleeper to refuse to stake at all — and how to handle each. Be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: it cannot settle the dispute, and there is something about the first-person character of the question that a staking game may simply paper over. Say how confident you are in the design, and single out the part of it you are least sure of.
