I belong to a small reading group that has been circling the question of whether habitat destruction, on balance, reduces or increases wild animal suffering, and we keep discovering that we disagree less about the ecology than about what we are being asked to compute. One of us says that clearing a forest is straightforwardly bad because it kills the animals living in it; another says the animals that die are mostly juveniles of highly fecund species that would in any case have been starved, parasitized, or eaten within weeks, so that what the clearing really does is cut short a vast number of lives that were unlikely to go well; a third says that if you count lives rather than deaths, a single hectare may hold billions of brief lives containing more suffering than satisfaction, so that the destruction of the habitat might score as an improvement on the ledger. Before we argue the case any further, I would like the specification first.

What I want from you, then, is the desiderata for a theory that could actually answer the question — a theory of net wild-animal welfare under habitat change, or what I have been calling a welfare ledger for a landscape. Set out what such a theory must deliver: which quantities it has to define (lives lived per unit of area and time, welfare per life, the proportion of lives worth living, deaths disaggregated by cause); which cases it has to get right (the clearing of a mature forest and its replacement by pasture, by plantation, or by secondary growth; the drainage of a wetland; the scraping of a seabed by trawling; an oil spill; a slow climatically driven shift in range); which distinctions it cannot collapse (a life that is short and bad from one that is short and good; suffering ended by a death from suffering caused by it; an averted birth from a prevented death; wild from domesticated; the welfare of a population from the welfare of its members); and which findings from population and community ecology it cannot contradict (density dependence, trophic cascades, source–sink dynamics, the fact that most animals alive at any moment are small and short-lived, and the fact that removing a habitat removes the animals in it without removing the pressure to reproduce elsewhere).

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp within the debate. I am especially interested in which requirements cannot be satisfied together. My suspicion is that the demand to count every individual animal, the demand to remain tractable with the kinds of data ecologists actually collect, and the demand that the ledger never license the destruction of a habitat that supports a great many lives cannot all be met at once, but I would like to know whether that incompatibility is genuine or an artifact of how I have stated the requirements. Skip any scoring of existing frameworks against the list; the list is what I need, and I will bring the contenders to the next meeting myself.
