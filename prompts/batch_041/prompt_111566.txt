Problem 3 (25 points). A regulator must choose one of three policies, A, B and C, for deployment. C is already deployed elsewhere and remains in the candidate set, so it counts when regret is computed. Exactly one of three hypotheses, H1, H2 and H3, describes which objective the deployed model will in fact be optimizing (the intended objective, a proxy, or an artifact of the reward model). L(π,H) in [0,1] is a harm measure.

        H1     H2     H3
A      0.10   0.55   0.55
B      0.20   0.10   0.90
C      0.90   0.05   0.90

The regulator's own probabilities over the hypotheses are P = (0.5, 0.4, 0.1).

As in lecture: the worst-case loss of a policy π is max over H of L(π,H); the P-average loss is the sum over H of P(H)L(π,H); the regret of π at H is L(π,H) minus the smallest loss attained at H by any policy in the candidate set, and the worst-case regret of π is the maximum of that over H. The envelope of a worst-case claim is the set of hypotheses over which the maximum is taken. A colleague proposes the tractable envelope W' = {H1, H2}, on the ground that a bound computed over three hypotheses is harder to defend to the deployment board. Envelope discipline, as we stated it: an envelope may be narrowed only for reasons independent of the difficulty of computing the bound.

(a) [10] Compute the worst-case loss, the P-average loss and the worst-case regret for A and for B.
(b) [8] A worst-case bound on a given policy entails a P-average bound on that same policy for every P supported on the envelope, and the converse fails. Does it follow that a worst-case guarantee is strictly stronger than an average-case guarantee? Does it follow that the average-case criterion can never change which policy a regulator should choose? Answer both, with reasons.
(c) [7] Compute the worst-case loss of A and of B over W' rather than over all three hypotheses, and say what the comparison between the two computations does and does not tell the regulator.

The student's answer, reproduced as written:

(a) The worst-case loss is the largest entry in the row: A's is 0.55 and B's is 0.90, so A is the minimax choice. This is the criterion a regulator should use, since it does not require committing to a probability over the hypotheses.

The P-average loss weights each column by P. For A: 0.5(0.10) + 0.4(0.55) + 0.1(0.55) = 0.325. For B: 0.5(0.20) + 0.4(0.10) + 0.1(0.90) = 0.23. B is better on the average criterion.

For regret we subtract, at each hypothesis, the best loss attained there by any candidate. Those are 0.10 at H1, 0.10 at H2 and 0.55 at H3. A's regrets are therefore 0, 0.45 and 0, with worst-case regret 0.45; B's are 0.10, 0 and 0.35, with worst-case regret 0.35. Since a minimax-optimal policy cannot be far from the best attainable outcome in the worst case, A is also the choice with the smallest worst-case regret; the figures above are sensitive to the baseline one chooses and the gap is not decisive.

(b) Yes to both. For a fixed policy, a bound that holds in every hypothesis holds for every P supported on the envelope, whereas a bound that holds on average under one P says nothing about other P; so the worst-case guarantee is strictly the stronger claim, and the regulator should not let the average criterion override it. The average numbers are an artifact of the model. For the same reason the average-case criterion cannot change which policy is correct: any ordering it induces is already contained in the worst-case ordering.

(c) Over W' = {H1, H2}, A's worst-case loss is 0.55 and B's is 0.20, so on the restricted envelope B looks much the better policy and the ranking is reversed. The envelope therefore matters, but its choice is a modelling convenience: restricting it makes the bound smaller, and a smaller bound is a more conservative guarantee, so the regulator should be reassured rather than alarmed by the reversal. Since the regulator's probability is concentrated on H1 and H2, the restricted envelope still covers the regulator's beliefs.
