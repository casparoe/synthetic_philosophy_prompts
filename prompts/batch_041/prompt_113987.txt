I'm a graduate student in a comparative literature seminar, and a recurring dispute in the seminar has crystallized around two ways of fixing what a literary text means, neither of which my classmates can state cleanly. On the first view, the meaning of a text is the meaning its author actually intended it to have, subject to one qualification: the intention counts only where the words and syntax, read by the conventions of the language at the time of composition, could carry it. If an author meant to praise and the sentence, by the ordinary rules of the language, unambiguously insults, then the text insults; the author's private intention cannot override what the language made available. On the second view, the meaning is not what the actual author privately intended but what an ideal reader would be warranted in attributing to the author — a reader who knows the text, its genre, its date, and everything publicly available about the context of its composition, but who is barred from the author's letters, diaries, and reported private remarks. On this second view, the governing question is always what the best-informed reader could justifiably take the author to have meant, not what the author in fact meant.

I'd like you to compare these two views carefully. Where do they agree, and why — that is, what is doing the work in each that makes them coincide on the easy cases? Then tell me where they must part company, and give me the cases that force the split: consider, for instance, (i) a poem whose lines, by every convention of the language, mean one thing, while the poet's private notebooks show she intended the opposite; (ii) a novelist who deliberately composed an ending so that no resolution can be settled from the text, intending the ambiguity itself as the point; (iii) a text produced by a process that had no author in the relevant sense — a randomly generated sequence, or a sentence found in a newspaper and reprinted as a poem; and (iv) an author whose ironic intent is recoverable only from the tone of a public interview. For each case, say what each view delivers and whether the two verdicts actually differ. Finally, adjudicate: which of the two views better explains our interpretive practice, and which of the four cases is the most decisive test between them? I am less interested in a survey of what critics have said than in your own reconstruction of the two positions, the precise point at which they diverge, and a defended verdict.
