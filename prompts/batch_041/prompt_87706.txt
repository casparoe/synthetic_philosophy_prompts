I'm a graduate student working on a paper about the ethics of deception, and a friend and I have been arguing about lying versus misleading for weeks. Our disagreement has hardened into two positions, and I want to see them fight it out properly instead of trading examples, so I'd like you to write the dialogue. The first character, whom I'll call the Symmetrist, holds that there is no moral difference between lying and merely misleading: both aim at a false belief in the hearer, and the difference in vehicle — asserting a falsehood versus implying one or letting a false assumption stand — is a fact about the speaker's technique, not about the wrong. She is happy to grant that lying is sometimes worse, but she thinks the extra badness comes from breaking a promise or a convention, not from the deception as such, and she will argue that in the cases we care about most, where the hearer is vulnerable or the stakes are high, the two are indistinguishable in wrongness. She has one technical argument I've never been able to answer: on the common-ground picture of conversation, where what is asserted gets added to a shared record that both parties treat as settled, a lie puts a false sentence into that record, whereas the misleading speaker never does — the record stays clean and only the hearer's private model of the world gets polluted. So if the wrong of deception is a wrong against the hearer's mind, she says, it is the same damage either way; and if it is a wrong against the shared record, then only the liar has done anything at all.

The second character, the Distinctionist, thinks the difference is real but located elsewhere. Her line is that assertion comes with a kind of warranty: you put your word behind the content, and the hearer is entitled to rely on it without further checking, whereas when you merely imply or allow, you have given no such warranty and the hearer is drawing an inference they are responsible for. She will press that this explains why a half-truth in a formal disclosure feels like a different kind of wrong from an outright false statement in the same document, and why the bare fact that a statement was true registers as a defense in some contexts and as a damning admission in others. The Symmetrist must then attack the warranty line — hearers don't experience it as a warranty they could decline, the inference can be as compulsory as an assertion when the question is direct and the answer is chosen, and talk of "responsibility" for inferences looks like blaming the hearer for trusting someone who arranged to be trusted — and the Distinctionist must answer that attack and then press a case of her own, not just restate her position; the Symmetrist must answer that in turn. I want at least two full exchanges in which each replies to the other's strongest point rather than the nearest one, and neither side may be a fool: the Symmetrist should not deny that promises and conventions matter, and the Distinctionist should not pretend that every implicature is equally avoidable. Have them also argue about hearers who are routinely expected to be suspicious — people whose word is discounted, or who are used to being second-guessed in institutional settings — and whether the distinction matters more or less for such hearers, and whether that tells for or against either position.

Please keep it to a maximum of 900 words, with no quotations, no citations, and no reading list. End with a short closing note, counted within that limit, in which you state how confident you are in the central claim the dialogue turns on and identify the single point in the exchange you are least sure about.
