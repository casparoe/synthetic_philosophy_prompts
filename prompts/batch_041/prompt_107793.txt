I'm midway through a dissertation chapter on the normativity of attitudes — half of it on value, half on belief — and I keep running into the same move in the literature and in my reading group. Someone lays out the problem of the wrong kind of reasons: a consideration can give you a reason to be in an attitude without bearing at all on whether the attitude is correct, or fitting, or warranted. The stock illustration is a threat — a powerful being will punish you unless you admire him, or unless you believe some falsehood — and the verdict is that this gives you a reason of the wrong kind. Then, instead of saying what makes a reason the right kind, everyone reaches for a label: object-given versus state-given, fittingness versus desirability, a reason for the attitude versus a reason for the state of having it. I want to stop before the labels. Please fix the purpose as follows: the account has to be strong enough that I could use it to defend a fitting-attitude analysis of value — the view that for something to be valuable is just for it to be fitting to value it — against this objection without begging the question, and general enough that I could run the same machinery for emotions and for belief, not just for admiration and desire.

So write me the desiderata: the requirements any adequate account of the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction has to meet. For each one, give me the case or the consideration that motivates it, and keep the requirements separate where they really are separate. Some of what I expect to see, though I'd rather you tell me what belongs on the list than fill in mine: the cases it has to classify correctly (the threat case; a consideration that does bear on the merit of the object or the fittingness of the attitude; ordinary evidence for belief; the awkward middle cases where a consideration looks wrong-kind for one attitude and right-kind for another formed on its basis); the distinctions it may not collapse, including the one between a class of considerations and a class of support-relations, since the same consideration can be right-kind for one attitude and wrong-kind for another, and the one between an attitude's not being fitting and its not being permissible or required; and the phenomenon that whatever else is true, the threat does seem to give me some reason to do something, so the account cannot simply declare wrong-kind reasons normatively inert. Add the theoretical constraints: it must not characterize the right kind of reason in terms of value or fittingness if value is what it is supposed to ground; it has to be consistent with whatever general theory of reasons it is paired with; it should be neutral about whether fittingness is primitive or analysable; it should explain why this is a problem at all, that is, what would actually be lost if the distinction collapsed; and it should give a unified treatment across belief, emotion, desire and admiration rather than four unrelated criteria. Say also whether the account has to take a stand on whether there are any right-kind reasons for attitudes in the first place.

A handful of cases I have been turning over, which I don't think are the standard ones, and I'd like you to say which the account must get right and which are merely optional pressure tests: an agent who by stipulation can form beliefs at will, so that we can ask whether the distinction is about what attitudes can be reasons-responsive or is a normative distinction that survives even under the stipulation; a reason to have a reason, as when you are told that if you acquire a reason to admire someone, something good will follow; a reason to have had an attitude in the past, say to have been grateful at the time; an institutional attitude, where a committee's bylaws require it to have confidence in a colleague, so the bylaw is the reason for the confidence; and reasons to admire a fictional character, where there is no object to have merit in the ordinary way. Then, on the list itself: mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of particular camps; flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied and say what would have to give; and tell me which single requirement does the most to narrow the field of otherwise plausible accounts. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, don't present a contested reading of the distinction as if it were the consensus — including where my own framing above already presupposes one — and be explicit about the points at which your answer is a judgment call rather than a report of agreement.
