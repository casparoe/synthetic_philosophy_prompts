I did infectious disease epi for 35 years, retired a couple years back, and have spent most of retirement trying to teach myself philosophy. Humbling. The thing that keeps happening is I run into a question I assumed was a settled empirical matter and find out it's contested all the way down. I want to practice committing to an actual number instead of hiding behind 'it depends,' so here goes.

Is the world becoming less violent? Give me your credence, but let me try to make it a claim that could actually be false. Define 'violent death' as a death caused by another human being who intended to kill — so no accidents, no disease, no other animals. Take the whole world. Take century-long averages so a single war doesn't dominate. Claim: the rate of violent death per 100,000 person-years was lower in the 20th century than in the 16th, 17th, and 18th centuries. What's your credence that's true?

But the number is the least interesting part. What I want is the decomposition — the sub-questions it rests on, a rough probability for each, one line of justification, and how they combine. Mine would go something like:

1. The definitional piece. Does the sign survive if we widen 'violence' to include deaths from state policy that were plainly foreseeable (blockades, forced collectivization, camp conditions)? And then again if we widen it further to structural stuff, preventable disease and the like? Give me your credence that the direction is the same under all three definitions.

2. The denominator piece. Per capita against world population, versus the raw count, versus per political unit, versus per unit of economic output. In absolute terms there are obviously way more of us to kill now, so I assume the interesting version is per capita, but tell me if you think the count is the morally relevant quantity and why. Credence the sign is invariant across these.

3. The data piece. Recent centuries are better recorded, there's a dark figure of killings that never made it into any record, and pre-modern rates get estimated off skeletal trauma and the like, which is its own can of worms. How much of the apparent decline do you think is a recording artifact? That's a separate probability, seperate from the others.

4. The statistics piece. Somebody told me war deaths are heavy-tailed, so the mean is dominated by a handful of enormous events and any trend estimate is fragile. Credence that's right, and if it is, does it sink the trend claim or just make the error bars huge?

5. The well-posedness piece. We only get one run of history, so there's no ensemble to compare against — no counterfactual distribution of worlds where we didn't get the state, the printing press, whatever. What's your credence that the claim even has a determinate truth value independent of which operationalization you pick, versus being an artifact of the definition someone chose?

Now the social part. My gut is 0.7. My brother in law, who reads alot of military history, says it's basically 0.95 and can't see why anyone hesitates. A sociologist friend says the question is meaningless and puts it at 0.2. Where do you think the three of us are actually disagreeing — which sub-question is carrying the disagreement?

Finally: which term in your decomposition are you least confident about, what one piece of reasoning (no internet, just think it through) would move it most, and what would settle the whole thing if we could somehow run the experiment we can't run. And if your answer flips sign depending on items 1 and 2, say so plainly rather than averaging over the definitions.
