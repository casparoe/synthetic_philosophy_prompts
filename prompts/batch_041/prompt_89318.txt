I'm the philosopher they've parked on my university's biosafety and ethics committee — long story — and next Thursday we've finally got a case I can actually get my teeth into. My PhD was on self-reference, which is presumably why the chair has dumped it on me.

The protocol is from a synthetic-biology group who want to build a minimal self-replicating protocell. Fine, that's their business. What's interesting is the argument they've built their justification around. They lean hard on Hofstadter's Typogenetics — the toy system in Gödel, Escher, Bach, where you've got strands of letters, and enzymes which are themselves strands, and the enzymes get translated into a little list of commands that cut and splice and shift other strands about, and if you're clever some strands turn out to code for an enzyme that rebuilds the very strand you started with. Their claim is that this is the same trick as Gödel's: a system that can encode descriptions of its own operations, so it can talk about itself. And once you've got that loop — a description, a machine that reads descriptions, a machine that copies them, and a description that describes all three — there's nothing left over for "self" to explain. The protocell, on their view, is just that loop done in chemistry.

One of our chemists thinks it's wordplay. His line is that "self-replication" is a label we stick on a process depending on where we draw the boundary — a photocopier copies itself if you're generous about what counts as part of it — and that no amount of abstract hand-waving about Gödel tells you whether a given bag of molecules is doing the real thing or is merely being described as if it were. He wants to reject the protocol outright. He's in the office next to mine and I'd rather this didn't turn into a scrap.

So: please draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday. It should land on a concrete recommendation — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — with a time frame attached, reasons the team could actually respond to, and a genuine engagement with the chemist's objection rather than a polite wave at it, because I think he's half right and I need to say which half in a way he'll accept. British English, please, it goes to the registrar and they're fussy about that sort of thing. Don't bother with citations or a literature review, none of us need them and it would only pad the thing out.
