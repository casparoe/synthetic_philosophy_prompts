Ok so bit of a weird one. I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training whatsoever, and through a series of bad decisions I've ended up as the token 'skeptic' on the board of a small psychical research society — the kind of group where people look into ghosts, telepathy, premonitions, that whole area. It's basically a revival of an old university society that went dormant decades ago, and next month we're having a dinner to mark getting it going again. They've asked me to give the after-dinner speech, and I've realised the only honest angle I've got is the guy who started the original thing: Henry Sidgwick.

Here's what I've picked up about him, and I want the speech built around it. He was a Victorian moral philosopher at Cambridge and one of the founders and the first president of the big Society for Psychical Research back in the 1880s. The interesting bit is he never became a believer — he died still saying he didn't know. And he wasn't a debunker either; he thought the question was dead serious. My understanding, and I'm probably mangling it, is that he thought reason pulls you two ways at once: it tells you to look after yourself and it tells you to look after everyone, and those two can genuinely come apart, and the only thing that would guarantee they line up in the end is if there's some kind of moral order to the universe — reward, punishment, a next life, whatever. So whether we survive death wasn't a hobby for him, it was load-bearing for his biggest worry. And he still wouldn't fake an answer. He also did this thing where instead of collecting spooky anecdotes he and his crowd ran a huge survey — asked something like seventeen thousand ordinary people whether they'd ever had a vivid hallucination and whether it lined up with somebody dying far away — on the theory that if you ask enough people in a boring, systematic way you can actually start putting numbers on even the weirdest kind of testimony, instead of trading stories nobody can check.

Now the reason this is relevant to my speech. At the dinner we're also launching a little phone app meant to do the same thing for our generation — people log coincidences, premonitions, 'I was just thinking about them right before it happened' moments — and we've made a big deal of declaring in advance, before we look at any of the data, exactly what we'll count as a hit. I want to actually say something real about that, and about whether Sidgwick would have thought a thing like that could ever settle anything, and what he'd have told a room like ours.

So please write the speech. Six or seven minutes, after-dinner, me on my feet with a glass in my hand, first person, in my voice. The room is split right down the middle and it matters: half of them have had experiences they genuinely can't explain and are quietly hoping this is the beginning of something; the other half are skeptics like me who mostly came for the food and will pounce on anything that sounds like I've gone soft. It has to land for both — not a debunking lecture, not a pep talk for the believers, and not a waffly 'both sides have a point' thing either, because that's the one move Sidgwick would definately have hated. Keep me honest: nothing in it he'd have objected to, and nothing where I claim to believe something about the afterlife that I don't.

And don't worry about getting names, dates and book titles right — I'm not citing anything from the podium, and I'd rather it sound like me than like a reading list. Just get his attitude right. Thats the whole speech.
