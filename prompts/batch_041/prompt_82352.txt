I have no academic training in philosophy, but for a few years I have been thinking about an idea I first met in a popular summary of G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica. The summary said that Moore regarded two things as the greatest intrinsic goods: the enjoyment of other people — friendship, affection, the company of those one loves — and the enjoyment of beautiful objects. What has stayed with me is not the list but the relationship between the two. Moore apparently used a method of isolation to test whether something is good in itself: you imagine it existing alone, stripped of its usual accompaniments, and ask whether it would still be good. I have read conflicting accounts of what he concluded about beauty. One account says he thought a beautiful world with no one to appreciate it would be no better than an ugly one. Another says he thought it would be better, and that this showed pleasure is not the only intrinsic good. I do not know which is right, and I am not sure the distinction is as clean as either summary suggests. I have also come across the idea of an organic unity in this connection — the thought that a whole made of a beautiful thing and a true appreciation of it can be more valuable than the sum of its parts — but I have never seen it explained in a way that made it concrete for me.

I would like you to write a short story that puts these ideas under pressure. The story should be about a woman in her late seventies named Ruth who has kept a small walled garden for forty years. The garden was designed jointly with her closest friend, Miriam, who died ten years ago. Ruth still tends it, and she has developed the habit of narrating its changes aloud — what has come into leaf, what has failed, what the light is doing — as if Miriam were still there to hear it. Ruth's nephew, Daniel, a graduate student in economics, comes to stay for a week and finds the habit troubling. He argues that Ruth's devotion is irrational: Miriam cannot appreciate anything, and the garden's beauty is doing no good. Ruth does not answer him with a thesis. She answers him with what she does.

The story should be roughly two thousand words. It should not have a character deliver a lecture on Moore or on the philosophy of value. The philosophical content should emerge from concrete details: what Ruth does in the garden, what Daniel notices, what the garden does across the week. Please include at least one scene in which the question of whether beauty can be good without an appreciator is forced by a specific event — a storm, a neighbor who wants to cut down a tree, a decision about whether to sell the land. Please also include a moment in which Ruth's friendship with Miriam is shown to be valuable in a way that is not reducible to either pleasure or beauty — something about loyalty, or about being known, or about the difference between remembering someone and continuing to act toward them. I am not looking for a resolution. I would rather the story end with the question still open, but with the reader having a clearer sense of what is at stake in answering it.
