I am preparing a graduate seminar on the ethics of wild animal suffering, and I want to use Hayao Miyazaki's Princess Mononoke as a case study. Please read the film through the lens of the debate over whether habitat destruction reduces wild animal suffering — that is, over whether the net welfare of a wild ecosystem is positive or negative, and what follows for how we should value the clearing or the restoration of wild habitat.

The film presents itself as an elegy for a vanishing natural world: the boar god's corruption and death, the rage of the boars, the wolves' doomed defense of the forest, the Great Forest Spirit that both gives and takes life, and Lady Eboshi's Iron Town cutting the forest down. The emotional pull is all in one direction — the forest is sacred, its destruction is a sacrilege, and the regrowth at the end is offered as consolation. The philosophical literature I am teaching raises an uncomfortable possibility that cuts against this: if the aggregate welfare of a mature forest is negative, if most of its inhabitants live brief lives dominated by hunger, fear, predation, and disease, then the destruction of that habitat may reduce total suffering, and the film's lament may be a lament for a vast engine of pain. I want a reading that takes this possibility seriously rather than dismissing it as a provocation.

So please do the following. First, if you were to draw up a welfare audit of the film's forest before and after the humans' destruction — counted in suffering and contentment rather than in species or trees — which way would the ledger tilt, and does the film's imagery push us to read the ledger one way while the arithmetic suggests another? Second, consider the ending, where the forest begins to grow back and the film treats this as a kind of healing. On the welfare calculus, is that a happy ending or a slow catastrophe — more habitat, more animals, more suffering? Does the film have the conceptual resources to see the difference, or does it simply assume that a flourishing ecosystem is good for the creatures inside it? Third, examine how the film distributes its sympathy: the suffering it asks us to mourn is concentrated in the large, charismatic, quasi-noble animals, while the small creatures that would dominate the biomass of any real forest go uncounted. What does that selectivity reveal about the film's implicit theory of whose suffering matters?

I want a reading that is faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of a philosophical thesis, and do not soften the thesis to protect the film. Where the film resists the reading — where its images or its drama genuinely tell against the welfare calculus — say so plainly. Tell me which scenes you would point the seminar to, and end by saying what the film adds to the debate that the argument alone cannot: whether it dramatizes something about the emotional grip of wild nature that the calculus cannot capture, or that it exposes as a bias worth resisting.

I do not need a bibliography, reading list, or citations. Lead with your overall verdict — the thesis of your reading — in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
