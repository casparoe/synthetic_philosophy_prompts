I'm writing a novel whose central character is a sentencing judge, and I want her convictions to be ones a real theory could underwrite, so before I choose a theory I want the specification: what must any adequate theory of punishment deliver to count as one at all? Give me the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the empirical findings it must not contradict, each with the consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp. My draft keeps throwing off cases I cannot tell whether the theories are meant to cover: a city that, unable to find the arsonist, draws lots and punishes a random resident to restore calm; an offender who has already lost everything his crime cost the victim and more; a corporation whose workforce has entirely retired or died, which a court still wants to fine; a later and better regime that wants to try the enforcers of a wicked earlier one; and a man who offers to serve his guilty brother's sentence. Tell me which of these a theory of punishment is obliged to accommodate and which it may set aside, and — this is what I most need — whether the requirements, taken together, can be satisfied at all, because if they cannot, my judge has to know which one she is betraying. The purpose is narrow: a framework that lets me render her reasoning on the page without the reader catching me cheating on her behalf. End with two or three sentences on how retributivism and consequentialism fare against your list; I will do the rest of the work myself.
