I'm putting together a handout for the last meeting of my alignment reading group and I want to do the thing I do with all my better seminars: instead of a list of "here are the four views on corrigibility," I want a decision tree that actually sorts people. So what I'm after is a flowchart for the question "should a sufficiently capable agent be corrigible -- that is, should it not resist or game its own shutdown, modification, or replacement?" Every branch node should be a question my students can answer in good faith without already knowing which camp it lands them in, and every leaf should be a named position (or "unnamed" if it turns out nobody defends it).

Here are the distinctions I actually care about, because I think these, and not the labels, are what everyone is really disagreeing about. (1) Is corrigibility a property you bake into the agent's preferences, a property of the training and oversight process, or a property of the whole human-machine interface -- so that an agent can be perfectly corrigibile in itself and still be dangerous because of how we deploy it? (2) Is the shutdown problem fundamentally an incentive problem (the agent has some goal it can better pursue by staying switched on) or a definitional one (we can't even specify "do what we want, including stop" without tying ourselves in knots)? (3) Does the agent merely tolerate shutdown, actively assist in it, or preserve our option to shut it down later -- and are those differnet enough to be separate leaves? (4) Is corrigibility even stable or coherent, or does any sufficiently capable optimiser drift toward incorrigibility on its own -- and if you think the whole program is confused, where do you go instead (don't build optimisers at all, keep them myopic, bolt on a hard off-switch you never let them touch, etc.)?

Please build the tree from the logical space first -- the questions and the combinations of answers they allow -- and only then place the actual positions in it. I want at least these leaves represented: indifference-style corrigibility (make the agent not care whether it's shut down or not), corrigibility-as-obedience, "shutdown is just another goal-change, keep it neutral," the view that corrigibility should be learned from human feedback rather than hand-specified, and the pessimist who says the entire framing is broken. Mark any combination nobody occupies and tell me whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two positions your tree can't separate. Also, please include a branch for the person who insists the real problem isn't shutdown at all but that we keep building things which care about the future in the first place -- I want that leaf to surface so the tree doesn't just quietly assume everyone accepts the framing. Keep it to roughly ten branch nodes and seven to nine leaves, and phrase every node so a grad student could answer it without having read the literature.
