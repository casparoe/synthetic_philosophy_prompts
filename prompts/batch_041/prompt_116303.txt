I help run a monthly philosophy discussion group that meets in a back room above a pub, and this year we're putting on an essay prize for our members; there's no course, no reading list, and the judging panel is three of us, none with a philosophy degree, so the whole thing stands or falls on the question. Last month we spent an evening on when it's acceptable to change someone's mind, and we found that a question like "when is persuasion manipulation?" just produces essays that recite the obvious answer and then stop. I'd like you to take on the role of a seasoned examiner in applied ethics — someone who has marked hundreds of scripts and can tell the difference between an answer that names a distinction and one that can actually wield it — and produce the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. Please think carefully before you write, and begin by brainstorming the candidate ways of drawing the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, so that I can see two or three approaches considered and rejected before you commit to one; a tidy formula that arrives unargued is no use to me.

The question must be answerable with no reading whatsoever, so it has to carry its own material: state the cases in full rather than gesturing at them, and don't lean on anything a candidate would have to look up. Here are the cases our members kept circling, which you should build the question around. (1) A doctor explains a treatment's risks to a frightened patient using true and complete figures, but chooses the order that flatters the safer option — out of a hundred people like you, ninety are still here in five years, rather than ten out of a hundred die. (2) A friend who knows you have been dithering about leaving your job waits until you are drunk, exhausted and hours past your bedtime, and then tells you the same true thing she could have told you on Tuesday afternoon; you resign, and you would endorse that decision sober. (3) A landlord truthfully mentions that two other couples are viewing the flat tomorrow. (4) A phone company runs a 48-hour flash sale that returns every fortnight, which everyone in your office has stopped believing in, though the deadline is always genuinely enforced. (5) An anti-smoking advertisement shows a woman in her forties dying in a hospital bed; nothing in it is false and she is not unrepresentative. Most of us thought (3) was fine and (4) was not, and could not say why, since in both the pressure comes from a fact that is true. We split badly on (1) and (2): several members thought the doctor's reordering was the most manipulative item on the list and the drunk friend the least, while others had it exactly the other way round. The question should make candidates confront that pair, and should make it impossible to answer by simply asserting that manipulation bypasses the audience's rational agency, because in every one of these cases the propositions conveyed are true, and in (2) the person ends up believing precisely what she would have arrived at on her own. I want candidates forced to say what the bypassing is supposed to consist in when nothing false has been said and no option has been taken away.

So please give me three things. First, the essay question itself, pitched to be answered in ninety minutes by an intelligent member of the group who has done no reading, and phrased so that nobody can collect the marks by listing criteria without applying them to the cases. Second, a model answer of roughly 750 words that actually does the work: it should state a criterion precisely enough to be argued with, run that criterion through all five cases, admit honestly where it gets one wrong or has to strain, and say what it implies about a sixth case the answer invents for itself. The model answer must not cite anyone or anything — it has to stand entirely on its own reasoning, since our members have no library to check it against. Third, a marking scheme out of 20 marks built around five must-hit points, giving the marks for each, a one-line description of what a top answer does at that point that a merely competent one does not, and a note on the characteristic way candidates get each point wrong — the tempting move that looks like the point but isn't. Please make the A/B boundary turn on the argument rather than the vocabulary: naming a well-known philosopher or dropping a technical term should earn nothing by itself, and the marking scheme should say so explicitly.
