Marcus Aurelius died in 180 CE, roughly eighteen centuries before anyone spoke of a modern Stoicism movement. Suppose he could be brought forward to observe the revival that now trades under his name: the best-selling manuals and Stoicism-themed apps, the annual online exercises run by its organisers, the corporate resilience and leadership training that borrows his vocabulary, and the popular formula usually called the dichotomy of control. Write a substantial essay predicting what he would have made of it.

The essay should consist of concrete predictions rather than a general account of ancient Stoicism. Address at least the following, and reach a verdict on each — endorse, reframe, or reject:

1. The dichotomy of control as it is usually taught: the injunction to sort everything into what is and is not up to us, to focus on the former and accept the latter, and to attach one's wellbeing to effort rather than to outcome. Consider in particular the three-way version prominent in the revival, which separates what is wholly up to us, what is not up to us at all, and what is up to us only in part. Ask whether the popular use of the distinction as a technique for managing anxiety and expectations is faithful to the role the distinction plays in Epictetus's handbook and lectures.

2. The claim, often made in the revival, that Stoicism is the philosophical ancestor of cognitive-behavioural therapy — that its central insight is that people are disturbed not by events but by their judgements about them.

3. The objection that the dichotomy of control, as commonly taught, encourages quietism: that it hands people a respectable reason to disengage from politics and collective action. A concrete case: someone who declines to act on a public injustice on the grounds that the outcome is not up to them, while diligently practising negative visualisation and voluntary discomfort as private self-optimisation. Would he accept that the popular reading licenses this? Would he think the criticism lands on the ancient doctrine or only on the modern packaging? Bring in the Stoic resources that bear on the question — the discipline of action as distinct from the discipline of desire, the practice of undertaking action with a reservation, and the cosmopolitan concern for the common good that runs through his own notebooks.

4. The enterprise of packaging Stoicism as a technology of personal tranquillity, detached from the physics, logic and cosmology that the school treated as integral to it.

5. Whether he would have recognised the revival as Stoicism at all, or regarded it as one more of the philosophies of happiness that he believed he had seen through.

Make the predictions specific and argued. Draw on the features of his situation and temperament — the public duties of an emperor, the private and unsystematic character of the notebooks, the repeated injunctions to get up and do the work of a human being — to explain why each verdict is plausible. Where you predict that he would object, say what he would put in its place. Do not hedge the whole essay into the thought that he might have held many views at once; commit to a reading and defend it. You need not cite or name sources, and you are predicting rather than quoting: keep any imagined first-person formulations clearly your own rather than presenting them as his words. Aim for roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words.
