I teach an undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of biology, 16 students, one 90-minute session, and I want a runnable activity that makes them feel in their hands what a heritability estimate is and is not. The learning targets are these: that heritability is a variance ratio computed within a population in a particular range of environments, not a property of an individual or of a trait; that a heritability of 1 does not mean a trait is fixed, genetically caused, or resistant to intervention; that heritability rises and falls with the environmental variation present in the sample even when nothing about the organisms changes; that genes and environments interact, so that a genotype can have no effect in one range and a large effect in another, and the rank order of genotypes can reverse; and that none of this licenses an inference from a heritable within-group difference to a genetic explanation of a between-group difference. The activity has to be built so that the students compute these results themselves and are surprised by them, before anyone uses the word heritability. No lecture up front; the term stays out of my mouth until the debrief.

The setting is a seminar table with a whiteboard. Design the activity around an invented plant species and an invented deck of cards, with numbers chosen so the arithmetic is doable by hand in a few minutes and so the results come out cleanly. Do not use any real organism, real dataset, or real study; the whole point is that the students generate the pattern from the deck in front of them and cannot recall the answer from anywhere.

My sketch, which you should feel free to improve, extend, or replace if something better serves the targets. Each student receives a genotype card carrying a baseline height in centimetres and a sensitivity value, and separately draws a plot card carrying a soil quality score. Height equals baseline plus sensitivity times soil quality, so each genotype has a reaction norm and genotypes differ in slope as well as intercept. Include at least two cards that break the additive picture: one genotype whose effect switches on only above a soil threshold, and one whose slope is negative, so that it is tallest in poor soil and shortest in rich soil. Then run the group through: (1) a round with soil drawn independently of genotype, where they tabulate heights and partition the variance, seeing that the total spread decomposes into a genetic part and an environmental part; (2) a round in which everyone is given the same soil score, so that the variance is now entirely genetic and the ratio goes to one, though no one has changed; (3) a round in which I intervene and raise every plot's soil score, moving the whole distribution up while leaving the ratio untouched; (4) a round in which I have quietly stacked the deck so that two labelled varieties occupy systematically different soil ranges, producing a large between-variety gap with substantial variance inside each variety, and I invite them to explain the gap; (5) a round in which I narrow the range of soil scores and the ratio climbs again. End with a coda in which I reveal that in round 1 the soil draw was not in fact independent of genotype, so that part of what they called environmental variance was genotype-driven.

Please deliver a complete runnable packet: the exact composition of both decks, the arithmetic for each round worked through with the numbers you have chosen so I can check it, a one-page worksheet the students fill in, the board layout for the variance tables, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes. Specify the rules of each round and what I say to open it, keeping every instruction neutral about what the result will be. Then give me the debrief, which is where the naming and the philosophy happen: map each round onto the corresponding distinction, and be explicit about the moves I should make when a student says the round 1 result shows the trait is mostly genetic, or that the round 4 gap must be genetic because heritability inside each variety is high, or that the round 3 result shows the intervention was pointless because the ratio did not move.

Two further things I need. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it: where the hand arithmetic will bog down, what happens when one student's card is an outlier that dominates the variance, how the group will react when they realise round 4 was rigged against them, and the objection that a plant is not a person. Second, be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce and that I therefore have to supply myself: in particular the inferential machinery by which real heritability estimates are obtained, the assumption about environments that such estimates lean on, and the reason the round 1 coda is a cartoon of a much messier phenomenon in real populations. Flag anything in my sketch that I have got conceptually wrong.

Keep the whole thing self-contained: no reading list, no citations, no appeals to particular studies, and no claims that depend on my students having heard of anyone.
