At a bar after a science-journalism thing last week, two friends of mine went three rounds over Dutch book arguments and I couldn't call it. Ines says the thing is basically unanswerable: if your credences violate the probability axioms there's a set of bets, each fair by your own lights, that loses you money no matter how the world turns out, so incoherent credences are definately a real defect and not just an aesthetic one. She then pushed the diachronic version — commit now to bet at your current odds, fail to update by conditionalizing when the evidence comes in, and a bookie can pick you off across time, so the argument reaches your future self and not just your present betting. Dev said every bit of that only bites if you actually take the bets, and a bookie has to get you to accept each one; once you smell the trap you decline the lot, and refusing individually fair bets doesn't make your credences inconsistent, it makes you someone who doesn't like being fleeced. She said the bets come one at a time and each is offered as fair, so declining all of them is itself a kind of incoherence — you're saying yes to each and no to the set. He said that's a defect in my betting policy, not in my degrees of belief, and anyway the whole "fair bet" identification smuggles in linear utility; give me a mortgage and diminishing marginal value of money and the equivalence between my credences and my betting odds just falls apart. Go through this move by move, say which points land and which are cheap, and tell me who's more right overall. One extra thing: is there a version of his objection that doesn't just collapse into "there are no norms on credence at all"?
