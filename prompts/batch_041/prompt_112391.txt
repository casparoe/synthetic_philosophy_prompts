I'm a science-and-ideas journalist and I'm writing a feature about how badly people's intuitions behave when you scale harms up and down — the piece keeps circling back to that old thought experiment where you have to choose between one person being tortured for fifty years and an absurdly large number of people each getting one barely-noticeable speck of dust in the eye. My editor is convinced the piece needs a sidebar readers can actually do, so I want a quiz: which view about aggregating tiny harms do you actually hold? Eight to ten questions, each one a concrete case with two or three options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the positions people really occupy in that argument — the straight aggregator who just multiplies; the person who says there's a floor of significance below which a harm doesn't count at all; the lexical-priority person who says no pile of trivial harms, however big, can outweigh one horror; the person-affecting type who thinks harms only add up inside a single life; and the one who says the whole comparison is a category mistake because the two things aren't on the same scale. Feel free to add leaves I haven't thought of, including unnamed ones that fall out of the logical space but that nobody seems to defend, and tell me when a combination is empty because the view is incoherent rather than merely unpopular. The thing I care most about: no question should be answerable by naming your own position. Ask about cases, not labels — nobody should be able to answer 'I'm a utilitarian' and be done with it.

Here's the angle that keeps this from being just the usual dust-speck rerun, and I've been argueing about this with friends for months. What actually decides people, I think, are the cases where the tiny harm is unwitnessed — nobody notices it, remembers it, or even could have noticed it — and the cases where the tiny harms all land on the same person instead of being spread thin across millions, and the cases where what's being distributed is a small risk of something awful rather than a small harm. So please build the tree so those three distinctions are doing real work: one branch for whether a harm has to be felt by anyone to count, one for whether the numbers add up across persons at all or only within a life, and one for whether we're aggregating certainties or probabilities. Seed the cases so they get progressively less comfortable — include one where the procedure you'd naturally reach for gives an answer your own framing won't like, and one where the specks get upgraded from dust to something like a stubbed toe, so we can see whether the same people flip. Also flag any two positions the quiz can't separate with these questions, and say whether that's because the questions are too coarse or because the two positions are really the same view wearing different hats.

Assume the readers are smart general readers who have never read any of the academic literature, so nothing can rest on a name, a title or a citation, but you can assume they'll take the questions seriously and will be annoyed if the options are obviously rigged. I'd rather have eight questions that actually split people than twelve that politely re-ask the same thing.
