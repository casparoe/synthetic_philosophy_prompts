Imagine that Johannes Kepler — the imperial mathematician who cast horoscopes for emperors and generals while insisting that astronomy and astrology answer to different standards of certainty — could be brought forward to inspect three developments that did not exist in his lifetime: first, a consumer astrology application that takes a user's birth data and uses a large language model to generate daily forecasts and, increasingly, consequential advice about career, health, and relationships; second, the contemporary reframing of astrology as a psychological and meaning-making practice rather than a predictive science, a symbolic vocabulary for self-understanding; and third, the accumulated record of controlled statistical studies that have repeatedly failed to find reproducible correlations between birth-chart features and measurable personality traits or life outcomes.

Reconstruct what Kepler would say about each of the three, and do so under the constraint that your reconstruction must respect the specific structure of his own position rather than a generic "early modern man of science" template. That position includes at least the following elements: that the celestial configurations do not act on the body by physical influx, but instead impress a character on the soul that is attuned to harmonic and geometrical ratios; that the demonstrable, geometry-grounded part of astrology must be carefully separated from its conjectural, rule-bound, and merely traditional parts; and that much of the judicial astrology of his own day was arbitrary and deserved criticism even though he continued to practise astrology for patrons. Treat these as hard constraints on what he could consistently hold, not as decorative background.

Then give a definite verdict on the boundary question: would Kepler endorse the astrology app as a legitimate continuation of his reformed art, demand that it be narrowed and reclassified, or reject it outright? Would he regard the failure of the statistical studies as vindicating his own restriction of astrology to general and soul-oriented claims, or as evidence that the studies are testing the wrong thing? And where would he draw the line between the "certain" and the "conjectural" in each of the three cases?

Give a committed answer: state clearly where you think Kepler would land on each question, defend that reading with reasons drawn from his actual commitments, and be willing to say that he would have been inconsistent or mistaken rather than smoothing him into a tidy modern position. Do not merely catalogue possible interpretations and leave the choice to me.
