Hey — I'm a science-and-ideas journalist, I write a column and the occasional 6,000-word feature, book in progress, and I've been asked to give the Thursday seminar in the philosophy department next month on the argument I'm making. I need you to build me a Q&A prep sheet.

The thesis, compressed: moral revolutions arn't won by argument. The case against slavery, the case against dueling — the arguments were on the table and basically unchanged for decades before the practice actually died. What killed them is that the practice turned into a status liability for the people whose opinion set the tone, and more specifically that one faction of elites figured out it could use the norm to make a rival faction look ridiculous. Argument is the soundtrack, not the engine. So when you want to know which of today's live moral questions will actually move, you don't ask who has the better argument, you ask who's positioned to score status points off the change.

And then I stick my neck out. My test case is the authorship norm — that the person whose name is on a piece of writing actually wrote it. I think it collapses fast, and not because anyone makes a compelling case (it's hard to even point to a victim), but because it becomes a way for one camp of writers to dunk on another. If the honor/interest story is right, it should flip. If the argument story were right, nothing should happen at all, since the arguments are so weak.

Here's the room I'm walking into. Mostly moral philosophers — several work on moral progress and moral epistemology, a couple are Kantians who'll want to know what "argument" is even supposed to mean in my mouth. Two historians of 18th/19th-century Britain: one on abolition, one on gentlemanly honor and dueling, and both will be savage about my evidence. One development economist who thinks culture is a residue and material incentives do all the causal work. One moral psychologist who runs norm-change experiments and will have data. Plus grad students who ask the annoying foundational stuff.

Give me the prep sheet: the eight to ten objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how much damage they do, each phrased in that person's vocabulary — the economist's version of "your mechanism is unfalsifiable" is not the Kantian's version — a two-minute reply for each, a note on where the reply is soft, and the one question I'm praying nobody asks. American English please, and skip the pep talk. I dont need encouragement, I need the ambush mapped.
