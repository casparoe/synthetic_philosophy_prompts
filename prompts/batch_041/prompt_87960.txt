I'm in a grad seminar on metaethics and we've spent two weeks on the Frege–Geach problem, and honestly I still can't tell whether it's a real objection or just a weird artifact of how semanticists write things down. Could you write me a dialogue between two philosophers about it: one an expressivist who thinks moral sentences primarily express non-cognitive attitudes (something in the Blackburn/Gibbard quasi-realist ballpark, but don't worry about getting their actual texts right — I care about the moves, not the exegesis), and one a descriptivist who thinks moral sentences represent moral facts and are true or false in the ordinary way. The descriptivist should press the embedding cases, but I'd rather they not just trot out the usual conditional example everyone uses in the intro textbooks — have them invent fresh ones, like "It's probably true that he was wrong to walk out on her", or "She stopped believing that eating meat is wrong a year before she stopped doing it", or a "because" construction like "He resented her because what she did was wrong". The expressivist should have real replies (appealing to the attitude being carried into the unasserted clause, or to logic as a constraint on combinations of attitudes rather than on propositions, or whatever else holds up), and then the descriptivist should come back at those replies — in particular asking what makes modus ponens valid on the expressivist's story, and whether the expressivist can get that without smuggling in truth in the full-blooded sense.

I don't want a punching bag on either side. The descriptivist shouldn't just say "but there are moral truths, duh", and the expressivist shouldn't say "who needs logic anyway". They should actually respond to each other's points rather than taking turns giving speeches, and alot of the dialogue should be them circling the same case from different angles until they figure out exactly where they disagree — is it about meaning, about validity, or about what "true" is doing?

At the end, can each of them say out loud which consideration would actually change their mind, and where in their own reasoning they think they're most likely to be wrong? Not as a tidy summary tacked on, more like each of them being honest about it in the middle of the conversation. I'm asking partly because my own view here is pretty unstable and I'd like to see what it would take to settle it.
