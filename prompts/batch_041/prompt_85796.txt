I serve on my high school's curriculum review committee, and I want to write a decision tree that sorts the positions people actually hold on how a history textbook should treat a nation's ugly chapters — the fight over the 1619 Project and the 1776 Commission report, or over Japanese and Korean textbooks — so that we argue about the right question instead of trading labels. Please build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of camps. Let the root split the purpose of a textbook (forming a shared civic story versus training students in how historians reason about evidence), then branch on at least these questions: whether the shared story should be one of achievement or of reckoning; whether, where specialists disagree, the textbook should display the live disagreement or report the current consensus; whether its voice should be 'we the nation' or 'the discipline'; and whether we owe the dead a particular kind of depiction. Make the tree work for two cases at once — a founding story that also involves slavery, and an atrocity that the perpetrators' descendants now study — and say which leaves fit only one. Phrase every node so a classmate could answer it without knowing where it leads, give each leaf a plain name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and flag any leaves nobody defends (and whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored) and any positions the tree cannot separate.
