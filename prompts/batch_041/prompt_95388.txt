I'm a graduate student in an interdisciplinary program, and I keep getting dragged into an argument with a colleague who insists that cultural change is a Darwinian process in the strict sense: that cultural items are genuine replicators, that they are selected for their own transmission-fitness rather than for their usefulness to the people who carry them, and that this level of explanation does real predictive work rather than merely redescribing ordinary learning. I can't tell whether this is a substantive empirical thesis or a verbal one, and I'd like a number rather than a verdict. Suppose we ran a long transmission-chain study in which participants repeatedly learn and then reproduce a mixed set of items — melodies, short stories, ritual sequences, tool-use procedures — and suppose the results came back as follows: fidelity of reproduction is high for melodies and short ritual sequences and much lower for stories and procedures, and which items survive to the end of a chain is predicted better by their memorability and ease of imitation than by their usefulness.

Two complications I want you to take seriously. First, on the reconstruction view, cultural transmission is not copying at all: each learner rebuilds the item from partial cues, so there may be no replicator in the strict sense, only a recurring attractor in human cognition. Second, a meme's-eye explanation may be fully compatible with an individual-level explanation — selection on items just is selection on the psychological dispositions that make items easy to acquire — in which case the two accounts are not rivals and the dispute is largely verbal.

Please give me your credence that the strict meme's-eye thesis is true, where I mean by that: cultural change is driven substantially by selection acting on cultural replicators whose transmission-fitness is not reducible to their usefulness to the individual carriers. Decompose it: the probability that transmission is faithful enough for selection to have anything to act on; the probability that what looks like selection is not better explained by cognitive attraction toward a small set of humanly natural forms; the probability that the cultural level of explanation is autonomous rather than a restatement of psychology; the probability that the thesis has determinate enough content to be true or false at all; and any further terms you think belong. Give each term a number and one line of justification, show how they combine into your final figure, tell me which term that figure is most sensitive to, and say what single further experiment or analysis would move it the most. For calibration, I'm at about 0.35 and my colleague is at about 0.85; I'd also like to know which term you think accounts for most of the gap between us.
