I'm a teaching fellow in a great books seminar that runs Shakespeare alongside ancient political thought, and in two weeks I'm doing The Tempest together with the chapters of Politics Book I in which Aristotle works out his account of natural slavery. The trouble is that everything my students find is the colonial allegory — Prospero as planter, Caliban as dispossessed native — and while I don't want to ignore that, I don't want to spend the whole session there either. I want the harder question of whether the play is actually arguing with Aristotle's theory of the slave by nature or merely staging it, so please read The Tempest through that theory and tell me what the play shows, complicates, or gets wrong about it. I'd like the reading to work from the substance of the argument rather than the slogan: that the natural slave is a piece of property and an animate tool, that he can perceive reason without possessing it and is therefore fit to be ruled but not to rule, that the rule is supposed to be advantageous to the ruled as much as to the ruler, that Aristotle himself notices that nature often fails to supply the bodily and psychological marks the theory requires, that he concedes that slavery resting on conquest and force rather than natural difference is conventional and breeds enmity rather than friendship, and that the Nicomachean Ethics has that strange little discussion of whether a master can be friends with a slave at all, or only with the human being who happens to be one.

The play, as I read it, hands us two servants to test the criterion against. Ariel's service is contracted and terminable — he was freed from one bondage and serves in hope of another liberation — whereas Caliban is treated as unfree by nature, with Prospero justifying his rule by Caliban's parentage, by the assault on Miranda, and by the failure of an education he says was repaid with ingratitude. But Caliban also claims the island by inheritance from his mother, argues his case like a lawyer, and speaks some of the most beautiful verse in the play, which looks to me like a standing counterexample to the perceiving-without-possessing distinction; and then there is the comic business with the drunken conspiracy, which either confirms that he cannot govern himself or exposes how the island's so-called civilizing power actually behaves. My co-instructor thinks I'm overreading and that the play is simply not that interested in the theory.

I don't need you to quote the play — I know it well, and I'd rather you work from scenes, characters, and plot than from lines. But I do want Aristotle taken seriously as a philosopher rather than as a villain, and I want you to be honest where the play resists the reading: where the romance and comic machinery undercuts any tidy verdict, or where Aristotle's position has resources a hostile reading misses. Tell me which scenes to put in front of the class and what to ask about them. And give me a verdict rather than a survey: does Shakespeare dramatize Aristotle's natural slave, refute him, or show that the concept is unstable in a way the philosopher half-knew? Take a definite position, defend it against the strongest objection you can see to it, and end by saying what the play adds to this debate that Aristotle's chapters and their modern critics don't.
