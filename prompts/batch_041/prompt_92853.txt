I work at a software company that's just opened a small office in Lagos, and I've somehow ended up refereeing a Slack arguement I can't follow, let alone settle.

We've got hardware stuck in customs. The guy running the Lagos office says we should just pay the "facilitation fee" the officer is asking for — everyone does it, and refusing means six weeks in a warehouse and a missed launch. Our finance lead in London says that's bribery, full stop, and once you start you never stop. Then our contractor in Nairobi wades in and says we're both being naive: the wrong thing here is a salaried official shaking down a company, whereas the envelope she'd hand over at a cousin's wedding is a completley different act, and lumping the two together is an insult. Finance says a gift you're expected to repay in favours is just a bribe with better manners.

Nobody's moved in three weeks, and I keep noticing we slide between four different arguments: what's illegal where, what actually damages the country, who's disrespecting whom, and who gets to decide any of it. I tried to pin it down with two cases — (a) the officer waves us through for free because I went to school with his brother, and (b) we pay the same money to his wife's consultancy for "advice" we don't need. Finance says both are corrupt; Lagos says (a) is a favour between people and (b) is just business; and neither of them thinks the other is even answering the question. Someone also said corruption is an "essentially contested concept", which I took to mean nobody can be wrong, which sounds like a cop-out.

My hunch, and please actually test it rather than agree with it: this isn't a disagreement about corruption at all, it's a disagreement about what a moral argument is supposed to do. London wants a rule you can apply without knowing who you are; Lagos keeps saying it depends on the relationship. If that's right, is it still a real disagreement, or two people answering different questions under one word?

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is there a hidden factual question doing the work (does the fee actually speed things up, or just fund a racket)? Is it a fight about which cases count as evidence — whether his shrug or our disgust is data about anything? Or is it bedrock, where neither side can give a reason the other is obliged to accept? And then, given the diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that cleanly seperates a gift from a fee from a bribe from extortion, a change of question, or the honest answer that this one can't be settled and we should stop pretending otherwise.

One last thing I want taken seriously: when someone said that if we're being consistent we'd have to call lobbying and "networking" corruption too, everyone went quiet. If the word swallows up Christmas presents and drinks after work, does it still do any work at all? Is that a real problem for the concept or just a cheap move?

British English please, and no reading list — I'm not going to read any of it, I just want the shape of the disagreement.
