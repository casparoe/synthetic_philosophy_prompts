I have been trying to get clear about a view I have come to accept about moral status, and I would like you to tell me what would count against it. The view is computationalist: a system matters morally in its own right — can be wronged, can be owed consideration — exactly when it instantiates the right kind of computation, where the right kind is whatever organization a mature cognitive science would cite in explaining why the system feels and cares about anything. On this view substrate matters only as a way of fixing the computation; duplicate the organization in silicon, in wetware, in a plumbing network, or in a sufficiently patient bureaucracy, and you duplicate the moral status. I am aware of the usual objections and I do not want them merely restated. What I want is a map of the view's exposure: which observations, arguments, formal results, or discoveries would count against it, ranked from the ones that should be fatal down to the ones that are merely awkward; what the world would have to turn out to be like for the view to be false, as opposed to merely inconvenient; and an honest verdict on whether the position as I have stated it has been qualified into something that cannot be falsified at all — and if it has, whether that is a defect in this kind of view or simply what any view about the boundaries of sentience has to look like.

To make this concrete, here is the shape of what worries me. First, indeterminacy: it seems that any physical system can be described as implementing indefinitely many computations, so I need to know whether the right kind of computation is discovered in the system or attributed to it by us, and whether a view on which moral status is relative to a description is still a view about the world rather than about our explanatory habits. A colleague in the seminar has proposed what he calls a counterfactual-trace criterion — a system implements a computation only if, for each state of the computation, there is some physical degree of freedom whose value would follow the computation's transition table under interventions on the relevant degrees of freedom — and I would like to know whether that rescues the view or merely relocates the problem, and what it implies for a rock, a slowly rotating storm system, or a slime mold that gets described as solving a maze. Second, triviality and the lookup table: if a vast table keyed to every possible input reproduces the input-output profile of a suffering animal without any of the intermediate causal structure, does my view say it is a patient? If yes, the view is implausibly cheap; if no, I need a principled account of what the table lacks, and I suspect that account will quietly become a claim about substrate or causal structure after all. Third, the commitments about timing and granularity: does a system that runs the right organization at one transition per year, or for a single second, or at sixty percent completion, have moral status, partial moral status, or none? My view seems forced to say something here, and I would rather hear the awkward options than a reassurance.

Finally, tell me what my view predicts that its rivals do not — what it would stake on insects, on cortical organoids, on present-day language models, on patients whose condition we cannot interrogate — and which of those stakes could actually be lost. I would personally find two kinds of evidence most persuasive: a system that clearly instantiates the right computation but that no reasonable person, on reflection, could regard as a moral patient, and a creature whose moral status nobody doubts but whose morally relevant processing turns out not to be computational at any level a cognitive science would recognize. If neither of those can be made sense of, say so plainly, and tell me what the view is actually risking when it makes a claim about who matters — including whether appeals to moral caution under uncertainty have made it unfalsifiable in practice.
