Question:

The paradox of fiction begins from three claims that cannot all be true: (1) we are genuinely moved by fictional characters and events; (2) being genuinely moved by something requires believing that it exists; (3) we do not believe that fictional characters and events exist. A proposed dissolution holds that the paradox trades on an ambiguity in 'moved'. On this proposal, fiction does produce a real occurrent affective response -- the tears, the racing pulse -- but not an emotion proper, since an emotion proper is directed at an object one believes to exist; the occurrent response is 'about' the fiction only in the way a shudder is 'about' a cold room.

(a) Reconstruct the paradox and the proposed dissolution, making explicit which of (1)-(3) the dissolution denies and why that denial is supposed to be more than a redescription of the data.

(b) Assess the dissolution. In particular, say whether the occurrent/emotion-proper line can be drawn without counting ordinary, non-fictional emotions as mere occurrent responses, and whether calling fictional responses 'make-believe' adds anything to the dissolution or merely renames it.

Student's answer:

The paradox arises because we seem committed to all three claims at once. The proposed dissolution rejects (2): if being moved does not require believing that the object exists, then (1) and (3) can both hold and the contradiction disappears. What earns the dissolution its keep is its insistence that the tears and the racing pulse are real affective events -- we are not merely pretending to feel them -- while denying that they are directed at a believed-in object.

This is essentially the make-believe account, on which our responses to fiction belong to a game of pretense: we imagine believing in the characters, and the feeling follows the imagined belief rather than any real one. On this reading the make-believe account and the dissolution are the same proposal, since both deny that the belief in (2) is required. The thought theory takes the opposite line: it holds that genuine emotions can take mere thoughts as their objects, and so it has to give up the occurrent/emotion-proper distinction -- for that view the reader's tears are as genuine an emotion as grief at a real bereavement.

The dissolution therefore succeeds. The paradox rested on a belief requirement that attention to what fiction actually does to us shows to be false, and the only question left is terminological: whether to call the response an emotion or a quasi-emotion. Nothing philosophical turns on the label.
