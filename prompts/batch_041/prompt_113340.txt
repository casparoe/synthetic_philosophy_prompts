Two criteria are commonly fused in the Buddhist case against a self, and it is worth knowing whether they can be prized apart. The first is permanence: if something were the self, it would have to be unchanging, whereas everything found in experience changes. The second is mastery: if something were the self, it would have to be in some measure one's own to command, whereas nothing found in experience answers to the will. In the familiar presentation the two are chained through a third term — what is impermanent is unsatisfying, and what is unsatisfying is not-self — and the chaining obscures which criterion is doing the work. Your task is to construct a pair of cases that separates the two criteria, and to show that the separation is genuine rather than stipulated.

Specifications:

1. In the first case the permanence criterion must rule out the candidate self while the mastery criterion leaves it standing; in the second case, the reverse. Derive each verdict from the case; do not assert it.

2. In both cases the candidate must be something a person could plausibly take themselves to be, or find within themselves — a candidate self, not a mere object. Do not let the two candidates differ in kind (one bodily and the other mental, say); whatever carries the separation must be a difference in a property, not a change of subject matter.

3. The cases must be ones a person could actually undergo or discover in an ordinary life. No fission, transplantation, duplication, teleportation, uploading, total amnesia, or other science-fictional apparatus, and no appeal to exotic metaphysics.

4. Do not use the stock illustrations: no chariot and its parts, no heap of grain, no flame passed from one candle to another.

5. After presenting the cases, do the bookkeeping. For each case, state which premises are true of it, which criterion fires, and why. Then say precisely what the two cases have in common and what differs; if what differs is two features rather than one, say so, and explain why a one-feature contrast cannot be had. Then remove the differing feature and describe what the pair collapses into.

6. Say which premise a defender of the self should reject, and what the rejection costs — whether it saves the self or merely relocates it.

7. Finally, address the gap. Neither criterion, taken alone, appears to conclude that there is no self; each concludes only that something of a certain description is not to be found. State the further premise that would be needed to reach the stronger conclusion, say whether it is credible, and say whether the middle term of the chain — unsatisfactoriness — does any independent work or merely bridges permanence and selfhood.

Each case should be a short paragraph; the commentary should be the bulk of the answer. The point of the exercise is to determine whether these are two arguments or one argument wearing two vocabularies.
