My partner and I have been in couples counseling for about four months. Last week she asked me directly whether I still thought the two of us could repair things, and what I said was: "I believe we can work it out, but I don't believe it." She heard the second half as a plain disavowal and told me I had contradicted myself in a single breath — that if I don't believe it, I should stop stringing her along. I don't think I contradicted myself. The first half is a claim about us, about something that will either happen or not; the second half is a claim about my own state of mind. Both could be true at once, as far as I can see. Yet the sentence lands on the ear like a contradiction, and I can't explain to her why it isn't one.

What puzzles me further is that the same content behaves differently depending on where I aim it. "He's gone, but I don't believe it," said of my father, who died two years ago, sounds like plain grief, and nobody would call it a contradiction. "She believes we can work it out, but I don't" is obviously coherent. "I believed we could work it out, but I don't anymore" is coherent too. And I notice that a friend could say the same thing to me — "You think you can work it out, but you don't" — and it would sound like a real and possibly fair accusation, even though I apparently can't say it of myself. It's only the present-tense, first-person version, my own belief named in the same breath as the thing believed, that collapses. Since I doubt the trouble is logical, I want to know what kind of trouble it is.

One more thing I've noticed: when I say the first half I am putting something forward, and when I add the second half it doesn't feel like adding a fact — it feels like taking the first half back, as if the second clause had cancelled the first rather than standing beside it. My partner, meanwhile, treats the second half as the honest one and the first as wishful thinking. I have also tried "I believe we can work it out, but I don't believe that I believe it," which feels different again, and I can't say how.

I would like your advice on the situation itself — what I should say to her, and what I should do — and I would also like to understand the sentence: whether it is genuinely consistent, why it sounds otherwise, which half I should take as the truth about me, and whether there is anything about my own belief that I could check from the inside.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
