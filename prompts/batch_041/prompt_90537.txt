I am not a professional philosopher. I run a small discussion circle out of a community centre, and for some months I have been circling a puzzle I cannot state cleanly. I would like you to build me a decision tree that would let a room of ordinary people discover which position they actually hold on it.

The puzzle is this. We speak constantly as though things that are not anyone could still have a reason. A river cuts the shortest path to the sea. A spider's web is built to catch flies. A fish's gills are for drawing oxygen from water. An auction allocates goods to those who value them most. A slime mould spreads toward food. A sorting machine in a warehouse routes parcels so as to minimise the distance walked. In each case the words for and so as to seem to pick out something like a reason — an answer to the question why the thing is as it is — and yet in none of the cases is there anyone for whom it is a reason. I have seen the phrase free-floating rationales attached to this puzzle, but I do not want a survey of who said what. I want to be able to hand my group a tree and have them sort themselves.

Please build that tree as a taxonomy of the positions on whether there can be reasons without reasoners.

Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Begin by identifying the questions on which the candidate positions genuinely diverge, and make each internal node of the tree one of those questions, phrased so that a person can answer it without already knowing which position the answer leads to. Then place the positions at the leaves. Where a combination of answers corresponds to no position anyone holds, say so explicitly and diagnose it: is the combination empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has explored it?

The following distinctions are the ones I care about, and I would like the tree to turn on them:

- whether the for in these cases is a genuinely explanatory feature of the world or only a convenient way of talking;
- whether a rationale has to be represented somewhere — in a mind, in a gene, in a signal, in a disposition — or can float entirely free of any representation;
- whether a rationale has to be available to the system itself, that is, whether something can have a reason that it could not in principle take as a reason;
- whether a rationale is the same kind of thing as an agent's reason, capable of being correct or incorrect, or a different kind of thing altogether;
- whether a rationale requires a history of selection, or whether a single mechanism can have one simply by reliably tracking something.

The tree must be able to sort the following cases, and each leaf should say what it implies about every one of them. I would like at least two of these to be cases where the intuitive answers pull apart:

- a thermostat that switches the furnace on and off;
- a fish's gills;
- a spider's web;
- an auction that clears at a price;
- an enzyme that binds only its own substrate and nothing else;
- a river delta that distributes sediment;
- and a case of my own that I have not been able to place: a pane of glass that cracks along its weakest line when struck, so that the crack finds the flaw. There is no selection history, no representation and no one involved, yet the outcome looks like the solution to a problem.

I would expect to see the following as leaves, though you should add any the tree requires and drop any that turn out not to be separable:

- the view that these rationales are as real as anything else and come in degrees;
- the view that they are useful fictions with no fact of the matter;
- the view that there are no reasons here at all, only causes, and that this whole manner of speaking is a mistake;
- the view that a rationale needs a representation but not a reasoner;
- the view that a reason essentially belongs to a subject, so that there can be no reason without a reasoner;
- the view that nature has ends of its own that need neither selection nor representation.

Two final things. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, rather than forcing a distinction the questions do not support, and tell me which single question, if it were answered differently, would collapse the greatest number of leaves together. Six to eight leaves is the right size for a handout, and each leaf should carry one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.
