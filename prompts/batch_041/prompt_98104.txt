I've fallen down a bit of a rabbit hole on the ancient Chinese School of Names, specifically Gongsun Long and his white horse dialogue — the one where one speaker argues that a white horse is not a horse and the other speaker is basically going, come on, obviously it is. The meme version is daft, but the actual argument is alot more interesting than that: the advocate says that horse is what designates shape and white is what designates colour, and what designates colour is not what designates shape, so a white horse isn't a horse; and then, when the objector points out that if you've got a white horse you've certainly got a horse, he comes back with the seeking argument — if you're after a horse, black and yellow horses will do, but if you're after a white horse they won't, so the two can't be the same thing. There's also a running thread about whether white and horse can be seperated from one another, and whether a white horse is a compound of the two. I'm a programmer, comfortable enough with type systems — subtyping and supertypes, product and sum types, the difference between a value and its type, intensional versus extensional equality, the way a WhiteHorse can be handed to something expecting a Horse but not the other way round. I've never studied any Chinese philosophy.

What I want is for you to take Gongsun Long's position — the thesis and the actual arguments, not the punchline — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of types and subtyping, as though I were going to write it up as a little typed language and see what typechecks. Go through each of his moves and say which type-theoretic notion it lines up with; don't just say oh it's subtyping and move on, because I don't think it's that simple.

Then the bit I actually care about: the residue report. Which parts of his argument carry over cleanly, which only carry over if you quietly distort them — maybe by importing a distinction between a type and the description of a type, or between a term and its type, or by pretending his compound talk is just a product type — and which parts have no counterpart in a type system at all. My hunch is that the shape-versus-colour business, and the whole question of what it is to designate something rather than describe it, won't fit, but tell me if that's wrong. For each piece that falls through the translation, say whether that's a hole in my vocabulary (there's something real there that types simply can't say) or a sign that his own claim was vaguer than the gloss makes it sound. And keep the translation honest — don't smuggle in the standard subtyping picture and then announce that he was right, or talking nonsense, all along.
