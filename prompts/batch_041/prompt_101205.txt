For a few years now I've been a fairly devoted reader of the rationalist blogosphere's long-running argument over whether it is ever licensed to say that a language model "believes" something, and I've come to suspect that most of the disagreement is less about any particular model than about which criterion of belief-attribution people are tacitly using. I'd like you to impose some order on that disagreement. Below I've collected five criteria that various friends and commenters have pressed on me; please sort them into tiers, from the one you'd trust most to the one you'd trust least as a test for whether a model genuinely believes some proposition, and give me a sentence or two of justification for each placement. The criteria are: first, the counterfactual-robustness test, on which a model counts as believing P only if P reliably modulates its behavior across a wide range of otherwise-unrelated prompts; second, the interpretability test, on which attribution is licensed only when a probe or circuit-level analysis recovers the content of P from the model's internals; third, the bare Dennettian test, on which any attribution is licensed provided it improves our predictions of the model's behavior, with no further demand that the attributed state be real in any deeper sense; fourth, the commitment test, on which belief requires the model to be disposed to defend P, to revise it in the face of counterevidence, and to treat its negation as something it must actively work around; and fifth, the functional-role test, on which a model believes P just in case P plays the right causal role in its transitions between inputs and outputs, regardless of whether P is recoverable by introspection, probing, or any human-legible method.

A couple of clarifications, since I know how these discussions tend to go. I'm not asking which criterion is true by the lights of some fully worked-out theory of mind; I'm asking which you would actually trust, the way one trusts a well-behaved instrument, if you had to decide tomorrow whether some particular claim about a model's beliefs was worth acting on. And please don't dissolve the ranking by announcing that all five are secretly the same criterion or that none of them can be assessed in advance; if you think two of them are equivalent, say so and rank them as a single tier, but then still rank the tiers against the others. Assume throughout that the model in question is a reasonably capable transformer-based system whose internals are partially, but not fully, accessible to the people applying these tests.

Give me the answer as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just paragraphs written the way you'd explain it to a friend over email.
