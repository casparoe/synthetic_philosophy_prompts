So I'm working on a piece — a kind of ideas-essay for a general-reader column — about Machiavelli and luck, and I want you to tear into my draft before I file it. I dont have anyone around who'll actually push back, so go hard and don't be polite about it.

Here's the argument I'm trying to make. Everyone knows the caricature: Machiavelli says you make your own luck, fortune favors the bold, virtù beats fate, all that. I think that's basically backwards, or at least it's doing sleight of hand, and here's the reading I want to defend.

My move is to split fortune into two jobs. First there's the deal you're handed before you do anything at all: what century you're born into, what family, what body, what talents. Call that the dealt hand. Second there's the ongoing noise — the stuff that happens once you're already playing, the plague, the invasion, the pope who suddenly dies and changes every alliance you'd built. Machiavelli's river image, the one where fortune is like a flooding river that tears up everything unless you've built dikes and embankments ahead of time during the calm season, is really about the second kind. You can build dikes for that. You can't build dikes for the first kind, because you're not around yet to build anything — you just are what you were handed.

So my claim is: virtù is a skill at the second kind and is more or less helpless against the first. Which means that when Machiavelli says fortune is the arbiter of roughly half our actions, the "half" is smuggling something in — it sounds like a clean 50/50 split, but it's really two different problems bundled together, and virtù only gets to compete in one of them. Against the dealt hand, virtù is zero. Change one variable in the dealt hand and the same man with the same virtù ends up a footnote instead of a founder.

Then the part I actually find interesting, and the part I most want you to check me on. If virtù is mostly the skill of adapting — of noticing the times have turned and switching your whole mode of operating to match them, cautious when the times want caution, impetuous when they want impetuous — then Machiavelli has a problem, because he also plainly admires the impetuous, the young men who just charge, and he says fortune is more often conquered by the impetuous than by the cold and calculating. But impetuosity is itself a fixed setting, isn't it. The impetuous guy isn't flexible, he's just fortunate that his one setting happens to match his era. So the two halves of the advice pull against each other: be adaptable, and also be bold. And if you genuinely can't change your nature when the times change — which Machiavelli more or less concedes, people don't swap their stripes — then the whole adapt-yourself advice is useless, and fortune wins by default, because eventually the times will turn and your one setting will be the wrong one.

My resolution, and I honestly don't know if it holds: virtù isn't the ability to adopt whatever setting you like, it's the ability to read which setting the times currently reward and to already be the sort of person who has that setting. So it's really closer to a meta-luck — you're lucky if your nature matches your age — dressed up as a skill. Which would make Machiavelli a much gloomier thinker than the self-help version of him, closer to a guy saying the best you can do is be fully yourself and hope the moment agrees with you.

Things I want your take on: (1) am I being too clever splitting fortune in two — does the text actually bear that out, or am I inventing a distinction and reading it back in; (2) is the adaptability-versus-boldness contradiction real, or am I manufacturing a tension by flattening what's really a more careful argument; (3) does the gloomy conclusion actually follow, or is there a reading where virtù still does real work against the dealt hand; and (4) be honest — is this interesting, or is it the kind of thing every grad student notices in week two and I'm just dressing it up as a column. Don't worry about page numbers or citations, I'm not asking you to source anything, I just want to know whether the reasoning holds up and whether there's more in it than I've found.
