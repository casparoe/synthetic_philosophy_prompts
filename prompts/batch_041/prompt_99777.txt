You are a philosopher of biology with working knowledge of the function and teleology debate, teleosemantics, and the philosophy of mind, and you are used to being asked for numbers rather than essays. I have been arguing with a colleague about a claim we cannot seem to state the same way twice, and I want your credence in it plus the decomposition. Call it the free-floating-rationale claim: for at least some biological traits, there exists a determinate set of considerations that would justify the trait's contribution if it were produced by an agent who grasped those considerations, and citing that set is part of the correct explanation of why the trait is there, even though no agent and no inner representation ever entertained it. Two examples will fix the target. First, the well-worn greylag goose that keeps making the retrieval motion after the egg it was rolling has been taken away, and that prefers a comically oversized model egg to a real one: the behaviour is well described by the rationale "get that egg back into the nest", yet nothing in the bird seems to be operating on that consideration. Second, the ethological finding that gull chicks peck not at the parent's whole beak but at the small coloured spot on it: the rationale "peck where the food is" survives, but it survives as a rationale nobody ever held, decoupled from the mechanism that actually drives the pecking. I take the goose case to show that the rationale and the mechanism can come apart; my colleague takes both cases to show that "rationale" talk is a convenient gloss we project, with no explanatory weight of its own.

Please give me your credence, as a number, in the claim as I have stated it, not in some nearby claim about function or about natural selection, and if you think the claim as stated is ambiguous, say how you are disambiguating it and then answer the disambiguated version. Build the number rather than announcing it: start by listing the sub-questions the credence actually depends on, and give each a rough probability and a one-line justification. I would expect the list to include at least (a) whether there is a determinate fact of the matter about what a trait is "for", independent of our explanatory interests, rather than some deflationary, interest-relative, or pluralist account being correct; (b) whether that fact is best rendered normatively, as a rationale or set of reasons, rather than as a bare causal disposition or a selection history; (c) whether the rationale can be genuinely free-floating, that is, whether it can do explanatory work with no reasoner and no internal representation posited, without smuggling agency back in; (d) whether citing the rationale adds explanatory power over the best mechanical account or merely redescribes it in a flattering idiom; and (e) whether there is a principled way to keep genuine free-floating rationales apart from the cheap post hoc rationalisations we can invent for any behaviour after the fact. Tell me how the terms combine, since I assume something close to a conjunction but you may judge that some of them are conditions rather than independent conjuncts, give the resulting number, and then identify the term your number is most sensitive to and what single observation, experiment, or argument would move it most.

For what it is worth, I sit around 0.4 and my colleague sits around 0.9. Before you answer, tell me whether the two of us are most likely disagreeing about one of (a) through (e) or somewhere else entirely, and whether the disagreement is substantive or is really about how to read the words "for" and "rationale". Think it through carefully, flag any place where you suspect you are pattern-matching to a familiar position rather than reasoning about the claim, and keep the final answer to the number plus the decomposition.
