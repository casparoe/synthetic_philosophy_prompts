After thirty years in the physical sciences I have spent the last two years reading philosophy, and I have become stuck on a decision problem I cannot put down. A fair coin is tossed until it first comes up heads. If that happens on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^n · 2^n / n dollars: minus two dollars if heads comes on the first toss, plus two if on the second, minus eight thirds if on the third, plus four if on the fourth, and so on. Computed term by term, multiplying each payoff by its probability 1/2^n, the expected value is the alternating sum of 1/n, which converges to minus the natural logarithm of two. But the series is only conditionally convergent — the sum of the absolute values of its terms diverges — and the rearrangement theorem I have now seen stated and proved tells me the terms can be reordered to produce any real total whatever, or none. A variant that adds a fixed positive amount to every payoff, better in every outcome with no compensating downside, inherits exactly the same trouble.

I want a poem that makes this felt rather than explained, and I want ordering to do the structural work, since ordering is precisely what the mathematics turns on.

Please write a poem of no more than twenty-four lines, in the voice of someone who has spent a career believing that a well-posed question has a well-defined answer and is now doing the arithmetic by hand. Give it a refrain whose wording is fixed but whose final slot holds the current running total, and let the refrain return at least three times, with a different total each time. Structure the poem so that the same term-lines appear twice: once in the order the series is naturally written, and once rearranged so that the running total climbs rather than settles. The totals in the refrain must be exact partial sums of the series in the order printed, so that someone who knows this problem can check the poem rather than merely recognize it. Let the closing lines say, without preaching, that the number the poem ends on belongs to the reading order and not to the gamble. I will read the rules aloud before the poem, so it need not restate them, and it should not depend on the reader recognizing a name or a citation.

After the poem, add a note of no more than one hundred words saying how confident you are that the mathematics and the philosophical point have been handled correctly, naming the single claim you are least sure of, and stating where the poem takes liberties with the idea or leans on a contested reading of it.

The whole response, poem and note together, must not exceed three hundred and fifty words.
