Here's the gist of what he wrote. Grounding is the relation between more fundamental and less fundamental facts; it's basically the metaphysical cousin of causation — causation is how one event brings about another, grounding is how one fact makes another obtain. Like causation, he says, grounding is irreflexive, asymmetric and transitive, so it's a strict partial order. Then he says the chain of grounding has to bottom out: you can't have an infinite regress of facts grounding facts grounding further facts, that'd be vicious, so there has to be a fundamental level of facts that ground everything else and aren't themselves grounded — and that's what fundamentality just is. He ends on the payoff, which he says is the whole reason grounding got popular: it lets you be a realist about ordinary objects while also saying they're nothing over and above the physical, since the table-facts are grounded in the particle-facts. His example for the asymmetry is that the singleton set containing Socrates is grounded in Socrates himself but not the other way round.
