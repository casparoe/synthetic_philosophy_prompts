I am a second-year graduate student who recently transferred out of a philosophy of science program and into a physics department, and I have spent the past several months teaching myself the foundations of statistical mechanics for the first time. The topic that has absorbed me most is Maxwell's demon, largely because so much of the contemporary discussion turns on questions Maxwell could not have posed in the form we now pose them: whether information is itself a physical quantity, whether the demon is finally exorcised by charging it for the thermodynamic cost of erasing its memory rather than for the cost of measurement, whether a computation can in principle be run with no minimum energy expenditure, and whether information is ever genuinely destroyed, as the black hole debate has forced physicists to ask. I run a small reading group that meets next week, and for the opening session I want to set up a careful exercise in counterfactual intellectual history rather than yet another summary of the standard story everyone has already heard.

Please write an essay that predicts, as precisely as the evidence allows, what Maxwell himself would have thought about these developments, organized issue by issue. I am less interested in a verdict of the form he would have agreed or disagreed than in a reconstruction of the specific commitments that would have driven his reactions. For each of the four issues above, say what position he would most likely have taken, what in his actual intellectual program supports that prediction, and where his nineteenth-century vocabulary or metaphysical commitments would have forced him to reframe the question before he could even address it. I am especially curious about the following: whether he would have accepted that information is a physical resource at all, or whether he would have insisted that the demon's advantage lies in knowledge rather than in any physical subsystem; whether he would have located the flaw in the demon's acquisition of knowledge or in its bookkeeping and memory; whether he would have granted that reversible computation escapes any minimum energy cost; and whether his statistical reading of the second law would have led him to resist the claim that information can be destroyed. I would also like you to flag which of your predictions rest on solid evidence about his views and which are frankly speculative extrapolations, to name the places where he would most likely have simply been wrong, and to close by ranking the four predictions from most to least secure. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind, since I am working offline and care only about the reasoning; and do not spend the essay rehearsing the conventional history of the demon, which I already know.
