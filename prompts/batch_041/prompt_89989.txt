I spent 35 years as a research chemist before I retired, and since then I've been trying to teach myself philosophy, mostly from lecture recordings while walking the dog, which is probably not the recommended method.

The other week I listened to a guest lecture on free speech, and the lecturer gave what he said was Mill's view, roughly this: the harm principle says the only legitimate reason to restrict what anyone does is to prevent harm to other people; since speech by itself doesn't harm anybody, Mill thought all speech should be legally protected, with exactly one exception — the corn dealer case, where Mill says an opinion may "justly incur punishment when delivered orally to an excited mob assembled before the house of a corn-dealer," because that constitutes "a positive instigation to some mischievous act." But he said that exception proves the rule, since it's really just speech turning into an act of violence. He also said Mill thought the cure for bad speech was more speech, that he basically invented the marketplace of ideas, and that Mill was clear being offended or disgusted never counts as harm — which is why he'd have refused to ban hate speech or Holocaust denial, and why he'd have told the neo-Nazis to go ahead and march so long as nobody's throwing punches.

I'm a scientist, so I like things I can check, and something about this didn't sit right, though I couldn't tell you exactly what. I have hunch it's the corn dealer bit. Can you go through it clause by clause and tell me what Mill would sign off on, what he'd wince at, what's a modern gloss being read back into him, and whether the lecturer's punchline — Mill protects everything short of incitement — actually follows from the parts that are right?
