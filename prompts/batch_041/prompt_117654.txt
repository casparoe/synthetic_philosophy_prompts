I've been reading Hume on taste and I've ended up in a spot I cant tell is stable, so I want a consistency check before I say any of this out loud in a seminar. Here's what I actually believe. (1) Beauty isn't a feature of the object, its a sentiment in whoever's looking — if you and I react differently to the same sunset, neither of us is getting the sunset wrong. (2) But I dont believe all taste is equal. Some people's read on a novel or a piece of music is just better than other people's, and saying that isn't just dressing up social status as discernment. (3) What makes a judgment of taste correct, I think, is that it matches what a good critic would feel — someone with real sensitivity, a lot of exposure, no axe to grind, and a cool head. (4) I dont mean "good critic" as some unreachable ideal. I mean it in a flat empirical way: the good critics are just the people whose verdicts the rest of us end up agreeing with once we've seen enough, so the standard is a fact about us rather than a fact about the painting. (5) I also think two people with equally good taste — same exposure, same sensitivity, no prejudice on either side — can stand in front of the same painting and one finds it sublime and the other finds it sentimental, and neither of them has made a mistake. (6) And I think a perfect forgery is exactly as beautiful as the original. Finding out it's a fake should change what its worth, not how beautiful it is.

So: which of these are genuinely pulling against each other, which only look like they clash, and what's the cheapest repair for each real clash — and what do I lose by taking it? I'd rather you tell me which single commitment is doing the most damage than hand me a list of everything thats slightly awkward.
