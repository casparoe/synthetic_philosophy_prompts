I'm a teaching assistant for a first-year course on political ideologies, and the same move keeps surfacing in my discussion sections. Ask what makes a position left-wing or right-wing and someone reaches for the horseshoe: draw the spectrum as a line, bend both ends toward each other, and observe that the far left and the far right curl round until they are nearly touching. The picture is then made to carry an argument. If the ends meet, the extremes are one phenomenon wearing two costumes; the spectrum is a single dimension whose middle is the only defensible place to stand; and the left-right distinction, having no positive content at either pole, reduces to a matter of temperament — the same fire, differently banked. I argued about this with my brother over the holidays and he deployed the horseshoe to end the conversation in about ninety seconds, so I want a proper audit rather than a rebuttal: I want to know how much of the argument the geometry is actually doing.

Give me the correspondence table first — what in the political domain is supposed to play the part of the endpoints, the bending, the distance from the middle, and the curvature that brings the ends together — then say where the mapping breaks, sorting the failures into those that are merely decorative and those that matter for the conclusion being drawn. Be explicit about what the source domain quietly imports and the target may not have: that the axis is one-dimensional and metric; that the two poles are symmetrical and equidistant from a centre; that a position is a point rather than a coalition of people who agree about some things and not others; that travel along the axis is a single kind of change. And press on the picture's real payload. It presupposes that each side is held together by something a single coordinate can measure, whereas the alternative on offer — that each pole is bound only by opposition to the other, or by a shared enemy, and so converges with its opposite precisely as the opposition intensifies — needs no geometry at all and may be the actual claim in disguise.

Close by telling me what survives: whether anything in the horseshoe's conclusion about the extremes, the centre, or the reality of the distinction can be re-established on the non-geometric reading, and what a better picture would be — a second axis, a network, a set of overlapping coalitions, or no spatial metaphor at all — along with the work that the better picture would leave undone. Write it as continuous prose with the table embedded; I don't need citations or a reading list, I'll supply those myself.
