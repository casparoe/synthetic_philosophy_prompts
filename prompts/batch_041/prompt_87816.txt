Economists and political theorists typically treat the tragedy of the commons as a problem of rivalrous consumption under open access: too many users each take too much of a subtractable resource, and the standard remedies — privatization, or the self-governance arrangements Elinor Ostrom associated with clearly defined boundaries, graduated sanctions, and nested rule-making — are designed for communities whose members are in principle free to leave. I want you to work with a variant I will call the locked-in commons, defined by two features: the resource is subtractable and non-excludable as usual, but the users have made relationship-specific and largely sunk investments that make exit prohibitively costly, so that the operative choice is not "extract more or leave" but "extract more or stay and bear the consequences."

Argue for a definite position on the following thesis: in a locked-in commons, the binding constraint is the cost of exit rather than the rivalry of the resource, and consequently the remedies that dominate both the market and the Ostrom traditions are misdirected — the effective intervention is one that lowers the cost of exit, or credibly threatens it, rather than one that regulates extraction or builds self-governance capacity.

Develop the argument on its own terms, using whatever concrete illustration best brings out the mechanism, and commit to the thesis rather than cataloguing the alternatives. State your reasons plainly, construct the strongest objection you can to your own position, and say what you take that objection to show. Be explicit about which steps rest on empirical assumptions you cannot verify, and give your honest confidence in the argument as a whole; but do not retreat into a survey that leaves the verdict open.
