I’ve been sitting in on a small philosophy of mind reading group that has spent the term on personal identity and uploading, and I want to predict what our facilitator—call her Dr. V—will say about a new case before she rules on it. Dr. V is a functionalist about mental states but she has always insisted that “survival” in these debates means numerical identity, not merely psychological similarity, and she has been unusually consistent across the cases we’ve discussed. Her verdicts so far: (1) a standard brain transplant, where A’s brain is moved into B’s body and B’s brain is destroyed, preserves A; (2) gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement of A’s brain with silicon chips that are functionally identical and are wired in so that each chip takes over before the biological neuron is removed preserves A; (3) a sudden replacement, in which all of A’s neurons are destroyed in one instant and a functionally identical silicon network is installed immediately afterward, does not preserve A; (4) destructive teleportation, where A is scanned, destroyed, and reconstructed from the scan at another location, does not preserve A; (5) a long coma or reversible shutdown of the brain, even one in which all neural activity stops for hours, preserves A; (6) splitting A’s brain and transplanting each hemisphere into a separate body means that neither resulting person is A; and (7) scanning A’s brain and using the scan to create a copy while A remains alive and intact means that A survives and the copy is a new person.

The new case is a procedure one of us called “scan-and-swap.” Each of A’s neurons is scanned while active, then destroyed, and a silicon chip is fabricated from the scan and inserted immediately to take its place. The chip is functionally identical to the neuron it replaces, receives the same inputs and sends the same outputs, and the procedure is carried out one neuron at a time over several months, so the whole brain is never replaced at once. There is, however, a brief gap of a few milliseconds between the destruction of each neuron and the first activation of its replacement chip. The resulting system has all of A’s memories, dispositions, and personality, and A is awake and conversing throughout the procedure. I want to know what Dr. V will say about scan-and-swap, but I am less interested in a bare yes-or-no than in the rule she is implicitly running. Lay out the candidate rules that fit her seven verdicts—for example, a rule that treats gradual replacement as sufficient because there is no moment at which the whole person ceases, a rule that requires continuous causal propagation from each original neuron into its replacement, a rule that treats psychological continuity plus a no-branching condition as what matters, and any other rule that fits the track record equally well—and say what each predicts about scan-and-swap. Then give me your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring that reading of her, and name the further case that would best distinguish the surviving rules from one another.
