I'm the story editor for a small history podcast -- six of us, an episode every two weeks -- and we're planning a season on the big why questions. You know the ones: why did the industrial revolution start in Britain and not in China, why did the scientific revolution happen in Europe and not in the Islamic world, why did the Maya collapse and not the Aztecs. Our excecutive producer keeps killing my pitches with "you can't ask it that way," and she can never quite say why, so I want to stop arguing about vibes and build us an actual procedure.

Here's the raw material I've got. A friend of mine who did a philosophy degree wrote me three pages of notes when I complained about this. Her main point is that a why-question never comes alone -- you're never just asking why P, you're asking why P rather than Q -- and the Q is doing most of the work, so that swapping it out gives you a different question with a different answer even though the P sits there unchanged. She says you can feel this by flipping a question around: why is there something rather than nothing sounds like the deepest question in the world, and why is there nothing rather than something sounds like a joke, and no fact has changed, only which outcome we're quietly treating as the default. She also thinks a lot of bad history comes from picking a foil that isn't a real alternative to anything, so you end up explaining why one country wasn't some other country, and that the units are usually rotten -- "China" across two thousand years, "Europe" across a dozen very different societies, as if each were one actor with one strategy. I suspect there's more in the notes that I'm not getting.

Could you turn this into a screening procedure we can actually run on a pitch in the ten minutes before we decide whether to commission it? I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, which step is where a question dies, and which steps are judgment calls dressed up as tests. Tell me which of her points have to survive into the checklist and which ones can be dropped, and be honest about what the procedure can't do -- I'd rather it say this one needs an actual historian than fake a verdict. Then run it on three pitches: (1) why did the industrial revolution happen in Britain and not in China; (2) why did the scientific revolution happen in Europe and not in the Islamic world; (3) why did the potato famine devastate Ireland and not Scotland. That third one is my pet project, so if the procedure kills it, kill it. One more constraint: our host is not a historian and neither am I, so whatever you write has to be usable by someone who can't reliably tell a good answer from a plausible-sounding one.
