I am a novelist, and for the past three years I have sat on the ethics committee of a mid-sized natural history and technology museum — a body that meets quarterly to advise the trustees on the questions the staff would rather not answer alone. We now face one of those questions, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before the vote.

The museum has built a touring exhibition called Minds Without Brains. Its premise is that minds come in degrees, and that the line between a creature that matters and a device that does not is nowhere cleanly drawn. Four displays carry the argument. The first is a colony of leafcutter ants, whose coordinated foraging the curators describe as collective decision-making. The second is a slime mould that learns, in the loose sense the catalogue uses, to cross a maze and to stop flinching at a harmless chemical. The third is a small homeostatic device the curators have nicknamed the preference engine — in substance an elaborate thermostat that holds a setpoint and that, when the setpoint is violated, emits a signal the catalogue calls distress and behaves in a way the catalogue calls trying to escape. The fourth is a wall of text asking visitors where they would draw the line.

A trustee has objected. Her view, roughly, is that the exhibition cheapens moral seriousness by placing ants and thermostats on a single scale, and that the preference engine in particular invites the public to mistake a sensor for a sufferer. A curator replies that the ants are no more than algorithms either, and that the trustee's objection proves the exhibition's point rather than refuting it. The committee has been handed a draft policy, prepared by staff, that would assign every being in the collection to one of five tiers of standing, from no standing at all to full standing, and would require welfare provisions — ventilation, food, the freedom to withdraw — for anything above the second tier. The exhibition is scheduled to open in nine weeks, and the committee votes at its next meeting, in three.

Please write the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — whether the exhibition opens as designed, opens with specified conditions, is modified, or is held back — with a time frame the committee can act on, and it should say what tier each of the four displays falls into under the draft policy and where, if anywhere, the policy itself ought to be amended. It should take the trustee's objection seriously rather than dismissing it, and take the curator's reply seriously too. Two further points. First, the board will not be impressed by a reading list: give me a self-contained argument, without citations, and do not lean on named theories the committee will not have read. Second, I have spent my working life inventing minds that are not human, and I am not persuaded that a five-tier ladder is the right instrument at all; if you agree, say so, and if you do not, tell me plainly why I am wrong. Aim for something between six hundred and a thousand words.
