I am a retired experimental physicist who has spent the last several years reading philosophy of causation, and I have constructed an argument that I suspect proves too much. I would like you to identify the step I should give up and to price the alternatives.

(1) C causally influences E just in case some intervention on C would change E, or the distribution of E.
(2) In a Bell-type experiment on entangled pairs, the choice of setting at one wing leaves the outcome statistics at the other wing untouched; the marginal distribution of the remote outcomes is invariant under the local setting. This is a theorem of the formalism and a robust experimental result.
(3) Therefore, by (1) and (2), the setting at one wing does not causally influence the outcome at the other.
(4) Bell's theorem tells us that no theory can reproduce the joint outcome statistics of such experiments unless the joint distribution fails to factorize into wing-local terms given any shared state.
(5) What (4) rules out, then, is not a causal connection between the wings but a certain factorization property of the joint distribution.
(6) A failure of factorization that leaves every marginal invariant cannot be used to send a signal, and relativity forbids signalling; so the experiments give no ground for saying that anything acts across the gap.
(7) Hence the nonlocality commonly attributed to these correlations is a misnomer: it names a formal feature of the joint distribution rather than a relation between distant events.
(8) The celebrated tension between quantum correlations and relativity is therefore an artifact of a vocabulary that assumes any irreducible correlation must be underwritten by a causal influence.

This proves more than I can believe. I am attached to the interventionist criterion in (1) and unwilling to surrender the empirical content of (2) and (4), yet (8) makes a large literature sound like a category error, which is itself grounds for distrusting the argument. Which step should I reject? Explain why the step you reject looked so compelling, and tell me what I would be committed to if I kept the empirical results and the interventionist criterion and refused to give up anything else.

Please state how confident you are in each part of your diagnosis, and single out the point about which you are least sure.
