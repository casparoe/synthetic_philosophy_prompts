**Three Cultures, or Two Variables?**

There's a three-way distinction I keep running into for how moral conflict works in different societies. In a culture of honour, a person's worth is held to live in other people's estimation, so an insult is a genuine loss that has to be answered; the person who has been wronged is expected to recover their standing personally, often by force, because referring the matter to someone else would itself be a confession of weakness. In a culture of dignity, worth is thought to be intrinsic and independent of anyone's opinion, so insults are beneath notice; wrongs are referred to impersonal institutions that are supposed to apply rules without regard to who is who. In what is usually called a culture of victimhood, moral standing attaches to having been harmed and slights are treated as morally serious; the wronged party typically does not retaliate in person but appeals to a wider audience, to be believed, to have the harm acknowledged, and to have the offender publicly marked down.

The usual framing treats these as three rivals and asks which is best. I think that is a mistake in the framing, and what follows is my attempt at a fix.

The three-way distinction, as I read it, runs two separate questions together. The first is where a person's moral standing comes from: from other people's ascription, from something intrinsic, or from harm suffered. The second is who is responsible for enforcing that standing when it is damaged: the injured party, impersonal institutions, or a moral audience. Honour is ascribed standing enforced by self-help. Dignity is intrinsic standing enforced by institutions. Victimhood, on my reading, is intrinsic standing enforced by an audience — and this is the claim I care about: victimhood culture is not a third theory of worth at all. It inherits dignity's metaphysics, in which your worth does not depend on what anyone thinks of you, and then attaches honour's agonism to it, minus the violence. What it genuinely adds is a different enforcement technology: documentation, testimony, and an audience that can be mobilised at scale.

If that is right, three things follow. First, the complaint that victimhood culture is dignity culture with an honour culture's temper is not a complaint from outside the typology; it is a structural description of it. Second, the friction people report is a mismatch between a metaphysics that says slights cannot touch your worth and a practice that treats each slight as an injury requiring public redress. On the intrinsic-standing premise the audience should not matter, yet the redress mechanism is entirely audience-dependent. That combination looks unstable to me, and I would predict it generates a great deal of conflict about standing rather than about the original harm. Third, if the two variables really are independent, the grid has an unoccupied cell: standing that is ascribed by others but enforced by impersonal institutions. I have been calling this deference culture — worth conferred by rank, title, or estate, and defended not by the person but by officials: sumptuary rules, licensing, a state that polices a status order. I cannot decide whether this is a genuine type the three-way distinction has missed, or merely a transitional phase in the decline of honour cultures, in which the violence has been taken from the gentry and handed to the courts while the ascriptive theory of worth has not yet been replaced. If it is the latter, my two-variable story collapses back into a one-variable story about how far the state has monopolised violence.

On the normative question — whether any of these is morally superior — I want to say yes, but not in the way it is usually asked. Each package looks stable only when the enforcement mechanism matches the theory of standing that people actually hold. Dignity culture looks plainly superior where institutions are trustworthy and like a bad joke where they are not, which is presumably part of why honour norms persist where the police are useless. So which culture is superior may be the wrong question, and which mismatches we are currently living inside may be the right one. I would rather be told that this is a well-known point I have re-derived badly than be told it is original.
