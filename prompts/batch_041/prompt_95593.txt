For a seminar that pairs classical Confucian ethics with twentieth-century fiction, I want a reading of Anthony Burgess's *A Clockwork Orange* (1962) and Stanley Kubrick's 1971 film of it through the Mencius–Xunzi dispute about human nature. The familiar reading treats both as parables of free will: the state takes a man capable of choosing violence and, by aversion conditioning, makes him incapable of choosing anything. I want the Confucian frame taken seriously on its own terms rather than used as a label for that reading.

Press these questions. Is the Ludovico technique a Xunzian solution or a travesty of one? Xunzi holds that human nature is bad and that goodness is the product of deliberate effort and, above all, of ritual — which does not extinguish desire but refines and channels it. The technique delivers artifice without ritual: it welds pain to desire instead of reshaping it. Does Xunzi's own standard therefore condemn the technique, making the Minister of the Interior a bad reader of Xunzi rather than a good one? Mencius, by contrast, holds that the four sprouts of virtue cannot be installed from outside, only cultivated, which makes the state's project not merely cruel but conceptually confused about what virtue is. Note that on both accounts the Alex who emerges from the treatment is not good, since both demand the agent's own effort; the disagreement about human nature may therefore not be what decides the case. Where do Alex's musical feeling, his flashes of something like shame, and his relish in cruelty bear on which philosopher the novel stands nearer to?

Then the endings. The British edition's twenty-first chapter has Alex outgrow violence of his own accord, drawn toward marriage and a son; the American edition dropped that chapter, and Kubrick filmed the truncated version, so the film leaves Alex relapsed and pleased about it. Does the twenty-first chapter vindicate Mencius — a sprout maturing of itself — or Xunzi — a man remade by the forms of family life? And does the film's bleaker ending turn the story into a Xunzian or Hobbesian parable that the novel refuses to be?

Close by saying what the work shows about this debate that argument alone does not, and where it resists the reading and an honest critic has to admit as much. I don't need citations or a reading list; work from scenes that are common knowledge.
