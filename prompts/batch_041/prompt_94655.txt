I have been trying to work out when a choice reveals a preference and when it merely reveals something about the choice situation, and my verdicts keep colliding. A man chooses tea over coffee on Monday and coffee over tea on Tuesday: I say his preference changed, not that he had a single preference all along. A woman buys a lottery ticket: I say that reveals a preference over lotteries, not a belief that she will win. A shopper buys the jam labelled best seller when she would have bought the other jar had the labels been swapped: I say this is a menu effect, not a preference between the jams. A person pays a friend to hide her cigarettes: I say this reveals a genuine second-order preference about her future choices, not merely a preference over outcomes. Someone reports A better than B, B better than C, and C better than A, and chooses accordingly on different occasions: I say no single utility function captures her, so at least one of her choices must be explained away. But a person who chooses a smaller reward now over a larger reward later, and then, when both are delayed by a year, chooses the larger one: I say that is a genuine change in what she wants, not an inconsistency to be explained away. My tentative principle is that a choice reveals a preference only when it is robust under changes that the agent herself would count as irrelevant. Please state that principle, or a better one, precisely, give its verdicts on cases I have not listed, and say which of my verdicts it forces me to give up — and what it says about someone who chooses the option she believes is worse for her because she wants to be the sort of person who keeps her word.
