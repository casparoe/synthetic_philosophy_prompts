Fifteen years ago I left the school I grew up in to teach modern history and political theory at a sixth-form college, and next month I have to go back and give the Founders' Day address. The school was endowed in the 1870s with money made a generation or two earlier in the carrying trade and then in indigo in Bengal; there is a portrait in the hall that I have walked past since I was fourteen, and I have sat through four Founders' Day addresses, all of which managed to say the word "benefactor" without flinching. This year the head wrote to me in advance asking me not to dig anything up, and I have agreed not to, which I suspect is itself an entry in the ledger I am trying to write. The audience will be mixed in a way that matters: the founder's descendants in the front row, a visiting party from our partner school in Kolkata, three hundred pupils most of whom are fifteen and have never heard of the Ferguson–Tharoor dispute over the Raj, and a local paper that will print whatever I say. I want an address of roughly eight hundred words that is genuinely an occasion speech — celebratory in form, spoken to teenagers, not a historiography seminar — and that does not lie to any of those groups.

I want you to work from a framework I have come to think the ordinary "balance sheet" metaphor obscures. A balance sheet presupposes a unit of account, a baseline, and a rule for netting one party's credit against another's debit, and the argument about empire usually proceeds as though all three were given by the facts, when in fact each is a choice. Make three of those choices visible in the speech without lecturing anyone about them. First: the counterfactual against which "what the empire built" is measured can be drawn at least three ways — no contact at all, the Mughal and princely order continuing, or the empire's own declared intentions — and the verdict flips with the baseline before a single fact is settled. Second: a ledger whose entries all appear twice, so that every railway is a debit somewhere, cannot be totalled unless you have already decided that the parties may be netted against one another, which is precisely the thing in dispute. Third: what we inherit is a survivor's ledger, because the institutions that outlasted 1947 are disproportionately the ones that could pay for themselves on the ground, so the archive of "what was left behind" is biased in a particular direction. Treat these as things the speech assumes rather than explains, and hold to two self-imposed rules that I would like the address itself to acknowledge: no contested statistics, since both sides dispute the numbers and the address should therefore live one level up, at the level of the ledger's rules; and no citations, no names of historians, no titles or dates — the argument has to carry itself on the audience's own experience of the school.

What I am most anxious about is the ending. A Founders' Day address has to hand something to the fifteen-year-olds, and I do not want the handover to be either "the past is complicated" or "you owe a debt." What I want is closer to a repair rule: an account of what a debit in an old ledger does and does not transmit. My own view is that obligations of states do not descend on individuals like estate debts, but that the distributional residue does — a narrower and much more awkward claim — and I would like the speech to build toward it, with the celebration and the accounting sharing the same sentences rather than taking turns.
