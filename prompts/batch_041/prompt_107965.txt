I've been lurking on here and on a couple of philosophy blogs for years (yes I'm the kind of person who reads the whole comment section, yes I've read too much of the sequences), and I'm now a year into a philosophy MA after a biochem undergrad. I'm applying for a small internal grant, four months of work, basically a summer, no lab costs, to write a paper on the concept of disease, and I need you to turn my half-formed idea into the actual proposal document.

Here's the idea, such as it is. Everyone fights about whether "disease" is a descriptive biological kind (the organ or system isn't doing what it's supposed to, by species-typical standards) or a normative one (it's a disease when it's bad for you, and the label is how we mark that). I think both sides are treating it like a predicate with a fixed extension, and I suspect that's the mistake. Two things I can't stop chewing on. First, the reference class thing: pick your comparison class — this population, that population, this environment, this age band, some notion of "designed" function — and the same physiology comes out either as dysfunction or as normal variation, and nothing in the physiology tells you which class is the right one. Second, the stakes thing: whether we call something a disease seems to depend on what can be done about it and what's riding on the label. A slightly off blood sugar reading gets to be a disease when there's a drug and a screening programme attached, and the whole pre-diabetes thing gets quietly walked back when it turns out the intervention doesn't help, even though the reading is identical. That looks less like a natural kind and more like a context-sensitive predicate of the "knows" or "tall" variety, where the comparison class and the stakes do the work. My hunch is that this dissolves a good chunk of the naturalism/normativism fight: the naturalist is right about the part the biology fixes (given a class), the normativist is right about the part where class and threshold get chosen, and the real question is what the mechanism of the context-sensitivity is and whether it's one mechanism or several.

I genuinely don't know if that's a real position or a confused one, so if you think the idea is bad, say so in the proposal rather than papering over it — but write it as if it's worth four months. Also, please don't include citations or a reading list; I'll slot those in myself, I'd rather the prose not lean on them, and I don't want you guessing at titles. Sections I want, in this order with these rough lengths: "One-paragraph pitch" (about 100 words); "The question" (150-200, stated so it has a determinate answer, not "explore the relationship between X and Y"); "Why it's still open" (250-350, why the existing positions don't settle it, plus the strongest thing the other side can say against me); "Method" (400-500, what I'd actually do week by week, including at least one concrete case I'd work through in detail and the reasoning that would show the flip is a flip in the predicate and not just a difference in facts); "What success looks like" (200, what a result would be, and the specific finding that would make me drop the idea); and "Scope" (100-150, what I'd cut if four months turns into three). Tone of someone writing to a grant committee, not a textbook.
