Last term I sat in on a seminar on philosophy of mind, and afterward a couple of us kept arguing over drinks about whether dreams are experiences. One of my friends works nights in a sleep lab; the other is a philosophy student with a taste for ordinary-language philosophy. I took notes, because the argument kept shifting and I could never decide who was ahead, and I would like you to adjudicate it move by move rather than simply declare a winner.

The lab friend opened by saying the question is settled by data: wake people out of REM and they report vivid dreams; pre-arranged eye-movement signals show that lucid dreamers can carry out instructions while still asleep; external stimuli get woven into the dream; the dream's apparent duration tracks real time. So dreams are experiences occurring during sleep. The philosophy friend answered that every one of these items is, in the end, a report. You never reach the dream except through what the dreamer says or signals, and — this was her main point — the dream report cannot be mistaken the way a report of a waking experience can be. If you sincerely say you dreamed of flying, there is no further fact you could have got wrong, whereas the whole mark of an experience is that you can misremember or misdescribe it. On her view, saying you dreamed something is closer to being disposed to tell a certain story on waking than to recording something that happened to you. The lab friend replied that the incorrigibility point is about grammar, not about what goes on in the head, and that lucid signalling actually supplies the independent check she wanted, since the response is produced during sleep, before any waking reconstruction could begin. She countered that a sleepwalker also does things during sleep without our saying he is experiencing anything, and that leaning on lucid dreams lets the exception carry the whole case.

Then the lab friend changed tack, and this is the part I most want you to sort out. Suppose, he said, we train a decoder on waking perception and it can reconstruct, from a sleeper's brain activity, the faces and scenes that the sleeper later reports, and the reconstruction and the report agree. Either they are both tracking a third thing — the dream experience — or they are two confabulations happening to coincide; the first is far simpler, so dreams are experiences, and a system with no experience would not be readable this way at all. She replied that the decoder might be drawing on the very same data the dreamer's own report-generating machinery uses, so the agreement proves nothing; and that even if it worked, it would show only that there is some contentful neural process during sleep, not that the remembered dream is that process. The dream, as remembered and recounted, might be a shadow constructed at waking, and the sentence "I dreamed X" might be about the shadow rather than about the process. At that point neither would move and we went home.

Please go through the exchange and say who is right about each specific claim — the incorrigibility argument, the lucid-signalling point, the decoder argument, and the shadow/reconstruction reply — rating each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and then say whether the two of them are even disagreeing about the same thing or are talking past each other, and what, if anything, both are missing.
