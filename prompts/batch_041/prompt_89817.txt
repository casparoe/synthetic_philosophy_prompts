So I'm maybe two-thirds of the way through a novel that's set partly in a state prison and partly in a parole hearing room, and the research has left me with a question I can't shake. The thing that keeps bothering me is how much of the system runs on remorse. The board asks a man why he did it, whether he understands what he took from the family, what he'd do differently — and wether he walks out can turn on whether they believe he means it. There's good-time credit for keeping your nose clean, programs you have to finish, a whole apparatus devoted to getting a person to say, in effect, I did this and it was wrong and I am the one who did it. And yet the same system, one room over, talks about addiction as a disease, about lead paint and childhood trauma and the adolescent brain, about the kid who was nineteen and high whose life had been nothing but a bad hand. It can't seem to decide whether he's the author of what he did or a symptom of it. We don't lock people up for their temperaments or thier genes, but we do keep them in when they won't reform, which seems like a strange pair of commitments to hold at once.

What I want to know is what the practice actually commits us to — not the theory people write about on the side, but the presuppositions baked into how a parole board operates and how we let people earn their way out with good behavior. Does requiring remorse, and treating release as something you earn rather than something you're simply cleared for, presuppose that the offender really was the source of the act in some robust sense — the kind of authorship that survives being told his brain and his history did most of the work — or is all that machinery perfectly compatible with a flatly naturalistic picture in which nobody ever had the kind of freedom that would make him the ultimate origin of anything? And here's the part I care about most: is the practice internally consistent, or does the medical, causal talk in one room quietly undercut the desert talk in the other? If you took seriously the view that retributive desert can't survive what we now know about how people come to be who they are, what would actually have to change at the hearing — would there even be a hearing, or just a risk assessment with a release date attached? And if instead you took the compatibilist line, that blame and desert are fine once you stop pining for a freedom outside the causal order, could you keep the remorse test as it stands, or would it have to be rebuilt around something else — capacity, say, or a willingness to become a certain kind of person going forward? Tell me what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely happens to fit, and where it's just underdetermined, so that two people who disagree about free will could both sign off on the same parole board without either of them feeling like a hypocrite.
