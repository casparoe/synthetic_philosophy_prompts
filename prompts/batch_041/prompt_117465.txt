I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether a hypocrite can blame, and we got nowhere, because we kept trading intuitions about cases neither of us had bothered to construct properly. I would like to settle the question with a case built to order, and I would like you to build it.

The disagreement is this. My friend holds what I will call the participant view: what gives a person standing to blame someone for violating a norm is being a party to that norm — someone to whom the norm applies, and who is therefore in a position to address its demand to the violator. On her view, being guilty of the very same violation does not strip a blamer of standing; if anything it presupposes standing, since one can be guilty of a norm only if the norm binds one. I hold the record view: what strips a blamer of standing is precisely that, her own guilt for the same wrong. Clean hands are what entitle a person to complain.

Construct a single scenario that pulls the two views apart in the direction I find hardest to swallow. A person who is herself guilty of the very violation she is protesting must come out as having standing under the participant view and lacking it under the record view, while a person entirely innocent of that violation — because the norm never applied to her — must come out as lacking standing under the participant view and having it under the record view. Both must be blaming the same person for the same act, so that the only thing varying between them is their relation to the norm. Make the norm internal to a local practice — a club, a trade, a game, a family, a workplace — rather than a general moral prohibition, so that membership in the practice is a matter of fact rather than a metaphor.

Constraints: contemporary and realistic; no courtroom; nothing science-fictional, no brains in vats, no omniscient or perfectly informed observers; one paragraph, dense if it has to be. The first blamer's guilt and the second blamer's innocence must fall out of the structure of the situation rather than being stipulated in a way that settles the question; I do not want a case that simply asserts that the hypocrite is sincere and the outsider is smug. Do not name any philosopher, cite any book or article, or hand me a reading list — I want the case and the argument, not the literature.

Then do the check. For each view, state the verdict it returns for each of the two blamers, and the step of reasoning that yields it. If either view, as I have stated it, does not by itself determine one of the verdicts, say so rather than quietly filling the gap. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to the divergence once that feature is removed. Say how a defender of whichever view comes off worse would attack the case — what she would call rigged, artificial, or question-begging about it — and give me your own verdict on whether the scenario is a fair test of the two views or whether I have stacked the deck, because I may well have.

One further thing, subordinate to the rest. My friend also floated the thought that standing might track whether the norm currently applies to the blamer, so that a member who has left the practice would lose her standing to blame those who remain in it; she was unsure, and so was I. If a third blamer who has since left the practice can be added without crowding the paragraph, add her and say what each view does with her. If it cannot, say why not.
