I'm a grad student in a jurisprudence seminar and somehow I've ended up running the review session on the Hart--Devlin debate, and the whole thing is driving me up the wall. Everyone in the class, myself included, keeps reducing it to "Hart says the harm principle, Devlin says society may punish immorality," and then we spend the hour arguing about whether society has a right to enforce its morality -- which is fine as far as it goes, but it means we never get to the joints that actually matter. Like: is the thing doing the real work in Devlin the intrinsic badness of immorality, or its tendency to break up a shared morality? Those look like completely different arguments -- one of them quasi-retributive, one straightforwardly consequentialist -- and I can't tell whether the "society just is its morality" premise is adding anything or whether it's decoration on top of a social-cohesion argument. Same with the offense stuff: is disgust a reason to criminalize, or only a reason to protect people from having the conduct forced on their attention? And can you be a legal moralist at all without believing there's any one determinate shared morality for a legislator to identify? I want a decision tree or flowchart that sorts people by what they actually think, built out of those forks rather than out of a list of names.

So: each branch node should be one real fork a student could answer without already knowing which leaf it leads to -- e.g. whether the criminal law's proper business is limited to conduct harmful to someone other than the agent; whether widespread disgust is itself a criminalization reason or only grounds for protecting people from exposure; whether the badness of immorality is intrinsic or instrumental; whether the state may criminalize conduct that harms no one but makes the agent worse as a person; whether there's a determinate social morality at all and how a legislator could be entitled to read it off. Leaves should carry the position name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Please make sure the tree can at least place Mill's harm principle, Hart's own moderate line, Devlin, Feinberg's offense principle, a Feinberg-style legal moralism, legal paternalism, and some perfectionist position -- and then tell me which combinations come out empty, and whether they're empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unappealing, or because nobody's bothered to defend it. I especially want to know whether your tree collapses Devlin into a plain consequentialist social-cohesion view, because I have a suspicion it does, and if so what the "society is its morality" premise was supposed to be doing that the cohesion argument wasn't already doing on its own. If two of your leaves end up saying the same thing about every case, flag that instead of pretending they're distinct.

Keep it to a page -- 800 words max -- and please don't pad it with the history of the debate or a reading list; I don't need sources, just the tree and your notes on the gaps. I'm definately going to put this on a handout, so terse is better than thorough.
