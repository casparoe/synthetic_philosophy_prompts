I coordinate the listing programme in the biodiversity branch of a national environmental agency. Our statute directs us to protect 'species' and, in certain circumstances, to extend protection to recognised subspecies and other units below the species level, but it defines none of these terms. The petitions that reach us increasingly turn on exactly the cases where the biological community is itself divided. Over the next two years my unit will have to reach a recommendation on roughly one contested petition a month, among them: a geographically isolated population of a widespread songbird that some specialists treat as a subspecies and others as an artefact of sampling; a parthenogenetic lineage of lizards that reproduces without males; a group of fish that interbreeds freely with a neighbouring form along a hybrid zone; and a captive-bred line whose wild ancestor is extinct. Each recommendation is consequential — it can trigger land-use restrictions and unlock or divert funding — and each is produced under time pressure by staff trained as ecologists rather than as philosophers, reviewed by a legal office, and signed off by a director who wants the reasoning to survive a court challenge.

The philosophy of biology has spent decades on what looks like our exact problem, and has if anything sharpened it: it has shown that 'species' is asked to do several incompatible jobs at once, that the leading criteria for drawing species boundaries — interbreeding, shared ancestry, ecological niche, overall similarity — can disagree about the very same organisms, and that some philosophers conclude there is no single correct answer to be found. I want that literature turned into something my staff can actually run.

Please write that procedure. Begin by giving us a compact, usable version of the underlying conceptual apparatus — in particular the difference between the species category and particular species taxa, the idea that species might be individuals rather than kinds, and the pluralist claim that no one species concept is uniquely correct — but translate it rather than lecture; assume the reader has had one undergraduate evolution course and no philosophy. Then set out the procedure itself as an ordered sequence of questions, indicating for each what would count as a sufficient answer and who on the team is competent to supply it.

I would like you to build the procedure around a distinction I have found useful and want you to develop: the difference between (i) delimitation, the question of where to draw boundaries in a continuous genealogy; (ii) ranking, the question of at what level of the hierarchy a boundary so drawn should count as a species rather than a subspecies or a mere local variant; and (iii) evaluation, the question of what legal and moral status the group so identified deserves. The procedure should make clear that evidence bearing on (i) and (ii) does not settle (iii), and it should mark the point at which the decision stops being a scientific one and becomes one of policy, so that my staff can record that in writing rather than smuggling a value judgment into the choice of a species concept.

Then run the procedure on three of the petitions above — the songbird population, the parthenogenetic lizards, and the hybridising fish — taking the facts as I have sketched them and supplying detail only where it does not change the outcome. At least one of the three should come out in a way my staff will not like; do not smooth that over. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: which cases it will misclassify, where it will merely launder a political choice as a taxonomic one, and what it would need from the science that the science may never provide.

I do not need citations or a reading list; I know the literature well enough to fill those in myself, and my staff will never read it. What I need is the procedure, in a form I can hand to them on Monday.
