I'm in a grad seminar on early analytic ethics and we've been working through G.E. Moore's Principia Ethica. There's a passage in the chapter called "The Ideal" where Moore says, "By far the most valuable things, which we know or can imagine, are certain states of consciousness, which may be roughly described as the pleasures of human intercourse and the enjoyment of beautiful objects." I get why he says that, but a friend in the seminar raised an objection that I can't stop thinking about: if the highest goods are states of consciousness, then it looks like what's valuable about my friendship with someone is just my own conscious experience of affection, not the friend themselves. Same with beauty—what's valuable is my enjoyment of the beautiful object, not the object. My friend says this makes Moore's view weirdly egoistic, or at least unable to account for why we value people and beautiful things for their own sake. But another person in the seminar said Moore can escape this by appealing to his idea of "organic unities"—the thought that a whole can have a value greater than the sum of the values of its parts, so the friend's existence and qualities can be part of the valuable whole even if the whole is a state of consciousness. My friend shot back that this just pushes the problem back a step: if the whole is still a state of consciousness, then the friend is still only a part of your experience, not something valued in themselves. I'm definately not sure who's right, and there's alot riding on it for my essay.

- "The Moorean defense" (about 300-400 words): explain the organic unity reply as charitably as you can, and say what it would need to show to succeed.
