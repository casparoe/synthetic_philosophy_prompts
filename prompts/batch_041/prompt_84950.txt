I've been in a reading group on longtermism and we've got ourselves stuck on the hinge of history idea -- the claim that we might be living through the most consequential stretch of human history, such that a few decades of decisions now matter more than whole centuries of ordinary history do. One of us threw out the 1975 Asilomar conference on recombinant DNA as a test case and now we can't agree whether it's a hinge or not. The facts, as far as I've got them: in 1974 a committee of leading biologists got the National Academy of Sciences to call for a voluntary halt on certain recombinant DNA experiments until the risks had been assessed; the conference itself was in February 1975, about 140 people, mostly biologists with a few lawyers and doctors thrown in; it ended with a set of voluntary containment guidelines which were then hardened into national rules, and the research carried on under them and became the basis of the biotech industry. Don't rehash the history at me, I want the analysis.

So analyse that episode as a candidate hinge, and I mean the mapping done properly: what criterion are we even using -- leverage over the long-run trajectory, irreversibility, how narrow the window was, how much the counterfactuals diverge -- and does Asilomar actually satisfy it? Be honest about the awkward bits: whether the guidelines changed the trajectory or just codified where things were already going, whether the moratorium was a real halt or a gesture by people who knew perfectly well the work would resume, and who was in the room versus who had the decision made about them. Then the direction I actually care about: what does the case do to the hypothesis? My own hunch, and I'd rather you attack it than be polite about it, is that Asilomar only counts as a hinge if hinges are typically governance moments -- a small group deciding how some new power will be held -- rather than luck moments, like a missile officer's nerve holding on a bad night, and that if that's right then hinges are neither rare nor especially unpredictable, which sits badly with the picture of one narrow window that we happen to be sitting in. There's also the survivor point I can't get past: if a hinge is the sort of thing that could have gone either way, then us being here to look back on the ones that went well is exactly what we'd expect either way, so what work is the hypothesis doing that a plain story about underlying trends isn't already doing? Say something too about the way the Asilomar name keeps getting reused -- the 2017 AI principles, the recent summitry at Bletchley Park -- because I can't tell whether that's a real inheritance of the 1975 template or a ritual that flatters everyone in the room, and whether that matters for the hypothesis or is just scenery.

Give me a verdict, but tell me where it's weakest: what would have to be true about the counterfactual history for you to decide Asilomar wasn't a hinge after all, and which step in your own reasoning is the one most likely to give way. And if there's a part of this where the hinge framing simply has nothing to say and you'd be forcing it to speak, say so instead of forcing it.
