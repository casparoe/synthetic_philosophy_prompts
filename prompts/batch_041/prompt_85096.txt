My friend's an archaeologist and we've been argueing about this on and off for about a year now. I'm a software engineer, I have zero formal background in any of this, so I'm probably mangling the terminology, but here's the thing: she has very firm verdicts about what counts as a collapse and they don't always land where you'd expect. Roughly, her calls so far:

- Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, around 1200 BC: collapse, no question. Palaces burn down, the Hittite empire is gone, the Mycenaean palace states are gone, the palace writing system drops out of use, the copper and tin trade dries up, populations fall.
- Classic Maya lowland cities, roughly 800 to 1000 AD: collapse. Cities empty out, the divine kingship ends, they stop putting up monuments and stop carving dates, the lowland population drops hard. She's definately not saying the Maya people themselves went anywhere — the farming, the languages, the calendar all continued — but she still says the thing that was there collapsed.
- Rapa Nui / Easter Island: collapse. Her reading of it is that the forest got stripped, statue building stopped, and the population crashed. She waves off the argument that what came after was a continuation of the same society; for her the statue era ending is the collapse.
- Norse Greenland, 15th century: collapse. Settlements abandoned, society gone.
- Western Roman Empire, 5th century: NOT a collapse, and she gets genuinely annoyed if you say it is. Her line is that the people didn't die off or leave, the farms and cities carried on, Latin carried on, the church carried on, and some new rulers turned up. A change of management, not a collapse.
- Fall of the Tokugawa shogunate, 1868: not a collapse. Same country, same people, new government, and it ends up more complex afterwards, not less.

Two new cases I want to spring on her:

(a) The Black Death in Europe, 1347 to 1351, where somewhere between a third and half of everyone died. But the states, the cities, the church, the universities, the writing and the long-distance trade all kept going, and the population and economy came back.
(b) The end of the Soviet Union in 1991. No mass die-off, no mass exodus, people mostly stayed where they were, but the state, the planned economy, the ideology and the whole imperial structure that held together about a sixth of the world's land just stopped existing.

What I actually want isn't the two answers, it's the machine she's running. Give me the rule, or rules, that reproduce every verdict up top, plus at least one rival rule that reproduces them just as well, what each of those predicts for (a) and (b) — I notice they seem to pull in opposite directions — your best guess at which rule she's really on and why, and then one more case, real or invented, that would actually settle it, meaning a case where the two rules disagree and you'd be surprised if she went the other way. And if you think she'd say both (a) and (b) are collapses, or neither one is, then tell me what single rule does that and whether it's really one rule or two rules stapled together.
