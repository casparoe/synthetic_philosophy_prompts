I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and over the past few months I have been reading about causation by omission in my spare time. I keep running into a debate that appears to be stuck, and what I want is a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement it actually is, not an argument for one side.

The dispute, as I understand it, runs like this. In ordinary life and in the law we treat omissions as causes: the gardener who fails to water the plants caused their death, the driver who fails to brake caused the crash, the doctor who withholds a treatment causes the patient's decline. Counterfactual accounts of causation seem to vindicate this, since had the agent acted the outcome would not have occurred, and so the omission stands in the right relation to the outcome. But a large body of metaphysical work resists the move. If causation is production — a real connection between events, a transfer of energy, a continuous process — then an omission is nothing at all, a mere absence, and an absence cannot enter into a relation or produce anything. On that view omissions at most allow outcomes that other events produce. Each side then presses the other: the production theorists observe that if omissions count, then so does everyone else's failure to water the plants, and we get a profligacy of causes that is absurd; the counterfactualists reply that the production theorist simply cannot account for the fact that we correctly hold people causally responsible for what they fail to do.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the real hidden variable is normativity. I suspect there is no such thing as a normatively neutral omission: to single out some inaction as an omission is already to invoke a standard the agent was expected to meet, so the category may be a normative one masquerading as a descriptive one. If that is right, the two sides may be disagreeing less about what happened in the world than about whether the word cause is supposed to track a mind-independent relation or a responsibility-attributing practice.

I would like you to diagnose the impasse. Is it verbal — two senses of cause, production versus difference-making, neither of which is the uniquely correct one? Is it a substantive disagreement about whether absences exist and can stand in causal relations? Are the sides answering different questions under a single name, one about what makes an outcome happen and one about what makes it the case? Or is my normativity hypothesis the right diagnosis, and if so, what does it imply for the debate? Please engage the positions as I have laid them out, and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a decision about absences, a change of question — or explain why none should be expected.
