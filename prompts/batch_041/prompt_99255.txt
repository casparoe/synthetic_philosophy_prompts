I'm putting together the final for a grad seminar I run on formal epistemology and decision theory, and I need one essay question on the common prior assumption and the agreeing-to-disagree result. Trouble is, every student can recite the punchline -- "rational people can't agree to disagree" -- without having any clue what's actually carrying the theorem. I want a question that makes them find the load-bearing assumption and say what the result does and doesnt tell us about real, stubborn disagreement between two people who each think the other is being unreasonable.

A few things I'd like it to pull on, without me just handing them the skeleton: the gap between common knowledge and plain mutual knowledge of the two posteriors; the odd fact that the usual summary credits "rationality" when the proof seems to lean on something else entirely; and the awkward bit that the common prior is itself exactly the kind of thing people fight about, so it's not obvious the theorem ever gets traction in a live dispute. I also want the question to force them through a case I keep throwing at them: two forecasters who've agreed on every question they've ever checked, share a prior over the state space, and still keep betting against each other on some proposition neither has looked at yet -- is that a counterexample to the theorem, a counterexample to the common prior assumption, or just a sign they don't have common knowledge of each other's posteriors?

Please write the question, a model answer of around 600 words, and a marking scheme that seperates an A answer from a B answer -- I want the marks to reward the student who can say which premise is doing the work, not the one who names the theorem and stops. Out of 20 is fine, with a one-line note per point on how people usually get it wrong. No citations or reading lists, please; I don't want them thinking I'm after the bibliography, and the answer should stand on the argument.
