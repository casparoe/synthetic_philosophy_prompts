A friend of mine is a historian of science who's made a career out of the right-but-early question. She referees alot of papers where someone argues that a dead prediction was actually just ahead of its time, and she's pretty ruthless about it. Her calls so far, roughly: Malthus, early, not falsified — the constraint is real, it binds hard in pre-industrial economies, and the escape came from something outside his model. The mass-famine predictions of the population-bomb era, falsified, no excuses — the trend went the other way and stayed there. Wegener and continental drift, early — laughed at for forty years and then vindicated. Phlogiston, falsified, nothing in it survived. Marx on the immiseration of the working class, falsified, and she's openly contemptuous of anyone who wants to call that one early.

Now here's a new one I want you to run past her. In the early seventies a soil scientist argued that the Green Revolution was borrowing against the future: continuous intensive cropping strips micronutrients out of soils, so global cereal yields would flatten out around the turn of the century and then start to fall. That didn't happen, yields kept climbing. But the mechanism — intensive monoculture depletes soil micronutrients and eventually that bites — is now completely standard, it's in every agronomy textbook, there's a whole literature on it. The catch is that every timing claim his followers have produced since has been wrong. They've pushed the date out three times, added carve-outs for irrigated land, then carve-outs for rotated land, and not one of those patches has ever generated a prediction that came true.

Predict her verdict, tell me which rules her track record is actually consistent with (I suspect more than one fits all five of her calls), which one she's probably running, and what further case would seperate them. I'm asking because I'm trying to write something about whether right-but-early is even a coherent verdict or just a polite word for unfalsifiable, so I care more about the structure of her reasoning than about the verdict itself.
