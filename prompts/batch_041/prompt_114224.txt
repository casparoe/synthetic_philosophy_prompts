I run a 90-minute session for a mixed group — about 20 people, mostly grad students from history, econ and STS, a couple of undergrads, no game theorists in the room so you can't assume any formal vocabulary. What I want them to actualy feel is path dependence: the thing where some small early accident gets amplified into a locked-in outcome that's now expensive to reverse. And then, in the debrief, I want them to feel the pull of the opposite suspicion — that the same outcome was going to arrive anyway and the accident was just decoration.

The bit I care most about is that this shouldn't be a lecture with a coin flip taped onto the end. The group should build the history themselves, with real decisions, and only realise afterwards what they built.

Design me the activity: rules, materials, a payoff scheme (no real money, small stakes are fine — snacks, bragging rights, the losing table does the tidying up), the round-by-round structure, and roles if you need them. I'll be running it with whatever's in the room: paper, index cards, a whiteboard, no projector. Don't use the term path dependence anywhere in the setup or rules — they only hear it in the debrief.

Two things I want built into the design specifically:

1. The lock-in has to be felt rather than reported. There should be a stretch where a decent number of people would privately rather be on the other option and can't get there, or won't pay the cost of getting there, and where the sunk investment in what they've already done is doing the work. Tell me where that moment should land in the timeline and what the facilitator must not say while it's happening.

2. It has to be runnable more than once, so the room can generate its own counterfactuals. Give me a second and third run with the same structure but a different arbitrary starting accident, and at least one run with the structure itself tweaked — returns dialled up or down, or the option that lost getting a subsidy. The comparison is the payoff: whether history is locked in depends on the shape of the returns and not just on the accident, and "it would have happened anyway" is a claim about that shape. But I also want the debrief to be honest that rerunning a model is not rerunning history, and that we've just cheated a bit to make the counterfactual visible.

For the debrief, map what actually happened in the room onto the difference between (a) an outcome that was contingent but is now stuck, (b) an outcome any route would have reached, where history only matters for the journey, and (c) an outcome that was reachable only in a window that has since closed. Say something about why asking "would it have happened anyway" with hindsight isn't the same as asking what was probable at the time.

Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what to do about it. And be frank about what the activity can't reproduce — I assume it can't give them the thing that makes real lock-in bite, which is that the roads not taken were never built and nobody will ever find out whether they were better. If there's another feature it can't reproduce, say so.

Start the reply with the activity itself — setup, then round one — no preamble about path dependence or about why games are good for teaching it.
