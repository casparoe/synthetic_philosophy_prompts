I am grading the final question on a take-home examination for an upper-level course in practical reason and the philosophy of action. The examination question and one student's answer appear below. Please grade the answer and give me the comments I should return with it.

The question was worth thirty-five points, distributed roughly evenly across parts (a) through (d). Grade on a standard letter scale with plus and minus, and give a score out of thirty-five.

EXAMINATION QUESTION

Question 4. Kavka's toxin puzzle. (35 points)

A billionaire places before you a vial of toxin that will make you painfully ill for a day if you drink it, but will leave no lasting effects. He will deposit one million dollars into your bank account at midnight tonight if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink the toxin tomorrow afternoon. You need not drink it; the money is yours hours before the appointed time, and you are free to change your mind once it has been paid. Assume also that his detection of your intention is perfectly reliable. For this version of the puzzle, add one further stipulation: you may not obtain the intention by artificial means. No drug, no hypnosis, no hypnotist, no implant, no third party who talks you into it; if the intention is to be had at all, you must arrive at it by your own deliberation.

(a) Explain why there is no reason to drink the toxin, and state what the puzzle is supposed to show about the relation between reasons to intend and reasons to act.

(b) Explain what work the added stipulation does. What becomes of the puzzle if it is dropped?

(c) Distinguish two things one might mean by a reason to intend: a reason to bring it about that one has the intention, and a reason that counts in favour of the intention itself. Is the million dollars a reason of the first kind, of the second kind, or both? Defend your answer.

(d) Consider the following response: intentions are subject to a norm of stability; to intend to do something is to be settled on doing it, and being settled on doing it is incompatible with knowing that one will not do it. Assess this response. Does it show that the intention is impossible, that it is irrational or criticizable, or neither? Does it show that the agent has no reason to intend?

STUDENT'S ANSWER

The offer is unusual because it attaches the money to an attitude rather than to an action. That is where the puzzle lives.

(a) Once midnight has passed, the money is already in my account. Drinking the toxin after that can only make me painfully ill for a day; it cannot bring me anything further, because the payment was never conditional on drinking. So there is no reason to drink — nothing I do at noon can change what I receive at midnight — and since I can see this in advance, I can see in advance that I will not drink. What the puzzle is meant to establish is that reasons to intend and reasons to act can come apart. The million dollars is a reason for me to be in the state of intending to drink tomorrow afternoon, and it is not a reason to drink; the reason for the attitude does not derive from any reason for the action the attitude is an attitude towards. If the puzzle works, practical reason contains reasons for attitudes that are not reasons for the acts those attitudes concern.

(b) The stipulation closes the easy escape. If I could obtain the intention by taking a pill, then the intention would be something I could manufacture at will, and the case would collapse into an ordinary problem about means and ends: take the pill, acquire the intention, collect the money, skip the toxin. Nothing would be left to puzzle over. The stipulation forces the intention to be reached by my own deliberation, which is the only version of the case in which the question whether the intention can be rationally formed has any bite. Drop it, and the puzzle becomes a puzzle about how to get an intention rather than about whether an intention of that kind can be rational.

(c) The money is a reason of both kinds, and the two kinds come to the same thing here, which is why the puzzle is less threatening than it looks. A reason to bring about an intention is a reason to do what produces the intention; a reason that counts in favour of the intention itself is a reason for the state. Since the billionaire pays for the state rather than for the act, the state is the thing of value, and the way to produce the state is to deliberate exactly as I would if I were going to drink. A million dollars is worth far more than one day of illness, so forming the intention is rational. And once the intention is in place, it gives me a reason to drink after all: an intention is the first part of the action it is an intention to perform, and I cannot coherently treat an intention I have formed as irrelevant to what I do.

(d) The stability response holds that an agent who intends to do something is settled on doing it and is not to reopen the question, so that intending is incompatible with knowing that one will not do the thing. If that is right, an agent who intends to drink while knowing he will not drink is criticizable: he has adopted an attitude whose constitutive norms he is violating, and the violation is one of coherence rather than of prudence. But the response proves too much. If stability forbids reconsidering an intention, then nobody could rationally change his mind about anything he had intended, which is plainly false — people rationally abandon intentions whenever circumstances change. At most the response shows that forming the intention would be strange. It does not show that the intention is impossible, and it does not show that I lack a reason to intend, since the million dollars is exactly such a reason. The puzzle therefore survives, and its lesson is that reasons for attitudes are not reducible to reasons for actions.

End of student answer.
