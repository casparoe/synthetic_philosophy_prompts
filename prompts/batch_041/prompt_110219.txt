In controlled donation after circulatory death, the protocol has become familiar: life-sustaining treatment is withdrawn, the heart stops, a stand-off period of a few minutes passes, and death is declared on circulatory grounds. In the protocols that use normothermic regional perfusion, the transplant team then re-establishes circulation in the donor's body, perfusing the abdominal organs and, in the thoracoabdominal variant, the heart itself, so that the heart begins beating again inside the chest. The cerebral circulation is excluded — by clamping the arch vessels or inflating a balloon in the descending aorta — so the brain is not reperfused. The organs are then recovered in far better condition than they would be after prolonged warm ischemia.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose about the nature of death, and about the relation between a living organism and a collection of living organs? Most jurisdictions recognize death either by irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory function or by irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem. Those two criteria are not equivalent, and the protocol seems to straddle them. It waits for the heart to stop, as though circulatory death were the operative criterion. It then restarts the heart, which suggests either that the donor was not dead by that criterion after all, or that death had already occurred by some other criterion and the beating heart is merely an organ being perfused. And it excludes the brain, as though the neurological criterion were the real one — yet it does not wait for the brain to be destroyed, only for circulation to stop and stay stopped for a few minutes.

So which theory of death does the practice implicitly require? That death is the irreversible loss of the organism's integrative unity, with the brain as the integrator? That death is the irreversible loss of consciousness or of the capacity for it? That death is simply the irreversible cessation of circulation, where irreversible means we have decided not to reverse it rather than that it cannot be reversed? Or does the practice betray the view that death is not a single biological fact but a status conferred by criteria that answer to our purposes — and if so, what constrains the conferral?

I am also puzzled by the asymmetry in how the two criteria are handled. In brain-death cases we remove organs from a body whose heart is still beating, and we call that body dead. In circulatory-death cases with regional perfusion we restart a heart in a body we have already called dead, and we call the result a corpse being perfused. What view of the body, or of the organism, makes both descriptions true at once? Does the practice presuppose that the brain's function is what unifies the body into a single living thing, so that a body without it is an aggregate of organs rather than an organism? If so, does that commit the practice to a particular view about which parts are essential — and would it license perfusing a body whose brain stem was still partly functioning but whose cortex was gone, or one whose brain function was merely suspended and could in principle return?

Finally, separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely accommodates. Could a straightforwardly dual-criterion legal regime — death is either circulatory or neurological, whichever comes first, and irreversible means we will not reverse it — license regional perfusion without any commitment about the organism as a whole? Or does the practice need the stronger claim that a brain-excluded body is not an organism at all? And if the practice is compatible with several theories of death, which borderline case would best tell them apart: an anencephalic infant, a patient in a persistent vegetative state, a body maintained on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation with no brain function, or a donor whose heart restarts spontaneously during the stand-off period?
