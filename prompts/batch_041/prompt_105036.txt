Since retiring from a career in the earth sciences I have been reading my way into the philosophy of history, and I find myself stuck in a dispute whose participants seem to conduct it at cross purposes. One camp holds that to explain a historical event is to show that it was to be expected, given antecedent conditions and general laws, and that without a law of some sort one has at best a redescription or a persuasive story. The other camp holds that historical explanation is narrative through and through: that historians explain by placing an event within a sequence of happenings, that the intelligibility comes from the followability of the story, and that the demand for laws is a philosopher's imposition on a practice that neither needs nor uses them. I would like to know whether these two can be brought into a genuine adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the joint statement such a collaboration would produce.

Structure the document as follows. First, a numbered section of agreed claims — claims about the logical form of an explanation, about what prediction and retrodiction do and do not establish, about the place of contingency, and about the minimum a historical explanation must accomplish — phrased so that neither signatory would feel the wording concedes anything of substance. Second, a table of the residual disagreements, each stated in language that the side it attributes to would accept as its own. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, with a note on which of the disagreements you judge to be merely verbal and why.

Two matters I want the document to engage directly, because I suspect they are where the real work lies. (a) Many historical explanations appear to be sentences whose content cannot even be stated without reference to events later than the one being explained — the significance of an action being fixed by what it turned out to lead to. Can the covering-law account absorb such retrospective descriptions without trivializing itself, and can the narrative account say why they explain rather than merely dramatize? (b) Whether a law that is only a ceteris paribus regularity, admitting of unstated exceptions, does any explanatory work, or whether the covering-law model survives every counterexample by retreating into such hedged laws — and whether that is a defect or simply what explanation in any historical science looks like.

For each crux, supply a case, argument, or comparison that both signatories would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about how it comes out before either is permitted to explain it away. I want to be able to hand the finished document to my former colleagues in the earth sciences, who will be impatient with anything that reads as a philosopher's score-settling.
