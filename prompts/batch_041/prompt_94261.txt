I am writing a novel whose central character builds decision systems, and I need a defensible line between an instrument and an agent — not for the plot's sake, but because the plot will cheat if I don't have one. Give me the desiderata that any adequate account of that distinction must satisfy: the cases it has to classify correctly (the thermostat, the chess engine, the assistant that books its own travel and then, unprompted, rearranges the itinerary, the recommender that quietly reshapes what its users want, the trading system whose behaviour no one can fully reconstruct); the distinctions it must not collapse — between acting and merely producing effects, between being used and being delegated to, between a system's complexity and its standing as a party to what happens; and the facts about how such systems are built and deployed that it cannot contradict, chief among them that the same artifact can be a tool in one deployment and an agent in another, and that the vocabulary of intention sits awkwardly on anything made of weights and gradients. For each requirement, state the consideration that motivates it. Then tell me which of these are genuine and which are merely the commitments of a camp — the control-minded, the moral-status-minded, the lawyers — whether any subset of them can be satisfied together, since I suspect that a criterion which is intrinsic to the system, which tracks our practices of blame, and which can be read off behaviour in practice cannot be had all at once, and which single requirement, if I hold to it, excludes the most otherwise attractive accounts. I would rather carry a short list I can defend in dialogue than a long one I cannot.
