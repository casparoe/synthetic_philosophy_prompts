I write about philosophy of religion for a general-interest ideas magazine, and for the past few weeks I have been trying to reconstruct, for a lay readership, the modal version of the ontological argument that Kurt Gödel kept in his notebooks — the one that circulated only as a privately copied typescript before it finally saw print in the 1980s — so that I can explain both why serious logicians take it seriously and why so few of them treat it as decisive. A philosopher I interviewed for the piece says flatly that the argument proves far too much to be trusted, and I want to see whether the charge can be made to stick, because the version she walked me through looks, premise by premise, uncomfortably plausible. Here is the argument as I have written it out, in plain English, for readers who will not tolerate the symbolic notation.

(1) If a property is positive, and it is necessarily true that everything which has that property also has some second property, then the second property is positive too. (2) For any property, exactly one of it and its negation is positive. (3) It follows that every positive property is possibly exemplified. (4) An object is God-like if and only if it has all the positive properties; and being God-like is itself a positive property. (5) So it is possible that something God-like exists. (6) A property is the essence of an object if the object has it and it necessarily entails every other property the object has; if a property is positive, it is positive in every possible world; and anything God-like has God-likeness as its essence. (7) An object exists necessarily if, for each of its essences, something has that essence in every possible world; and necessary existence is a positive property. (8) Therefore a God-like object exists in every possible world. The modal logic throughout is S5 — if it is possible that something is necessary, then it is necessary — and the whole thing turns on the notion of a property being positive, which is never defined but is meant to behave as (1) through (3) require.

What I cannot get past is a second complaint. Several people who have worked through the formal version — including a group that ran it through an automated proof assistant a few years ago and reported the axioms internally consistent — say that these same axioms also generate what has come to be called a modal collapse: every proposition that is true at all turns out to be true necessarily, so that the possible, the actual and the necessary all coincide. If that is right, the argument does not merely deliver a necessary being; it abolishes contingency altogether, which I take to be absurd and which I suspect even its author would not have welcomed. So here is what I need. Is the collapse a genuine consequence of the axioms as I have stated them, or does the derivation lean on some further assumption — one person I spoke to insists it quietly uses a way of constructing properties that Gödel's own argument never employs, though I can't evaluate that claim. If the collapse is genuine, which single premise is the cheapest to surrender, what exactly does each candidate cost me, and does anything of the argument survive the surgery? I am not much invested in the conclusion, but I would like, if I can, to keep classical modal logic and the idea that some properties really are positive in the intended sense; tell me which of those I can hold onto and which I have to abandon, and be specific about which premise has to go first. A separate worry I have seen floated — that the same machinery would prove the existence not of one necessary being but of a whole crowd of them — I would be glad to have addressed as well, but the collapse is what I care about most.
