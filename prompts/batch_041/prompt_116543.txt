ok so this is partly for a seminar paper and partly to settle a fight, so bare with me.

I'm a PhD student in (broadly) political economy and I have a running argument with a friend in the department about whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes, and we've gotten to the point where we just talk past each other, so I want to force us to write up the thing we could actually both sign.

Here's the split. I'm on the side that says a famine is basically a political event: what kills people is not the weather or the blight by itself but the way food is distributed and the way a government chooses to respond — who gets rationed, who gets requisitioned, who gets censored, who gets blamed. On my view "natural disaster" is usually a story told afterward to let someone off the hook. My friend thinks that's romantic posturing. He says that when the rains fail or the potatoes rot there simply isn't food, and no amount of political cleverness manufactures calories out of nothing; politics turns the dial on severity but the trigger is exogenous and often the binding constraint.

We keep citing the same three cases at each other — the Bengal famine of 1943, the Irish famine of the 1840s, and the Great Leap Forward famine — and we each think the cases prove our side. (We also both gesture at the old claim that famines basically don't happen in democracies with a free press, and we disagree about why that correlation holds.) So please write the joint statement we'd produce after a real adversarial collaboration on this.

What I want in it:

1. An agreed-claims section (numbered) — starting with what we can both accept about what a famine even is (is it excess mortality? a food crisis? a social breakdown? does it have a threshold?), and then the claims we can both sign about how environmental shocks and political arrangements each contribute, phrased so neither of us feels it's been smuggled in.
2. A disagreements table — the real residual disagreements, each one stated in wording that the person who holds it would accept as fair, plus the crux underneath each.
3. For each crux, a concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence that both sides agree would actually bear on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Don't do this generically; use the specific cases, and add any other case you think cuts against one of us.

Some things I specifically want you to wrestle with, because I think they're where the meat is:

- Don't just rehash the tired "food availability vs. entitlement" framing. I want you to decompose each of the three famines into a trigger term, a pre-existing vulnerability term, and a response term, and say how much weight each camp wants to put on each term and why. Where you can, say what each camp predicts about the counterfactual: same harvest but different politics, and same politics but different harvest.
- I keep telling him famines are like earthquakes — the political structure is a fault line that's been loaded for years, and the drought is just the slip that releases it. He says that's a metaphor doing the work of an argument. Take that seriously: either turn it into a claim that could actually be tested, or say why it can't be.
- The free-press/democracy correlation: get us to agree on what the correlation even is before we fight about the mechanism (accountability and information vs. it just being a proxy for state capacity, foreign attention, or wealth).
- If part of our fight turns out to be verbal — about how we're each using "natural," "political," "cause," or "responsibility" — say which part and why, rather than pretending we still disagree substantively.

Don't give me a reading list or citations, I don't care about that, I want the actual content. Casual is fine, but it needs to be exact enough that we'd both put our names on it.
