I'm a retired physicist — forty years of condensed matter, never touched logic — and I've been reading philosophy of maths since I stopped working. One thing I can't get past, and I want to use it on a historical fight. Let me put down how I've got it, and you tell me where I'm wrong.

Löwenheim–Skolem: any first-order theory with an infinite model has a countable one. So if ZFC has a model it has a countable one, call it M. But ZFC proves Cantor's theorem, so inside M there's a set that M says is uncountable. Both things are true: there's no bijection from that set onto M's naturals living inside M, and M is countable, so there is one outside it. The countability is missing, not the uncountability. I know that's the standard resolution.

Skolem's own conclusion was stronger. He read the result as showing that 'uncountable' and the rest have no absolute meaning, that set-theoretic notions are relative to an axiomatization, and that no first-order axiomatization can capture what mathematicians mean by 'set'. Zermelo wouldn't have it. He thought the trouble was the logic not the sets — that 'definite property' isn't a first-order notion, that his axioms were meant to be read against a stronger logic, and that once you do that the models are pinned down, the countable M being a mere fragment rather than the universe of sets.

Here's the ask. Take that episode — the two of them talking past each other in the 20's, and then the later revival of the paradox by people who wanted to turn it against realism about mathematics — and run it through the internal/external distinction: the idea that the view from inside a model and the view from outside it have to be kept apart. Is Skolem's step from 'countable model' to 'no absolute uncountability' just a failure to keep them apart, or something the distinction doesn't cover? Is Zermelo's move — strengthen the logic until the intended interpretation is the only one — a solution, a change of subject, or assuming what was to be proved? And do the later anti-realist uses of the paradox inherit Skolem's step or take a different one?

Then turn it around. What does the episode show about the internal/external distinction itself — does it earn it's keep, or does it just relabel the disagreement? And tell me whether this objection is real or cheap: I keep reading that the fix is to go second-order, where the only models are ranks of the one hierarchy, so the intended interpretation is secured after all. But if the second-order quantifiers are themselves understood set-theoretically, hasn't the fix just assumed the universe it was meant to secure?
