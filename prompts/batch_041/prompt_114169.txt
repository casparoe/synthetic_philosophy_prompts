I have no academic training in philosophy. I have been reading about illusionism about phenomenal consciousness on my own for about a year, and I have constructed an argument against it that I cannot find my way out of. I would like you to tell me which step to give up, why it looked undeniable to me, and what giving it up costs. Please answer in your own words; I have read some of the literature but could not tell you who says what, so pointing me at things to read will not help me.

The argument, as I have written it out:

(1) An illusion is a case in which something seems to a subject to be a certain way when it is not that way.

(2) Therefore every illusion requires a subject to whom things seem some way.

(3) Illusionism holds that phenomenal consciousness — that there is something it is like to undergo an experience — does not exist, and that our confidence that it does is itself an illusion, produced by our inner states seeming to us to have a felt character.

(4) That illusion is an illusion, so by (2) there is a subject to whom things seem some way.

(5) But for things to seem some way to a subject is just for there to be something it is like for that subject.

(6) Therefore illusionism presupposes exactly what it denies, and refutes itself.

Two things make me suspicious of my own argument rather than of the position it attacks. The first is that it seems to prove too much. If it is sound, then the fact that something seems a certain way to me settles that it is that way in respect of its felt character, so there could never be a discovery that a feature of my experience which seems plainly present is not really there at all — yet we do make such discoveries, or so it appears: the blind spot in each eye leaves no gap in what we see, and people are regularly shown to have missed things they were certain they had seen. An argument that rules out there being anything to find out about experience seems to have overshot. The second is that I cannot tell whether I have a genuine objection or only a quarrel about a word, because everything turns on seem and its cousins.

I would rather not give up (1) or (2); they look like nothing more than the definition of the word. I am also unsure what an illusionist would say about the force of the conviction itself — whether it can be explained without helping himself to the very thing he denies — and I suspect that the answer to that question is what decides whether the position is worth taking seriously.
