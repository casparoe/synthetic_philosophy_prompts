I'm a fifth-year PhD student in phil (working on the complaint model, so this is unfortunatly on brand) and I've somehow ended up convening a small two-day workshop on contractualism and the aggregation problem. Six speakers, maybe 25 people in the room, and I have to give the opening remarks — five minutes, after coffee, before the first paper.

The room splits about half and half. Half are econ-adjacent or consequentialist-leaning and think the aggregation problem is a non-problem that contractualists keep re-discovering; the other half will notice within ten seconds if I so much as imply that numbers count. There's also one senior person who thinks the whole debate is a confusion and is listening for signs that I've already picked a side. I'd like the remarks to be genuinely warm without being mush, and I don't want the econ people to feel lectured at.

Here's what I'm stuck on. Normal opening remarks aggregate. 'Such a strong group' — aggregate. 'The most exciting area in ethics right now' — aggregate. 'We had to cut an abstract for time and Priya's didn't make it' — that one's the worst, because it's a real complaint held by a real person in the second row, and if I say it to the room I'm justifying it to the room instead of to her.

So I want the remarks to do the thing rather than announce it: every line justifable to each person in the room one at a time, nothing true only because of how things net out across the group. Two rules I want to hold myself to, and you should hold me to them in the commentary: (a) one clause per paper, and no comparatives or superlatives anywhere — a ranking is an aggregation; (b) nothing that only makes sense if the room were a single unit with a single interest. And please don't let me get cute about it — if I stand up and say 'these remarks will not aggregate', everyone will spend five minutes watching me fail.

One thing I've been chewing on that might do some work here: I think the interesting version of the problem isn't trolley-shaped, it's the small stuff — whose question gets taken first, who gets the long comment, who lost a slot — where contractualism can't just say 'numbers don't count' and walk away, but also can't obviously say anything else. My half-baked move is to seperate a complaint from a disappointment: someone has a complaint only if justifying the outcome to them would require appealing to facts about them (that they're one of many, that they're low maintenance, that they're junior); they have a disappointment if the justification can be given entirely in terms of their own priorities, which they'd endorse on reflection. If that holds up, everyone who loses something over the next two days should lose in the disappointment register. Tell me in the notes if it's doing real work or if it's just me dressing up 'suck it up'. And if it breaks, tell me what to actually do about Priya — she's a third-year in another department, she's genuinely good, and I don't want to pretend the cut didn't happen or bury her in consolation.

Format I want back:

1. 'Remarks as delivered' — the actual thing, ~450 words, written to be said out loud, no stage directions, no meta-commentary.
2. 'Line by line' — ~200 words: the three or four places I came closest to aggregating, which person each line was aimed at, and why they couldn't reasonably reject it.
3. 'The line I couldn't use' — ~100 words: the thing I most wanted to say and the specific person it would have failed.
4. 'Where the frame strained' — ~150 words: where the complaint/disappointment split did work, where it broke, and what I should do about Priya if it broke.
