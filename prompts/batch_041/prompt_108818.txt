I'm a novelist, not a philosoper, and next month I'm giving a talk to a philosophy of mind reading group — mostly physicalists and cognitive scientists, plus two historians of Islamic philosophy who actually know Avicenna and disagree with each other about what the flying man is for. I'm defending what I've been calling the narrator-without-a-scene reading of Avicenna's flying man: the thought experiment isn't really an argument for the soul's immateriality, it's a limit case of what novelists do when they write a first-person voice with no body and no world. Strip away the senses and the body, and what's left isn't a bare metaphysical ego; it's the capacity to say I am, which is a narrative achievement, not a substance. My sketch: (1) the setup asks us to imagine a self with no sensory input and no bodily awareness; (2) what survives is self-awareness; (3) self-awareness of that kind is the construction of a narrator, not the perception of a simple soul; (4) so the flying man supports a narrative account of self, not dualism. The physicalists will hear me smuggling in dualism; the historians will hear me psychologizing a metaphysical proof.
