I've been down a rabbit hole in Hofstadter and McGraw's Letter Spirit work — the project where a program has to invent a whole alphabet that hangs together in one style, and the hard part is that every letter it draws still has to be the letter it's supposed to be even when the style is pushing the shape somewhere strange. It's made me realise I dont have any clear idea what an account of letter-identity is even meant to deliver. Before I read one more type-design essay or perception paper, write me the desiderata: what does any adequate account of what makes a shape an 'a' have to be able to do?

Pin the standard to this purpose, since I know adequacy is relative: good enough to (a) decide, for any shape you put in front of it, whether it's an 'a', and (b) underwrite a program that designs new alphabets in a consistent style and never emits something that isn't a letter of the alphabet it's working in. That's a working spec, not a philosophical nicety, so the criteria should be ones I could actually test against cases.

Give me the list — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts about readers and type it can't contradict — with the case behind each one, and mark each as non-negotiable or as a preference of one camp (type designers, reading researchers, semioticians, whoever). Things I know I want in it:

- allographs: the double-storey 'a' of a serif text face and the single-storey 'a' of a geometric sans share almost no structure, and blackletter 'a' is a different beast again. Whatever letterhood is, it survives enormous structural variation.
- orientation: rotate a cup and it's still a cup; rotate 'b' and you've got 'q', rotate 'd' and you've got 'p'. Children learning to read go through a stage of writing them the wrong way round. So the property is robust to distortion but not to reflection — say why that should be so for letters when it isn't for most things we recognise.
- cross-alphabet homoglyphs: Cyrillic 'а' and Latin 'a' are the same shape, and they're different letters. Shape alone can't be identity. But then how do I read an 'a' in a typeface for an alphabet I've never seen? I suspect this pair is what kills the most candidates, but tell me if I'm wrong.
- novel typefaces: I can read a font I've never met before, so 'the set of a's I've seen' is out.
- gradedness: a smudged, badly proportioned 'a' is still an 'a' and a bad one; a shape 51% of the way to 'd' — is there a fact of the matter? Does the account need a sharp line, or is it allowed to be vague, and vague in what dimension?
- category vs style: 'this is an a' and 'this is an a in the style of the rest of this alphabet' are different judgements and the account shouldn't collapse them — though Letter Spirit suggests style pressure can push an 'a' to the edge of being an 'a' at all.
- context: 'l', 'I' and '1' are largely told apart by their surroundings, and a letterform mid-word is read differently from the same shape standing alone. How much of letter identity is positional?
- boundary cases: the ampersand, ligatures like 'æ', the long s, thorn. Are these letters? Does the answer fall out of the theory or get bolted on?

Then: which single requirement does the most excluding work against the obvious candidates (pure shape similarity, descent from the Phoenician alphabet, designer intention, reader convention)? And flag any two requirements you think can't be satisfied at once — I'd rather have a list with a real impossibility in it than a tidy one that's secretly inconsistent. Skip the scorecard of named theories, I'll do that myself.
