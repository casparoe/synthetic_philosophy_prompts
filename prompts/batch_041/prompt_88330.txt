I am preparing a long-form interview for a public humanities podcast with a scholar who defends a deliberately unorthodox thesis about the agricultural revolution. The thesis is not the familiar claim that early farmers were worse off in health or leisure than foragers. It is this: the agricultural revolution was a mistake because it initiated an intergenerational exit-cost ratchet. On this view, the relevant harm is not a drop in welfare but the progressive foreclosure of subsistence alternatives. Each generation that invested in land, storage, domesticated animals, and inherited infrastructure faced higher costs of returning to a foraging or mixed economy, and the cumulative effect was to make a once-reversible way of life effectively unavailable. The scholar also argues that this ratchet, rather than agriculture as such, is the deep source of durable inequality, because it converted temporary asymmetries in luck or skill into inheritable positions. She is careful to say that no single person or group chose the transition as a whole, and that the thesis is about the structure of the transition, not about the wisdom of any particular Neolithic community. She also distinguishes option-foreclosure from welfare-loss: on her view, the value of an option is not reducible to the expected utility of taking it, so a society can be worse off in the relevant sense even if its members would not choose to leave. She is a philosopher by training who works on collective agency and historical wrongs.

Draft ten to twelve interview questions for a 45-minute conversation that go beyond having her restate the thesis. I want at least one question each on: (i) the unit of harm and the counterfactual—who exactly is supposed to have been wronged, and compared to what; (ii) the difference between a mistake, a tragedy, and a trap, and whether the thesis needs an agent who could have done otherwise; (iii) the empirical basis for the exit-cost ratchet and how it could be distinguished from ordinary path dependence; (iv) the relationship between the ratchet and the origins of inequality, including whether agriculture is being blamed for something better explained by sedentism, storage, or inheritance; (v) the charge that the thesis romanticizes hunter-gatherers or treats them as a baseline of freedom; (vi) what would count as evidence against the thesis, and whether any counterfactual historical case could do so; and (vii) whether the thesis is a form of nostalgia or a secular theodicy of progress, and what distinguishes a structural critique from a romantic one. For each question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Press on the conceptual joints of the thesis, not on her bibliography. Avoid questions that ask for reading lists, citations, or surveys of the literature, and avoid personal or career questions. The questions should be answerable from the thesis as stated plus general reasoning about values, causation, and historical explanation.
