I run a small workshop series for graduate students in political theory and public policy, typically twelve to sixteen people with mixed backgrounds, and I have a single ninety-minute slot to fill. What I want out of it is an activity that makes participants feel, rather than hear, the claim that usually gets compressed into the phrase "the Nordic model is a package deal": that a high-tax, high-transfer system is not a set of policies that can be lifted out and installed elsewhere, but an equilibrium sustained by what people expect of one another, where the expectations and the institutions may stand to each other as cause, as effect, or as neither cleanly. The standard public goods game delivers half of this on its own — free riding, conditional cooperation, the decay of voluntary contributions — and I do not need another version of that. What I want is a structure that gets at the second half: the difference between an institution that works because people already trust one another and one that works because it makes trust either unnecessary or self-reinforcing. Please build that hinge into the design, and build it so that the participants' own choices generate the evidence they will then argue about in the debrief.

Concretely, I would like a payoff scheme that requires no money; roles or positions with genuinely divergent interests, which I imagine as participants drawing private cost-of-contribution cards from a distribution none of them knows, so that nobody can tell in advance whether they will turn out to be net contributors or net beneficiaries; and, the part I care most about, a rule-choosing phase in which the group fixes the tax rate and the enforcement regime before anyone learns their own type. Then, in a later round, have a rate imposed on them from outside at roughly the level the group chose for itself, so that they can feel in their own payoffs what it is to receive an institution they did not author. I want the thing to run in about fifty minutes with a twenty-minute debrief, and I would rather the group not be told at the outset that the game is about trust — the name for what happened should arrive late, in the debrief. The debrief itself should map the moments of the game onto the parts of the exportability debate: the argument that the Nordic outcomes are explained by prior homogeneity and small scale, the argument that they are explained by institutions which generate their own legitimacy, and the argument that the causality runs both ways and cannot be separated. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect at least one round to produce either near-universal cooperation, which dissolves the tension, or near-total defection, which makes the remaining rounds pointless — and how to handle that without rigging the payoffs. Also tell me frankly which feature of the real thing the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply in the debrief instead.

One note on how I will use this. I am presenting it to colleagues who work on comparative political economy, and several of them will be irritated if a classroom game is treated as evidence about anything, so keep the design and the diagnosis separate. In the parts where you are drawing on the empirical literature on trust, tax compliance, and the Nordic cases, mark clearly what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who know the material, rather than presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
