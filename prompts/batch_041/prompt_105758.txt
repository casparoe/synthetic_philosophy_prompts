I'm a novelist — currently deep in a historical novel about the slow collapse of a (fictional) empire — and the thing that keeps tripping me up isn't the history, it's the metaphysics of cause and blame that the plot keeps forcing on me. So I've been collecting my gut verdicts about which things "caused" an outcome, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I don't want a reading list or citations (can't look anything up right now anyway) — just the principle, stated as sharply as you can manage, plus what it says about the cases below and a case or two I haven't thought of.

Here are the verdicts. (1) Two conspirators each independently wire a bomb to a ship's hull; each bomb alone is enough to sink her; both go off; she sinks. I want to say both men caused the sinking. (2) Same setup, except the first bomb sinks her before the second one can do anything — now I want to say the second man didn't cause the sinking at all, even though his bomb was exactly as sufficient, and even though if the first had failed his would have done the job. (3) A house burns down: the dropped match caused it, the presence of oxygen didn't, even though no oxygen, no fire. (4) In my empire, the collapse happens the year the harvest fails, and I keep writing that the harvest "caused" the collapse — but everyone in the story knows the whole thing was already hollow, that some shock was coming. I still want to say the harvest caused it. (5) A general's defection hands the rebels the capital; I also want to say the regime's decades of rot caused it, and somehow I don't feel those two compete. (6) And the one I can't settle: a character takes her own life the week her brother's letter arrives. I want to say the letter caused it and the long depression only made it likely — but I'd also happily say the depression caused it. Is (6) just (1) over again, or is it (5)?

Notice (1) and (2) are the pair that bothers me most: both men were sufficient, both intended it, only the timing differs, yet my verdict flips. And look at (3) against (4) and (5) — sometimes I count background conditions as causes and sometimes I flatly refuse to. So: is there one account that gets all six, or do I have to give one or two up? If the honest answer is that I'm quietly doing two different things — one when I ask "did X make a difference," another when I ask "what explains this / who's to blame" — then say so, and tell me which of my verdicts each of those handles and which it can't. I'd also like to know what the surviving principle predicts for something I haven't listed, like two fires converging on one house, or a war caused by an assassin's bullet and by a century of tariff policy. Plain language please, I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I'm definately going to argue with you about whichever verdict you tell me to drop.
