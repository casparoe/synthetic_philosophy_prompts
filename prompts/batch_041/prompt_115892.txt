Two colleagues of mine — an economic historian and a development economist — spent most of a long drive arguing about whether we can ever know why some countries are rich, and I ended up in the back seat refereeing without ever getting a word in. I want to lay out their exchange as faithfully as I can, with the moves numbered, and I'd like you to adjudicate each one rather than just pick a winner.

Both of them accepted the same background. Some countries are vastly richer than others; the leading explanations appeal to institutions (property rights, constraints on elites, the rule of law), to geography (climate, disease burden, coastlines, agricultural suitability), and to culture (trust, attitudes toward work and saving, orientations toward the future).

(1) The historian opened by saying the institutions literature is circular. You measure institutional quality by things like how safe investors feel from expropriation, and then you use it to explain prosperity. But investor confidence is essentially a forecast of future prosperity, so you have explained wealth with a guess about wealth. He added that the Korean peninsula settles the matter anyway: one people, one language, one landscape, split in two, with the southern half rich and the northern half poor. Whatever you want to call the cause, it is not geography or culture; it is the system.

(2) The economist said the Korean case is not the controlled experiment it is advertised to be. The line was drawn by outside officers on short notice for military reasons, not by a randomizer; the two states then accumulated entirely different bundles of treatment — alliances, aid, trade access, military posture, industrial policy, leadership — so institutions cannot be isolated from everything traveling with them. He added that the two halves were not identical at the start: roughly two-thirds of the peninsula's heavy industry, along with the mines and the hydroelectric dams, sat in the north, while the south was the farm belt, and by many estimates the two economies were comparable in per capita income into the 1970s. So the slogan that they began from the same line and diverged immediately is, in the details, false. And a single case, however striking, cannot carry a general causal claim; with one observation you can always find a story.

(3) The historian said that standard is impossible to meet. You cannot randomize institutions across countries. If only a randomized trial counts, then no historical evidence can ever answer the question, and that cannot be right, since we accept non-experimental evidence in medicine all the time when the intervention is large and the rival explanations are implausible.

(4) The economist said there is a middle path, the one the colonial-origins literature actually takes: use a historical variable — say, the mortality rate European settlers faced in different colonies — as an instrument for the institutions those colonies ended up with, on the theory that where settlers died they built extractive arrangements and where they survived they built settler institutions. Then he turned on his own example. The credibility of the whole thing rests on the exclusion restriction, the assumption that settler mortality affects today's income only through institutions. But mortality is also a marker of the disease environment, which bears on health, agricultural productivity, and human capital today, so the instrument can be correlated with the outcome for reasons having nothing to do with institutions. He also said the historical mortality figures are patchy, that critics have gone back to the colonial records and recoded them, and that on the recoding the first stage weakens or disappears.

(5) The historian conceded the measurement worries but said the alternative is worse. If you throw out the instruments, you are left with correlations and anecdotes, and then the field degenerates into whatever story sounds best. He would rather have a flawed instrument that forces everyone to state their assumptions out loud than a debate with no identification strategy at all.

(6) The economist said he would accept that, but only if people stop talking as though institutions, geography, and culture were three horses in a race. He said the causal structure is nested: geography shapes where settlers went and what they could grow, which shapes institutions, which shapes investment, which shapes income; and culture is partly downstream of institutions and partly upstream of them. So asking which one wins is a category mistake. The real question is the shape of the causal graph and the size of the arrows.

(7) The historian then made the move that stuck with me. He said: fine, but notice that we can never observe the counterfactual, because calling an arrangement extractive or inclusive is defined by comparison with what would have happened under a different arrangement, and there is no such observation. And we only ever study the countries that survived to be studied, so the sample is selected on survival. He said the honest answer is that we know some things — that institutions matter at the margin, that geography matters at the margin — and that we do not know, and cannot know, the relative weights. Anyone who tells you otherwise is smuggling in a counterfactual.

(8) The economist said that if that is the conclusion, the question was ill-posed from the start. He said why some nations are rich is really at least two questions — why the level differences exist today, and why the growth takeoff happened where and when it did — and these have different answers and different evidence. He also said the nation is the wrong unit: prosperity is a property of cities, regions, and networks, and the nation-state is a recent container we have mistaken for the object.

They went back and forth a few more times and both ended up annoyed at me for refusing to take a side. Please do what I could not. For each of the eight moves, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then tell me what each of them is missing, and say who, if either, is more right overall. Please write in American English, and please do not hand me a reading list — I want your reasoning about the arguments as they were actually made.
