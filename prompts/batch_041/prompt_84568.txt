Ok so I've been reading about constitutional monarchies for fun, which is a wierd hobby, and I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a name for.

Take the monarch's power to refuse to sign a bill. Everyone agrees it's still there — nobody repealed it, it's on the books — and as far as I can tell it hasn't been used since the early 1700s. But the reason it hasn't been used isn't that anyone would get arrested. It's that using it would be the last thing the monarchy ever did. So it's a power that only works while it sits there unused, and it stops being a power the second it's used. That's backwards from every other kind of power I know of, where using it is how you find out if it's real.

Same shape with picking a prime minister when the election doesn't decide it. Everyone says the monarch chooses someone, and everyone also says the monarch has no choice. Both of those are supposed to be true at once. And it holds together because nobody can pin it down: if you actually wrote a rule — the monarch shall ask the leader of the largest party, say — you'd either be handing the monarch real discretion or taking it away, and either way the thing you were trying to preserve is dead.

Then there's the private meeting. Some Victorian or other said the monarch's whole job is three things: being consulted, encouraging, warning. And the reason that's the entire job is that advice given in private can't be quoted back at the government, and the day it can, it's over. Influence that has to stay invisible in order to exist.

I don't think it's only monarchies. My old boss could overrule any decision in our department and never once did, and we all knew it, and it changed what we bothered to propose. My mother-in-law gets consulted about everything and decides nothing, and yet nothing happens without her. A union guy I know can call a strike and hasn't in fifteen years, and the company quietly gives him things so he won't.

So is there a name for this? Unwritten rule and convention aren't it — those just tell me it isn't in a statute. Strategic ambiguity is about what you tell an adversary, not about the authority you're holding. Deterrence covers the unused-power half until you notice that deterrence gets stronger once it's been tested, and this thing dies. A taboo is enforced by disgust, not by the fear that using it would dissolve it. What I'm after is narrower: rules that only work while they stay vague, powers that only work while unused — and I'm honestly not sure those are one phenomenon or two, so if it's two, tell me what seperates them. I don't need a label so much as the carving: what exactly the thing is, what it isn't, where its edges are. And which of my examples isn't actually an example, because I suspect the mother-in-law is doing something different from the king.
