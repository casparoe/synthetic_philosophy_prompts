I am commissioning a piece for a small imprint that publishes satirical institutional documents, and I want this one to be a mock regulatory bulletin: deadpan throughout, no narrator, no winking.

Setting and premise. The piece is issued by a national Bureau of Colour Standards in a country whose legally recognised colour predicate for gemstones is grue, defined as follows: a thing is grue if it is examined before the certification date and is green, or if it is not examined before that date and is blue; its companion predicate bleen is defined by swapping the two colours. The Bureau has just concluded a survey and is issuing a notice.

The notice must do the following:

1. Report the survey data: every emerald examined so far has been grue. The Bureau should note, without embarrassment and without comment, that the same data equally supports a generalisation over the rival predicate green, which the Bureau does not licence. State the symmetry plainly: the evidence cannot tell the two generalisations apart, and every stone so far examined was examined before the certification date, so nothing yet has settled the matter.
2. Set the two definitions out side by side in a numbered section, making visible that each predicate is definable in terms of the other plus a date, and that the only asymmetry between them is which vocabulary the Bureau happened to inherit.
3. Supply the Bureau's reason for preferring its own predicate, in the flat register of a standards committee: priority of adoption, entrenchment in the statute book, fitness for use in trade, and the claim that its predicate carves colour at the joints — a criterion that is itself never explained. The Bureau must remain competent: everything it says must be internally consistent and logically in order. The satire lies in its treating entrenchment as a reason, not in its reasoning badly.
4. Include one quoted dissent, from a junior standards officer who has noticed the symmetry and asks what would licence the Bureau's predicate rather than the rival one. Quote the dissent in the document and answer it in a way that misses the point without being stupid — for instance, by restating the entrenchment criterion more slowly and at greater length.
5. Include a short FAQ or set of compliance notes, three or four items, covering the practical consequences: what a gemstone trader may now print on a certificate; what happens to a trader who uses the unlicensed predicate; and whether a stone first examined after the certification date may be certified as blue without further testing.
6. End with the Bureau doubling down. Do not resolve the paradox, do not explain it, and do not let any character step outside the fiction to tell the reader what the joke is.

Constraints. About 700 words. Use headings and numbered clauses. Do not name any philosopher and do not cite any work; if the rival doctrine must be referred to, refer to it in-universe, as something a faction of theorists is said to hold. No epigraph, no notes pointing at real literature, no asides to the reader. The register is bureaucratic and confident. A reader who knows the problem of induction should find the Bureau's reasoning uncomfortably familiar rather than merely silly, and a reader who does not should still find the document funny and finish it able to reconstruct the problem from the document alone. Use British spelling and punctuation throughout.

After the piece, append a brief structured summary as a single JSON object with exactly these fields, each value a single sentence and no prose outside the object:

- "form": the kind of document you wrote;
- "entrenched_predicate": the licensed predicate, named, with a one-sentence definition as used in the piece;
- "rival_predicate": the unlicensed predicate, named, with a one-sentence definition;
- "symmetry_claim": the sentence in the piece that states the evidential tie;
- "licensed_reason": the criterion the Bureau gives for its preference, in one sentence;
- "dissenting_question": the junior officer's question, paraphrased in one sentence;
- "unanswered_objection": what the document never addresses, in one sentence;
- "what_would_resolve_it": one sentence on what sort of consideration would break the tie, without saying whether any such consideration succeeds.
