I recently spent the better part of an evening arguing with an old friend about whether the small conventions that organize everyday life — how much to tip, how to queue, whether to jaywalk — carry any real moral weight, and whether a visitor who neither knows nor shares them is bound by them. We kept talking past each other, sliding between "that's just how it's done here" and "that's simply wrong," and I would like to see the dispute given the satirical treatment it deserves rather than re-litigated badly.

Please write, in the deadpan register of an official consular travel bulletin, a document issued by a fictional foreign ministry office — call it the Office of Moral Compliance — addressed to its citizens traveling abroad, in which the office has formally adopted the position that local conventions are strict moral obligations binding on visitors no less than on residents. Aim for roughly 650 to 800 words, in American English, formatted as a bulletin: a short preamble stating the office's doctrine, then a set of numbered advisories keyed to specific cases (tipping, queueing, jaywalking, and at least one further everyday convention of your choosing), and a closing section on remedies or sanctions.

The comedy must come from getting the moral philosophy right, not from manufacturing absurdity. The bulletin should reason the way a sincere adherent of the view would: it should treat conventions as generating obligations rather than mere expectations; it should take up what happens to a visitor who could not have participated in creating the convention, has no reciprocal stake in it, and may not even be able to comply without knowing it; and it should sort the listed conventions into an in-universe classification scheme — say a Bindingness Scale running from Category I (courteous but optional) through Category IV (obligatory for all persons present within the jurisdiction) — assigning each case a category with a straight-faced justification. The office should also have to decide, at least once, whether a convention binds because of its content or merely because of the coordination it secures, and should follow its own reasoning to a conclusion that a reader will find uncomfortable, without the document itself ever registering the discomfort.

The result should be legible to a reader who knows nothing of the literature but should reward someone who does: a philosopher ought to be able to see, without its ever being stated, that the bulletin is marching straight into the distinction between convention and morality, the fair-play and reciprocity problems, the tension between "ought implies can" and a duty to observe norms one cannot know, and the question of whether a norm's obligatoriness can outrun its locality. Keep it deadpan throughout; do not let the author wink.
