Ok so I'm a novelist — literary fiction, three books, I live in the weeds of craft talk (POV, focalization, free indirect, scene vs. summary, the through-line, A-plot and B-plot, the implied author, unreliable narrators, who gets an interior life and who's just set dressing). I'm a total amateur when it comes to history as a discipline though, so fair warning.

I've been mainlining global history for a year and I keep running into the same fight. One side says "world history" is just European history with everybody else as extras — the camera's bolted to Europe and the rest of the world only enters the frame when Europe bumps into it. The other side says fine, but you can't tell the past from nowhere; every telling is *from* somewhere, so the honest fix is to multiply the vantage points instead of pretending you have none. And then someone always mutters that "from everywhere at once" isn't a viewpoint, it's a fiction — and not the good kind.

Here's what I want. Take that whole quarrel, both sides, and restate it for me in the vocab I actually think in. Be as faithful as you can — don't hand me a summary and call it a translation, actually run the historians' claims through my machinery: narrator, focalizer, free indirect, through-line, protagonist vs. setting, whose desire drives the plot, what's scene and what's summary, where the implied author's allegiances leak through the seams.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. What survives translation cleanly. What I had to distort or fudge to make it fit (and say so when you do). And what in their arguement has no counterpart in my toolkit at all.

A few things I specifically want you to press on: is the "tell it from everywhere" ambition the historical version of an omniscient novel — and is my gut right that omniscient was always a con, a limited POV wearing a god costume? Does history have something fiction structurally can't say — causal weight that isn't anyone's wanting, structures that act with no character to act through, a story with no author and no implied author to leak through? Or is that just me not having a word for it?

And the meta-question, don't dodge it: is the residue pointing to a genuine limit of my vocabulary (fiction really can't carry what they're carrying), or is it showing that the historians' fight is half a fight about narrative craft that they'd mostly defuse by admitting they're storytellers too? Which is it, and be specific about which of their claims survives once you strip the storytelling out.
