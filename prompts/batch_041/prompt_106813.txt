OK so I write a weekly ideas column for a science-and-ideas site — linguistics, philosophy, that kind of beat — and I keep getting into the same fight with readers about slurs. Everyone wants to know whether a slur "literally means" something derogatory or whether it's just a rude word, and I keep realizing I don't have a clean way to help people figure out which theory they actually hold. So I want to run a quiz: which theory of slurs do you actually believe?

Here's what I need. Eight or so questions, each one a concrete case or a straight claim, with two or three answer options, and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the main positions. And please don't let the questions just be "do you think slurs are semantic or pragmatic?" — that's exactly what I'm trying to avoid. Each question has to be a case or a test where the positions genuinely come apart, so a reader can answer it without knowing which theory it's nudging them toward.

The distinctions I care about, in rough order of how much they matter to me:

- whether the derogation is part of what the word contributes to the literal truth-conditions, or whether it rides on top of a neutral descriptive meaning. So: does swapping the slur for a neutral phrase like "a person of X descent" ever change whether the sentence is true?
- whether the offensiveness survives being embedded — under negation, in a conditional, in a question. ("There are no Xs in this room." "If he's an X, then..." "Is she an X?")
- whether the word can be used without derogating at all — reclaimed in-group use, a history lecture, a quotation, a linguist writing about the word itself. If a theory says "never, not even then," I want that visible on the tree.
- whether we're dealing with a statement that can be true or false at all, or with an action — something you do to someone rather than say about them.
- and whether a slur is a seperate kind of thing from ordinary profanity, or just a ruder version of it.

I want the leaves to cover at least these, and to name them where there's a standard name, otherwise just describe the view: the view that the derogation is baked into the literal meaning; the view that it's a conventional aside, not-at-issue, that projects; the view that it's presupposed; the expressivist view that slurring expresses an attitude rather than stating a fact; the view that the word isn't semantically special at all and the harm comes from a social prohibition on using it that can be lifted in the right context; and the view that using a slur is itself an act of subordination — a move in a social practice, not just a description.

Build it as a tree first: the questions, then the answer-combinations they allow, then where each position lands in that space. If some combination of answers doesn't match any position anyone actually holds, say so and tell me why — incoherent, just unattractive, or just something nobody's bothered to defend? And if two of the leaves come out identical on every question I've listed, don't fake a distinction — flag it and tell me what extra test I'd need to pull them apart.

One more thing: don't let the whole thing collapse into "well, it's alot of context." The entire point is that these views disagree about something.

Audience is smart general readers, not grad students, so keep the questions short.
