So I've just had a proper two-hour row with my mate Rosie about hate speech laws and I need to work out whether I actually believe anything I said to her. She's just back from a year in Chicago and she reckons the Americans have got it right: the state shouldn't be in the business of punishing what you say about a group, only what you do, because being offended isn't the same as being harmed and you can't trust a prosecutor to tell the difference between hating a religion and criticising it. Fair enough, I said. Then I went and looked up what we actually do here, and now I can't work out what our law thinks its doing.

Here's the pattern as far as I can tell:

- Stirring up racial hatred: you're guilty if you use words that threaten, abuse or insult a racial group and hatred is likely to be stirred up. You don't even have to have intended to stir it up.
- Stirring up religious hatred: only if the words are actually threatening, and only if you specifically meant to stir up hatred. So insulting or abusing a religion is legal. Insulting or abusing a race isn't.
- A while back they took insulting behaviour out of the general public-order offence, so it's now only threatening or abusive behaviour that can get you nicked for causing harassment, alarm or distress. The argument for that was precisely that being insulted isn't the same as being harmed and the law shouldn't be policing hurt feelings.
- The stirring-up offences aren't about the group's feelings at all. Nobody has to be offended or upset for them to bite. They're about hatred being stirred up in other people.

So the same system tells me: offence isn't harm; an insult about someone's race can be a crime; an insult about someone's religion can't; and the wrong in the stirring-up offence isn't done to the insulted person anyway, it's done through the people listening.

What does all that presuppose? Does it commit the law to a particular view of how words harm — not by offending their target but by moving a third party, with the audience as a potential attacker? Does the race/religion split presuppose that race is something you are and religion is something you think, and is that even how religious people experience it? Is any of this internally consistent, or is it just a pile-up of political compromises wearing a principle as a hat? And a seperate question I can't get my head round: which bits does the law strictly need, versus which bits it's just as happy to do without — could you run the same offences on a completely different theory of harm and nobody would notice? If you swapped the theory for Rosie's American one, what here would have to go? And does the mess I've found actually help her case, or hurt it?

Don't just tell me what the statutes say, I can read those. Tell me what the thing is committed to.
