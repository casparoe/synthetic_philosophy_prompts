Last Christmas the argument at my sister's kitchen table was, on the face of it, the oldest one going: whether a tree that falls in a forest with nobody there to hear it makes a sound. My brother-in-law, who is a civil engineer and does not hold with any of it, said the question was a con — sound is a pressure wave travelling through air, the wave is either there or it isn't, and ears have nothing to do with the matter. My cousin, who reads a great deal of popular philosophy and enjoys winning, said no, that an Irish bishop had settled this three centuries ago: that things exist only in so far as they are perceived, and that one cannot so much as frame the thought of a tree existing unperceived, because the moment one frames it the tree is before one's mind and therefore perceived. My nephew then asked whether the camera at the end of the garden counts as a perceiver, which nobody could answer, and my cousin closed by saying that the tree goes on existing because God perceives it — which struck me as either the end of the discussion or a change of subject. I have been turning it over since, and I have come to suspect that the one-liner is doing at least half a dozen quite different jobs, and that the four of us were not really disagreeing about the same thing at all.

What I would like is a menu rather than a verdict of your own. Take the tree question and set out the distinct precise claims a person might be making in answering it, ordered from the utterly trivial, through the ones that are merely about how the word 'sound' is used, up to the full-blown metaphysical ones, and say of each whether it is a platitude, a serious and defensible position, or simply false, with the strongest reason for thinking so. I am especially interested in the ones that are easy to run together: sounds as experiences versus sounds as disturbances in the air; 'unperceived' meaning 'perceived by nobody' versus 'not perceived by me'; whether the perceiver has to be a mind, and if so what work that word is doing, since the camera suggests it cannot just mean something that registers the event; and whether the tree's going on existing when nobody is looking is the same claim as the original one or a further claim that needs an argument of its own, given that the same move would seem to be needed at every level.

Then tell me, for each reading, which of the four of us it would rescue and which it would convict, and which reading people are usually relying on when they use the tree question to win an argument about whether the world depends on being observed. And as to my cousin's argument, that one cannot conceive of an unperceived tree, I should like to know exactly how much it proves: whether it reaches the metaphysical conclusion, or only something about the limits of imagining, or a tautology in disguise — and if there is a gap, precisely where it lies. I do not want the history of the debate or a list of things to read. I want the readings separated, with your reasons, so that I can take them back to the table.
