So I'm in this human behavioral ecology class and costly signaling is getting used for like four diffrent things and its driving me nuts. Here's the pattern I keep noticing, in my own words. Something only tells you about the person doing it when it's harder for the wrong kind of person to do it, not when it's just hard. Two examples from my own life: (1) unpaid internships. The internship itself shows nothing about ability, but anyone who has to earn money over the summer can't take one, so it ends up sorting by family money rather than by the trait employers think they're reading. (2) The character I main in a fighting game has a combo system that takes hours to learn, but the hours only turn into rank if you already have fast reactions, so the practice time is only affordable for people who were good to begin with. My roommate says a deep voice is also a signal of size, but nobody spends anything there, you just can't make the sound without being big. Same thing, or three different things? And do any of them have their own names? I know there's supposed to be a distinction between paying a cost and being unable to fake something, but I can't find where the line actually falls. Then the case that breaks me: paying dues to a club, or buying the professor's own book for his class. The cost goes to the receiver instead of getting burned. Is that the same phenomenon or something else entirely, and what happens to the whole story when the receiver is the one collecting the money and therefore has a reason to want you to spend more? Please dont just tell me its costly signaling and call it a day. I need the boundaries: what each of my cases is actually an instance of, and what to call the ones that turn out not to have a name.
