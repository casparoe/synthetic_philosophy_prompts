For the past year I have sat on a municipal heritage committee that adjudicates contested plaques, street names and museum labels, and I keep meeting the same structural problem: a community's living memory of an event and the archival record disagree, and somebody has to decide which gives way. I have been trying to write down the principles we actually invoke, and I would like you to rank them by how much weight each should carry when they conflict — not which is most often decisive in practice, but which ought to be decisive on the merits. Our working list, in no particular order, is this. (a) The deference principle: where the record was produced by methods the community itself accepts, the record wins. (b) The constitutive principle: some community memories are not claims about the past at all but practices that make a group what it is, so they cannot lose a factual contest, because they are not entered in one. (c) The harm principle: whichever version, if institutionalised, imposes the greater continuing injury on living people should give way. (d) The jurisdiction principle: the community has standing over its own past and outsiders — including archivists — do not, save where they can show an affected party. (e) The silence principle: gaps in the record are not evidence against memory, so a thin archive leaves memory standing. (f) The revisability principle: the record is corrigible and memory is not, so the record should be the one that yields, since it can be repaired later. (g) The shared-world principle: correcting a community's memory damages a common world in a way that correcting a document does not, and that damage is a cost to be weighed rather than a sentiment to be managed. (h) The democratic principle: the community, not the committee, decides what its past was.

Give me a ranking from most to least weight, with a sentence or two on each placement. I care more about the reasoning than the order, so if two items sit so close together that the ordering is arbitrary, say so rather than manufacturing a distinction.

Two further things. First, I suspect the list is not homogeneous — some of these look like rules for settling a question of fact and others like rules for deciding what to do once the question of fact is settled — and if that is right I would rather you tell me than rank blithely across the gap. Second, if the whole framing is confused — if, for instance, 'the community's memory' and 'the archival record' are not two homogeneous things that can be weighed against one another at all, or if the ranking is being asked of items that do different jobs — say that plainly instead of producing a tidy list to order. I would rather have a short answer that pushes back on the premise than a long one that does not. Please write in British English.
