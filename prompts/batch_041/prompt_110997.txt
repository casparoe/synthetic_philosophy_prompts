The rational-choice chapter of a general-audience handbook of economics that a friend lent me contains the following summary of Aumann's agreement theorem, and I would like you to audit it.

"Aumann's agreement theorem shows that disagreement between rational agents is impossible. If two agents are rational and share the same information, they cannot hold different posterior beliefs about the same proposition. The proof turns on common knowledge: once it is out in the open between them that each holds a particular credence, and both are updating by Bayesian conditioning, their credences must coincide. The upshot is that when two people who know each other's opinions continue to disagree, at least one of them must be thinking irrationally, which is why the result is sometimes called the no-disagreement theorem. Critics object that the theorem assumes a common prior, and since real people evidently have different priors, the theorem is false; the standard reply is that the parties need only talk it out, since each will learn the other's evidence, the two will converge, and the disagreement will evaporate. The practical moral is that persistent disagreement about anything -- politics, religion, or which of two candidates is better qualified -- is a sign that someone has stopped reasoning properly."

I know the theorem only by reputation, and the closing part is the part I find most persuasive: the thought that two people who genuinely talk something through must end up agreeing matches how I have always assumed arguments are supposed to work.
