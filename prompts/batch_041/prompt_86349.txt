I'm in a small reading group that has been working through the free-will literature, and we keep circling back to a case nobody can quite place. A solitary digger wasp drags a paralyzed cricket to her burrow, leaves it at the threshold, goes inside to check the nest, comes back out to haul the cricket in; if the cricket is nudged a few inches away while she is inside, she retrieves it, sets it down at the threshold, and checks the nest again — and she will run that cycle over and over as long as the experimenter keeps moving the cricket. The behavior looks like purposeful housekeeping right up until the loop closes, at which point it looks like a fixed routine that a certain state of the world switches on. A friend started calling the pattern sphexishness, after the genus, and the word has stuck in our group. I am not interested in whether the nineteenth-century naturalist reports are literally accurate, or in whether later ethologists found the wasp more adaptable than the story suggests; I want the stylized case treated as the object of debate rather than as an open empirical question.

What I would like from you is a dialogue between two people who have thought hard about this and disagree. The first is a compatibilist: she holds that sphexishness comes in degrees, and that what separates us from the wasp is not the absence of a routine but the presence of a capacity to take one's own routine as an object — to notice that one is looping, to ask whether the policy being executed is a good one, and to revise it. On her view the wasp is sphexish without qualification, whereas we are sphexish only at the levels below our reflective monitoring, and that difference is where responsibility lives. The second is a skeptic: she holds that the reflective level is itself a routine, that every apparent stepping-back is just a longer loop, and that the felt openness of deliberation is an artifact of the environment's complexity together with our ignorance of our own mechanism. Have them actually engage with each other. The skeptic should press the compatibilist on what happens once the reflective capacity is triggered by a simple detector — suppose a creature only ever steps back when it registers that it has just repeated an action — and the compatibilist should press the skeptic on whether the charge that a process is merely a routine does any work once we have no independent grip on what a non-routine process would look like. I would also like them to take up a case I invented for the occasion: an agent who is genuinely flexible at the first level but whose flexibility is itself a fixed routine, switched on by detecting first-level loops. Ask each of them what their view predicts about such an agent, and then about an agent with three or four levels nested inside one another, and where, if anywhere, either view thinks the ladder stops being a mechanism.

Neither character should be a strawman, and neither should be allowed to talk past the other, so let each respond to the other's actual formulations rather than to a stock version of the position. Close with a short note in your own voice: say which side you think faces the harder remaining problem, and state plainly what argument or piece of evidence would move you off that verdict — the consideration that would flip it, or the point in your reasoning where you would most expect it to fail.
