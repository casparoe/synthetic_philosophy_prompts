The occasion for my question is a chapter in a popular book about the mind that I read last month. Its summary of the argument runs roughly as follows. Philosophers used to believe that the meaning of a word is the idea in the speaker's head. Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment destroyed that view. Imagine a planet exactly like ours, inhabited by people who are molecule-for-molecule duplicates of us, except that the liquid in their lakes and rivers is not H2O but a different compound, XYZ, which looks, tastes, and behaves exactly like water, and imagine all of this taking place at a time when nobody on either planet has any idea what the stuff is made of. The duplicate of me on that planet is in precisely the same mental state as I am when he says 'water,' and he means the same thing by it that I do; the only difference is that his word picks out XYZ while mine picks out H2O, which is simply Frege's old distinction between sense and reference applied to chemistry. What this shows is that the meanings of words are not fixed by anything inside the speaker but by the stuff in the environment. The moral of the story is that 'water' means, for each of us, whatever liquid happens to fill the local lakes, so that no two people can ever be certain they mean the same thing by a word until they have checked the chemistry of everything they are talking about. Putnam also observed that most of us cannot tell an elm from a beech, yet our word 'elm' still refers to elms, which proves that the meaning of a word is decided by the community and its experts rather than by the individual speaker. The chapter concludes that the private, inner conception of meaning is finished, and that this is also why translation between languages can only ever be approximate.
