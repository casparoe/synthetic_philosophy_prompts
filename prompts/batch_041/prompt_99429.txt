I am a retired experimental chemist who has lately been sitting in on a graduate seminar in the philosophy of language, and I am trying to reconstruct the instructor's implicit doctrine before I ask a question she has already answered. Over the term she has ruled on a series of cases, and I have recorded her verdicts as follows.

1. Of a familiar ethnic epithet, she says it is derogatory, and derogatory in a way that belongs to the word rather than to the speaker's tone; she calls this "lexical" derogation and treats it as part of what the word means.

2. Of that same word used by members of the targeted group among themselves, affectionately or ironically, she says it is not being used derogatorily — but she adds that the word has not thereby changed its meaning, and that an outsider who used it "in the same friendly spirit" would still be derogating.

3. Of a bland noun phrase with no derogatory content on its face, she says that in a certain sneering context it is derogatory. She calls this "discursive" derogation and insists it is a different phenomenon from the first.

4. Of a term that was once a slur and is now used neutrally by many, including in official documents, she says it is no longer lexically derogatory, though it remains "live" for those who remember the older use.

5. Of an epithet aimed at a dominant or historically advantaged group, she says it is a slur too, though a lesser one, and that the target's power affects only how much damage the word does, not whether it is a slur.

6. Of a coded phrase with no derogatory content whatever, which an initiated audience nonetheless understands as signaling contempt for a group, she declines to call the phrase itself a slur, but says its use can be derogatory in the discursive sense.

7. Of a slur printed in a newspaper's quotation of a hate crime, or read aloud by her in class, she says no slurring has occurred, because the word is mentioned rather than used.

The new case, which came up last week, is this. About ten years ago, members of a marginalized community coined a new word as a warm self-description, a term of endearment they apply to themselves. Outsiders have recently begun using it in public to refer to the community with evident contempt, while many insiders continue to use it warmly among themselves. The word's literal content is an innocuous descriptor — a word for a shared occupation or region, with nothing pejorative in it, and no history of having been used against the group before its coinage.

I would like your best guess at how she would rule on this case. Specifically: would she say the word itself has now become lexically derogatory? Would she call the outsider's contemptuous use a slur-use, a merely discursive derogation, or nothing special? Would she call the insiders' use derogatory? I care less about the verdicts than about the rule she appears to be running. Set out the candidate rules that fit her seven earlier verdicts equally well, say what each predicts about the new case, name the ones the new case separates, and give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it. Tell me which further case would best tell the surviving rules apart, and — if it turns out that no single rule accommodates all seven verdicts — say so plainly and tell me which verdicts are in tension.
