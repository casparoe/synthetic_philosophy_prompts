I am a novelist, and I have spent the last several years writing fiction in which the interior life of a single character is the entire subject — the sort of book in which very little happens and everything is at stake in what a person notices about her own experience. Lately I have been reading my way into the philosophy of mind, and I keep circling a problem that an epiphenomenalist view of qualia seems to create for anyone, novelist or philosopher, who wants to treat introspection as a source of knowledge. If the felt quality of an experience is caused by physical goings-on in the brain but causes nothing in turn, then the report a person makes — "this hurts," say — cannot be caused by the hurt itself; it must be caused by the underlying physical state, which makes the felt quality and the report two effects of a single cause. The worry is that this leaves our judgments about our own experiences either uncaused by the experiences they purport to be about, or reliable only by coincidence, and I want a thought experiment that presses on precisely this point.

Here is what I need, and I want the case engineered rather than merely narrated. Keep it to one realistic contemporary episode — one character, one experience, one report — with no zombies, no brains in vats, no possible-world machinery, and no science-fictional devices. Hold every observable fixed: the same report, the same dispositions, the same physiology, everything a third party could detect. Then, crucially, make the reliability of the report nomological under every reading of the case, so that the epiphenomenalist's report tracks the experience by law and not by luck; the only thing that may vary between readings is whether the felt quality causes the report or is merely a common effect, alongside the report, of one physical cause. I want the disagreement to survive even after reliability is taken off the table, because what I am really after is whether anything remains to disagree about once the sole remaining difference is causal contact. After the scenario, give me the verdict each of three readings reaches on whether the character's report counts as knowledge of her experience — epiphenomenalism about qualia, the view that the felt quality directly causes the report, and the identity theorist's view that the felt quality just is the physical state — and say why each reading lands where it does. The verdicts have to fall out of the theories rather than be built into the case.

Finally, tell me honestly which feature of the case is doing the work, and whether removing it — say, letting the correlation between experience and report be brute rather than lawlike — collapses the epiphenomenalist position back into the old coincidence objection. Close with the strongest line of defense an epiphenomenalist could mount against your own case, and with a clear separation between what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who study this; I do not want a contested verdict dressed up as the consensus.
