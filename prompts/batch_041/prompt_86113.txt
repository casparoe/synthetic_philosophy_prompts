After thirty years as a physical chemist — much of it spent on the thermal behaviour of clay minerals, a detail that will matter below — I retired and set about reading philosophy in earnest, as I had promised myself for decades. I have no formal training in the subject and no wish to pretend otherwise. But one dispute has lodged itself in me and will not come out, and I have reached the stage of wanting to see it mocked properly, which I take to be a form of respect.

The dispute is whether a statue and the portion of material it is made from are one thing or two. I would like a satirical piece built on it, and I would like the satire to be fair — the kind a specialist can laugh at without wincing.

The form I have in mind is a pair of letters, about 800 words in total and roughly evenly divided. The first is from the claims department of an art insurer, refusing to pay for a figure that was crushed in transit. The second is the reply from the work's owner. The insurer's letter should proceed in numbered clauses, in the deadpan register of a company that has recently discovered metaphysics and is delighted with itself; the owner's reply should be in ordinary prose, increasingly exasperated but still rigorous. Neither writer should ever wink at the reader, and the comedy has to come out of the arguments themselves. A reader who has never opened a book of metaphysics should still find it funny; a reader who has should not find it cheap.

Some material I would like the letters to make use of, because these strike me as the genuinely difficult cases and I am tired of the easy ones:

- The matter sat in the artist's studio for years before anything was made of it, so that if the figure and the lump are one thing, that one thing appears to be both two years old and forty years old.
- The figure was fired, and firing alters the material chemically, so that the finished work appears to survive a change the clay itself plainly does not survive.
- Squash the figure into a ball and the figure is gone while every particle of the stuff is still there. The letters should not be able to avoid noticing that a single accident has returned two verdicts.
- If there are two objects in one place, sharing every part and every property, the insurer wants to know what makes them differ, and the owner should have an answer more substantial than a wave at the artist's intentions.
- And if the count is allowed to be two, the clerk should ask, reasonably, why the policy covers the statue and the lump but not the lump minus a single grain, or the lump plus the speck of dust resting on it.

The insurer may, if it suits the character, try the line that strictly speaking there are no statues at all, only particles arranged statuewise, and that particles cannot be destroyed, merely rearranged — and it should then have to reckon with the fact that it has been collecting premiums on statues for a century. That is the joke I most want to see land, and I would like it to land on both parties.

Please close with a short note in your own voice on where the letters take liberties with the underlying positions or lean on a contested reading of them; I would rather have that than any list of sources, and I am in no position to check references in any case. And if the brief itself is confused — if, say, the satire cannot be fair to both sides at once, or I have misdescribed what is at issue — please say so plainly instead of producing something that merely sounds like an answer. I would rather be contradicted than humoured.
