Read Sophocles' Oedipus Rex through the lens of Good's theorem on the value of information -- the result that, for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of information is never negative, because the agent can always ignore it. Oedipus spends the play acquiring information: Tiresias's accusation, the Corinthian messenger's news, the shepherd's testimony. It destroys him. Is the play a counterexample to the theorem, or does it expose which of its assumptions fail -- that information is free, that the agent can choose to ignore it, that his preferences are fixed, and that learning does not itself change the world? Give me a reading that takes both the play and the theorem seriously: where the theorem illuminates Oedipus's insistence on knowing, where the play resists being reduced to an illustration of decision theory, and what the play adds to the philosophical debate about whether ignorance can be preferable. Argue from plot and structure, not quotation.
