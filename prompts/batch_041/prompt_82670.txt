I am a professor of philosophy, and I have been asked to deliver the memorial address for my colleague and friend, who died last month after a long illness. The service will be in the department, and the audience is mixed: his widow and two teenage children, who are not philosophers and who are grieving; and his colleagues, several of whom work on the foundations of quantum mechanics. He spent the last decade defending a version of quantum immortality. He believed that under a many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics there is always a branch in which he survives a fatal event, so from his own first-person perspective he never experiences death. He was not a sensationalist about it. He thought quantum suicide was a flawed argument if it was meant to prove that death is an illusion, and he insisted that the measure of branches in which one survives in good health could be vanishingly small. He also held a deflationary view of personal identity: the self that survives is not a soul or a persistent ego but a pattern of continuity, and he thought this made the first-person/third-person gap even harder to close. His widow has asked me to be careful not to turn the service into a seminar, but she also said she does not want a eulogy that pretends he believed something he did not. He would have considered it a betrayal to be comforted by a lie.

Please write the address. It should be a genuine eulogy, not a philosophy lecture, and it should be deliverable in about six minutes. It has to land for both halves of the room. The family needs to hear something that honors their grief and does not tell them that their grief is a mistake. The physicists need to hear something that does not cheapen his arguments or pretend that the many-worlds interpretation is settled. The address should make the first-person/third-person distinction do real structural work: it should not say that he is gone in a way that he would have rejected, and it should not say that he is still here in a way that would make the family's loss unreal. Find a way to say something that functions as a statement that he is at peace for the family and as a statement that the measure of his survival is not the measure of his life for the physicists, without using either phrase. You can use one concrete, non-technical image to carry the distinction, but do not let it become an exposition of the physics. Please avoid the usual quantum-suicide sensationalism, and avoid any quotation from his work or anyone else's; paraphrase his views instead. End with something that the physicists will recognize as intellectually honest and the family will find humane.
