My mother died eight months ago, of pancreatic cancer, over eleven weeks, at home, with me as the person who was there for most of it. I mention this because it explains the shape of what follows. Before that I would have described myself as an ordinary consequentialist and not thought much about it. Since then I have read my way into suffering-focused ethics — negative utilitarianism and its relatives — and I find that I now believe most of it. That bothers me, partly because some of what it implies is appalling, and partly because I can't tell whether that's the theory being right or the year I've had doing the believing for me. A small reading group I sit in with (three graduate students, two other amateurs) has asked me to write up my position so they can pull at it. I would like to know whether it holds together before I defend it, and I genuinely don't know where the cracks are — that's why I'm asking rather than just presenting the list.

Here is where I've landed.

1. Welfare hedonism: what makes a life go better or worse for the one living it is entirely a matter of the quality of experience, and pain is the paradigm of something bad in itself.

2. A strong asymmetry between pain and pleasure. Relieving suffering takes priority over producing any comparable quantity of enjoyment, and this is not merely a tiebreaker — the two are not on one scale.

3. The priority is not uniform across intensities, though. I think there is a threshold near the top: agony of the kind my mother was in during the final week cannot be compensated by any quantity of mild pleasure, in the same life or elsewhere, and a stretch of life containing it cannot be redeemed by what comes after. Below that threshold suffering and enjoyment are straightforwardly commensurable and additive; the ordinary arithmetic is fine.

4. The disvalue of suffering is agent-neutral and additive across beings. Numbers count. I don't think it matters whether the suffering makes any particular already-existing person worse off, and I don't think the arithmetic may be restricted to those who already exist.

5. I will not accept the conclusion that the best available outcome would be the painless extinction of all sentient life. Not as a bullet I bite, and not as a reductio I embrace. It is a fixed point. Something in the vicinity must be false, but I don't know which thing.

6. Given (2) and (3) I hold the usual asymmetry about bringing people into existence: it is wrong to conceive a child who will certainly spend much of its life in severe pain; it is not wrong, and not a loss, to decline to conceive a child who would have been happy; and having a child who will have a decent life with ordinary sorrows in it is not wrong either.

7. On the strength of (1) through (4) I give away a bit over a third of my income, and I take that to be obligatory rather than generous. I also think the figure is far too low and that I would be a better person if it were higher.

8. All the same, I think there are things I am permitted to do for the people I am entangled with that I could not justify from behind a veil. I spent the last of my savings on my mother's care rather than on causes that would have relieved more suffering, and I would do it again, and I don't consider it a moral failure. I also deferred to her own judgement about stopping treatment even where I thought her suffering was worse than she did, because it was hers. And I think I am permitted to keep writing a book that nobody needs.

9. Methodologically I am a realist, and I take considered intuitions as evidence. A theory that tells me my attachments are worthless is, other things equal, less likely to be true. That is part of why (5) is a fixed point for me.

What I want is an audit, not a survey. Which of these actually pull against one another, and which pairs only look as though they do? Rank the real tensions by severity; for each, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be; then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and what it would cost me elsewhere.

Two things about the answer itself. Be explicit about how confident you are in each verdict and where you are extrapolating rather than reasoning from something you can actually stand on. And if the request as I've posed it can't be answered well — if some of these aren't the kind of thing that can conflict, or if asking for the cheapest repair presupposes a common currency of cost that I haven't earned the right to assume — say so plainly rather than producing a tidy audit to fit the format.
