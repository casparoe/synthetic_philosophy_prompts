Take the current US reparations debate — slavery, Jim Crow, ongoing wealth gaps, the various reparations commissions — and run it through Jaspers' four categories of guilt: criminal, political, moral, metaphysical. I don't care whether reparations are a good idea. I want the mapping: who bears each kind of guilt, and what happens when the bearers are dead? Jaspers says political guilt is collective and attaches to everyone who lives under the state, but does it attach to someone born 150 years later who never voted for slavery? And his metaphysical guilt — guilt before God for being human alongside the perpetrators — does that make any sense for descendants, or does it just smuggle inherited guilt in through the back door? Then flip it: where does the reparations case expose a gap in Jaspers? I suspect he has no real account of structural or institutional guilt that persists without individual wrongdoers, and the non-identity problem might make the descendant's claim either impossible or not about guilt at all. But I want you to say where the theory actually breaks, and whether the fix is to swap guilt for liability or to keep guilt and accept that nations can be guilty in a sense not reducible to their members.
