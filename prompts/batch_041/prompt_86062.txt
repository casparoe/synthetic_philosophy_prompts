I teach early modern philosophy here and I've been on our animal care and use committee for two years now, which has mostly meant skimming mouse protocols and voting yes, but this term we've got a case that's turned into an actual fight and I have to circulate a written opinion before Thursdays meeting. A neuro lab wants to run a study on octopuses: repeated noxious stimulation, then watching for sensitization and avoidance learning, and the PI's cover note explicitly leans on Descartes, arguing that since animals are machines with no thought and no feeling, insisting on analgesia here is sentimentality, that it would confound the results anyway, and that we'd be projecting a human-shaped psychology onto a creature that hasn't got one. Meanwhile our own mandate says we base decisions on "the best available evidence about the capacity for suffering," which I think quietly assumes the whole question away. So what I need from you is the memo I'll send round: a recommendation that is actually a recommendation, approve or approve with conditions or send it back, with the conditions written so a lab manager could implement them (dosing, observation windows, a stopping rule, a vet on the protocol), a date by which the PI has to respond, and I want it to definately engage the Cartesian argument rather than the usual shrug that it's 400-year-old science.

The philosophy is the part I care about and the part I don't want hand-waved. As far as I can reconstruct it, Descartes' case for the beasts being automata isn't the crude "they don't seem like us" thing people remember; it's an argument about what a machine could in principle be built to do, with the decisive mark being language, since he thinks no beast can put words together in the flexible, occasion-appropriate way that even the dullest human can, and no conceivable machine could either. But that only gets him to "we can't prove the brutes think"; to get all the way to "only humans think" he needs a further premise, that every human being has a rational soul, which is not something the behavioral evidence delivers, and I suspect the PI's version quietly drops that premise while hanging onto the conclusion. Tell me where that reconstruction is right and where I've got it wrong, and do the same for two things I know will come up at the meeting. R., who sits to my left, will say the whole discussion is a distraction and that we should just be cautious whenever suffering is possible, full stop, and I want to know what's actually wrong with that, or right with it. And I've drafted a standing rule I'd like the committee to adopt: for species where the evidence is contested we presume insensibility, with the burden on whoever claims the animal can suffer, but the presumption flips if the animal shows a lasting behavioral change after a noxious procedure, at which point analgesia is mandatory unless the PI can show it would wreck the study. Is that rule coherent, does it beg the Cartesian question or actually answer it, and what would you change about it.

One thing: no citations, no reading lists, no "see also" — I don't need a bibliography, I need something I can read aloud to eight people who disagree with each other, so keep it as continuous prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs the way you'd write an email to a colleague. Lead with the bottom line and the date, then the reasoning, and be honest about the strongest thing the other side has going for it, because if I'm going to lose the vote on Thursday I'd at least like to lose it for the right reason.
