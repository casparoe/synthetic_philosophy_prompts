I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am at work on a long essay about what the past fifteen years of platforms, feeds, and engagement metrics have done to the very idea of a public. The piece keeps circling a critique I want to use honestly rather than decorate the essay with: Kierkegaard's attack on the press and on the public, written in his 1846 review of a popular novel of the day — the argument that the press conjures a public that is an abstraction rather than a body of persons, that the age is characterized by leveling, in which individuals are ground down into interchangeable units, and that reflection and commentary have displaced action and decision. I have read enough to be unsettled and not enough to be confident I am not flattening him.

What I want from you is a dialogue, not an exposition. Two characters, both serious, neither a fool.

The first is a working investigative journalist — someone who has spent twenty years on accountability reporting, has been threatened, sued, and briefly jailed, and who believes that the press and the public it addresses are the load-bearing institutions standing between citizens and arbitrary power. She should be able to defend the abstraction on its merits: the whole point of a public is that it is not a faction, not a crowd in a square, not a named body of people who can be intimidated one at a time; anonymity and abstraction are what allow the powerless to speak and allow power to be named.

The second is a scholar of Kierkegaard who believes the critique has been vindicated by everything that has happened since, and who is not merely nostalgic for a pre-modern world. He should be able to argue that the leveling Kierkegaard described has been completed rather than superseded: that the press, and now the feed, converts action into commentary, distributes responsibility so widely that no one holds any of it, and produces an entity that is at once everything and nothing — a public that exists only as a set of statistics and sentiment, incapable of decision and therefore incapable of holding anyone to account, including itself.

Neither character may be a strawman. Each must land at least one point the other cannot simply wave away, and each must concede something real when the other presses hard enough. They must answer each other's actual sentences rather than deliver alternating monologues. Let them fight over specifics: whether the abstraction of the public is a defect or precisely what makes accountability possible without degenerating into factional war; whether leveling is the opposite of equality or a deformed version of it; whether the demand for the single individual is a religious ideal unavailable at scale or a standard we can still hold institutions to; whether a crowd is ever a public and a public ever a crowd; and whether the algorithmic public is a corruption of Kierkegaard's public or its fulfillment. Give the scholar the strongest version of the claim that the online mob is not a betrayal of the public but its logical completion, and give the journalist the strongest version of the claim that the alternative to a leveled, anonymous, commenting public is not authentic individuals but silence and impunity.

Set the exchange somewhere concrete and unglamorous — a conference hotel bar, a newsroom after deadline, a faculty corridor — so the argument has a physical situation. Do not resolve it. End in a genuine impasse, or in one of them shifting position only partially and grudgingly, and let the reader see exactly what the shift cost. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words.
