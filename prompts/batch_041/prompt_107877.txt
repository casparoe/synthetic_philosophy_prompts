I have no academic training in this area, but I have spent years reading and arguing about whether religion is what keeps people decent, and one comparison keeps doing the heavy lifting on one side of it. The comparison is to a surveillance camera. The picture is this: a group of people is a room, and decent behavior in the room is maintained by the sense of being watched. God, or the gods, is the camera. It does not matter that no one has ever seen the footage; what matters is that everyone believes the camera is running. On this picture, once a society stops believing the camera is on, the room slowly empties out. People keep behaving for a generation or so, out of habit and out of what they absorbed as children, but that behavior is coasting on a stock that is being spent, and the trust that lets strangers deal with one another is exactly that stock. The conclusion drawn from it is that a secular society can look healthy for a while but cannot generate or sustain trust on its own, because it has switched off the one thing that ever made anonymous strangers behave when nobody was looking.

I would like an audit of how far this actually holds. Please lay out the mapping explicitly, in a table if that is easiest — what corresponds to the camera, the room, the person watching the monitor, the footage, and the moment when someone discovers the camera was a dummy — and then tell me where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in what the comparison quietly imports from its source. A camera is a piece of equipment that a society can buy more of, and it only ever catches outward acts; the analogy seems to smuggle in a watcher who is external, detectable, and replaceable, and a monitored person whose compliance is a matter of not getting caught. Someone in these arguments always adds that the camera is really a mirror, that the point is the watcher we internalize, and if that is right the whole picture changes.

Tell me which of the disanalogies actually matter for the claim about trust among strangers in large, anonymous societies and which are decoration — a small town where everyone knows everyone does not need the camera at all, for instance. Then tell me whether the conclusion survives: if the comparison fails in the ways you describe, does anything remain of the claim that secular societies cannot sustain trust on their own, or was the analogy carrying the argument the entire time? If you would replace it, say what you would replace it with and why the replacement does the work better — or whether no single comparison can carry this claim.
