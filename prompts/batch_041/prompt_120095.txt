OK, so I'm a third-year PhD student in epistemolgy (social epistemology side, my advisor works on testimony) and I'm writing a chapter where Twelve Angry Men is my running case study, and I've hit a wall with the reading I've been pushing, so I want you to either fix it or kill it.

Here's the setup I'm working with. Eleven to one, and the eleven and the one are about as close to textbook epistemic peers as fiction gives you: same evidence, they all sat through the same trial, same general competence, no asymmetry in expertise, no one has information the others lack. That's exactly the configuration the conciliationism vs. steadfastness fight is about — same evidence, same competence, disagree. So: is Juror 8 a steadfast hero or an epistemic dogmatist, and more importantly, what does the film itself think he is?

My thesis, which I'm no longer sure survives contact with the movie: the film is a deflation of peer disagreement. Every reversal in that room gets bought either with a new piece of evidence (the knife, the old man's shuffle down the hall, the marks on the woman's nose) or with a diagnosis of the dissenter's motives (Juror 3's son, Juror 10's racism, Juror 7 just wanting to get to the ballgame). Nobody ever changes their mind because a peer disagrees with them. Which leaves me with two readings and I can't decide between them: either the movie is an unwitting argument that real peer disagreement dissolves once you dig into it, or it's a story where 8's steadfastness is vindicated precisely because being stubborn is what *generates* the evidence everyone else then shares. Is the honest verdict that the film ducks the question?

Things I want you to push on, because they cut against me:

- 8's opening move isn't a contrary belief at all, it's "I don't know, I just want to talk." That's closer to refusing to settle than to steadfastly holding a credence. Does that make him not a peer-disagreement case at all?
- Juror 9's early defection looks testimonial — he's moved by the fact that 8 was willing to stand alone. Is deferring to the brave guy an epistemic reason or a moral one wearing an epistemic costume?
- Should I instead model the jury as a single agent with one shared evidence base, so this isn't inter-personal disagreement but a group that's internally inconsistent, like a person who can't make up their mind? I like this move but I suspect it's a dodge.
- The unanimity rule plus a life on the line. Does 8 have a *practical* license for his stubbornness even if he lacks an epistemic one? If the cost asymmetry is doing the real work, is the film secretly about decision under risk rather than about belief?

And here's the thing I actually think the literature misses: the movie is obsessed with the social cost of changing your mind in public — the order of defection, the silence, everyone watching you walk over to the other side. Conciliationism models revision as private arithmetic over credences. Does the film show us something about the mechanics of rational deference that the arguments don't, or is that just psychology and irrelevant to the epistemic question?

Give me the reading, scene by scene where it matters, faithful in both directions — don't flatten the movie into an illustration of a position, and don't bend the epistemology to save the movie. Tell me straight where it resists the reading. End with what, if anything, the film adds to the debate that argument alone doesn't. American English, please, and don't bother with citations, I know the literature — I just want the argument.
