My closest friend and I spent most of last weekend arguing about our country's place in the annual international happiness rankings, and we broke off without settling anything. She is an analyst at our national statistics office, and her case runs roughly like this. The ranking is the best cross-nationally comparable measure anyone has of how a population is actually doing; our country has slipped several places over the past decade; a falling score is therefore a real fact about our people's lives; and the government ought to adopt an explicit target of returning to the top ten, with ministries assessed on whether the national score rises. She has now asked me to co-sign a short open letter to the finance ministry endorsing that target, and I have to decide whether to put my name to it.

My instinct is that something here is confused, but I could not say precisely what. I said a government cannot manufacture happiness by chasing a number, and that the ranking may not be measuring happiness in the first place. She replied that if you cannot measure something you cannot manage it, that the ranking is the only measure we have, and that a declining score is an obligation rather than a statistic.

I would like your help working out who has the better of the argument, in a form I can actually use when we resume the discussion rather than a lecture. Two things in particular need disentangling.

First, does the ranking measure happiness at all? I know enough to be suspicious. The whole edifice rests on a single survey question in which people are asked to picture a ladder numbered from zero at the bottom to ten at the top, where the top rung is the best possible life they can imagine and the bottom the worst, and to say which rung they currently stand on; each country's figure is an average of that one answer, pooled over three years of surveying. Because every respondent anchors the scale to their own conception of the best and worst life, I keep wondering whether the number can move even while lives stay much the same, and whether "happiness" here means anything like what my friend seems to think it means.

Second, suppose for the sake of argument that it does measure something real: should a country try to top the ranking? Is there a difference between a people becoming happier and a country winning a league table, and if so, what follows for policy?

I want a clear verdict, not a survey of considerations. Tell me which of my friend's claims survive and which do not, whether the policy she proposes follows from the parts that do, and what I should say about the open letter. Keep the whole thing under 600 words.
