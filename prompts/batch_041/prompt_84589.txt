I recently finished a popular history of AI -- the author is a respected science journalist rather than a specialist, and I won't name the book -- and its chapter on the frame problem left me unsure how much of it to trust. Here is the gist of its account.

The frame problem, it says, is the problem of getting a machine to know what does not change when it acts. A system that reasons in a formal language has to be told, for each action, that the action leaves almost everything else untouched -- that moving an arm does not repaint the wall, drain the fuel tank, or shift the furniture -- and because this has to be stated explicitly, in axioms, their number explodes. The book credits McCarthy and Hayes with naming the problem and Dennett with generalizing it: Dennett's contribution, on its telling, is that the real difficulty is not the count of axioms but the prior question of which of its beliefs a reasoner should consult at all, so that the frame problem is at bottom the problem of relevance. The book then reports that the technical version was eventually solved, first by the closed-world assumption and later by nonmonotonic logics, while the deeper version remains open -- which, it concludes, is why rule-based AI stalled and statistical learning took over.

I have read enough rationalist blogs to carry a different picture into this -- I tend to think of the frame problem as being about relevance, and about the odd situation of an agent that is itself part of the world it reasons about -- so I am not a neutral reader, but neither can I locate where the book goes wrong. Please audit the passage. Which claims would someone who works on this accept as fair, which would they wince at as a familiar oversimplification, and which are simply mistaken? In particular: is the identification of the frame problem with the problem of relevance sound, or does it collapse two things that the technical literature keeps apart? Is the verdict that the closed-world assumption and nonmonotonic logics solved the technical problem a fair one, or does it overstate what those devices accomplish? And does the book's closing inference -- that this history explains the field's turn to statistical learning -- follow from anything true in the account, or is it a just-so story?
