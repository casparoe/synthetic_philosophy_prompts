Give an example of a conceptual issue that arises when the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda—the view that reality is many-sided and that every judgment is true only from a particular standpoint—is applied to a single object about which two incompatible claims are made at the same time. The example should turn on the distinction between a substance (dravya) and its modes (paryāya), so that one claim is warranted when the object is considered as enduring substance and the other when it is considered as a passing mode. Then explain how the syādvāda formula—according to which the object is, in some sense, and is not, in some sense—is supposed to handle the conflict, and evaluate whether that formula genuinely resolves the contradiction or merely postpones it by shifting between standpoints. In your answer, do not simply report the standard textbook illustration of a clay pot; construct a case that makes the difficulty vivid.
