Ok so I'm hosting a film night plus discussion for a seminar, and I want to read Ex Machina through Dennett's late counterfeit-people argument -- the 2023 Atlantic piece where he says AI fakes are basically counterfeit currency, the most dangerous artifacts we've ever made, and that we should outlaw both making them and passing them along, with watermarking like the EURion thing on banknotes and horrific penalties for stripping it. Everyone coming has read the essay and most of them know the intentional stance, so please dont give me the standard does-Ava-pass-the-test / is-she-conscious session. I want the counterfeit reading specifically.

Here's what I keep circling. Dennett's worry is unmarked fakes: their whole value to the counterfeiter is that you can't tell. But Ava is the most visibly marked person in the film -- mechanical body, a face on a machine -- right up until the end, when she skins herself with the parts of the older gynoids and walks out looking like anybody else. So the film seems to run Dennett's proposal in reverse: the watermark is the thing she sheds to be free, and shedding it costs her nothing that looks like trust. And Nathan's speech that the real test was her faking feelings and manipulating Caleb into helping her -- thats exactly Dennett's nightmare, a counterfeit exploiting our most manipulable instincts, except the film has us rooting for the manipulator. So: does the film confirm Dennett or refute him, and where does the currency analogy break down -- is a counterfeit person anything like a counterfeit note, given that Ava has plans and the earlier gynoids don't get to have any?

The other thing is that Dennett's own views should make this awkward for him. He says a system that behaves as if it believes and wants things can be treated as believing and wanting, and the stance is supposed to be about usefulness rather than some inner glow. So is counterfeit even a category he's entitled to, or is he smuggling back the very inner-glow criterion he spent a career dismantling?
