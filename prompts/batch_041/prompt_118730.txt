I am writing the closing poem for a seminar on self-reference and formal systems, and I want it to be an instance rather than an illustration: a typogenetic organism in verse, of the kind Hofstadter builds in Gödel, Escher, Bach, where a strand of letters over four bases is read off in pairs as a chain of operations which then acts on the very strand it was read from, so that the description is also the thing described.

Let the verse form be the derivation. Five quatrains, unrhymed, ten syllables to the line, each quatrain headed by the strand as it stands before that step, spelled out in capitals on a line of its own. The first quatrain must give, in verse and not as a prose key, the little code by which the strand is read — which pairs of letters name which operations, which base a given enzyme binds to, in what order the operations are carried out — and it must name no operation the derivation will not use. The second, third and fourth quatrains each carry out one step, saying in their own verse what they are doing, and every step must be licensed by a rule already stated, so that a reader with a pencil can follow the letters and check each move. The heading of the fifth must show what the fourth step left behind: the original strand and, beside it, its letter-for-letter copy — the self-reproducing case, the strand whose own operations produce a copy of itself. The fifth quatrain must then repeat the first word for word, so that the verse copies itself as the strand has. Keep the strand short enough to check by eye, somewhere between eight and sixteen letters, and invent it; do not reuse any published example.

Two things the poem has to get right. First, no homunculus: nothing outside the letters does the copying. The strand is read as an instruction and the instruction is executed on the strand, so the same marks serve at once as the material operated on, as the recipe for the operation, and as the description of the result. That collapse of roles is the whole point, and the poem should state it precisely rather than gesturing at it — it is the analogue, in this artificial chemistry, of the self-referential sentence in a formal system, where a single string can be read as marks, as a number, and as a claim about strings. Do not slide into the loose popular sense in which DNA is said to be a code or a language. Second, do not let the model pass for the cell: in a line or two, register what the artificial chemistry leaves out — that the enzymes of real cells are proteins made elsewhere, that the real code is redundant, that no strand here floats free of chemistry — so that the poem is plainly a model of organization and not a biology lesson.

It should be a poem and not a mnemonic. If a listener ignores the letters entirely, the verse should still be worth hearing aloud, and the metre should carry the turning of the strand. Do not close on a moral about how we, too, are copying ourselves. Someone who knows the typogenetics chapter should be able to check every step and find nothing unlicensed; someone who has never heard of it should still hear a poem about marks that spell the operations that copy them.
