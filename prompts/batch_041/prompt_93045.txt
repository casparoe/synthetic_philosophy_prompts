I run a grad seminar on democratic theory, and last week one of my students, Maya, put together an argument she's convinced demolishes the whole all-affected-interests picture of who belongs to the demos. She's sharp, and I've been staring at the thing for a week without finding the loose brick, so I'm bringing it here. Her reconstruction goes like this:

(1) Someone belongs to the demos for a decision -- gets a vote, counts as one of the people who decide -- just in case the decision affects her interests. Call it the stake principle.
(2) The stake principle attaches no prior restriction to the kind of party that can be affected: not citizenship, not residence, not species membership, not whether the party exists yet.
(3) Climate policy affects the interests of people abroad, of people not yet born, and (on any halfway plausible account of animal interests) of a great many non-human animals.
(4) So all of those parties belong to the demos for climate policy.
(5) Nothing in (1) or (2) makes climate special; the same reasoning runs for tariffs, for fishing quotas, for a city zoning vote, for a condo board deciding about a fence.
(6) So every decision has a demos stretching as far as its causal ripples.
(7) But a demos whose membership is just the set of everyone downstream of a decision isn't a constituency at all -- it's the world, and the world can't vote, can't deliberate, can't be bounded, can't have a we that decides.
(8) So the stake principle is either empty (everything is a demos) or it destroys the very thing it was meant to define.
(9) Therefore the stake principle is false, and some pre-political fact has to fix the boundary of the demos.

I'd definately rather keep (1) than (9) -- I do think stakes ground membership somehow -- but if I keep (1) I seem stuck with (7). So which step is doing the damage, and what does it cost me to give it up? And is (7) even true? Does a demos have to be bounded by something external to the decision it makes, or can a demos constitute itself?
