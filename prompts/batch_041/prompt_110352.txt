I'm a novelist — my new book is about a translator at one of the international tribunals, and the hinge of the plot is the afternoon she has to decide which word goes into the official record — and in three weeks I'm on a panel at a human rights conference where I've been asked to say something about whether genocide is still a word worth fighting over. The room is mostly international criminal lawyers, a couple of historians of the Holocaust, and one woman who runs a diaspora NGO for a community that's spent twenty years trying to get the label applied to what happened to them. My line, roughly: everyone fights about whether a case meets the definition — intent, which groups count, the courts' habit of letting you destroy only a substantial part of a group and still call it genocide — and I want to say that's the wrong fight, because the word isn't a caption, it's a demand. Calling something genocide is a speech act with a body count; it's a move in a game of obligations, and no amount of evidence will ever settle it, because settling isn't what the word is for. (The list of protected groups is itself a piece of 1948 horse-trading — political groups got left off it.) So: write me my Q&A prep sheet. The objections I'll actually get from this room, in their language, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they do, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where the reply is thin, plus the one question i'm praying nobody asks. No reading lists please — I won't have internet at the venue, I want the arguments, not citations.
