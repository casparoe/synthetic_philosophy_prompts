A couple of people on my team got into it at lunch about whether "generations" like Boomers and Gen Z are real things or just made-up bins, and I've been chewing on it since because I honestly could'nt tell who was winning. Can you act as a philosopher of science / social ontology type and adjudicate it move by move? Please think it through carefully first -- maybe lay out the positions in your own words and note where they might be talking past each other -- then give a verdict on each step and say who's more right overall.

Here's roughly how it went. Priya said generations are astrology for people who think they're too smart for astrology: the bins are arbitrary (why 1946 to 1964 and not 1948 to 1966?), nudge the cutoffs by three years and you get a different set of people in each generation, and nobody can state the rule that says where one generation ends. Dan said the arbitrariness of the boundary doesn't make the underlying thing unreal -- we can't say exactly how many hairs make a man bald, but baldness is a real fact about a real head, and some cutoffs clearly capture more than others. Priya said the baldness analogy fails because hair count is a physical fact while "Gen Z" is a marketing category someone invented, and if the thing exists only because people use the word, it's a label, not a real kind.

Dan said fine, so it's a social kind -- money exists only because people use it too, and nobody says money isn't real. Then he added that the label does actual work: tell a cohort it's a generation and its members start comparing themselves to it, reading articles about themselves, acting like the description, so the category makes itself more true. Priya said that's exactly what makes it useless for explaining anything -- if the label creates the pattern, citing the label as the cause is circular, and he's just described a self-fulfilling prophecy, not a kind.

Dan said it's not circular, because the label is shorthand for a real bundle of shared causes -- coming of age when everyone got smartphones, graduating into a particular job market -- and once you unpack it you've got mechanisms, so the problem is lazy usage, not the category. Priya said even granting that, you can't isolate the generational part: for any difference between 20-year-olds now and 20-year-olds in 1995, it could be an age effect, or a period effect (everyone's like this now), or a birth-cohort effect, and those three are perfectly tangled together in any dataset you could ever collect, so "Gen Z is X" can never really be pinned down. Dan said that's a problem with a study design, not with whether cohorts exist, and needing a modeling assumption to get an estimate doesn't mean the thing you're estimating is fictional.

They asked me to pick a side and I said I'd think about it, which was a lie. So: who's right about what at each step, what are they both missing, and is there any defensible sense in which generations are real kinds -- and if so, is it the sense Dan wants or something weaker? Also flag anywhere they're just using words differently.
