I run a monthly discussion circle out of the back room of our public library. There are no credentials behind it — a dozen or so adults, a retired teacher, a night-shift nurse, a couple of people who just like arguing — and we work through a chapter or two a month and then talk for two hours. This term we have been going around and around on when failing to save someone is as bad as killing them, and our conversations keep stalling in the same spot: someone says she never did anything, someone else says she was the only one who could have, and we start over. I have no training in this and cannot look anything up, so I have been trying to build a line of my own rather than borrow one, and I would like to end the term with a written exercise that forces the group to actually use it instead of recycling the same examples.

Here is what I have landed on. What turns an omission into a killing, I think, is not that the body stayed still but that the person who died was counting on the agent for something the agent had already led them to expect. I have been calling this the reliance baseline: the standard against which an agent's failure is measured is whatever the victim had been given reason to count on, given how the agent had already behaved. If a swimmer drowns at a lake where no one was ever watching, nothing was taken from her. If she drowns because the person who took the job of watching walked away, something was. On this view the usual line between doing and allowing is not the real line at all; what matters is whether the agent's own conduct built the baseline that the victim fell below.

So for the last session I want one essay question that puts that idea under real pressure instead of letting it coast, along with a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme worth 25 points. The question should make an answer apply the reliance baseline to three cases: one where the reliance comes from an explicit promise, one where it comes from nothing more than the agent's continued presence, and one where the supposed victim could not have relied on anything at all — a wild animal, a person not yet born, anyone who has no idea the agent exists. I want that third case to be the one that does the damage. It should also force an answer to whether the reliance baseline genuinely replaces the doing/allowing line or merely smuggles it back in under a new name, and to whether there is any case where reliance is plainly present and yet the omission plainly is not a killing. Please keep the whole thing free of names, works, and citations; we cannot check anything, and I do not want people padding with attributions. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a strong answer has to make and what each is worth, and add a short list of the particular mistakes that should cost points, with a one-line note on each.
