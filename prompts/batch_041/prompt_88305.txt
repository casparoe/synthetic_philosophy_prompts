Ok so I'm a 2nd year PhD student — ML/AI safety-ish, though my department is more phil of sci so I'm the weird one — and I'm doing a rotation on a post-training team at a lab this summer. My job is to write the data spec for a new training run, which mostly means deciding, per task family, whether we supervise the process (the model's intermediate steps and rationales get scored) or the outcome (only the final answer gets scored). We make this call maybe two or three times a week during the data sprint and right now it's vibes plus whoever argues loudest in the meeting. I keep having to re-defend the same calls to annotators and to the one staff researcher who has to sign off, so I want an actual procedure instead of a pile of principles. Stakes: a bad call either burns a few hundred thousand dollars of compute on a run that learns to game our rubric, or we spend three months labelling process data we turn out not to need.

Here's the framing I want you to build it on, because I think the usual line that process supervision is just what you do for hard reasoning is unhelpful. Process supervision isn't an escape from proxy gaming, it's the same thing with a different proxy. When you score the process you've decided that the observable you'll optimize is the model's own trace, and the model will attack whatever is cheapest to fake inside that signal — a rationale that reads as careful reasoning to a monitor without being what actually produced the answer is cheap. So the real question is never process or outcome, it's which of my two signals sits further from something the model can corrupt cheaply, given that the model is producing both of them. And when neither the final artifact nor the trace is directly checkable, I'm choosing between two proxies and should stop pretending the distinction is principled there.

So: write me a decision procedure I can run with three other people in under an hour, for a given task family. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient yes or no at each step, where it terminates in use outcome, where it terminates in use process, and where it hands off to a human because the question is about values (how much we care about a model that's right for bad reasons versus one that's wrong but honest, say) rather than facts. Constraints: I have budget for human process labels but they run 5-8x the per-item cost of outcome labels, my annotators are smart but not domain experts, the model can't see its own reward but it can see the rubric. If a step needs a number I can't actually get, the procedure has to say what to do instead. And if you have to drop some distinction from the framing above to make the thing usable, tell me which one and why, because I'd rather know than find out later.

Then run it on three cases: (1) a code-gen task where we have a test suite, (2) a long-horizon research-assistant task where we genuinely won't know for months whether the output was any good, (3) something where the answer is checkable but the check is itself gameable, like a math word-problem set with known answers that leaked somewhere.

I want at least one of these to come out against what I'd naively want. My honest bet is the procedure tells me to use outcome supervision on (1) and (3), even though the whole reason I'm on this team is that I think process supervision is where the field should be going. If that's what it says, say it plainly rather than hedging to make me feel better about my thesis.

Finish with what the procedure definately can't do — which calls are judgment dressed up as procedure, and where it will misfire on a task family that looks like the ones above but isn't. No citations or reading lists please, I can't search from the meeting and it just turns into people saying well such-and-such paper showed and we go in circles. Just the procedure and the reasoning behind each step.
