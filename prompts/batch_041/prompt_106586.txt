Last month I sat in on our school's debate club, where the motion for the evening was whether the town should go ahead with an artificial turf field for the high school — the kind filled with pellets made from recycled tires. The evidence on the pellets is genuinely unsettled; the town's own report says the studies point in different directions, and the board's main argument for building is that the field would let three teams practise at once instead of one. After the formal debate ended, two of the club's strongest speakers, Priya and Marcus, kept arguing with each other, and I have been turning their exchange over in my head ever since, because I honestly could not tell who was winning. Priya opened by saying that when a new material might be harming children and we do not yet know, the responsible thing is simply not to install it — that is what precaution means, and the town should not be running an experiment on its own students. Marcus said that no material is ever proven safe in that absolute sense, that grass fields have hazards of their own, and that a rule which forbids every option is not a rule for choosing anything. Priya replied that she was not proposing a ban, only that the burden of proof should sit with whoever wants the change, since the people exposed to the risk are not the people who profit from it. Marcus answered that this is just a device for preferring whatever already exists, that the current field is not risk-free either, and that the burden cannot be assigned merely by asking which option is new — the students who would lose practice time pay part of the cost of our uncertainty too, so shifting the burden is a choice about who absorbs that cost, not a neutral default. Priya then narrowed her principle: it only applies to a particular kind of risk, namely one that is serious, cumulative, borne by people who cannot consent, and hard to undo once the field is in the ground. Marcus said that this was the whole problem in miniature, since deciding whether the pellets fall into that category is precisely the scientific question everyone is fighting about, so the principle cannot guide us until we have already done the research it was supposed to spare us from doing. Priya came back with a concrete proposal: for any decision of this shape, describe the world in which the field is already installed and someone is proposing to dig it up and lay grass, then ask whether the arguments for going back sound weaker than the arguments for going forward — if they do, that is a sign we are protecting a default rather than applying a principle. Marcus said the test was a useful diagnostic but that it cuts against her, because in the reversed world the students who had been playing on turf for a decade would have their own reasons to keep it, which shows the default is not sacred in either direction, and that this is his point rather than hers. They stopped there and the club adviser moved on to the next item.

Could you go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about each specific point, rather than just who wins overall? I would particularly like to know which of Marcus's objections genuinely land and which are cheap shots, whether Priya's final test really does the work she thinks it does or whether Marcus has correctly turned it against her, and what both of them are missing — above all, whether there is a version of Priya's precautionary idea that survives everything Marcus said, and if so, exactly what it has to give up in order to survive.
