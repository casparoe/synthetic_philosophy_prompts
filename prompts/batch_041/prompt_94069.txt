I'm in the second term of an ancient philosophy survey and we've just hit the Epicureans. There's a stretch of the Letter to Menoeceus in our course reader that I've now read about six times without really getting it, and I have to say something intelligent about it in seminar on Thursay, so I'm hoping you can walk me through it. It's one of those old translations, so the English is stiff — here it is exactly as it appears in the reader:

"We must also reflect that of desires some are natural, others are groundless; and that of the natural some are necessary as well as natural, and some natural only. And of the necessary desires some are necessary if we are to be happy, some if the body is to be rid of uneasiness, some if we are even to live. He who has a clear and certain understanding of these things will direct every preference and aversion toward securing health of body and tranquillity of mind, seeing that this is the sum and end of a blessed life. For the end of all our actions is to be free from pain and fear, and, when once we have attained all this, the tempest of the soul is laid; seeing that the living creature has no need to go in search of something that is lacking, nor to look for anything else by which the good of the soul and of the body will be fulfilled. When we are pained because of the absence of pleasure, then, and then only, do we feel the need of pleasure. Wherefore we call pleasure the alpha and omega of a blessed life. Pleasure is our first and kindred good. It is the starting-point of every choice and of every aversion, and to it we come back, inasmuch as we make feeling the rule by which to judge of every good thing. And since pleasure is our first and native good, for that reason we do not choose every pleasure whatsoever, but ofttimes pass over many pleasures when a greater annoyance ensues from them. And ofttimes we consider pains superior to pleasures when submission to the pains for a long time brings us as a consequence a greater pleasure."

The thing is, our lecturer keeps calling this whole position 'tranquilism' — the idea that what ultimately matters is only the absence of craving and suffering, i.e. no pain, no fear, no restless wanting after things you lack — and says Epicurus is the classic source for it. But I can't make the passage line up with that. Two sentences in it seem to be pulling in opposite directions: on the one hand 'the end of all our actions is to be free from pain and fear', which sounds like the goal is a purely negative thing, an absence; on the other hand 'we call pleasure the alpha and omega of a blessed life', which sounds like pleasure is the goal, full stop. And then 'When we are pained because of the absence of pleasure, then, and then only, do we feel the need of pleasure' — I sort of see that he's saying that once you're not in pain you don't need anything further, but that seems like a huge claim, and I can't tell whether he's smuggling in the idea that not being in pain just is a pleasure (which feels like a trick, like he's defined his way out of a problem) or whether he really means the absence of pain and the presence of pleasure are two different things with different kinds of value.

So what I'd like is a careful reading of the passage: what is the actual argument running through it, sentence by sentence, and what is 'the tempest of the soul is laid' supposed to be describing — the cessation of pain, the cessation of craving, or both at once? Does 'fear' in 'free from pain and fear' belong on the suffering side of the ledger or the craving side? And given that he goes on to say that some desires are natural and necessary (you still have to eat, presumably), what could 'the absence of craving' even amount to — is the target only the 'groundless' desires, and if so is the tranquilist slogan just a loose paraphrase of something more modest? Last thing, and this is really what I'm trying to work out: does this passage actually commit him to tranquilism, or is my lecturer reading a modern slogan back into a text that is really just a version of hedonism — pleasure is the good, and tranquility is simply the name of the best or most reliable pleasure? I'd like to know which reading the wording actually supports and where the passage is genuinely ambiguous, because I suspect I'm definately going to get asked exactly this in seminar. I don't need a reading list, I just want the passage explained properly.
