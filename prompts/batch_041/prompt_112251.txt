I am writing a paper on whether video-game characters can be objects of moral concern, and the discussion keeps collapsing into a single line: 'it's only a game.' Most recently, when I suggested that a game which invites the player to inflict prolonged suffering on a named and characterised figure might be something worth objecting to, a colleague answered that it is only a game and treated the matter as closed. I am not persuaded, but I cannot say precisely what his remark was claiming, and I suspect it was doing several different jobs at once.

Please set out, as a menu rather than a single preferred reading, the distinct claims that 'it's only a game' could be making, and for each give a verdict -- trivial, substantive and defensible, or false -- together with a statement of whether it would actually support the use to which my colleague put it. The candidates I would like you to work through, along with any I have missed, are these:

- the events depicted are fictional rather than real, so nothing actually happened to anyone;
- nothing that happens within a game has consequences outside it, so no one's interests are affected;
- players consent to the constitutive rules of the game, so no move made within it can wrong them;
- games form a bounded domain with norms of their own, so ordinary moral appraisal does not reach inside them;
- the activity is undertaken for amusement, so its stakes are low and whatever happens in it matters less;
- only actual beings can be wronged, so no fictional character can be a victim and the only possible victims are real players;
- the correct description of what the player does is not 'inflicting suffering on X' but something like 'making a move in a game', and under that description no moral predicate applies at all;
- the player's responses are make-believe rather than genuine, so no real cruelty, or real enjoyment of cruelty, is present.

Then say which of these the phrase has to be relying on if it is to do the work it is normally asked to do, and whether any single reading can cover both of its standard uses: dismissing concern about what happens to fictional characters within a game, and dismissing concern about what players do to one another in and around games -- harassment, the deliberate ruin of another's play, cheating.

I would also like the readings tested against a constraint. Many of those who say 'it's only a game' are untroubled by a game that rewards the killing of characters, yet object strongly to a game that invites sexual violence against them, even though both are equally fictional and equally without consequence for anyone real. State whether that combination of attitudes is coherent, and if it is, which reading of the slogan accounts for it; if no reading does, say what else must be doing the work.

Give the answer as a numbered list of readings, each with its verdict and a sentence or two of argument, followed by a short section on the two standard uses and a short section on the asymmetry.
