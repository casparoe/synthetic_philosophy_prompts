Alright, longtime LW / Overcoming Bias lurker here, first time posting. I've basically beleived for years that Solomonoff induction is *the* formalization of Occam's razor — universal prior, all computable hypotheses weighted by 2^-K, mixture predictor, that's the whole game. I want you to try to actually break that belief rather than nod along. So: what would count against the claim that Solomonoff induction is the correct (normative, not just formal) formalization of Occam's razor?

Things I specifically want you to dig into, not just wave at:

- The invariance theorem only makes the prior machine-independent *up to* an additive constant in K, and that constant can be arbitrarily large. So is the universality/objectivity story doing real work, or is it a technicality? Could someone game it by picking a reference machine under which their pet hypothesis has a tiny description? Is that a genuine defeater or does it wash out?
- The dominance/convergence guarantees are stated as cumulative or eventual bounds — total error finite, but the bound scales with the complexity of the true hypothesis, which for anything real is astronomically large. Does that guarantee anything at all about the finite-data regime we actually live in, or is it an 'eventually' that never arrives? Concretely: if someone built a computable approximation that beat the universal mixture on every real-world dataset we've ever collected, would that count against the theory or just be exactly what you'd expect?
- Does the whole framework secretly presuppose a computable world, i.e. that the data are generated by some program? If so, is that a substantive empirical commitment that could turn out false, or a harmless idealization? What would the world have to look like for it to fail, and would we ever be able to tell from the inside?
- Occam's razor as people usually state it (don't multiply entities, prefer the simpler theory) vs. what Solomonoff actually formalizes (shortest program length under a fixed UTM). Are those the same thing? If not, does that undercut the claim that he formalized Occam, or is 'Occam' just a nickname that's stuck?
- Uncomputability / the halting problem. I don't care that it's uncomputable in practice — everyone says that. I care whether uncomputability makes it the wrong *normative* standard, e.g. because there's no fact of the matter about the right prior, or because the right idealization should have been something computable all along.

Then rank these by how much they should move someone sitting at ~0.8 that Solomonoff is the right formalization. Which would a defender just shrug off as already-accounted-for, and which are the ones that should actually lower the credence? And be honest with me: has the position been quietly insulated from disproof by retreating to 'well, it's the ideal limit' / 'it's about asymptotics'? If so, is that fatal for a normative claim, or actually fine?
