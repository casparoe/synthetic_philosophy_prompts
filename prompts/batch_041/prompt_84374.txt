For the better part of a year a small group of us — two philosophers of science, a cognitive scientist, and a pair of engineers who build interpretability tooling — have been meeting fortnightly to argue about whether trained neural networks represent anything, and more precisely about what kind of realism the interpretability literature is entitled to when it speaks of features, circuits, and concepts. Most of us arrived by way of the alignment-adjacent blogs, where realism about discovered features is often treated as though it had already been settled somewhere offstage, and I suspect that assumption is carrying a great deal of hidden weight. We have now decided to stop reading and start working, and we have roughly a year of slack to spend: a modest workshop budget, one visiting speaker, and perhaps a day a week from each of us. My question is which of the open conceptual questions in this area should absorb that year, and in what order. I am far less interested in an exhaustive inventory than in the shape of the argument: which questions sit upstream of which, which ones a small group could genuinely advance by thinking hard rather than by running more experiments, and what we might learn in the next year or two that would reorder the list.

To give you something to push against rather than a blank page, here are the candidates we keep circling. First, whether the success of a probe or a sparse autoencoder establishes that the network represents the feature recovered, or only that the feature is decodable from the activations, and whether that distinction is a substantive one or a muddle about what representation talk was ever for. Second, whether the relevant relation should be understood structurally, as some form of isomorphism or systematic co-variation, or functionally, in terms of the causal role an internal state plays downstream, and whether the two can come apart in a way that matters for practice. Third, whether there is any fact of the matter about the true features of a network given the non-identifiability of its basis under transformations that leave behaviour intact, and if there is not, what exactly the practice of feature discovery is reporting. Fourth, whether mechanistic interpretability needs the concept of representation at all, or whether an austere instrumentalism — content talk as a predictive stance rather than an inventory of inner causes — would serve the science better and cost nothing. Fifth, whether any of these can be settled before deciding what would count as representing in a system with no designer and no downstream consumer of the information, which strikes some of us as the real question and others as a category error.

What I want back is an agenda with an argument behind it, not a survey and not pointers into the literature. Rank the questions, and be explicit about the trade-offs you are making in doing so: how much weight you are giving to whether a question is upstream of others, how much to whether it is answerable by argument alone, and how much to whether its answer would change what interpretability researchers actually do on Monday morning. Say plainly which of our five candidates you would drop and why dropping it is cheap, which of them are the same question wearing different clothes, and what would have to turn out to be true for your ordering to come out the other way.
