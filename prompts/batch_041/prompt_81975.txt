I spent my working life as an ecologist and my retirement reading philosophy, where I have got stuck on the literature about convention. Two bodies of work have run in parallel for decades without meeting. One treats a convention as a regularity in behaviour that solves a coordination problem with several equally good solutions: the option people settle on must be salient to them in advance, each person conforms because she expects the others to, and each would have been content with a different regularity had that one been established instead. The other studies conventions as the output of imitation, reinforcement and selection, which generate stable, arbitrary regularities among agents who need not be rational, or informed about one another's expectations, at all. Each side says the other is not really discussing convention; and my own field supplies the puzzle in miniature, since arbitrary regularities of just this kind take hold in populations of animals that can hardly be said to expect anything of one another.

My hypothesis is that two questions are being asked under one word: what gives a regularity the standing of a convention, such that it is correct to say that people ought to follow it and not merely that they have reason to given what others do, and how such a regularity arises and persists. If that is right, much of the quarrel is verbal, though I doubt it is wholly verbal, since the two sides disagree about whether a regularity that no one could articulate or justify is a convention at all. Please diagnose rather than adjudicate: one question at two levels of idealisation, or two questions? Is the real issue whether common knowledge is constitutive of convention or merely typical of it? And what would count as progress — a formal result tying the dynamics to the analytical conditions, a distinction, a case — or should we expect the two literatures to keep passing each other by?
