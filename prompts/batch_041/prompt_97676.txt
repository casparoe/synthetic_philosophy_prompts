I am the daughter of a woman with mid-stage Alzheimer's disease, and next week I have to speak to the ethics committee at her care facility. Eight years ago, when she was fully competent, she signed an advance directive after watching her own mother die in a nursing home. The directive refuses life-prolonging treatment, including antibiotics for pneumonia and artificial nutrition, once she can no longer recognize her children or follow a conversation. It also states that she wants these instructions to hold even if she later protests, on the ground that the woman who protests will not be the woman who signed. Today she recognizes us, cannot follow a conversation, and says she wants to live; she enjoys music and seems content. The committee must decide whether to treat a pneumonia with antibiotics and possible hospitalization. I am not asking you to decide for me. I want to hear the strongest possible dialogue between the two views that are pulling me apart, so that I can see what each side actually has to say when it is not being caricatured.

You are a philosopher with expertise in personal identity, autonomy, and the ethics of advance directives. Think carefully about the strongest version of each position before you begin. Do not cite or name any real books, articles, or authors; no bibliography or reading list; invent no fake citations. Work only from the case and from the arguments you construct.

I want the dialogue to be organized around a distinction I have not seen put exactly this way, so treat it as your own analytic device: an advance directive can be read as a command issued by an author or as a forecast made by a forecaster. On the command reading, the earlier self wrote the script and the later self is a character in it; the earlier self's authority is the authority of authorship. On the forecast reading, the earlier self was predicting what she would want under conditions she could only imagine; a prediction can be falsified by what actually happens, and the later self's actual wants are evidence that the forecast was wrong. Let one speaker defend the command reading and the other the forecast reading. Let them press each other on whether the distinction is real or whether every command is just a forecast with authority borrowed from the author.

Both speakers should also address a feature of the directive I will stipulate: it contains what I will call a renegotiation clause. It allows the later self to change the directive only if the later self can understand the original reasons for it and state a coherent alternative. The present self cannot meet that condition. Is such a clause a genuine exercise of autonomy, or does it make the earlier self's authority unfalsifiable? Does it matter that the earlier self included it precisely because she feared a later self who would be unable to understand it?

Write a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers: A, who defends the authority of the earlier self, and B, who defends the authority of the present self. Neither is a strawman. They should respond to each other's actual points rather than talk past each other. Use my mother's case as the running example. They should discuss, at minimum: (i) whether psychological continuity, narrative continuity, or biological continuity is what grounds the authority of a preference; (ii) whether the present self's contentment is evidence of well-being or an adaptive preference produced by the very condition the earlier self feared; (iii) whether the earlier self's refusal is best modeled as a promise, a contract, or a self-binding precommitment, and what happens when the bound self cannot release the binder; (iv) whether the present self has autonomy at all, and whether that helps or hurts its claim; and (v) what third parties, including me, the caregivers, and the state, are owed. At least once, each speaker should make a concession that costs them something. At least once, each should ask the other a question the other cannot answer without giving up a premise.

Please structure your response with exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Brainstorming (150–250 words): the strongest one-sentence thesis for each side, and the two hardest questions each side must answer.

2. The dialogue (1,200–1,800 words): labeled A and B, with at least six substantive exchanges each. Keep the voices distinct and the argument moving.

3. Where they agree (150–200 words): what both sides should be able to accept, even if it does not settle the case.

4. The unresolved crux (200–300 words): the single question that divides them and cannot be settled by more facts.

5. What I should ask the ethics committee (150 words): two or three questions I can put to them, not a verdict.

Do not end with a synthesis or a recommendation. I want the disagreement at full strength.
