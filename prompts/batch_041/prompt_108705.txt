I teach a 90-minute session on affirmative action and quotas — 20 undergrads, mix of philosophy and public policy, all of them have loud opinions and almost none of them know how any real quota regime actually operates. I want a runnable activity where they sit on an admissions committee and find out for themselves that a quota isn't one thing: that the same number can work as a floor for one group and a ceiling for the same group, depending on where it binds. No lecture up front, don't name any of this until the debrief.

Here's my rough sketch, fix it where it's bad. Four tables of five. Each table is the admissions committee for the fictional flagship program at a fictional public university: 10 seats, 20 applicants on printed cards. Every card has a name, a score out of 100, a group label, and a couple of biographical lines. Make a few of the scores adjacent and noisy (78 vs 79, 81 vs 81) so the merit line feels arbitrary rather than sacred. Use invented group names, not real ones — I don't want them pattern-matching onto whatever fight they've already had on Twitter. Reveal the real-world analogues only at the end.

Round 1: rank by score, take the top ten. Easy, and they'll be smug about it. Round 2: at least 4 of the 10 seats go to Group B. Re-rank. Crucially, make each table record which named applicants gained and which lost a seat — I want them to see whose seat the floor was paid for, because it isn't the top of either group. Round 3: add that no group may hold more than 6 of the 10 seats. Now the floor they just defended has turned into a cap and the B applicants who were sitting at ranks 5 through 8 are out. That flip is the thing I want them to feel in their hands. Round 4: slip in a wildcard card — two applicants whose group membership is genuinely contestable, one with a single grandparent from the group, one with the label but a prep school and a family that's clearly comfortable. Tables have to decide and defend it. This is the beneficiary-definition problem and it should annoy them.

If there's time, a round 5: the table may exceed the 4-seat floor if it drafts a public justification that the other tables vote to accept or reject. Warn me about how to seed a rejection so the burden of explanation doesn't turn into a rubber stamp.

Give me: materials list, the exact wording of each rule as the tables receive it, a per-round timeline that fits 90 minutes with 25 left for debrief, what each table writes down, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto the floor/ceiling distinction, the incidence question, remedy-versus-diversity justifications, and the definitional problem. Then what will predictably go wrong the first time — I'm guessing someone checks out by saying this is just discrimination, and that the comply-or-explain round collapses into mush — plus how to handle each. And be honest about what the activity can't reproduce, especially the long-run stuff: who applies, who gets prepped, and the fact that in real institutions the score itself is manufactured upstream.
