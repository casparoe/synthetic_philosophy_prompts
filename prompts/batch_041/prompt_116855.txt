I'm a novelist — four books in, working on the fifth — and I've got myself tangled up in a metaphysics problem I can't shake loose. The new book is about a woman in her early fifties who's diagnosed with early-onset dementia. She's a planner, a control freak, and years before anyone suspected anything she'd signed one of those advance directives, the kind where you set out what you want done if you ever lose the ability to decide for yourself. Now she's lost it, more or less, and the family's at war over her: her daughter wants to follow the document to the letter, her husband thinks the woman sitting in front of him is a different person now, someone who deserves a say in her own remaining life. And the practice around all this — the law, the hospitals, the way people actually behave — seems to sit on both sides of that fence at once, which is what's got under my skin.

Here's what I'd like from you, and it's a genuine philosophy question, not a plot question. When we honour an advance directive — when we let the earlier, competent person's wishes govern what happens to the later, incapacitated one — what does that practice presuppose about personal identity over time? Does it presuppose the endurantist picture, that a person is wholly present at every moment of her life, so that the woman who signed the form is literally one and the same person as the woman who now bats the pills away? Or is it compatable with the perdurantist picture, where a person is more like a long four-dimensional object stitched together out of momentary temporal parts, so the earlier signer and the later patient are different parts of the same thing rather than the same thing through and through?

Reconstruct the implicit theory for me. What does the practice only make sense under, which rival views does it quietly rule out, and do its different bits actually presuppose the same thing? Because I keep noticing the practice is wierdly selective — we let the past self bind the present one on medical treatment, but we don't let her bind the present self on, say, whom she can marry or whether she can still vote, and the demented woman keeps her house and her name and her husband as if nothing had changed at all. Is that an inconsistency, or is it coherent under some view I'm not seeing? Tell me plainly where the practice is underdetermined, such that an endurantist and a perdurantist could both sign off on it for entirely different reasons. And then tell me what would have to change in the practice if we swapped the view it actually leans on for the other one — what would honoring the directive look like, concretely, on the other picture.

Don't give me reading lists or citations; I don't need the literature, I need the arguement. I don't want the usual autonomy-versus-welfare lecture either, I can get that anywhere — I want the identity question taken seriously. And write it in American English, please. The book's set in Ohio and I've got enough voices in my head already.
