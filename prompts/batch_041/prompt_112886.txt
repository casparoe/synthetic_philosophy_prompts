I sit on the research staff of a national asylum appeals tribunal, and I have been asked to forecast how the tribunal will resolve a case now pending before it, on the strength of the tribunal's own decided cases. The relevant track record, in summary:

- Protection granted to a claimant persecuted by the state's political police for organizing an opposition party.
- Protection granted to a claimant pursued by a religious militia in a district where the police were outnumbered and had effectively withdrawn.
- Protection denied to a claimant extorted by a criminal gang that also preyed on his neighbors; the police answered complaints, and the gang had no political, religious, or ethnic aim.
- Protection granted to a woman subjected to severe violence by her partner, in a country where the police routinely declined to intervene, on the finding that women in that country share a common immutable characteristic.
- Protection denied to a claimant from a district of generalized civil-war violence who was not singled out in any way.
- Protection granted to a claimant set upon by a mob for his religion while the police stood by and did nothing.
- Protection denied to a claimant who faced prosecution for evading a military draft, on the finding that the law applied to everyone and the prosecution was not aimed at his opinions.
- Protection denied to a claimant whose feared harm was prolonged destitution, on the finding that economic hardship, however severe, is not persecution.

The pending case: the claimant refused to sell his farmland to a business consortium with ties to a regional political faction. The consortium's armed wing burned his farm, killed a relative, and is now hunting him. The local police are unable to protect him -- the militia outguns them -- but there is no indication that the militia cares about his opinions, religion, ethnicity, or any group he belongs to; it wants the land. The state to which he has fled also argues that it should not be the one to receive him, because he passed through a neighboring country en route, and that country would admit him.

I would like your forecast of the tribunal's decision, together with the reasoning I should expect. More useful to me than a bare yes-or-no is the following: state the two or three rules that fit the decided cases equally well; say what each rule predicts for the pending case; tell me which rule you think the tribunal is actually running, and why; and name the further case -- a variation on the facts above, or a new one entirely -- that would best force the tribunal to reveal which rule it is using. Finally, address the secondary question the state has raised: on the tribunal's own precedents, does the claimant's mere transit through a third country relieve the receiving state of responsibility, or do those precedents commit the tribunal to a position on that question at all?
