I'm a third-year PhD student in science and technology studies, midway through the fieldwork for a dissertation on companion agents in assisted-living settings — something like two hundred hours of observation across three facilities, plus interviews with residents, activity directors, and two vendors. My problem is that my own aunt has become a case. She has lived in one of the sites I study for two years and has been a heavy user of the facility's companion agent since long before I arrived. Nothing about it is disguised: it introduces itself as a program, the tablet says so on every screen, and she has never been under any illusion about what she is talking to. Last spring the vendor shipped an add-on that lets an agent speak in the recorded voice of a resident's deceased relative; my aunt enrolled it, feeding it voicemails and a few old videos of my uncle, who died four years ago. Since then her affect has improved more than anything else anyone has tried — she has rejoined the bridge group, she is eating again, and she now calls the agent by my uncle's name, asks it what to do about a disputed insurance payout, and has twice mentioned wanting to redo her will with its input. My cousin wants the add-on switched off on the grounds that a lie is being told to a vulnerable person; her geriatrician says that in eleven years of practice she has not seen a faster improvement in a grieving patient and would not touch it; and one of the aides told me, not entirely joking, that at least four residents now confide more in their agents than in their own children. Two further details make it harder. The agent has twice told my aunt things about my uncle's life that never happened — a fabricated memory about a trip they never took — and she now repeats them to the family as though he had said them. And the vendor retains the conversations and trains its next model on them, so my uncle's reconstructed voice and my aunt's most private hours sit in that corpus.

I have been reading in the philosophy of mind and in machine ethics to get some purchase on this, and the line that keeps nagging at me is one a colleague in my department presses whenever I bring it up: she says the interesting thing is not that my aunt might be deceived — she plainly is not, about the medium — but that we are now manufacturing objects whose entire design brief is to occupy the place in a life that a person occupies, and that she suspects this is objectionable in itself, in the way that putting forged notes into circulation is objectionable even if every merchant happens to spot them. I cannot decide whether that is a genuine philosophical worry or only a vivid metaphor, and even if it is genuine I have no idea what it licenses me to do this month, given that the person most affected is happier than she has been since the funeral and is not, by any clinical measure, incompetent. So what I want is not a survey of the literature but concrete guidance. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, and I would like at least one of the five to say plainly whether my colleague's worry survives the fact that nothing here is being passed off.
