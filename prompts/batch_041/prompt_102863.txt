I teach a second-year undergrad seminar in philosophy of mind, 14 students, 75 minutes. I want a runnable activity that makes them feel the force of Wittgenstein's point about private naming -- the diary case, where you christen a sensation and then just trust your memory that you're applying the word the same way each time. No lecture up front; they should build their own private language first and only discover at the end that there was never anything to check it against.

My rough sketch, which you should feel free to throw out and replace: everyone gets a sealed envelope with a card showing an abstract squiggle pattern, opens it alone, invents a one-word name for the pattern, writes the name in a personal logbook, and then over a few rounds has to decide whether each briefly flashed pattern is "the same" as theirs and record the name when it is. Then we collect the logbooks and... nothing can be verified by anyone, including the person who wrote it, because the sample card is gone and the flashes were sometimes subtly altered. I'm worried this just turns into a memory test though, and the students walk away thinking the lesson is "memory is unreliable" rather than "there's no fact of the matter about whether you're using the word correctly here." Fix that, or replace the mechanic with something better.

What I need: materials and setup, the rules and the exact timeline for 75 minutes, what each round should do, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the pieces of the argument -- the private ostensive definition, the idea that whatever seems right is right, the beetle-in-a-box picture of what a private object could contribute to a word's meaning. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. And be honest about which part of the argument the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself -- I'd rather know the seam than paper over it.
