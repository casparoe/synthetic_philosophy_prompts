I'm a philosophy of biology PhD student and I'm in the weeds of a chapter on what is life — definitions, cluster concepts, the whole mess. My problem is that every source I read uses the same four examples (virus, prion, fire, crystal) and then hands down a one-word verdict, and I've stopped being able to tell which verdicts are arguments and which are just vibes. So instead of another essay, rate some cases for me.

For each item below give me a number from 0 to 1 for how alive it is — 0 = definately not alive, 1 = definitely alive, 0.5 = I honestly can't call it — plus one sentence naming the single feature that's doing all the work in that verdict. Then sort the whole list into tiers. Don't wriggle out of it by saying it depends on the definition; if it depends, pick a definition, say which one, and score under that.

The cases:

- an ordinary bacterium (anchor)
- a virus: genome in a protein shell, can only copy itself by hijacking a cell's machinery
- a virophage: a tiny virus that can only replicate inside the replication factory a bigger virus has built inside a cell
- a prion: a misfolded protein that makes other copies of the same protein misfold; no nucleic acid, no metabolism
- a plasmid: a loop of DNA that copies itself inside a bacterium and can hop into other bacteria, but builds nothing on its own
- a mitochondrion: has its own genome and divides on its own schedule, but most of its proteins come from the host cell's genes and it dies outside the cell
- a transmissible cancer: a clone of cells that spreads from animal to animal within a species (dogs have one, Tasmanian devils have another), evolves, has its own genome, and is arguably one lineage that's been going for centuries
- a lab protocell: a fatty acid vesicle with a self-replicating RNA inside that evolves under selection in a test tube, but only because a grad student feeds it and resets the conditions
- a crystal in a supersaturated solution that keeps seeding new crystals identical to itself: no variation, no metabolism, no boundary it maintains
- a self-replicating program in a simulated world: mutates, competes for memory and CPU time, no chemistry at all
- a stripped-down synthetic bacterium whose genome has been cut to the few hundred genes it needs to grow and divide in a rich broth, so it can't make most of its own building blocks
- a bacterium with its DNA removed that carries on metabolising and keeping its membrane intact for a while
- fire (anchor)

Two more things. First, I have a hunch the real dividing line isn't metabolism but whether the thing has its own evolutionary lineage — a history of descent with modification that selection acts on. So score the whole list a second time on that criterion alone, and tell me exactly which cases flip order between the two scorings, because I think the flips are where the interesting philosophy lives. Second, give me a single number: what fraction of the list do you think a room of biologists would actually agree is alive, and why that number rather than higher or lower.

Finally, tell me whether a 0 to 1 scale is even the right shape for this. If aliveness is a cluster property rather than a degree, say so and tell me what I should be asking instead.
