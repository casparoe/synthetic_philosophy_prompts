I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and for several months I have been reporting a long feature on guilt, shame and the moral life of societies. The reporting has gone well; the argument I keep having with my sources has not, and I want your help diagnosing why it will not resolve, rather than adjudicating it. Please act as an expert on the philosophy and anthropology of the emotions — someone at home in moral philosophy, cultural psychology and the ethnography of honour — and treat this as a piece of conceptual diagnosis rather than a survey. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming the possible diagnoses before committing to one.

The question I put to everyone is this: which is the better moral emotion, guilt or shame, and can a culture deliberately choose between them? Three camps have formed, and after a year of conversations none has moved.

The first camp holds that guilt is better, for reasons that sound almost like design specifications. Its object is an act, so it is answerable to reasons; it can be discharged by apology, restitution or changed conduct; it does not require an audience; and it treats the person as an agent who did something rather than a thing that is something. Shame, on this account, is either an instrument of social control or a wound to the self, and what it reliably produces is concealment, withdrawal and sometimes rage rather than repair.

The second camp holds that shame is at least as good, perhaps better. Its object is the whole self, and that is a virtue rather than a defect, because morality is not exhausted by discrete acts. It is relational through and through, even when the relevant others have been internalised, and it is the emotion that makes someone care about being the kind of person who does not do that. Guilt, on this view, is the thin bookkeeping emotion of a society that has already converted its obligations into transactions. Some of my sources in this camp point to traditions in which shame is a capacity to be cultivated rather than a pathology to be cured.

The third camp says the comparison is malformed: you cannot rank two things when the terms are the folk-psychological labels of one particular historical moment, and when the famous contrast between guilt cultures and shame cultures was a mid-century construction that never carried much evidential weight.

The second half of my question divides my sources just as sharply. Some insist that cultures do choose, and point to the machinery: catechism, schooling, honour codes, rituals of public apology and public disgrace. Others say that choosing an emotion is like choosing to find a joke funny — you can arrange the conditions under which it appears, but the arranging is not the choosing, and what looks like a culture selecting its emotions is usually a set of institutions selecting which emotions are permitted expression.

Here is my hypothesis, and I would like you to treat it as a hypothesis rather than a premise. I suspect the parties are not disagreeing about emotions at all, but about two different objects that both carry the name 'shame': the episodic feeling of exposure that a particular person has, and the standing social arrangement that decides what is exposed, to whom, and with what consequences. If that is right, most of the normative heat is about the arrangement and most of the empirical work is about the episode, and the two sides talk past each other while believing they contradict each other.

What I want from you:

First, a diagnosis. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about what the words pick out, a disagreement about the criterion — about what 'better' is even supposed to mean here, whether accuracy, motivational efficacy, respect for persons or the stability of an order — a hidden empirical question, or two different questions being answered under one heading? Lay out at least three candidate diagnoses, say what each would predict about the shape of the argument, and tell me which you find strongest and why.

Second, test my hypothesis rather than assuming it. If it is wrong, tell me what the real axis of disagreement is.

Third, diagnose the 'can a culture choose?' half separately. I suspect the two halves are entangled, because the case for one emotion being better may depend on a picture of how emotions are acquired that the other camp rejects. Tell me whether that is so.

Fourth, given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a piece of evidence, a reframing, or an admission that no progress should be expected — and what a good journalist should stop asking.

Please do not give me a literature survey, a reading list or citations; I have my own sources and no way to check yours, and I would rather you argued from the positions as I have described them. Write in British English. Be direct about where you are uncertain, and if you think the question is ill-posed, say so and tell me what the well-posed version would be.
