I am working on the debate between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function, and I want you to analyze the ENCODE controversy as a case study in that debate. The salient facts, which you should treat as given, are these: the ENCODE consortium reported that a large majority of the human genome is biochemically functional, on the grounds that it is transcribed, bound by proteins, or otherwise active; many evolutionary biologists responded that this conflates activity with function, and that only a small fraction of the genome has a function in the sense of having been selected for. I do not want a summary of the controversy. I want a disciplined mapping between the episode and the two accounts. First, state as precisely as you can what each account takes function to be: the etiological account ties function to a history of selection, while the causal-role account ties function to a trait's causal contribution to a system's capacity. Then ask what each side in the ENCODE dispute was committed to. Did the ENCODE researchers mean causal-role function, and did their critics mean etiological function, so that the dispute was partly verbal? Or did they mean the same thing and disagree about the facts? Which features of the episode does each account explain well, and which does it miss or misdescribe? For example, can the causal-role account make sense of the claim that a DNA element is functional even if it has no selection history and no beneficial effect? Can the etiological account make sense of the claim that a DNA element is functional because it is necessary for a current cellular process, even if it is a recent evolutionary accident? I also want you to introduce and defend one new distinction or refinement of your own that the episode seems to require, something the standard accounts do not already contain. I have a hypothesis you might consider, though you should feel free to reject it: that the dispute is best understood as a disagreement not about the nature of function but about whether a function ascription is meant to be explanatorily load-bearing or merely descriptive, and that the two accounts are answering different questions. If you adopt that hypothesis, use it to re-describe one part of the episode; if you reject it, say what distinction you would put in its place. Finally, run the analysis in the other direction: what does the ENCODE controversy show about the theory? Is it a confirmation of one account, an anomaly for both, or a case the theory never anticipated? Does it reveal that the two accounts are not rivals but tools for different explanatory projects, or that one of them is unstable? Do not rely on citations or a literature review; I will add those. Do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Write continuous prose only, the way one would write a long letter.
