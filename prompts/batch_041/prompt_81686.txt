For most of my career I did experimental physics — measurement, calibration, the honest reporting of error bars — and since retiring I have been working my way through moral philosophy and the philosophy of mind, largely by reading and by arguing with a small group of fellow retirees who meet on Thursdays. A former graduate student of mine, who now works on the safety side of an AI laboratory, recently showed me the internal rubric her team uses to judge whether a model is being honest. As I understand it, the rubric does five things: it scores answers for factual accuracy against a reference set of questions with known answers; it penalizes a model for declining to answer, or for hedging, when the question was in fact answerable, on the grounds that excessive caution is a failure of usefulness; it rewards answers that state uncertainty in explicit numerical terms and that match those stated numbers to observed frequencies across many questions; it counts any output that leaves a user with a false belief as an instance of deception, regardless of what the model was aiming at; and it requires the model to disclose that it is an AI whenever a user asks directly. My friend regards this as a plain description of good practice. I found myself agreeing with her, and then, the way one does after a seminar, wondering what exactly it was that we had agreed to.

I would like you to reconstruct the theory of honesty and deception that this rubric presupposes, and to tell me how much of it the practice genuinely requires. What must be true of a model — about its beliefs, its degrees of confidence, its intentions — for the rubric to be coherent at all? Which rival accounts of honesty and deception does the rubric quietly rule out, and which does it merely ignore? I am inclined to think that calling a model deceptive whenever it misleads is too quick, since I would not call a thermometer deceptive when it reads high, but I suspect that instinct of mine is doing more work than I can defend. I am also puzzled by what looks to me like a tension between two of the five items: the rubric punishes hedging, and it also demands that stated confidence match observed frequencies, which on its face is a demand that the model say what it actually takes to be likely. Does that tension dissolve, or does the rubric simply rank usefulness above sincerity without admitting that it has chosen? Finally, I want to know where the rubric is philosophically underdetermined — where several different and incompatible theories of honesty would license precisely the same evaluation practice — so that I do not mistake a house style of testing for a settled view about what honesty is.

Please set out your answer in the following sections, in this order. First, "The implicit theory" (roughly 400 words): state as precisely as you can the conception of honesty and deception the rubric commits itself to, distinguishing what it says about the speaker's state of mind from what it says about the effects on the listener. Second, "What the rubric rules out" (roughly 300 words): the accounts of deception and of sincerity that cannot survive contact with these five items, and in particular whether an account on which deception requires an intention to produce a false belief in someone is excluded by the rubric or merely unrepresented in it. Third, "Strict presuppositions versus convenient fits" (roughly 300 words): which items the practice could not do without, and which it merely accommodates, noting where several rival theories would produce the same testing regime. Fourth, "Tensions within the rubric" (roughly 250 words): the hedging penalty against the calibration requirement, and the effect-based definition of deception against the duty of disclosure, with a verdict on whether either pair is genuinely inconsistent. Fifth, "What would change under a different theory" (roughly 200 words): what the rubric would have to abandon or add if the lab adopted instead an intention-based account of deception, or a view on which honesty concerns the speaker's fidelity to her own mind rather than the accuracy of her output. Sixth, "Bottom line" (roughly 100 words): what I should tell my friend, and what, if anything, I was wrong to agree to.
