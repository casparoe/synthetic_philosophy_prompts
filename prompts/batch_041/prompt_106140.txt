I am a professor of philosophy whose research is in formal epistemology, and for the past three years I have chaired a standing scientific advisory committee — twelve members, mostly epidemiologists, engineers and two clinicians, none of them trained in probability beyond an introductory course — that meets fortnightly to produce a single agreed probability for each of a handful of contested quantities, which we then pass to the ministry that funds us. The recurring difficulty is never disagreement about priors; it is that almost nothing we learn arrives as a clean fact. A member reads out a colleague's preliminary analysis and reports that the colleague is "about 70% confident"; a draft report is leaked and we must decide what to make of it, given that we cannot verify it and do not know who wrote it or why; two members independently summarise the same unpublished dataset and we must somehow avoid double-counting it; someone forwards a preprint that reports a p-value and we have to decide what, if anything, that licenses. At present each member adjusts their own credence by feel and we average, which I suspect is incoherent in ways I can name but cannot yet repair. I know the apparatus for updating on uncertain evidence exists — Jeffrey conditionalization, probability kinematics, the rigidity condition, and the alternative of treating every report as a noisy signal and conditioning on the fact that the report was made rather than on its content — but I have never seen it turned into something a committee can actually run in the twenty minutes we have between agenda items, and my attempts to explain the difference between the evidence proposition and the observation have not landed with anyone.

Please act as an expert in Bayesian epistemology who is also practised at converting normative principles into protocols that intelligent non-specialists can follow. Begin by thinking carefully and setting out a short brainstorm of what the theory genuinely requires and where the real choices lie, before you write anything prescriptive. Then give me the procedure itself: the questions the chair should put to the committee in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what to do when the input is a person's stated credence rather than a frequency or a likelihood, how to handle two reports of one underlying piece of evidence, and, crucially, where the procedure must stop and hand the matter over to judgment because the question is one of values, of who bears the burden of error, or of how to reconcile heterogeneous priors. Be explicit throughout about which steps are genuine constraints of the theory and which are conveniences I am smuggling in. Then run the procedure on three cases: a member's "70% confident" summary of a colleague's unpublished analysis; a leaked draft whose provenance and motive we cannot establish; and a preprint reporting one small study whose result contradicts our current estimate. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like — I would far rather find out now than in the meeting — and tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do, and how it will misfire when what has arrived is not really evidence about a partition we have already fixed. Write for a reader who is intelligent but not a probabilist: no formulas without a plain-English gloss, no appeals to literature I would have to go and look up, and no hedging about the fact that some of the residual work is mine.
