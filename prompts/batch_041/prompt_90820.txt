So I have to give a toast at my sister Maya's wedding on Saturday, and the seminar I'm taking this term has kind of ruined my ability to do it the normal way. It's a course on signaling and cooperation — last week was the handicap principle, the idea that a display only proves anything if it's expensive enough that a faker couldn't afford to fake it. Act like someone who knows this literature cold and also actually knows how to write a toast, and think carefully before you commit to an approach.

Here's the situation. About six years ago Maya's fiancé Dev turned down a funded PhD offer abroad to move to the city where she had a job, and he lived in my parents' garage apartment for two years. My aunt Renata lent him $9,000 for the move and he never quite payed it back. And then there's the thing the whole family finds ridiculous: every year on the anniversary of the day they met, the two of them drive four hours each way to eat at a diner in a town neither of them is from, then drive back the same night. Nobody can explain why. They call it "the loop." Aunt Renata calls it a waste of a perfectly good Saturday, which, honestly, it is.

There will be about 80 people there: our family, most of whom still think Dev is definately a flake, and their friends, a bunch of econ grad students who will visibly wince if I say the word "signal."

I want the toast to get the room to see that the waste is the point — that the loop and the move and the garage apartment are the only kind of proof two people can actually give each other, because anything cheaper could be faked. But I can't just say their sacrifices prove their love, and I can't act like the sacrifices were free: some of that cost landed on Aunt Renata and my parents, not on Dev, and if I gloss over that she'll be furious and she'll be right. And I really can't justify any of it by how it turned out, because "and look, it all worked out" is the exact argument I'm trying not to make.

Please start by brainstorming two or three possible spines for the toast — the one idea that would run through the whole thing — and say which you'd pick and why. Then write it: five minutes max, American English, nothing that sounds like a lecture, and it has to land for Aunt Renata and the econ students in the same room.

Then add a short note just for me, separate from the toast: where is this reading most likely to be wrong? What would change your mind about whether their sacrifices were really a signal of anything, as opposed to two people just making each other's lives harder for no reason? And where does the toast take liberties with the actual theory?
