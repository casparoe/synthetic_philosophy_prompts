Compose a deadpan institutional press release of roughly 700–900 words, issued by a fictional national research agency, announcing the results of a long-running programme in what is sometimes called indoor ornithology: the study of non-black objects that are not ravens — shoes, chiefly — as evidence for the hypothesis that all ravens are black. Invent the agency, the laboratory, the personnel, the grant numbers, and the dates. Do not allude to real institutions, real papers, or real researchers, and include no references of any kind. The release must remain straight-faced from first line to last: no winking, no authorial aside, no sentence that concedes the joke. Its humour should come entirely from the epistemology being exactly right, so that a reader who works on confirmation theory laughs rather than winces, while a reader who has never encountered the paradox can still follow the piece as the story of an agency that has found an absurdly cheap way to do science and cannot be talked out of it.

Let the release argue its case in the terms the paradox actually supplies. Its stated rationale should be Nicod's criterion together with Hempel's equivalence condition: the hypothesis that all ravens are black is logically equivalent to the hypothesis that all non-black things are non-ravens; a white shoe is an instance of the second; therefore the shoe confirms the first. Follow that rationale where it genuinely leads rather than to a caricature of it. It should yield, among other things: an argument that the programme is far more cost-effective than fieldwork on ravens, since ravens are scarce and shoes are abundant, and so generates confirming instances at an enormous multiple of the rate achieved by conventional ornithology; a claim that the same item of footwear, by the same logic, confirms hypotheses that cannot all be true together — have the same shoes confirming that all ravens are black and that all ravens are green, and treat this as evidence of the method's robustness rather than a difficulty for it; and a precedent drawn from chemistry, in which an ice cube held in a colourless flame is taken to confirm that all sodium salts burn yellow, offered by the agency as proof that its reasoning is established practice rather than a novelty.

Two voices should press against the agency's self-congratulation without the release ever conceding that they are right. An internal statistician, quoted respectfully as a valued colleague, should report that the programme's evidence raises the probability of the hypothesis by an amount he has had to express in scientific notation, on the ground that a white shoe is nearly as likely to be observed whether the hypothesis is true or false; the release should present this figure as a triumph of rigour. A rival laboratory, whose work consists of looking at ravens, should object that the programme has misdescribed its own sample space: what an observation confirms depends on how the observation was generated, and an object drawn at random from the world and found to be a non-black non-raven is very weak evidence, whereas an object drawn from the class of non-black things and found not to be a raven would be strong evidence; the release should treat this as a technicality of survey design and return, undismayed, to counting instances. Let the release also register, without settling, the question of what the hypothesis is even about — whether it quantifies over every object in the universe, in which case footwear is fair game, or over ravens, in which case the programme is quietly answering a different question from the one it advertises.

Close with the agency's forward plan: a larger grant, an expanded catalogue of non-black non-ravens, and an appendix offering the same footwear as confirmation that all emeralds are green and, in a companion table, that all emeralds are grue — a white shoe being neither green nor blue, hence not grue, and not an emerald, hence an instance of all non-grue things are non-emeralds. Keep the register bureaucratic, confident, and unruffled; avoid exclamation, sarcasm, and any sentence that explains its own joke.
