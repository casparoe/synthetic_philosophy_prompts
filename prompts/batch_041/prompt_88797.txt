I serve on the ethics advisory panel of the Lesedi Community Health Trust, a non-profit that runs HIV care and prevention programmes in a region of South Africa where ubuntu is not a line on a brochure but a working idiom: people describe a person as a person through other persons, and a household, rather than an individual, is often what shows up at a clinic, negotiates care, and absorbs a diagnosis. The clinical programme reflects this. When a client tests positive, the counsellor's protocol treats disclosure to a named treatment supporter, usually a relative, as part of the care plan rather than an optional extra; the supporter is enrolled alongside the client, given the same adherence training, and the household is the unit the programme follows up. A client who declines a supporter is still offered care but is routed to a lower-intensity adherence track and coded as unsupported. The research arm, by contrast, runs an observational cohort on treatment outcomes under strictly individual procedures: written consent from the participant alone, a confidentiality rule under which any staff disclosure of a participant's status to a relative is a reportable breach, and a data governance policy in which the participant owns her data and may withdraw it.

The panel has been asked to advise on a proposal to bring research into line with care: household consent in addition to individual consent, a household-level benefit-sharing arrangement, and a relational confidentiality rule under which a participant's supporter may be told results bearing on the supporter's own health, with or without the participant's agreement. Some members see this as extending the Trust's existing ethic; others see a communitarian paternalism being smuggled into a setting where the individual's interest in controlling information about her own body is the whole point.

Reconstruct what the existing practices already presuppose. Treating the clinical disclosure protocol and the individual-consent research protocol as two practices of one institution, say what conception of the person each requires or merely fits with, whether they are in genuine tension or presuppose compatible things at different levels of description, and where each is underdetermined among the views that would explain it. Be careful to separate a conception in which relations constitute the person — so that harm to a person is ipso facto a change in her relations, and the relevant unit of consent or standing is partly the relation — from a weaker conception in which relations merely shape a person's formation and capacities while the individual remains the unit of moral concern, and from a merely paternalistic or beneficence-driven rationale that could generate the same protocol with no relational claim at all. Then assess the proposal: which conception it adds, which it drops, and what follows for supporter, client, and household if the panel adopts it.
