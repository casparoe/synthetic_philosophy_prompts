'The chapter claims that influence comes in two kinds. Persuasion addresses a person's reason: you offer her considerations, she weighs them, and whatever she decides is genuinely hers. Manipulation addresses everything else — her emotions, her habits, her cognitive shortcuts — and thereby gets her to act without deliberating on the merits. Because manipulation bypasses a person's agency in this way, it is wrong as such, and a wrong of that kind cannot be traded off against good consequences. On this view, advertising that works through imagery rather than argument is manipulation, and so is a physician who stresses a treatment's survival rate instead of its mortality rate; both remain objectionable even when they move people toward things that are good for them. Manipulation is thus the middle term between persuasion, which respects autonomy, and coercion, which overrides it outright.'
