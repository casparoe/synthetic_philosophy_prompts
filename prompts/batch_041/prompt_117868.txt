I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I keep tripping over one sentence in the fights I write about: "accountability, not punishment." I heard it again last month, when a conference withdrew an invitation to a researcher over misconduct allegations that were several years old, and the organizers said they weren't punishing him, they were holding him accountable — as though that distinction settled the ethics of the case. I don't believe it settles anything, but I can't say precisely why, and I'd like to be able to. Lay out the distinct claims that sentence could be making — the terminological one (that "punishment" just means formal state sanction, so anything else is by definition not punishment), the conceptual one (that punishment involves hard treatment imposed for a past wrong, while accountability involves being made to answer for it), the normative one (that the response must be justified by repair, deterrence, or the standing of those wronged rather than by desert), the procedural one (that accountability is proportionate, revisable, and can come to an end, whereas punishment fixes a status) — plus any others worth separating, as a list I can take to the next panel, and for each reading tell me whether it marks a real distinction, restates a platitude, or functions as a dodge that evades exactly the questions punishment raises. Then say which reading the organizers most likely had in mind, whether it actually supports the conclusion they drew, and what the strongest defensible version of their position would look like.
