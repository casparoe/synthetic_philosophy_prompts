I'm running a 75-minute session for my philosophy of well-being seminar, about 18 undergrads, and I want them to actually feel the pull of tranquilism — the view that the only thing intrinsically good is the absence of craving and suffering, and that pleasure and satisfied desire are at best neutral. Don't name the view until the debrief; I want them to walk into the uncomfortable place on their own.

Here's the rough mechanic, fix it if it's broken. Everyone gets a craving ledger: three cravings at intensity 2, and your suffering score is the sum of active intensities. Five rounds, 2 action tokens per round. Options: (a) satisfy a craving — 1 token, it goes quiet this round and you bank a "jolt" point, but next round it's back at intensity +1; (b) sit with a craving / let it go — 1 token, drop its intensity by 1, gone at 0; (c) chase a new pleasure — 1 token, +2 jolt points now, but a fresh intensity-2 craving appears next round. Lowest final suffering score wins the main thing, and I want to dangle a small separate prize for most jolt points so there's a real tug-of-war. Optional social layer if it fits: if someone ends a round with more jolt points than you, your craving intensities tick up by 1 (comparison breeds craving). Keep two flavors of "pleasure" distinct in the bookkeeping — the loud jolt of satisfying, and the quiet ease of not needing — because the reveal is that only the second kind ends up mattering.

Give me rules and materials, a round-by-round timeline, a debrief that maps what happened onto the view (the treadmill, why satisfaction didn't actually quiet anything, the asymmetry they felt between the two prizes, the objection that this makes life bleak or ranks a placid life above a striving one, and how a tranquilist answers that), what will predictably blow up the first run — I'd bet someone just does nothing and wins and the room feels cheated — and an honest note on what the game can't show, e.g. that it bakes in the assumption that satisfaction regenerates craving (an empirical claim) and that no game settles whether pleasure is intrinsically good.
