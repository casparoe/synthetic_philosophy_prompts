ok so my friend in debate club is obsessed with these arguments where instead of proving something, you show that you already had to assume it just to think or ask the question at all. he calls them transcendental arguments, which i guess means you get the answer for free out of the fact that you can think at all. here's the one he gave me about the world outside my head. i typed it out how he said it:

(1) If I want to doubt that there's a world outside my mind, I have to be able to think the thought "maybe there isn't."
(2) A thought only means something if it's about something, and if it could be true or false.
(3) A thought can only be true or false about something if there's a way things are that doesn't depend on my thinking it.
(4) So doubting the outside world already assumes there's a world that doesn't depend on me.
(5) So the doubt eats itself and I can't even seriously ask whether the outside world is real.

I actually like where this lands, and (1) and (2) seem bulletproof to me, I dont see how you could even doubt without thinking a thought. The problem is he says you can swap the words out and get the same free answer for basically anything. Swap in "other people have minds" and it works. Swap in "the future will keep resembling the past" and it works. He even says you can do it for God, because to think about the world at all you have to assume something made your mind and the world match up. If the same five steps prove all that, then something in the steps has to be broken, and I want to know what.

So which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me? I'd really rather not lose (1) or (2), and honestly I'd like to keep the outside world conclusion too if that's on the table.
