My friend Dave and I spent like three hours last night going back and forth about killing vs letting die and I want to settle it with an actual decision tree instead of just repeating our two favorite moves at each other. Every time I tried to say what the difference is, he said thats just the difference between moving and not moving, who cares, and honestly I didnt have a comeback, which is why I want the whole thing written out.

So heres the ask. Build me a tree where every fork is a question about a concrete case that Dave could answer without already knowing which position his answer lands him in, and every leaf is a named position on whether doing harm and allowing harm are morally different. Ten to fourteen leaves, and dont let any path go more than five questions deep or he wont finish it. Give the leaves descriptive names (the flat no-difference view, the normal-course view, that kind of thing), not names of philosophers, and dont quote anyone or hand me a reading list. I dont care who said what, I want the logic of the space. One sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment, the case or implication that makes that view wince.

Leaves that have to be in there whether or not your tree produces them on its own:
- the flat no-difference view, where the only thing that matters is whether things go worse overall and doing vs allowing is at best a flag that happens to correlate with it
- the rights view, where negative duties not to interfere are stricter than positive duties to help and that asymmetry is the whole content of the distinction
- the harmful-agency view, where what's special about doing harm is that you direct it at someone while allowing is failing to shield them, so the agency part does the work and not the grammar of action vs inaction
- the intention view, where the real line is intended vs merely foreseen harm and doing vs allowing is a leaky proxy for it
- the difference-making view, where doing means your behavior is a difference-maker relative to some baseline and allowing means you're part of the background, with the moral weight inherited from whatever makes difference-making matter
- the normal-course view, where allowing lets the statistically or biologically normal course of events run and doing diverts it, and the relevance comes from some entitlement to the normal course
- the social-practice view, where the distinction is a real social kind, a way our blaming and excusing practices carve things up, but not a fact about moral weight
- the degrees view, where there's no line at all, just a gradient of causal involvement, and the binary is a convenience of law and language
- the blame-but-not-wrongness view, where permissibility is identical either way and what differs is how much you're to blame or what you owe by way of explanation
- the agent-causation view, where the distinction only exists if agents are a special kind of cause and it collapses for anything that isn't a genuine initiator

Cases the tree has to be able to route:
- the doctor who pulls the plug vs the doctor who never starts the ventilator
- the surgeon who stops in the middle of an operation (is stopping a doing or an allowing?)
- the lifeguard who never gets in the water vs the lifeguard who swims halfway and turns back
- the guy who unscrews the only lightbulb in the operating room vs the guy who just never replaces it
- a firing squad where the other nine would have shot anyway, so did this one make any difference at all
- someone who starts a fire and then doesnt warn the people inside, doing and allowing in the same person
- the person who declines to give a kidney vs the person who takes one out
- the parent who never feeds the kid vs the parent who takes the food away mid meal
- the person standing in the doorway blocking the paramedic vs the person who just never opens the door
- a case where the situation is chaotic enough that there's no determinate fact about what would have happened if you'd done nothing

Two forks I care about most, so build them in even if they seem fussy. First, whether a position needs a well defined answer to the question of what would have happened if you'd done nothing at all, because a couple of my cases are ones where that counterfactual is genuinely undefined, and I want to see which positions survive without a baseline. Second, whether the difference is a line or a gradient, and if someone says gradient whether they still get to say there's a real moral difference or whether the whole thing dissolves into more-or-less causal involvement.

Then two more things. Say which cells in your space come out empty, and for each one whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's coherent but nobody would want it, or because it just hasnt been explored. And if two of your leaves answer every case I listed the same way, flag them as indistinguishable on my cases instead of pretending the tree separates them.

Last thing, because it's the reason I started this arguement in the first place: I read something online claiming the distinction is really about which of two causal routes you're on, the route that runs through the threat or the route that runs through the victim, so it's a claim about the shape of the causal graph and not about action and omission at all. Tell me where that lands in the tree, and whether it's actually a separate leaf or just a redescription of the difference-making view. If its a redescription, say so.
