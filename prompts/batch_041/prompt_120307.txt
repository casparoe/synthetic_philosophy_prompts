Question: Explain the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, and assess whether it succeeds. Be specific about exactly what the objection is a complaint about, and consider at least one reply the utilitarian could make.

Student's answer:

"Utilitarianism says we should maximize total happiness, adding up everyone's happiness and picking whatever gives the biggest sum. The separateness-of-persons objection, which is associated with Rawls, says this treats society as though it were one person, so that one person's suffering can be cancelled out by another's pleasure in the way that one hour of my own pain can be cancelled out by two hours of my own pleasure. That's supposed to be illegitimate because people are separate: my pain is mine and your pleasure is yours, and they dont add up into some single quantity that belongs to anybody.

I take the objection to be the same as the Kantian complaint that utilitarianism treats people as means rather than ends, since in both cases the worry is that some people get used for other people's benefit. Read that way, it does show that a crude utilitarian calculation can be unjust, since it can license sacrificing an individual whenever the numbers work out.

But I dont think it succeeds against utilitarianism as such. The utilitarian can just say that everyone's happiness counts equally and nobody's is given extra weight, which is what it means to respect persons as separate and equal. And if you want to be careful about distribution, you can move to a weighted version where gains to the worse off count for more — prioritarianism — which takes care of the objection without giving up aggregation. The deeper problem is that the objection proves too much: we all accept that a person can trade off their own present good against their own future good, and nobody says it's wrong to study instead of party because present-me and future-me are separate people. So the fact that utilitarianism trades off between people can't by itself be what's wrong with it.

So the objection is a useful corrective to crude act-utilitarianism, but it doesnt refute consequentialism."
