Ok so I've been chewing on the drowning-child thing for a while — you know the one, you're walking past a shallow pond, a kid is drowning, you could wade in and grab him but it wrecks your shoes, and basically everyone agrees you have to do it. I have a view about what that case actually proves and I want you to try to knock it down.

My view is this: the pond case only proves something about situations where you're the only one who can help. I don't think the strength of the intuition comes from any cost-benefit math — it comes from you standing there alone on the bank with nobody else around. Now in the real world, for pretty much anything I could give money to, there are millions of other people who could give too. I'm not the difference between a kid living and dying, I'm one of a crowd. So the case gets you a duty to rescue when you're uniquely placed, and real-world giving just isn't that, and so the whole thing doesn't demand what people say it demands.

But here's the part that bothers me, and it kind of cuts against my own view from the other side. If uniqueness is really what's doing the work in the pond case, then the pond intuition might be way more fragile than it looks. Put thirty people on that bank and have none of them move — would we still say any one of them is obligated? My gut says a lot of people would say it's monstrous but that no particular one of them is required, like the responsibility smears out across the crowd. And if thats right then the case is doing two different jobs at once and I can't tell which one is real.

And then theres a further wrinkle. Even granting uniqueness, there isn't one pond, there's a million ponds and I'm standing at the edge of all of them at the same time and can only reach a few. If each pond hands me a duty, do those duties stack until the total demand is infinite? Because if so my view collapses under its own weight, and the only escape I can see is some cap on how much can be demanded of any one person — but every cap I try to state just turns out to be whatever I was already planning to do anyway, which smells like cheating.

What I want from you: what would actually count against my view, sorted from the stuff that should make me drop it to the stuff that's merely awkward. Not the standard you're-a-bad-person guilt trip — I've heard it, and I'm not asking whether I'm decent, I'm asking whether the argument works. I'm especially interested in (a) cases where the uniqueness condition fails and the intuition survives anyway, (b) any real argument that the diffusion-of-responsibility thing is a bias rather than a genuine feature of the moral facts, and (c) whether there's a version of my view that isn't just me dressing up the fact that I don't want to give more money.

Also be straight with me about whether my view can be defended in a way that makes it basically unfalsifiable — like, every counterexample just gets absorbed by me saying well in that case you ARE uniquely placed after all. And if it is unfalsifiable, tell me whether that's actually a problem for a view like this or whether it's fine.

One last thing, and it matters to me: sort out which parts of this are actually settled among people who've thought hard about it and which parts are live disagreements that get presented as settled. I dont want a fake consensus. If two camps are just talking past each other, say so.
