I've been building this little tabletop robot with my nephew — Raspberry Pi, a camera, a cheap mic, and a small language model running on a laptop next to it — and the whole plan was that it would learn the names of like twenty things in the house (mug, apple, sock, the cat, the back door) and then say simple stuff about them. It sort of works, and that turned out to be the problem. When it says mug, what's actually happening is a token getting emitted that correlates with a blob in the camera frame and with whatever the training text says about mugs, and I honestly can't tell you whether any of that means anything to the robot or whether I've just built an extremely fancy doorbell. My roommate is a cognitive science grad student and she says I'm re-inventing the symbol grounding problem, which, fine, she's right, but now I have a more specific gripe. Everyone argues about whether this system or that system is "really" grounded, or whether grounding is just more text, or whether you need a body, and nobody ever says up front what an account of grounding is even supposed to deliver. So before I read another thing about it, I want the spec.

Give me the desiderata: what any adequate account of what it is for a symbol in a system to be about something has to do — the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't be allowed to collapse, the things we already know that it can't contradict — with the case or the consideration behind each requirement, not just the requirement on its own. Seed the list with the stuff I keep running into: my robot; a thermostat and its on/off state; a pocket calculator; a lookup table that maps words to other words; a system trained only on text that can answer questions about the world; a parrot that says the right thing at the right time; a dog that sits on command; a person who learned a word from a definition and never saw the thing, or who is blind and still uses color words correctly. And on the distinctions: I want to know whether the account can keep apart a symbol meaning something *to the system* versus meaning something to me and my nephew, and whether it can keep grounding apart from reference, from causal correlation, from use, from being right, and from understanding, or whether it has to argue that some of those actually collapse into each other. Also tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just commitments of one camp, and — this is the part I care most about — whether any subset of them can't be satisfied together. It seems to me you can have grounding come in degrees, or have it be strict enough to rule out the thermostat, or have it be learnable from language alone the way we all learn most of our words, but probably not all three, and if that's right then the list of requirements is itself the interesting result and I want to see it spelled out.

One more thing: I'd like the account to tell me what I could actually check, since I want to know whether my robot's word is grounded and I can't tell by looking at it. If the requirements make grounding undetectable in principle, say so, that's an answer too. If you have room at the end, run a quick pass over the usual suspects — causal and teleological accounts, the Chinese room intuition, embodied or enactive views, the "it's all just more text" line — but keep it short, the criteria are what I'm after, and I don't want a reading list because I will absolutely never get to it.
