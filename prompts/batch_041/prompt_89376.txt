Thirty-four years as a field geologist for a state survey taught me to want a method before I trust a conclusion; five years of retirement spent reading moral philosophy have given me a large vocabulary and no method at all. Since January I have chaired the autonomous systems incident review panel of my state's department of transportation. The panel does not assign legal liability, which is for the courts, but it must decide within two weeks of each incident whether any person or organization bears moral responsibility, and what the department should require before the system returns to service. Our caseload so far includes an automated shuttle, sidewalk delivery robots, drones flown by county agencies, and autonomous container-handling equipment at the port. I am the only member who has read any philosophy, I work up each case alone on the first afternoon, and the department secretary signs whatever finding we send up.

Please write me a decision procedure I can run myself on that first afternoon. I need the questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it must stop and hand the question to the full panel because what is at issue is a value rather than a fact.

The procedure has to do at least the following. It must keep the backward-looking question, who is to blame and how much, apart from the forward-looking one, who must change the system and who pays for the change, and tell me what to do when the two come apart, which I suspect is often. For every agent in the chain — designer, trainer, vendor, deployer, operator, remote monitor, maintainer, the official who approved the deployment — it should test three separate things: whether the agent could have known what the system would do, whether the agent could have acted to prevent it, and whether acting was within the agent's authority. It must then tell me when a pattern of failures across those cells amounts to a genuine absence of responsibility and when it is only an evidentiary hole — when we cannot reconstruct what happened rather than when nobody could have known. The panel keeps conflating the two, and they have opposite consequences: a hole calls for better logging, an absence calls for a rule.

Two wrinkles I cannot settle alone. First, tracing: responsibility often recedes to an earlier choice — deploying without a remote kill switch, accepting a vendor's over-the-air update by default, specifying an optimizer's objective in terms of harm-weighted cost. How far back may the procedure trace before it is inventing a standard after the fact and blaming people for not having met it? Second, learning systems: if the deployed system's behavior changed after the designers were done with it, their ignorance may be genuine, while someone's decision to deploy a system that changes may not be.

Be honest about which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, and about where the procedure will misfire; I would rather know its edges than have it look tidier than it is.

Then run it on three cases: a delivery robot that, swerving to avoid a cyclist, struck and injured a pedestrian, where the manufacturer's planner had been instructed to minimize harm-weighted cost; a shuttle that struck a cyclist at an unprotected left turn after an over-the-air update the operator had accepted by default; and a case of your choosing that the procedure resolves in a way I will not like — I would rather watch it land on the low-paid remote monitor who had forty seconds and thirty screens, or on my own panel for having approved the deployment, than have it flatter us.

Write the answer in American English, and spare me citations and reading lists; I want the procedure, not the bibliography.
