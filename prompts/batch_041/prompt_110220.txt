I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a behavioural ecologist, and to keep her honest I have spent the better part of a year reading the primary literature in evolutionary psychology. The upshot is that I now hold firm but entirely untheorised convictions about which of its explanations are science and which are what my character, in a sour mood, calls storytelling, and I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, governs those convictions, and where the principle forces me to surrender one of them.

Here are my verdicts. That disgust at spoiled food, at certain animals, and at sexual impropriety is a system shaped by selection, with its own facial expression, its own physiology, its own developmental schedule, and a characteristic pattern of over- and under-firing, strikes me as a real finding, whatever the eventual details. That the nausea of early pregnancy is a defence, calibrated to the window when the embryo is most vulnerable to plant toxins and easing as that window closes, strikes me as real too. That kin-directed care scales with relatedness, so that a person will risk more for a sibling than for a cousin and more for a cousin than for a stranger, strikes me as real, chiefly because the ordering was derived before anyone went looking and could easily have come out the other way. But the familiar story about men's preference for a particular waist-to-hip ratio, explained by its tracking fertility, strikes me as storytelling, even though it made a numerical prediction in advance and the prediction held up. So does the claim that emotional tears evolved as a signal of need or submission. So does the claim that depression is a bargaining tactic for extracting care from one's circle. And so does the claim that music exists because it helped our ancestors bond, even though I cannot myself say what would count as a better explanation of music.

My own attempt at a principle runs like this: an evolutionary explanation earns the name of science when it predicts something about the trait's fine structure that you could have got wrong, not merely that the trait is there but its shape, its triggers, its exceptions, and it is storytelling when the trait's bare existence is the only thing it predicts, or when the prediction was plainly chosen after the data were in. Tested against my verdicts, though, it comes apart in a way I cannot repair. Read strictly, its second clause condemns the waist-to-hip story, which I am happy to condemn, but it also condemns the morning-sickness story, which I want to keep, since the timing of the nausea was common knowledge long before anyone proposed the defence. Read leniently, it vindicates the morning-sickness story, which I want, but it also vindicates the waist-to-hip story, which I want to reject. So the two cases I care most about separating seem to sit on the same side of every version of the line I can draw. Is there a better principle that gets all seven verdicts, or must I give one up? If I must, which one, and what does the surviving principle say about a case I have not listed, say the hypothesis that blushing exists to advertise sincerity, and about whether any of this can be kept distinct from storytelling without leaning on evidence I could not possibly have?
