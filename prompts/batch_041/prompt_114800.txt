I have been arguing for weeks with two friends — one a law student, one an intensive-care nurse — about what makes consent valid, and the argument has left me with the suspicion that my verdicts are more consistent with one another than any principle I can state, which is either a discovery or a rationalization. I would like you to treat the following as data and tell me what principle, or small family of principles, actually captures them. (1) A patient consents to an appendectomy after the surgeon explains the risks, the alternatives, and what recovery involves: valid. (2) The same patient consents on the same terms, but the surgeon has quietly arranged for a junior resident to do most of the cutting and does not mention it, because the patient is nervous and would only fret: invalid — even though the patient would have said yes anyway and the operation goes perfectly. (3) A partner conceals a spouse, or an income, or a diagnosis of infertility; the sex that follows is valid. The same partner quietly removes the condom, or lies about having had a vasectomy: invalid. (4) A mugger's threat makes the surrender of my wallet invalid. An employer in a one-company town who says the contract is this or the door is valid, though I think the employer a scoundrel. (5) A professor who offers a passing grade for sex gets no valid consent at all. I notice that the professor threatens a small harm and the employer a large one, and yet only the professor's threat voids the consent. (6) A fifteen-year-old's consent to a vaccine is valid; the same fifteen-year-old's consent to sex with a thirty-year-old, or to donating a kidney, is not. A man in the early stages of dementia can validly consent to a haircut and cannot validly consent to selling the house his children grew up in. (7) A prisoner who volunteers for a paid medical experiment has not validly consented; a desperately poor man who agrees to sell a kidney is a case I cannot settle, and I want to say no for the prisoner and yes for the kidney seller, or the reverse on alternate days. (8) A boxer consents to being hit; a duelist cannot consent to being killed; a competent terminally ill adult can consent to being helped to die.

One friend proposes that the deception cases turn on whether the lie concerns a boundary fact — something about what is being done to my body, and by whom — or a background fact about the world around the act, and that only the former voids consent. That handles (2) and (3) and says nothing at all about (4) and (5). My own tentative principle is that consent is valid when the person grasps the boundary being crossed, is not being moved across it by someone with no right to move them, and has the capacity to weigh a decision of that size; I suspect this is three clauses doing the work of one, and that at least one of my verdicts has to go.

What I want is not a survey of positions but a principle that earns its keep against these cases. State it, or the small set of rival candidates, as precisely as you can, and for each clause name the case that forces it. Then tell me what the principle predicts for cases I have not listed — in particular, whether consent to sex given the night before, to be acted on while I am asleep, is valid; whether a liability waiver signed by a woman in advanced labor is valid; whether consent is valid when a patient is told a risk exists but not how likely it is; and whether a worker's agreement to a hazard the employer concealed but did not misdescribe is valid. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and say whether you would revise the principle or the verdict. I am also unsure whether my verdicts are about consent being invalid or merely about consent being wrongfully obtained; if the principle needs that distinction, tell me where the line falls.
