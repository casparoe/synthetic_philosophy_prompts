I am writing on whether growth-rate maximization — the Kelly criterion and the views that descend from it — can be defended as a criterion of practical rationality rather than as advice about bet sizing, and I want the bar fixed before I defend any particular version. Set out the desiderata: what any adequate theory in this area has to deliver — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the results it must be consistent with — giving, for each requirement, the consideration that motivates it. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of one camp or another, and be explicit about any subset you think cannot be jointly satisfied; the conflicts are the part I most want. Close by naming the single requirement that does the most to exclude candidate theories.

The theory is to serve as a norm for a single agent facing compounding risks over an indefinite horizon, who must estimate the odds rather than be told them, and who may have to live off the stock rather than merely accumulate it. The cases I keep returning to, and which I would want the specification to speak to, are these. A one-off wager that multiplies the agent's holdings by 1.5 on a win and by 0.6 on a loss, with even chances: a five per cent gain in arithmetic expectation and a geometric expectation below one, so that a maximizer of expected value takes it and a maximizer of expected logarithmic value refuses. The same wager repeated, where the average across many independent players grows without bound while almost every individual trajectory decays, and where no strategy improves on the growth-optimal one along almost every trajectory in the long run. An agent whose aim is not growth but the attainment of a target sum before ruin, for whom, when the game is unfavorable, the growth-optimal stake is the wrong stake and a policy of staking the maximum permitted, or exactly what the target requires, does better. An offer of an astronomical payoff at an astronomical improbability, together with the older puzzle of a game whose expected value is infinite while its logarithmic value is finite. And an agent who overestimates the edge, and whom overstaking punishes more than understaking. A theory that speaks only of limits, or only of one-shot decisions, or that slides between maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth and maximizing a growth rate, should not survive the list.

Then, briefly — I want the criteria to carry the weight, so keep this short — say how three approaches fare against your list: expected utility maximization with logarithmic utility, the asymptotic growth-rate criterion, and a criterion that maximizes the probability of reaching a goal before ruin.
