I would like a careful diagnosis of the following argument, which a colleague circulated in a reading group on the philosophy of promising. He takes it to show that promissory obligation is far less robust than we suppose. I suspect he is too quick, but I want the flaw identified rather than merely gestured at, and I want to know what repairing it would cost.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) When I promise to meet you at noon, I undertake an obligation to meet you at noon.
(2) Suppose that at 11.55, through an emergency I could not have foreseen and cannot now avert, I am unable to reach you by noon.
(3) At 11.55, then, I cannot meet you at noon.
(4) Ought implies can: one is not obligated to do what one cannot do.
(5) Therefore at 11.55 I am not obligated to meet you at noon.
(6) If I am not obligated at 11.55, then either the obligation lapsed the instant performance became impossible, or it never attached to the act of meeting you but only to my trying to meet you.
(7) If it lapsed whenever performance became impossible, then the promise binds only for as long as keeping it remains possible — that is, it binds only while it is convenient, which is not what we take a promise to be.
(8) If it attached only to trying, then no promise is ever an undertaking to do the specific thing promised, which is likewise not what we take a promise to be.
(9) Either way, the promise does not bind me as we ordinarily think it does.

He then runs the same argument with a variant in which the emergency was one I could have prevented — I left too late, and the road was blocked as a result — and says the conclusion is unchanged: in both cases I could not meet you at noon, so by (4) I was not obligated to.

The claims I would most like to keep are (4) and, if I am honest, (7) and (8), which seem to me exactly right about what a promise is not. So I am persuaded that the fault lies elsewhere, yet I cannot see where it is. Please identify the step or the equivocation responsible, explain why it is easy to overlook, and say what I would have to concede about the nature of promissory obligation in order to reject it — in particular, whether any recognisable account of promising survives, or whether the argument, once repaired, still leaves promises less binding than we thought. Please set out your answer in British English.
