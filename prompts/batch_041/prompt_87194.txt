I'm a grad student in philosophy who's been sitting in on a history of physics seminar, and there's a move I keep seeing that I dont have a name for. The first place I noticed it was with Gödel's rotating-universe solution: the whole universe is filled with slowly swirling dust, and because of the swirl, if you go far enough in a rocket you can loop back and meet your younger self. People then say that since this spacetime has no global way of slicing it into a shared present, and since it contains these closed loops, time must be an illusion, or at least passage is not a fundamental feature of reality. I've seen the same shape of argument in quantum gravity: someone points out that the basic equation has no time variable in it at all, and concludes that time is emergent or unreal.

What I want is not the conclusion -- I know people call that the block universe, eternalism, the unreality of time, etc. -- but the inference pattern itself: from the fact that a fundamental description lacks a preferred global time (or has closed causal loops) to the claim that temporal passage is not real. Is there an established name for that specific move? I've heard it called Gödel's argument, but that just names the guy, and it doesn't cover the quantum gravity version. It's not the relativity-of-simultaneity argument either, because that's about different observers disagreeing about what's simultaneous, not about the global structure of a solution. What I really want is the carving: what exactly is the move, what premises does it need (e.g., that time has to be a global ordering, or that anything absent from a fundamental description cant be real), where does it sit relative to the block universe and the problem of time, and when is it a good inference versus a conflation of a model's global features with the local experience of passage? If there's no name, can you coin one and define it so I can tell when someone is making this move rather than just asserting eternalism? I'm not after a bibliography, just the concept.
