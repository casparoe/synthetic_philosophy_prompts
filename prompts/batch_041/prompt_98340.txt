Okay, heres a thing I keep tripping over. Amartya Sen's liberal paradox — the "impossibility of a Paretian liberal" — is built on a toy example with two people he calls Lewd and Prude and a single copy of Lady Chatterley's Lover. Lewd ranks the outcomes: Prude reads it > Lewd reads it > it's binned unread (he'd most enjoy Prude being forced to read it). Prude ranks them: binned unread > Prude reads it > Lewd reads it (he'd rather suffer it himself than let Lewd enjoy it). Minimal liberalism then hands each man the say over his own reading — so Lewd-reads > nobody-reads, and nobody-reads > Prude-reads — while both men prefer Prude-reads to Lewd-reads, so Pareto gives Prude-reads > Lewd-reads. Cycle. Textbook.

Now the part I want you to chew on: that novel wasn't hypothetical. In 1960 Penguin was prosecuted at the Old Bailey under the Obscene Publications Act 1959 for putting out the unexpurgated Lady Chatterley's Lover, and the prosecutor, Mervyn Griffith-Jones, asked the jury "Is it a book that you would even wish your wife or your servants to read?" The jury acquitted and the book went on open sale. The Act also gave the defence a "public good" route — publication "in the interests of science, literature, art or learning, or of other objects of general concern."

What I want is a two-way analysis. First run the trial through the paradox: map it properly — who's the prude in the courtroom, who's the lewd, what are the actual social states, and notice that the nosy/meddlesome preference (the one about what other people read, as opposed to what you yourself read) is carrying all the weight. Does the real case fit the toy model or does it strain it — for instance, the nosy preference now belongs to the state and the majority rather than to one man, and the "prude" isn't obviously protecting any reading of his own. Then run the paradox back through the trial: does the acquittal count as the social choice honouring liberty over Pareto, or does the majority-against-minority shape of the real dispute mean Pareto isn't even the right lever? What does the statutory merits defence add that Sen's three-way choice never had room for? And does the episode confirm the paradox, fall outside what it can reach, or suggest the whole result only bites when you insist on feeding meddlesome preferences into the Pareto base (as against the move of restricting Pareto to self-regarding preferences, or of redescribing the rights as rules of a game that fix who moves first)?

Dont just recite the theorem back at me. Keep the mapping disciplined — who's a player, what are the options, what counts as a preference here — and be honest about where the theory goes quiet instead of forcing it to speak.
