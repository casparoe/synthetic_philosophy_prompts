Two friends of mine, both graduate students in philosophy, became embroiled in a dispute after a seminar on Leibniz, and I could not decide who had the better of it. The dispute began when one claimed that the thesis that this is the best of all possible worlds is not merely false but incoherent. His argument was that possible worlds admit of endless improvement: take any world and remove one gratuitous toothache, or add one more fulfilled life, and the result is better. If for every world there is a better one, there is no maximum, and therefore no best world for God to choose. My other friend replied that this treats perfection as a single additive quantity. On Leibniz's view, a world's perfection consists in the harmony and compossibility of its parts; one cannot simply add or subtract a happy person or a pain without altering the entire network of substances and their relations. The first friend responded that even a holistic perfection is still comparative: for any world W, consider a world W* exactly like W except that it contains one less gratuitous pain. If W* is possible, it is better than W, so W is not the best. The second replied that W* may not be compossible with the rest of W; one cannot isolate a single pain and remove it without changing the identities and relations that make W the world it is. The first said that this renders the best world hostage to a mysterious holistic metric; unless one can specify what makes one world better than another, the claim that this world is the best is empty. The second said the metric is objective: greater perfection means greater harmony, variety, and order, and these are comparable even if we cannot calculate them. The first then shifted ground. Even granting a unique best, he said, the claim that God must choose it raises a problem about freedom. If God is essentially good and wise, He cannot choose a less-than-best world. But if He cannot do otherwise, His choice is not free; it is a necessity of nature, like fire burning. The second said that freedom does not require the ability to do otherwise; it requires acting from one's own nature and judgment, without external coercion. God is free because He acts from His own perfect wisdom and goodness, not because He could have been irrational. The first replied that this makes God's goodness a constraint on His freedom rather than an expression of it, and that if there is no real alternative, calling it a choice is misleading. The second said that the alternative worlds are genuine possibilities, just not ones a perfect being would actualize, and that the world remains contingent because it depends on God's free decree even if that decree is infallibly good. The first said that this is a verbal fix: if God's nature determines the outcome, the outcome is necessary, which contradicts the idea that God could have created a different world. They asked me to adjudicate. Please go through their exchange step by step, saying who is right about each specific claim and what, if anything, both are missing. Do not simply declare a winner overall.
