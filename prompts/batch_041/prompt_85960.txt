I'm a novelist — fourth book, and its the first one where the plot has painted me into a corner I can't think my way out of, so I'm outsourcing the thinking. Here's the corner. My protagonist has been dosed with something that wipes short-term memory in one very particular way, and he's driving a route he knows cold: two exits, identical to look at, about twenty minutes apart. He has to take the second one. Take the first and he drives straight into the men waiting for him; sail past the second and the road runs out at the coast, where someone is also waiting. The drug does exactly one thing to him: at each exit he has the same experience — same sign, same headlights, same thought, "this is it" — and he can't tell whether he's at the first or the second. Whatever rule he fixed on before he set off, he applies it twice, the second time blind. So "always take the exit" kills him at the first; "always drive past" kills him at the second; and if he flips a coin at each exit he has, at the very best, a one-in-four chance of getting out — and even that is only true if the coin-flipping is the right thing to do, which is the bit I can't work out. I gather this is what people call the absent-minded driver, except I've set the payoffs myself and made them lethal, because the scene has to be tense, not academic.

What I want from you isn't the answer, it's the spec. What does any theory of rational choice — or rational action, or practical reason, whatever the right label is — actually have to be able to do before I can hand it a scene like this and trust it? Give me the full desiderata list: the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it isn't allowed to collapse (I have a hunch act-versus-plan is one of them, but you tell me), and the results it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each one, so I can see why it's a requirement and not just a taste. Then your honest read on which of those requirements are real and which are just habits of one camp, whether any two of them can't both be satisfied (that's the part I most want — if the list is incoherent then my scene is incoherent and I'd rather know now), and which single requirement does the most work at ruling candidates out. A table is fine for the last bit. I'll score particular theories against the list myself later; that's definately not what I'm after here.

And since one scene can't test anything, here are the variants I keep spinning off, all of which the theory has to survive without me fudging: (a) he gets a faint clue — a road sound, a lit sign, the feel of the tarmac — that makes it 70/30 which exit he's at; (b) his partner is in the car behind, dosed the same, on the same route, and they agreed on a rule before they started, so their fates move together; (c) he's allowed to rig the car first — a kitchen timer, a note taped to the wheel, a passenger who isn't dosed — and I want to know whether "leave yourself a note" counts as solving it or as cheating; (d) he isn't sure he hasn't already gone past both exits; and (e) it isn't one man but a relay of five, each handed the wheel for one stretch, each told "this is the first exit," none of them able to see the others or check the odometer. Don't send me a reading list or a bibliography — I can't look anything up right now anyway — I want the criteria in your own words, each one tied to the case that forces it.
