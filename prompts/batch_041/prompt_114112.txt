I am assembling a four-year research program on pure time preference and the ethical discounting of future welfare — three funded positions, weighted toward philosophy and decision theory rather than empirical economics — and I want to commit those positions to the questions that are genuinely load-bearing rather than to the ones that are merely famous. To make the agenda concrete, I am working with a framing that cuts across the usual debate. Under the single heading of discounting the future there are at least three separable claims: first, an axiological claim that future welfare counts for less in the goodness of outcomes, independent of who knows or decides what; second, a decision-theoretic claim that a planner should weight far-future outcomes less because of uncertainty, irreversibility, and the option value of waiting; and third, a preference-respecting claim that a social welfare function should simply inherit the time preferences that actual people happen to have. My working hypothesis is that the first is indefensible, the third is a category error, and everything defensible that travels under the name of pure time preference reduces to the second — and that once one accepts this reduction, the agenda changes shape considerably.

Given that framing, which open questions in this area most deserve the resources, and in what order? Please defend the ordering rather than merely listing: for each question, say whether it is upstream of the others, whether it is tractable for philosophers and decision theorists rather than for economists, and what argument or result over the next few years would move it up or down the list. I am particularly interested in which questions look central under the standard framing but become peripheral once the first and third claims are set aside, and which neglected questions become central in their place. I would rather have a defended agenda with the trade-offs made explicit than a survey of positions.
