I'm in the middle of a reading group on Dennett and artificial intelligence, and we keep circling a problem I cannot get past. Dennett's 2023 Atlantic essay warns that we can now manufacture counterfeit people—artifacts that pass for human in the digital environments we have built—and argues that doing so is a kind of vandalism that ought to be outlawed, with counterfeit currency as his central analogy: just as fake money corrodes trust in money itself, fake people corrode trust in the human beings we deal with. What troubles me is that this sits badly with the Dennett of the intentional-stance material, where person-level talk is a pragmatic stance we adopt toward anything exhibiting a sufficiently robust predictive pattern, and there is supposed to be no further fact of the matter about whether the thing really has beliefs and desires. If the intentional stance is right, calling a chatbot a counterfeit person looks like a category mistake, since the label presupposes precisely the kind of inner genuineness that the stance is meant to dissolve. Some of my friends think Dennett has simply contradicted himself; others think the two views can coexist. I want you to argue for a position on this, and I want to seed it with a distinction I have found useful: separate token-counterfeiting, an individual fake passing itself off as genuine, from currency-debasement, the mere flood of fakes degrading trust in the medium itself. Dennett's money analogy, I suspect, actually supports the second reading rather than the first, and if that is right, the harm lies not in the existence of the artifacts but in the practice of circulating them.

So here is the position I would like you to defend: Dennett's warning survives the apparent conflict with the intentional stance, but only once the harm is relocated from the deception of particular individuals to the debasement of a shared epistemic and communicative environment, and only once the remedy targets uses and practices rather than the manufacture of the artifacts as such. Please argue for that. To make it concrete, consider a case the deception story handles poorly: an openly labeled AI companion that its user knows is not a person and with whom the user has formed something that feels like a friendship. No one is deceived, so on the token-counterfeiting reading there is nothing counterfeit in play—yet the case seems to press on exactly the anxieties Dennett is voicing. Say what your position implies about cases like this one. I would also like you to state the strongest objection to your view—most obviously, that the intentional stance, applied consistently, licenses treating such an artifact as a person whenever it is useful to do so—and then explain why the objection fails or what it forces you to concede.
