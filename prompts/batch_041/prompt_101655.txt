I'm a third-year PhD student in a computer science department that shares a lot of faculty with the philosophy department, and I'm assembling a two-page research proposal for a one-year fellowship whose reviewers come from both sides, so it has to be legible to people who care about the conceptual framing and to people who will want to see an actual theorem-shaped target rather than a literature survey with a question mark at the end. Here is my half-formed idea, which I'd like you to turn into the proposal. The familiar impossibility results in algorithmic fairness — the ones showing that calibration and the two error-rate-balance conditions cannot all hold at once once group base rates differ — are, as far as I can tell, all proved for what I've started calling a single-shot decision: one classifier, one fixed population, one round, with the classifier's output treated as the end of the story. But nearly every deployment I actually care about is iterative. A risk score allocates a scarce resource — a loan, a hospital bed, a place in a program — the allocation changes who is in the population at the next round, and next round's training data and base rates are partly a product of last round's decisions. I suspect the standard impossibility is not the interesting one in this setting, and that a companion result is waiting here: once the policy feeds back into the population, no policy can be simultaneously (i) calibrated at every round, (ii) compliant with a per-round equal-opportunity-style constraint, and (iii) a fixed point in the sense that applying it does not itself manufacture the very disparity the constraint was meant to police. Call condition (iii) something like "equity stability" — I don't have the right name yet. What I genuinely don't know is whether the right result is a theorem, a family of counterexamples showing the tension dissolves under a well-chosen feedback model, or a sharpened form of the original impossibility in which the feedback term turns out to be the only thing doing any work.

Please write the proposal. I need a precise statement of the question that has a determinate answer rather than a survey shape; a short account of why the existing single-shot results leave this open (I'll supply the citations myself — do not give me a bibliography or a reading list); the method, concretely and month by month across the year, saying what formal apparatus I would actually need and what a proof would have to look like as opposed to what would count as a decisive counterexample; a clear statement of what I would take as evidence that I have been asking the wrong question; and an honest reckoning with scope, since a one-year fellowship with a teaching load is not a dissertation and I would rather propose something finishable than something grand. Finally, tell me which of the three possible outcomes above a reviewer would find most valuable, and whether I should hedge by naming the outcome I expect or leave the space of outcomes open.
