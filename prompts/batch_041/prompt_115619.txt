A mate of mine who's deep in the open-weights fight keeps saying Mill settles the whole thing: publish the weights, the harm principle only bites when someone actually gets harmed, and you can't be answerable for what other people do with your work. I think thats far too quick. Can you work out what Mill would really have said about a lab releasing the full weights of a frontier model, plus enough of the recipe that anyone can strip the safety training off it in a weekend, and then tell me where his framework starts to strain?

I want the specifics, not a summary of On Liberty. His distinction between circulating an opinion in the press and delivering it to an excited mob outside the corn-dealer's house — does a set of weights look more like the pamphlet or the speech to the mob? Whether harm can stretch to a small bump in the odds that thousands of strangers do something awful, where the releaser never touches any of them. What he'd make of a race between labs in which the first release is irreversible and resets everyone else's incentives. And whether his willingness to let despotism stand over peoples he thought weren't yet fit for self-government (he spent his career at the East India Company, after all) would leave him comfortable with gated or licence-only access for some countries — I suspect that bit gets skipped.

Then the democratising case: one release, against a handful of labs and a couple of states. Give me a verdict, his likely reasoning, two or three places where you're extrapolating rather than drawing on him, and the strongest Millian case for the opposite answer. I'd rather you flag the guesswork than pretend it's all in the texts.
