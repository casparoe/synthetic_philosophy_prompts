A colleague who teaches a survey course on science and religion recently gave me the following account of Darwin on morality and religion. I am not in a position to grade it, and what I want from you is a careful audit rather than a general essay on Darwin. His account, as I recorded it, ran roughly like this:

Darwin's real contribution to ethics was to explain where our moral feelings come from. They are instincts, assembled by natural selection because groups whose members helped one another outlasted groups whose members did not. Conscience is the inner echo of those cooperative habits, and the peculiar pull we feel when we say that something simply ought to be done is the pull of an instinct that paid for itself in survival. Darwin saw clearly that this leaves morality with no foundation in the nature of things: right and wrong are not discovered but manufactured, and the codes people argue about are just the ones that happened to work. He softened this only by allowing that the moral sense, once in place, could be extended in wider and wider circles — to strangers, to other peoples, eventually to animals — and he treated that widening as the true measure of moral progress. On religion, natural selection did to the argument from design what it had already done to the fixity of species, and Darwin's own faith did not survive the encounter: the death of his daughter Annie settled the matter, and by the end of his life he was a settled atheist who regarded religion as a consolation that human beings had invented because they could not bear an indifferent universe. What he kept from his upbringing was a lingering respect for the ethical teaching of the Gospels, which he thought could be retained after the metaphysics had been thrown away.

Please go through this claim by claim and tell me which sentences a Darwin scholar would accept as a fair statement of Darwin's own position, which they would wince at as an oversimplification, and which are not Darwin at all but the work of later hands. I am especially interested in two things. First, the account slides from a claim about the origin of our moral feelings to a claim about their authority; I would like to know exactly where that slide occurs, whether any conclusion of that kind is actually implied by what Darwin argued, and what someone sympathetic to the account could say in its defence before conceding the point. Second, tell me which of these claims are matters of live disagreement among historians rather than settled facts — I would much rather be told that something is contested than receive a confident correction. I am also curious about the internal coherence of the account: whether its explanation of the moral sense is consistent with itself, and what a defender of Darwin's own position would object to in it. Finish by telling me which sentences would survive if the errors were repaired, so that I know what I can safely repeat in a seminar. I do not need a reading list or citations; I want the substance of the audit.
