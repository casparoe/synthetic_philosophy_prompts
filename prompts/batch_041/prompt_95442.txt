My partner and I are midway through an IVF cycle, and last week the clinic handed us a table of our embryos — a column of chromosomal results, a column of single-gene findings, a column of polygenic risk scores for a dozen conditions and traits — and asked us to say which one to transfer. I had expected to find the ethics of this hard in a general way. What surprised me is how uneven my own reactions have been: some rows in that table I ruled out in a second, others I have been circling for days. I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to, and I am prepared to be told that one of them has to go.

Here is what I actually think, case by case.

An embryo carrying two copies of the variant that causes Tay-Sachs: we ruled it out without discussion. Not merely permitted, I think — it would have been wrong to transfer it. The easiest case by a wide margin.

An embryo carrying a BRCA1 variant, with a very high lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancer beginning in adulthood: also ruled out, and I am confident about it, though it feels less like a duty and more like an obvious good.

An embryo with a polygenic score putting it in the top few percent for risk of asthma — a common, treatable, unpleasant condition: I would also rule this out, and my confidence here is real but I notice I would have trouble defending it against someone who said the difference was small.

An embryo that is merely shorter than the others by genetic prediction, or that scores lower on a polygenic index for educational attainment: I will not select on these, and I am uncomfortable with parents who would. Yet the expected difference in how well these lives go seems to me at least as large as in the asthma case. I cannot make these two verdicts sit comfortably side by side.

Sex selection, for a family with three sons who want a daughter: I am uneasy but would not call it wrong. Sex selection driven by a preference for sons over daughters: clearly wrong. I feel the difference sharply and cannot immediately say what it consists in.

Deaf parents who want to select an embryo that will be deaf like them, using an embryo known to carry a variant causing congenital deafness: I think this is wrong, and this is the one I keep reopening. I am willing to grant that a child raised in a signing household may have an entirely good life, and even that congenital deafness is not a disease in the way Tay-Sachs is. My verdict does not budge, and I do not trust my reasons for it.

Selecting an embryo because it is a tissue match for our older child, who needs a transplant: fine, and not merely as a regrettable necessity. If it is acceptable to choose an embryo partly for the sake of another person, I cannot see why it is unacceptable to choose one for the sake of the parents, which is what the family with three sons is doing.

An embryo carrying a variant that causes trouble only in a specific environment — a severe reaction to fava beans, say, or lactose intolerance: fine to pass over, though on reflection the condition exists only relative to a diet and a culture. That makes me wonder how much of what I am screening against is like this.

Deliberately transferring an embryo with Down syndrome, having screened the others: I think this is permissible, and I would not call the parents wrong, but I would find it strange in a way I cannot fully justify.

One further feature of the situation, which I assume matters: whichever embryo we transfer, the others never become anyone at all. So I cannot say we are harming the ones we leave out; there is no one there to be harmed.

The principle I would have offered a month ago, if asked, is roughly this: among the embryos available, bring into existence the one whose life can be expected to go best, where the life in question is the life of the child who would result. I offer it so that it can be tested and repaired rather than merely assessed.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of candidate principles stated precisely enough to disagree with, that captures as much of this as possible. Please tell me which verdict of mine each clause is there to accommodate, what the principle says about cases I have not listed — including one where the parents' interests and the child's interests diverge, and one where the trait in question is a difference rather than a deficit — and, honestly, which of my verdicts it cannot save. If the choice is between a complicated principle that preserves everything I have said and a simpler one that requires me to abandon the deafness verdict or the height verdict or the savior-sibling verdict, I would rather see both laid out and be told what each costs than be told that my intuitions are consistent when they are not.
