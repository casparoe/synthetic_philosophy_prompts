For the last few months I've been working through the case for eliminative materialism about the propositional attitudes, and I've come out further along than I expected, so what I want is not a defence but a stress test. Put the view this way: our ordinary talk of beliefs, desires, intentions and the rest is not a neutral description of anything real but the surviving vocabulary of a folk theory of behaviour, an empirical posit on the order of phlogiston or caloric, and it will be replaced rather than vindicated as cognitive science matures, so that a finished account of the mind will describe its causal economy without quantifying over beliefs and desires as genuine kinds. I want to add the sharper, more contestable claim that is really doing the work for me, because I suspect it is where the position either earns its keep or fails to: that as the predictive sciences of behaviour mature, the belief/desire vocabulary will be retained chiefly in legacy contexts — law, blame, self-help, the first person — and will quietly drop out of the models that actually predict and explain what organisms do, so that within a generation or two the best such models will not be phrased in terms of propositional attitudes at all.

Tell me what would count against this, sorted by how decisive it would be. I don't mean only the stock objections; I want the observations, arguments, intuitions, empirical findings, and formal results that should genuinely move someone who currently holds the view, including anything that has arguably already happened and any way the mind could turn out that would embarrass it. Say which of these a committed eliminativist would themselves concede as counting against them and which they would dismiss, and be honest about the border between the two. I am also worried that the position has quietly become unfalsifiable — a friend who builds computational models of decision-making insists that any successor theory will be a mere notational variant of belief-talk, and I can't tell whether that is a real objection or a way of guaranteeing that nothing could ever count against eliminativism. So tell me what would distinguish a genuine elimination from a redescription, whether my own prediction above is falsifiable in practice, and, if the whole thing is insulated from counterevidence, whether that is a fatal problem for a view of this kind or simply what we should expect from a high-level empirical thesis about the future of a science.
