I am preparing a graduate seminar in the philosophy of history and social epistemology, and for the next session I want a worked case study rather than a survey. The case is the archive of the East German Ministry for State Security — the Stasi records — and its afterlife since 1989: the surveillance apparatus that generated a vast documentary record of the population it policed, the hurried destruction of files as the regime collapsed, the sacks of torn paper that survived, the later reconstruction of some of those fragments, and the legal and moral uses to which the files were then put, including the exposure of informers and the reading of one's own file.

The framework I want you to test against it is one I teach, adapted from the literature on archival silence. It distinguishes four moments at which power shapes what a record contains: inscription (what the state deems worth writing down, and from whose standpoint); preservation (what is kept, what is destroyed, and who decides); access (who may read the record, under what conditions, and in what capacity); and narration (whose history the surviving record can be made to tell, and whose it cannot).

Please work the case through each moment in turn, naming the relevant agents at each and what their interests were, and then tell me where the framework bends or breaks. I expect at least two strains, and I want you to say plainly whether the framework can absorb them or whether they mark its limits. The first is that this archive is not principally a record of absence but of excess: the state recorded the interrogated, the watched and the reported-on at extraordinary length, while remaining largely silent about its own reasoning and about the ordinary lives that surveillance never reached. The second is that destruction here is not a mere failure to preserve but an act with an author and an intention, and that the later reconstruction of shredded documents yields a source that no one originally wrote — the archivist, in effect, becomes a co-author. Consider whether these are anomalies for the framework or confirmations of it, and what each implies for the broader claim that silences in the sources are produced rather than accidental.

Run the analysis in both directions, as I would ask of my students: use the framework to make the case intelligible, and use the case to test the framework, including the question of whether a single case can do that work at all. Where the framework has nothing to say — I suspect it has little to say about the victim as reader, about what it is to encounter oneself as an object in someone else's record — say so explicitly rather than improvising a fifth moment. If any part of the case turns on a factual detail you cannot verify, flag it rather than asserting it; I would rather have a hedged analysis than a confident one resting on contested numbers.

Please write the analysis in British English.
