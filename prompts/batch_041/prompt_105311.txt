I am a high school student preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club, and I have been trying to understand the knowability paradox without relying on any secondary literature. The argument I have reconstructed is this: suppose every truth is knowable. Now take some truth that is never actually known, call it p, and form the conjunction of p with the proposition that no one ever knows p. If that conjunction is knowable, then there is a possible situation in which someone knows both that p and that no one ever knows p. But if someone knows that no one ever knows p, then in that situation p is not known; and if someone knows that p, then p is known. That looks contradictory, so it seems to follow that not every truth is knowable; indeed, if every truth is knowable, then every truth is known. A friend in the club says this is just a trick: the proof only works because we let one person's knowledge of a conjunction count as knowledge of each conjunct, and because we treat knowable as could be known by someone at some time rather than something more restricted. Another friend says the real issue is that the argument shows only that there cannot be an unknown truth if all truths are knowable, which is a reductio of the anti-realist's knowability principle rather than a proof of omniscience. I would like a clear explanation of what is actually going on conceptually.

Could you explain what the paradox is really about — what the knowability principle is meant to do, what assumptions the derivation depends on (for example, the factivity of knowledge, the distribution of knowledge over conjunction, the existence of unknown truths, and the modal force of knowable), and why the conclusion is thought to be troubling? I am especially interested in whether the argument refutes the knowability principle itself or only a particular formulation of it, and whether there are ways of stating a broadly anti-realist view that avoid the result without simply denying that there are unknown truths. Please also address the friend's objection that the proof equivocates on knows or on knowable.

I would like the answer to separate clearly what is settled among people who study this from what is genuinely contested; where there are competing readings, please flag them as competing rather than presenting one as the consensus. I do not need citations or a reading list — just a careful conceptual explanation that would make sense to someone who knows the basic terms but has not seen the technical literature.
