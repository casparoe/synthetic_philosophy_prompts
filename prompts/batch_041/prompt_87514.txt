Analyse the New Chronology of Anatoly Fomenko — the revisionist chronology he developed from the 1970s onwards, largely in collaboration with Gleb Nosovsky — as a test case for the demarcation of science from pseudoscience, and then tell me what the case does to the demarcation criteria rather than only what they do to it. Fomenko is a genuine mathematician, a topologist at Moscow State University and a member of the Russian Academy of Sciences, and the programme is presented as a quantitative one: he claims to have devised empirical-statistical methods and applied them to chronicles to show that many historical events are misdated.

Here are the essentials, so that nothing in your answer turns on details you would have to look up. The programme holds that events conventionally assigned to ancient Greece, Rome and Egypt actually occurred in the Middle Ages, more than a thousand years later; that the conventional chronology is largely an artefact of early modern scholarship; and that the duplications can be detected statistically — by comparing the lengths and sequences of dynastic reigns, the distribution of names across chronicles, and the overlap between narrative texts. Fomenko also rejects the physical dating techniques, disputing dendrochronology and radiocarbon dating in particular, and argues that the evidence marshalled against him is not independent of the chronology it is used to confirm: the calibration curves, the tree-ring sequences and the dating of astronomical records all presuppose a framework, so that their mutual agreement is circular rather than corroborative. Historians and archaeologists, for their part, classify the programme as pseudohistory and largely decline to engage with it in detail, while it retains a substantial lay readership in Russia.

I want the analysis run through a criterion of my own devising, in two parts. First, exposure: a research programme is exposed to a body of evidence to the degree that, in advance of seeing that evidence, some specifiable outcome of it would be treated by the programme's adherents as counting against a central claim rather than against a peripheral one. Second, survival accounting: any inference from the absence of evidence — to the non-existence of a thing, to the forgery or lateness of a document, or to the silence of the record — must be paired with an explicit account of why the evidence would have survived to the present if the thing had existed or the document had been genuine.

What I want from you:

1. The mapping. Which claims in the programme are central and which peripheral; what, in advance, would have counted as exposure for it; and whether the statistical apparatus changes anything of substance or merely changes the register in which the claims are made — that is, whether quantification functions here as evidence or as rhetoric.

2. The same two-part test turned on the orthodox side. Do its practitioners meet their own standard of exposure and of survival accounting? Take the circularity charge seriously on its merits rather than dismissing it: how much of the dating evidence is genuinely independent of the conventional chronology, what work does that independence do, and where does the orthodox case rest on an inference from silence that it has not costed.

3. Where the criterion misdescribes the case. My own suspicion is that the first part is too psychological — that it locates the difference in the attitudes of adherents rather than in the structure of the claims — and that the second part is too weak, since a sufficiently inventive practitioner can always supply some survival story. Tell me whether that is right, and identify what the episode contains that neither part captures.

4. The return trip. What does this case show about demarcation criteria themselves? Does it favour the view that pseudoscience is a matter of the structure of a programme, of the conduct of its adherents, or of the ratio of problems solved to problems deferred — and does it suggest that any single criterion must fail here, or that the search for a single criterion is the wrong enterprise altogether?

Use only criteria you can state and defend; do not give me a survey of the demarcation literature or a reading list. Where a step in your argument depends on a fact about the programme or its critics that I have not supplied, mark it as an assumption rather than asserting it. Write the whole answer in British English.
