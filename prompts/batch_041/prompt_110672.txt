I'm putting together a reading group on how archaeologists get from objects to beliefs and institutions, and I want the spec written out before we start argueing about methods. So: the desiderata for a theory of that inference — what any adequate account has to deliver. Give me the cases it has to get right (a burial with rich grave goods, a cache of figurines, an abandoned house, a midden that some people insist is a ritual deposit, plus a couple where the obvious inference has actually gone wrong), the distinctions it can't collapse (belief vs practice, emic vs etic, absence of evidence vs evidence of absence, function vs meaning), and the results about how the record forms that it can't contradict — taphonomy, equifinality, the fact that the same material pattern can come out of very different behaviour. Someone in the group keeps saying the right image is a cable, not a chain: many weak independent strands rather than one strong link, and that this should change what we're allowed to demand of any single line of evidence. Take that seriously as a candidate desideratum and tell me whether it's a real requirement or just a nice metaphor. Flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be had together, and say which single one rules out the most otherwise popular approaches. Keep the weight on the requirements and the case behind each rather than on scoring schools.

Sections, in this order and roughly these lengths: "The cases it has to get right" (~400 words); "Distinctions it can't collapse" (~250); "Constraints from how the record forms" (~250); "The cable idea, taken seriously" (~200); "What does the most excluding, and what's contested" (~200). In that last section be explicit about which of these demands archaeologists genuinely agree on and which are live disagreements — don't sell a contested one as the consensus.
