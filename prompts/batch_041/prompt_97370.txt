Hi — novelist here, not a philosopher, so bear with me.

I've written myself into a hole. My new book has a first-person narrator who, on the last page, turns to the reader and promises her something — promises to tell the truth about a particular thing, promises to keep a secret, take your pick. The narrator is definately made up. I'm not. But the sentence sits there on the page doing... something. And I keep arguing with a friend who actually did a PhD in philosophy about whether I've made a promise.

So give me your actual credence in this claim, and I mean a number: when an author writes, in the voice of a fictional first-person narrator, a sentence that promises something to you the reader, the author has made a genuine promise to whoever reads the book — with the same normative force as if she'd said it out loud to a friend. Not a pretend-promise, not a literary gesture, an actual promise, the kind you can be wronged by breaking.

Then decompose it. I want the sub-questions the number depends on, a rough probability for each, and how they combine:
- whether fiction contains real illocutionary acts at all, or only pretend ones
- whether the promisor is me or the narrator, and whether a fictional person can be the one who's bound
- whether that you can pick out real readers well enough for the promise to have a proper beneficiary
- whether promising needs uptake, or an intention to be bound, that a novelist doesn't have
- whether genre conventions or the reader's expectations can manufacture the obligation where the intention is missing
- and whatever else you think belongs in there.

My number is around 0.3, hers is 0.75. So also tell me which sub-question our disagreement most likely lives on — is she just more willing to let fiction count as real, or do we actually disagree about what promising is? And which term you're least sure of, and what would move your number the most.

One more wrinkle, and I think it matters: I'm writing this one in second person, and the narrator occasionally addresses specific real people by name. Does that change anything, or is it alot more of the same mess?
