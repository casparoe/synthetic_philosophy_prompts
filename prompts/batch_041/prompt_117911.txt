I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and in three weeks I have to deliver the citation at a foundation dinner where the lifetime-achievement prize in documentary film goes to Marta Vogel. Eight minutes at a podium, in front of a room that splits cleanly in two. On one side: filmmakers, critics and funders, people who know the aesthetic arguments and will hear every hedge. On the other: the family from the valley where Vogel shot The Salt Line in 1990 — the brother and sister of the girl whose collapse is the last shot in the film, their neighbours, and a few of the people who worked on the lawsuit the film was supposed to serve.

The facts the speech has to survive are these. Vogel got access to that family by telling them the footage would be used in their case against the smelter; the finished film dropped the case entirely. The closing sequence is an unbroken four-minute shot of a fifteen-year-old girl learning on camera that her father had signed away the family's claim; she is named in the credits and was identified in the press for years afterwards. The film is also, I think, one of the two or three best documentaries made in this country, and the reason is that shot. I said as much in print at the time, in three hundred words I would rather not reread. Vogel has never apologised; she has always said the film was true. The brother and sister have asked me, through the foundation, not to pretend the shot doesn't exist and not to make the evening about them. Vogel speaks after me.

What I want from you is the speech itself. Three things have to hold at once. It has to make a real case for the prize rather than a shrug or a lifetime-achievement euphemism, by naming what that film does that almost nothing else in the form does. It has to state the cost plainly, in a sentence the family in the third row can sit through without wincing at the euphemism or at the exposure. And it has to be honest about the question underneath, which I doubt anyone in the room has settled: whether the wrong here is external to the work — a fact about how it was made — or internal to it, something the film asks of its audience every time it is shown. I want the audience to leave seeing that the disagreement is not about whether betrayal is bad, but about whether it belongs to the artwork at all; and I want them to see it without being lectured. Three positions should be audible in the room: that ethical defects do not touch aesthetic value; that they do, because a work which invites us to take pleasure in cruelty is worse for it; and that they do only when the defect lies inside the work, in what it solicits from its audience. I am not asking you to settle that question. I am asking for something I can stand behind when the press asks me about it afterwards, and that does not corner Vogel, who has to speak next.

Nine hundred to eleven hundred words, written for the ear: no footnotes, no vocabulary the neighbours cannot follow, and nothing that treats the film people as though they need the plot explained. Then a note of about a hundred words on the two places where that philosophical distinction forced a choice in the drafting.
