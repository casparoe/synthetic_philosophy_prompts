I chair the clinical guidelines committee at a mid-size teaching hospital, and I have to write the opinion we vote on at next month's meeting, so I'd like you to draft it for me.

Here's the problem. We're revising our protocol for when to start a particular preventive treatment, and the two people on the committee who know the most about it disagree with each other — and they've disagreed in three straight meetings now without either one moving. Same evidence, same trials, both extremely good at this. Call them A and B. A wants us to lower the threshold for starting treatment; B wants to keep it high. A leans on a subgroup finding from a big trial that B thinks is underpowered; B leans on a mechanistic story that A thinks is carrying more weight than it can bear. Underneath that, they disagree about how much a certain kind of observational data should count, and I suspect that traces back to different priors neither of them can fully spell out. Also relevant, maybe: B has been in the field about fifteen years longer and A trained under B. I dont know if that matters for how we should treat the disagreement but it keeps nagging at me.

The rest of the committee is split on what to do with the disagreement itself. One camp says: if our two best people disagree and neither can point to a mistake in the other's reasoning, then we can't honestly claim to know which way is right, so we should suspend judgment and default to the more cautious option. The other camp says: we're not supposed to track who's arguing, we're supposed to track the arguments — so just read both cases and go with the stronger one, even if that means siding with A against B; that's what the evidence supports and the rest is psychology.

I know the labels for this general area (conciliationism, steadfastness) but I do not want a literature review, I want a decision. Two things I keep going back and forth on and want the memo to hit head-on. First: if the conciliatory line is right, doesnt it undercut itself? Plenty of competent people reject it, and by its own lights that should make you less sure of it. Second: does it even make sense to apply individual-level norms about belief to a committee that has to produce one number? And a third, smaller one — is there anything to the idea that the right response to a disagreement you can't resolve is neither to conciliate nor to stand firm but to go get more evidence, and if there is, is that actually available to us here, or is it just a fancy way of ducking the decision?

So: give me the written opinion. A clear recommendation with a time frame — do we adopt A's threshold, B's, keep the status quo, or something else, and by when — plus the reasoning, plus an honest statement of the strongest thing the losing side has going for it. Don't cite anything, just argue it out. And put the bottom line at the very end as a final line beginning 'Verdict:' followed by the recommendation in a few words.
