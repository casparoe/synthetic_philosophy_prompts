You're a philosopher of mind and a metaphysician who's spent years on personal identity and on the metaphysics of computation — take that role seriously and think carefully before you answer. Start by brainstorming a rough list before you commit to anything.

Here's the deal. I've got a modest budget to support a small group — say three people, one year — working on mind uploading, and specifically the gradual-replacment version: swap out your neurons one at a time for functionally equivalent silicon until nothing biological is left. The sudden scan-and-rebuild version reads as a copy; the gradual version reads like it might be survival. I want to know where the real open questions are and which ones are worth that year.

I don't want the standard tour (branching, teletransporters, the usual Parfit furniture). Two things I've been chewing on that I think are under-explored, and I want you to weigh them against whatever else you'd put on the list:

1. A "drifting replacement": every individual swap is a locally perfect functional match, but the process is long and noisy enough that by the end the whole system has slid into a very different functional organization than it started with — no single moment of discontinuity, no preserved feature, just a slow drift. Is there a fact of the matter about whether the end-state is the same person as the start-state, or does the question dissolve? And does this differ in kind from ordinary aging, which is arguably also gradual replacement at the molecular level?

2. Stealing the causal-historical theory of reference — the picture where a name refers because of a causal chain running back to an original dubbing, not because of any description the speaker associates with it — and pointing it at the first person. Maybe "I" is like a rigid designator whose reference is fixed by a causal chain, so "is the upload me?" reduces to "does the chain of reference thread through the replacement, and to which successor?" Would that clarify anything, or just relocate the mystery?
