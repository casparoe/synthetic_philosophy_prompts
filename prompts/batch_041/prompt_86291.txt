Write me a dialogue between two people who both work on AI safety and are both sharp, but who disagree at a pretty fundamental level about whether safety work should be aiming for worst-case guarantees or just doing well on average over realistic inputs. Give one of them the mindset of a cryptographer: they think a system isnt safe unless it holds up against an adversary who's allowed to know your entire design and gets to choose their attack last, and that anything short of that is just a bet that nobody will bother looking for the hole. Give the other the mindset of an ML engineer: they think worst-case reasoning against an unbounded adversary is a well known reciepe for vacuous, useless systems, and that in practice you're always negotiating against some threat model, so the real questions are what the distribution of actual attackers looks like and how much you're willing to pay to shrink the tail.

To keep it from floating off into abstractions, have them fight over a made-up case. Lab A ships a model behind a hard rule-based filter that's supposed to make certain dangerous outputs impossible by construction — it blocks them at the output layer, no exceptions, that's the selling point. Lab B ships a model that's been red-teamed to death, thousands of adversarial prompts, jailbreak competitions, the works, but with no hard guarantee whatsoever, just a very strong empirical track record. Each of them thinks the other lab's approach is the irresponsible one, and I want to see that come out.

Some stuff I'd like them to actually dig into: whether "safe by construction" survives an adversary who's allowed to encode things, route around the filter, or just use a different tool entirely; whether the crypto analogy really cuts the way the cryptographer thinks it does, given that in crypto it's famously worst-case hardness that's the wrong notion and average-case hardness that you actually need for anything useful; whether "the average case" is even a stable thing when your own model's outputs are part of what shapes the distribution you'll be evaluated on; and what each of them would actually say to a regulator who asks, flat out, so is it safe or not?

They should respond to each others actual points rather than talking past each other, and neither should be a pushover — I dont want the crypto person being naive about impossibility results or the ML person being cavalier about tail risk. If they end up agreeing on something small, fine, and if they don't, also fine. Skip the citations and the name-dropping of researchers, just let them argue.
