I am a postdoc in a computational social science group, and next month it is my turn to run the weekly "one idea in forty-five minutes" session. The audience is about a dozen people — a few economists, two machine learning engineers, and a couple of philosophers — all of whom are fluent in the basics of game theory (they know the Prisoner's Dilemma cold and can define Nash equilibrium without notes) but none of whom, as far as I can tell, has ever encountered the idea I want to teach. I want to spend the session on program equilibrium: the setup in which the players of a one-shot game do not choose moves but submit computer programs, each program is given the other player's source code to read before it runs, and the payoffs are whatever the two programs output. A pair of programs is in equilibrium when neither player could improve her payoff by unilaterally substituting any other program for her own. What I want them to leave believing is not the definition but the demonstration that carries it: a program that cooperates if and only if the text it is handed is character-for-character identical to its own source, and defects otherwise, is a best response to itself in a one-shot Prisoner's Dilemma, so mutual cooperation is reachable even though defection dominates in the underlying move-level game.

Don't explain the idea to me; plan how I should explain it. I want the order in which the pieces have to go and, for each step, the earlier piece it depends on — I am convinced the whole thing collapses if any step is presented before its prerequisite, and I want to know exactly where that happens. Name the one example that should carry the session, say what it must have and lack in order to do that work, and tell me whether that example should be the Prisoner's Dilemma itself or something smaller that runs first. I want the misconceptions you would expect this particular audience to form, in the order they will form them, and precisely where in the sequence I should head each one off; I am already anticipating two, and I would like you to tell me whether they are the right two. The first is that the programs must simulate one another, which would drag the halting problem into the room. The second is that this is just tit-for-tat, or some folk theorem of repeated play, in disguise. Beyond those, I am worried about the audience feeling cheated — that the trick is a trick and not a result — and I want to know how to address that without pretending the setup is doing no work.

Finally, tell me what I should deliberately refuse to explain at this level and say so out loud, since I would rather leave them with a clean gap than a garbled sketch; give me one question to ask at the end that will show me whether the idea landed rather than whether they can recite the definition; and tell me the one thing about the setup that I should openly admit to the room I am hand-waving. No reading list, please — there is no time for it, and I would rather they leave with the construction in their heads than with three titles they will never open.
