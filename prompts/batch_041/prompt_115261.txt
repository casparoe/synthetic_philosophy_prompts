I have just come away from a long argument with a friend about Maimonides on animal sacrifice. My friend maintained that Maimonides treats the sacrificial commandments as a temporary concession to the historical circumstance that the ancient Israelites were immersed in idolatrous sacrificial practice, so that the commandments lose their point once that circumstance is gone. I found myself defending a stronger reading: the concession is to a permanent feature of embodied religious psychology, not merely to a historical contingency, which makes the commandments instrumentally necessary rather than intrinsically good. I want to turn this dispute into an exam question for a graduate seminar in philosophy of religion. Please write the question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme with five must-hit points totaling 20 marks, each accompanied by a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. The question should force students to distinguish the historical-contingency reading from the psychological-permanence reading and to say what each implies about whether the sacrificial commandments can be called rational; it should not be answerable by summarizing the Guide. Include a sub-question asking whether Maimonides' rationale would extend to a revealed religion with no prior sacrificial practice. No citations, reading lists, or direct quotations; paraphrase any textual claims.
