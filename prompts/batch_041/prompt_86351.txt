I host a small philosophy podcast, which I began after retiring from a career in experimental physics, and next week's guest is a philosopher of science who thinks the no-free-lunch theorems for learning and optimization are routinely overread. His line, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows: the theorems average a learner's performance uniformly over all possible objective functions, so what they establish is only that no learner is superior without some measure commitment; every successful learner carries an assumption about which functions matter, and that assumption is empirical and defeasible rather than a priori. He adds that the theorems have nothing to do with the problem of induction as Hume posed it — which algorithm wins on average across all possible worlds is one question, whether the future will resemble the past is another — and that the demand for a non-circular justification of the uniformity principle is not so much unmet as misconceived, since justification is always relative to a domain and a purpose. Write me eight to ten interview questions that force him to work rather than restate this. I want at least one question pressing on whether the claim that every learner carries a measure commitment is anything more than the theorem restated; one on whether dissolving Hume's demand changes the subject from justification to psychology; one on how his account treats a predicate like grue, and whether it excludes such hypotheses a priori or only through experience; and one on whether an empirical, domain-specific warrant for induction can survive the fact that any track record of inductive success is itself an inductive inference. Where a question admits the reply he is most likely to reach for, add a follow-up that takes that reply as its starting point. Please leave out anything about his intellectual biography.
