I have recently begun serving on a hospital committee that decides whether to adopt medical devices whose failure rates are well characterised, or newer devices whose failure rates are genuinely unknown to us, and I was struck by how my own choices changed depending on whether the well-characterised option was displayed alongside the new one. That sent me back to the Ellsberg literature, and I have drafted the argument below. I would like your comments on it, and I would rather have hard criticism than encouragement. I do not need references, reading lists, or citations; I will handle the literature myself. Where you are unsure whether some point is already established, please say that you are unsure rather than guessing, and where I have made a straightforward error of reasoning, say so plainly.

What I want to defend is this claim: the classic two-urn demonstration, together with the usual controls meant to isolate a single phenomenon of ambiguity aversion, does not identify a mechanism. At least three distinct mechanisms reproduce the classic pattern, and they come apart in one three-option design. If the claim holds it matters for practice, because the three mechanisms have different normative standing and different implications for how a committee like mine should present its options.

The classic pattern is the preference for betting on an urn of known 50/50 composition over betting on an urn whose composition is unknown, when the two bets are presented together. Three rival explanations, each of which reproduces that pattern:

First, worst-case reasoning. An act is evaluated at the least favourable probability distribution compatible with what you know. For the unknown urn the worst case is zero red, so the known urn wins.

Second, spread aversion. Your best estimate for the unknown urn may be one half, exactly as for the known urn, but you dislike the dispersion of your second-order beliefs and so take the known urn. On the usual setup, with all compositions equally plausible, this too reproduces the classic pattern.

Third, comparative ignorance. The aversion is produced by the side-by-side presentation of an option whose probability you can state and one whose probability you cannot, or by a felt lack of competence in the domain, and it weakens when no such comparator is salient.

These come apart once two more options are added. Let each urn hold 100 balls, with a red draw the winning event. Urn K contains exactly 50 red balls. Urn P contains at least 60 red balls, with nothing further specified. Urn Q is a fair coin flip between an urn with exactly 60 red and an urn with exactly 100 red.

The predicted rankings are as follows.

Worst-case reasoning ranks Q above P above K: the floor of 60 already beats 50, and Q leaves no residual ambiguity because its second-order distribution is fully specified, giving it a value of 0.8. This has a diagnostic consequence I find striking: if the classic aversion is driven by worst-case reasoning, it should reverse whenever the ambiguous urn's floor exceeds the known composition, so a two-option variant of the classic design should already be informative.

Spread aversion ranks P above Q above K, provided the dispersion penalty is smaller than the thirty-point advantage over K. P and Q share a mean of 0.8, but Q's second-order distribution puts all its mass at the two endpoints and is therefore the more dispersed of the two.

Comparative ignorance ranks K above P and Q, with P and Q roughly together, since both are ambiguous and the aversion is triggered by the presence of K.

So the comparison of K against P and Q separates comparative ignorance from the other two, while the comparison of P against Q separates worst-case reasoning from spread aversion.

The points where I am least confident, and where I would most like you to press:

(a) The P-versus-Q contrast assumes that the dispersion penalty is sensitive to the shape of the second-order distribution rather than only to the width of its support. If the penalty tracks the range alone, P and Q share a range and spread aversion predicts the two roughly together, which collapses my separation of it from worst-case reasoning. Is there a version of the contrast that does not beg this question?

(b) The design assumes that subjects treat Q as fully specified risk rather than as a compound lottery they decline to reduce. If they fail to reduce, Q looks much like P and the design loses its bite. How serious is this, and can it be handled by elicitation rather than by assumption?

(c) Urn Q is described in two stages and is harder to parse than urn P, so a difference between them may reflect comprehension or processing cost rather than any attitude toward imprecise belief.

(d) Telling subjects only that an urn contains at least 60 red balls may invite them to read the floor as informative about the experimenter's intentions, in the way that a bare minimum in ordinary conversation often is.

(e) I want to say that the classic demonstration is uninformative about mechanism, but a reader may reply that it was never meant to be informative about mechanism, and that it is a demonstration of a choice pattern rather than an experiment. Is my claim doing real work, or am I attacking a position no one holds?

The practical payoff I am after is this. If comparative ignorance is the driver, the remedy for my committee is presentational, and the aversion should not survive a change of framing. If worst-case reasoning is the driver, the aversion is a coherent response to what we actually know and should not be talked away. If spread aversion is the driver, the attitude is a genuine one toward imprecise belief, it survives even when the imprecision is favourable on average, and it cannot be removed by framing. Please tell me whether that three-way payoff survives your criticisms, which of the three mechanisms you would expect to dominate in a population of ordinary decision-makers, and what you would cut.
