I've been reading rationalist and effective-altruism blogs and forums for years, and I've never gotten a clean account of the priority view. A post I came across recently summarized it as follows, and I'd like to know how much of it I should keep:

"The priority view says that benefiting people matters more, morally, the worse off those people are: a given gain in well-being counts for more, the lower the level of the person who receives it. So far as I can tell it's egalitarianism with a friendlier face — the two views give the same verdict in almost every real case, since both hold that an unequal outcome is worse than an equal one, and both tell us to push resources toward the bottom until the gap narrows. Like other consequentialist views, it ranks outcomes by how much weighted well-being they contain. Its real foundation is the diminishing marginal utility of income: a hundred dollars to someone sleeping rough buys far more well-being than a hundred dollars to a hedge-fund manager, so the hundred dollars to the rough sleeper should weigh more heavily in the moral calculus. The standard objection to views of this kind is the leveling-down objection. If equality is what matters, then a world in which everyone is equally badly off is better than a world in which everyone is better off but unequally so, the worst off included. The priority view inherits that objection, and since almost nobody thinks leveling down is an improvement, the view looks unworkable. The lesson, I take it, is that we should go back to plain utilitarianism, with perhaps a mild thumb on the scale for the poor."

My instinct has always been that the whole point of the priority view was to get around the leveling-down objection, but I couldn't reconstruct the reasoning if you asked me to, and I suspect I'm mixing it up with something else. I don't need references — I want the substance.

Please go through the summary claim by claim. For each one, tell me what a philosopher working on distributive justice would accept as stated, what they would call a defensible simplification, what a defender of the priority view would object to, and what is simply wrong. I'm especially interested in the places where the summary's errors are load-bearing for its conclusion: does the closing verdict follow from any part of the summary that survives correction, and if not, what would follow instead? Where a distinction can be made vivid, give me a concrete case. In particular, I'd like one pair of outcomes on which the priority view and plain utilitarianism disagree, and a second pair on which the priority view and a rule of "always help the worst off first" disagree. Finally, if you had to rewrite the summary so that a careful defender of the priority view would sign off on it, what are the two or three changes that matter most?
