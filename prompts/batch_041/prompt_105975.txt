Having spent my working life in the natural sciences, I have only recently begun to read philosophy in earnest, and I have grown wary of the capsule summaries that circulate in popular introductions and secondhand reports. I have just encountered the following account of David Lewis's theory of convention, and I should like to have it audited before I let it settle into my understanding:

'Conventions, on Lewis's account, are at bottom agreements. When a group repeatedly faces a coordination problem, its members settle on one of the available solutions and treat that settlement as binding; the convention is the implicit bargain they have struck, held in place by each person's expectation that the others will keep to it. Because any of several solutions would have served equally well, conventions are arbitrary—there is no reason to prefer driving on the right to driving on the left—and Lewis concludes that they are for that reason rationally groundless: we conform to them only because others do. It follows that conventions are fragile, sustained only so long as some external authority or sanction keeps people in line. Generalizing the point, Lewis suggests that moral norms are simply conventions that have hardened over time, so that the evolution of norms amounts to the slow accumulation of arbitrary bargains, and morality rests on nothing firmer than the direction of the road.'

I should be grateful for a careful, claim-by-claim audit. Which parts would a reader well versed in Lewis accept as substantially right? Which are the sort of simplification a competent popularizer is entitled to make? And which are genuine misrepresentations—places where the summary runs together two things Lewis takes pains to distinguish, attributes to him a conclusion he would reject, or mistakes a familiar objection to his view for the view itself? Where the passage distorts, I should like to know what a defender of the account would put in its place; and in particular I want to know whether the sweeping conclusion it draws at the end—that moral norms rest on nothing firmer than the direction of the road—follows from anything Lewis actually holds, or whether it depends entirely on the earlier errors, so that correcting them would leave it unsupported. I confess I am inclined to think the summary is largely fair and that the arbitrariness of convention is the heart of Lewis's position; but I have learned to distrust my first instincts in this field, so please tell me where that inclination is itself a mistake.
