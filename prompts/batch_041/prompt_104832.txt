I teach a graduate seminar in the philosophy of economics, and next week a colleague from the economics department is joining us to discuss the welfare-theoretic foundations of behavioral public policy — defaults, nudges, and the rest of it. In preparation she circulated a short précis, and since I will be chairing the session I want to be certain I can separate what a careful economist or philosopher of economics would endorse from what is merely loose talk. My own instinct is that her précis is broadly the received view, but I have been burned before by the received view, and I would rather discover the trouble now than in front of the students.

Her précis reads: "Revealed preference theory, developed by Samuelson and given its modern axiomatic form by Houthakker, holds that a person's preferences are nothing over and above her choices: to prefer A to B just is to choose A when both are available. This was a decisive advance because it freed economics from the introspective psychology of the utilitarians, replacing talk of quantities of pleasure with a purely behavioral, ordinal notion of utility. Because utility on this view merely represents the ordering of choices, it makes no sense to say that someone chose what was worse for her, or that her preferences were distorted; and since preferences cannot be measured in a common unit, it is a scientific error to make interpersonal comparisons of welfare. Recent empirical work — preference reversals, framing and context effects — has refuted the theory by showing that choices are too inconsistent to reveal any underlying ordering. The moral is that utility is a useful fiction, and that welfare economics should give way to the study of choice behavior."

What I need from you is an accuracy audit I can act on. Go through the précis claim by claim: which parts would a well-informed economist or philosopher of economics accept as stated, which would they accept only with qualification or would wince at, and which are simply wrong? I am especially interested in three places where I suspect compression has done damage. First, the claim that preferring just is choosing: is that a definition, an empirical hypothesis, or neither, and what auxiliary assumptions about opportunity sets, stability, self-regard, and error does the reduction from choice to preference actually require? Second, the slide from ordinal utility to the impossibility of interpersonal comparisons: is that a theorem, a methodological stance, or an overstatement, and what would someone who works on the measurement of welfare say? Third, the announcement that preference reversals and framing effects have refuted the theory: what exactly do those findings falsify, and what survives them? I would also like to know what a defender of the choice-based program — someone who takes the behavioral evidence seriously but keeps the revealed-preference framework — would say in reply, whether the précis's closing inference about the fate of welfare economics follows from anything true in it, and, where the disputes are live rather than settled, which parts are consensus and which are contested, since I will be chairing and would rather not attribute a contested view to the whole room.
