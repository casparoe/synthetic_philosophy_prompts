I'm working through Maimonides' Guide of the Perplexed with a couple of friends (we're using the old Friedländer translation, which probly doesn't help) and we've ground to a halt in part 3, chapter 32. Here's the paragraph that's causing the trouble:

"But the custom which was in those days general among all men, and the general mode of worship in which the Israelites were brought up, consisted in sacrificing animals in those temples which contained certain images, to bow down to those images, and to burn incense before them; religious and ascetic persons were in those days the persons that were devoted to the service in the temples erected to the stars, as has been explained by us. It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action."

A bit further on he says God let this kind of service continue and transferred to His own service what had been worship of created beings — build Him a temple, offer the sacrifices, bow down, burn incense — while forbidding any of it toward anything else, so the traces of idolatry got wiped out without upsetting anybody.

What I can't work out is whether he's saying the sacrifices were never wanted for their own sake at all, just a concession to what the Israelites happened to be used to. Because a few paragraphs later he puts the obvious objection in the reader's mouth — these are fully spelled out commandments, with fixed seasons, how can they be only means to some other end, why not command the end directly — and his answer is basically that God doesn't change human nature by miracle, so the people had to be led the long way round, like the Israelites being taken through the wilderness instead of the short road. Is that an answer, or is it just moving the bump in the carpet? And if the sacrifices really are a means, does that make them second-class commandments, or is that the wrong way to read it? Also: the example he reaches for is prayer and fasting, so is he saying those are concessions too? Someone in our group called this "divine accommodation" and I want to know if thats the right label or if he's doing something narrower. Help me get straight on what this passage actually commits him to, and where the text leaves it open.
