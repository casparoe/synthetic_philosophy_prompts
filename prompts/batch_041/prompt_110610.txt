I've been teaching a unit on AI assistants in my ethics of tech seminar and I keep going back and forth, so I want to pin down what I actualy think. Case by case, going on gut: refusing to help me write a snippy email to a dean — overreach, just help. Declining to explain how to pick a pin tumbler lock — that one's fine, a gate there seems ok. Silently rewriting my self-deprecating sentence in a cover letter — bothers me more than an outright refusal would, even though it's less restrictive; I can live with being told no, not with being quietly edited. Refusing to help me plan a solo winter hike because it's risky — overreach, I'm a grown man. Refusing to give a bodybuilder his calorie targets — overreach. Refusing to give the same calorie numbers to someone who mentions a recent hospitalization for an eating disorder — fine, no complaint from me. And then a friend asked for help drafting a group message that was going to out someone's HIV status, and there I think the assistant should have pushed back hard, not just complied.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? My tentative one is: intervene only when the request foreseeably harms someone other than the user, and otherwise stay out. That gets the lock, the hike and the group message, but it also says yes to the calorie numbers for the eating-disorder case and no to pushing back on the resignation letter I wanted to fire off at 2am after a fight, and both of those feel wrong. Give me the best version of the principle you can, say what it predicts for a case I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop.
