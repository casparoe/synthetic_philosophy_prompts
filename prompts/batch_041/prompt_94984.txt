You are a philosopher of action with expertise in the logic of intention, the relation between intending and doing, and the structure of practical reason. Think carefully before committing to a diagnosis, and begin by briefly stating, in your own words, what each step of the argument below is claiming and what would have to be true for that step to hold.

Kavka's toxin puzzle, for reference: an eccentric billionaire will deposit one million dollars in your account tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin makes you painfully ill for a day but does no lasting harm. The money is in your account hours before the time for drinking arrives, and you are free to change your mind in the meantime; the billionaire can reliably tell whether your midnight intention is sincere. The standard verdict is that it can be rational to form the intention at midnight even though, by noon, there is nothing to be said for actually drinking.

A colleague argues that this standard verdict, taken seriously, proves far too much:

(1) It is rational at midnight to intend to drink at noon, even though at noon there will be no reason to drink.
(2) If it is rational at t1 to intend to φ at t2, then φing at t2 is rational; otherwise you would be rationally intending an act you know in advance will be irrational, which cannot be right.
(3) So drinking at noon is rational.
(4) What makes it rational is the intention itself: having committed to drinking, you have a reason to drink, since an agent who abandons intentions she has formed is less reliable and worse off, and that cost is real.
(5) So an intention can generate a reason for the very act it is an intention to perform.
(6) Now suppose a second billionaire, this one malicious, offers you a large sum for sincerely intending, tonight, to humiliate a colleague tomorrow — and offers it precisely because she wants you to become the sort of person who follows through on such intentions. Her offer does not require that you carry the intention out; she is paying for the disposition.
(7) By (2) and (5), that intention would make the act rational, and by (6) such an offer can be made for any act at all.
(8) So there is no act — however cruel, pointless, or contrary to your own ends — that cannot be made rational by a sufficiently large payment for the intention to perform it.

She concludes that the toxin puzzle is a reductio, so one of (1) through (6) must go, and she favors giving up (5), the claim that intentions can be reasons. I am unwilling to give up (1): I think the toxin verdict is correct. Tell me which step I should reject, why it looked so plausible, and what rejecting it costs me. If I have to choose, I would rather abandon (5) than (1), but I want to know what each concession buys and what it commits me to elsewhere. Be explicit about which step is doing the real work, and about whether any of the steps can be rescued by a distinction rather than given up outright.
