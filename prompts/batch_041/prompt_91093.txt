I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and a discussion at my book club last week left me with an argument I cannot shake. We were talking about whether guilt or shame is the better moral emotion — whether a person, or a whole culture, ought to be organized around one rather than the other — and one member of the group, who used to practice criminal law, said that shame is simply a panopticon. The reference was to the prison design in which a single tower at the centre can see into every cell while the inmates can never tell whether anyone is actually in it, so that they end up watching themselves; his claim was that this is exactly what shame does, that you carry an internalized gaze around with you and conduct yourself as though you might be seen, while the actual watching is done by nobody. Guilt, he said, is a different structure altogether: it is like a courtroom you operate inside your own head, where the question is not who is looking at you but whether you broke a rule you yourself accept, and where the verdict attaches to what you did rather than to what you are. He drew two conclusions from the comparison — that guilt is the more mature and autonomous of the two emotions because it does not depend on an audience, and that a culture can in effect choose between them, so a society that wanted to become a guilt culture could get there by dismantling the arrangements that keep people visible to one another and thereby defusing shame.

I would like the analogy audited rather than either waved through or dismissed, and I would like the audit conducted at the level of detail where the matter actually gets decided. Begin by laying out the correspondence explicitly — I am happy to read a table — telling me what is meant to play the role of the tower, the inmates, the architect who designed the building, the uncertainty about whether anyone is watching, and the resulting self-policing, and which features of the prison are supposed to be inherited by shame. Then tell me where the mapping breaks. Two things already bother me, and I would like them addressed head-on. First, if the tower is nothing more than the bare possibility of being judged, then a person's conscience, which is precisely the thing that makes guilt guilt, looks like a tower as well, and the analogy appears to distinguish nothing. Second, I can imagine feeling ashamed of something in complete privacy, with no audience actual or possible — some thought I would never act on and no one could ever learn — and I want to know what the tower is in that case, or whether the example shows the analogy is doing something other than what my friend takes it to be doing. I am also suspicious of his second conclusion: even if the analogy held up, it seems to assume that moral emotions can be installed or removed by rearranging social structures much as one might re-architect a system, and I would like to know what that assumption imports.

Please finish by telling me whether either of his conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, which of those disanalogies matter for the particular work he is asking the analogy to do and which are merely decorative, and what analogy you would substitute if this one turns out to be carrying more weight than it can bear. If the honest answer is that the analogy can establish something weaker than what he wants — a risk that shame is unusually easy to weaponize, say, rather than a verdict on which emotion is better — I would rather hear that than a tidy resolution.
