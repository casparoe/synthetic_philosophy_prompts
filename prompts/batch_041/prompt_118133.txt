I need to put my position on the strategy-stealing argument in writing for a philosophy of mathematics reading group, and I would like a consistency check before I commit to it. Here is what I hold. (1) In Hex the first player has a winning strategy, and the strategy-stealing argument establishes this without exhibiting the strategy. (2) That argument is licensed only because Hex is monotone, in the sense that a stone already on the board can never be a liability, and because Hex cannot be drawn; the monotonicity premise fails in chess, where zugzwang makes an extra move a liability, so the parallel inference to a White win is unavailable. (3) Chess is nevertheless determined: exactly one of White wins, Black wins, and draw with best play is the case, though nobody knows which. (4) A non-constructive existence proof delivers genuine mathematical knowledge, so knowing that a winning strategy exists is a real result even if no one can write the strategy down. (5) I am a constructivist about mathematical existence: a claim that something exists is established only by producing the thing, or a procedure for finding it. (6) I take the strategy-stealing argument to explain why the first player wins in Hex, since it shows the win cannot belong to the second player, while also holding that an explanation of a combinatorial fact must let you see the mechanism that produces it. Which of these are genuinely in tension, and which only look like they are? Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, give the cheapest revision for each, and say which single revision would resolve the most.
