I am a senior in high school, and I am trying to settle an argument that has been running in my philosophy club for several weeks. In calculus this year we spent a good deal of time on infinite series, and our teacher offered the convergence of the geometric series — one half plus one quarter plus one eighth, and so on, summing to exactly one — as the definitive answer to Zeno's paradox of the tortoise: since the infinitely many half-distances that Achilles must cover add up to a finite total, nothing stands in the way of his catching up, and the whole difficulty is an artifact of missing mathematics, since Zeno advanced his argument some twenty-five hundred years ago, in the fifth century BC, when no one had the notion of a limit. I found this satisfying until a friend in the club proposed an analogy I cannot get out of my head. A film projector produces the appearance of smooth, continuous motion even though every frame on the reel is a perfectly static photograph, and no one supposes that the projector must somehow complete an infinite sequence of intermediate stages in order to manage it. If a finite stock of still images suffices for motion, then the fuss about infinitely many stages looks like a distraction, and the arithmetic treatment looks like the whole story. I have always assumed that the companion arguments about arrows and racecourses are variations on the same arithmetic mistake.

What I would like to understand are the most important implications of what I take to be the settled state of the question. If the paradox was at bottom a confusion about whether an endless sum can have a finite value, and that confusion has now been cleared away, what follows for our understanding of motion, of time, and of the relation between a mathematical description of a thing and the thing itself? Does the resolution establish that motion is nothing over and above a body's occupying successive positions — that it is, so to speak, an arithmetic fact rather than a physical one — and if so, does that mean that everything about motion that can be settled has been settled? And if the paradoxes really are dead, I would be grateful if you would say plainly what, if anything, remains for philosophers to dispute, since my club's end-of-year essay must be about a live question, and I would rather not write fifteen hundred words that a university student would find naive.
