In my graduate seminar, a colleague spent a session on the familiar image of the self as a center of narrative gravity — the self as an abstraction we posit to explain why a person's attitudes, memories, and actions hang together, much as we posit a center of gravity to explain why a body balances where it does — and then pushed the image further than I have seen it pushed. His claim is that since a center of gravity is no fiction but a uniquely determined, measurable property of a mass distribution, one that genuinely explains an object's behavior, the narrative self deserves the same status: an objective property of the person, about which there are facts of the matter, and to which psychology and morality may legitimately appeal. I want an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on his conclusion.

Give me the correspondence explicitly — what is supposed to play the role of the mass distribution, of the frame of reference, of the definition that fixes the center, of the computation, of the observer, and of the observable behavior the posit is invoked to explain — and then separate the disanalogies that bear on his use of it from those that do not: the independence of the distribution from the computation performed on it, uniqueness and frame-invariance, the absence of rival computations that are equally good, the question of what the "mass" would be, and the awkward possibility that the distribution is partly constituted by the very narrative said to be computed from it. Does the realist conclusion survive, or was it carried by precisely the features the target lacks? If the latter, say what analogy would do better. Tell me how confident you are in your claims, and flag the point you are least sure of.
