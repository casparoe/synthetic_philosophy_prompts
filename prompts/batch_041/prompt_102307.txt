I have been trying to work out what I actually believe about the units of selection, proceeding from gut verdicts on particular cases rather than from any theory, and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Please treat the following as data to be explained, not as claims I am confident in.

My verdicts:

A killer plasmid in a bacterium carries genes for both a toxin and its antidote; a cell that loses the plasmid is killed, so the plasmid spreads through the population even though it costs its host. I think it is correct to say that the plasmid lineage is what is being selected here, and not the bacterial cell.

A transposable element in a mammal copies itself into more than half of the sperm a male produces, so it reaches more offspring than the rest of his genome does. I also want to say the element is selected — but I notice that I feel this is a *different* kind of thing from the plasmid case, and I cannot say why. The element builds nothing; it simply rides.

Stalk cells in a slime mold die in order to hold up the fruiting body, and the spores they lift are usually close relatives. I think the right description is that a cell lineage is being sacrificed for the aggregate, and yet I resist calling this group selection.

A bird gives a loud alarm call that draws a predator toward itself and away from its flock. I think this is straightforwardly selection on the individual acting for the group, and I do not feel tempted by a gene-level redescription, even though I know one is available.

A liver cell lineage that ignores the body's growth-arrest signals outcompetes its neighbors until the organism dies. I think the cell lineage is selected, and — to my own surprise — I think this is genuinely the *same* phenomenon as the plasmid case, despite the fact that the liver lineage, unlike the plasmid, is destroying the very vehicle it depends on.

A trait that lowers each carrier's survival but raises the population's chance of surviving a famine I do want to call group selection, even though by the reasoning I used in the plasmid case one could redescribe the very same trait at the gene level. This is the pair I cannot reconcile: structurally the two look alike, but my verdicts come out on opposite levels.

A mitochondrial gene in a plant sterilizes the plant's male function, which benefits the mitochondrion (transmitted only maternally) at the plant's expense. I have gone back and forth on whether to say the mitochondrial genome is the unit of selection here or the plant is, and I would like the answer to settle it.

I have a tentative principle of my own, which I call the replication-tracking principle: an entity is the unit of selection just when the variation that makes a difference to whether a *token* of it is copied is variation in the entity's own properties, and just when the copying in question is descent rather than mere persistence. I suspect this principle either collapses into triviality or else forces me to drop one of the verdicts above, and I would like to know which.

What I want from you is this. State the principle, or the smallest set of competing principles, that best fits the verdicts, with each clause motivated by the specific case that demands it. Tell me explicitly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and must overturn, and why the cost of keeping it would be worse. Say what it predicts for at least two cases I have not listed — I am particularly interested in a case where the candidate unit is a lineage of learned behaviors copied between individuals rather than a lineage of genes, and a case where the candidate unit is a consortium of unlike organisms that must reproduce together or not at all. If my replication-tracking principle can be repaired, repair it; if it should be discarded, discard it and say what replaces it.
