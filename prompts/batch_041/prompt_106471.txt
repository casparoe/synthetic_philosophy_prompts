Before I read any more papers on when some things make up a further thing, I want the spec first. Write me the desiderata that any adequate theory of composition has to meet — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts it has to stay consistent with — each one with the case or consideration behind it.

At a minimum I'd want it to say something about: Tibbles the cat losing his tail (and why there isn't also a "cat-complement" sitting on the mat); a statue and the lump of clay it's made of, occupying the same place at the same time; the problem of the many, i.e. my cat versus the zillion cat-minus-one-atom candidates; whether composition can be vague at all; gunk — matter with no smallest parts; things that gain and lose parts over time; and scattered objects whose parts aren't touching.

Two cases I'd like it to handle that I don't see discussed much, and honestly I care about them as much as the classics: (a) a slime-mold-ish creature that habitually pulls itself into two halves that wander off and later fuse back — is that one thing that split, or two things that merged, and does the verdict change if the halves are "the same" material? (b) me and my mitochondria and gut bacteria: if obligate symbionts are doing a lot of the living, are they parts of me or just things inside me? Whatever rule you give should return a verdict here without going ad hoc.

Then tell me which requirements are negotiable and which aren't, and whether any two of them can't both be satisfied — that's the part I actually want. And which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories? Don't bother scoring the theories against the list, I'll do that myself.
