I direct ethics education for the chaplaincy and palliative care service of a large teaching hospital, and I need a decision procedure our chaplains, nurses, and residents can run in a ten-minute case conference. The recurring difficulty is a patient's religious utterance that sounds like a factual claim — God will cure me; my mother watches over me; this illness is a punishment — and a clinician who must decide on the spot whether to question it, correct it, refer the patient for psychiatric assessment, or simply receive it. Our present defaults, cheerful agreement and the reflex referral, are both wrong, and none of my staff has read any philosophy.

Build the procedure out of Wittgenstein's treatment of religious belief rather than out of generic cultural-competence guidance. It should take the form of ordered questions, each with what counts as an answer and what that answer licenses, and it should mark its own limits explicitly: the point at which it stops the user because a picture has been mistaken for a hypothesis; the point at which it declares that the utterance is functioning as a prediction and can be argued with as such; the points at which it hands off to a capacity assessment, to safeguarding, or to the patient's own faith leader; and the points at which Wittgenstein gives no guidance at all and the team must simply decide. The distinction I most need operationalized is the one between an utterance held as a hypothesis, which stands or falls by evidence and for which having no grounds is a defect, and an utterance functioning as a picture that governs a life, for which having no grounds is no defect at all; so the procedure should include a question about what, if anything, the speaker would treat as counting against the utterance, and a question about what the utterance requires or forbids in their conduct. It has to survive the fact that the same words can do both jobs inside one conversation and can switch jobs mid-sentence, and it must contain a step that separates a clinician's inability to enter the picture from a defect in the belief. Do not let the clinician's own naturalism serve as the standard of reasonableness, and do not let the procedure harden into the rule that religious utterances are beyond criticism; the question is which kind of criticism fits, not whether criticism is permitted.

Then run it on three cases: a patient who refuses a curative treatment because God will heal him; a patient convinced that her late husband visits her at night, whom the team suspects is psychotic; and a patient who believes his cancer is a divine punishment and has stopped eating. At least one of the three should come out in a way the clinical team will dislike, and you should say which and why. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do.
