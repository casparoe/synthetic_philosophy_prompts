I chair the workforce monitoring review panel at a mid-sized logistics company. Roughly twenty-five proposals a year come to us — van telematics, warehouse pick-rate dashboards, wearable wellness schemes, transcription tools that record meetings — each with a business case and a data-protection assessment, each needing a recommendation from my panel within two weeks, and each touching several thousand employees who have no realistic way to opt out of the job. I know Foucault's work on discipline, normalization, and biopower well enough to see that a good deal of what we do is a version of what he describes; my panel — an HR business partner, a labour lawyer, and an operations manager — do not, and they will not read him. Please write me a decision procedure I can run with them in a ninety-minute session on any proposal that arrives, and that will produce a recommendation I can defend to the Chief People Officer and to the works council.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions, and for each question: what counts as an answer, what a clear case looks like at that step, and whether the step is a factual finding or a judgment the panel has to own rather than settle. It should end in a small number of verdicts, and it must be able to return the verdict that a proposal is not a disciplinary apparatus at all; a procedure that finds normalization everywhere is worse than useless to me. It should also let us say of a proposal whether it works on individuals, on aggregates, or on both, and why that difference changes the verdict. Write it as a checklist a non-specialist can follow, with the theory in the background rather than on display.

Be honest about the translation. Foucault is doing genealogy and diagnosis, not applied ethics, and he refuses the frame my panel and my lawyers will reach for: power as prohibition, power as something a group possesses, power as legible from the intentions of whoever designed the system. Tell me which of his distinctions have to survive the compression for the procedure to be worth anything, which can be dropped, and where the procedure is doing something his analysis would refuse — in particular, it must not smuggle in a test of freedom, consent, or proportionality and present the result as his. If consent and coercion belong in the procedure at all, say where and why, and do not make them the first question. The General Counsel will run a proportionality analysis regardless; say what my procedure adds that hers will miss, and how to write the recommendation so that it survives her reading.

Apply it to three proposals.

First, pick rates: individual dashboards, a weekly percentile ranking of every picker, a coaching trigger for anyone below the fortieth percentile two weeks running, and a leaderboard by shift.

Second, a wellness scheme the company calls voluntary: a wearable tracks sleep and heart-rate variability, produces a team readiness score that shift managers can see and informally use when scheduling, and shows the individual nothing but their own numbers and an algorithm's advice.

Third, telematics: an automatic alert when a van exceeds the limit by more than eight kilometres per hour, and an automatic deduction from the month's bonus. No ranking, no score, no dashboard, no coaching.

For each, give the verdict, the questions that decide it, and the reasoning at the steps where the panel will want to argue. I expect the second and third to come out in an order I will not like; if the procedure makes the harsher proposal look better than the humane one in the respect that matters, show the reasoning plainly and tell me whether the panel should trust it. Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire, how we will notice that we have applied it badly, and what it cannot decide for us.
