A system whose rules include the rules for changing rules, and which permits those rules to be changed by the very procedure they define, faces what I will call the self-amendment problem: if the rule that authorizes a change is the rule being changed, what authorizes the change? I want the problem worked through against a real institutional episode rather than a toy case, and then the episode turned around to test the problem.

The episode is the United States Senate's handling of its own cloture rule. Rule XXII requires three-fifths of senators duly chosen and sworn to end debate, and it protects itself: ending debate on a proposition to amend the standing rules takes two-thirds of those present and voting. In November 2013 the majority did not attempt to amend the rule. It raised a point of order that the cloture threshold for nominations other than Supreme Court nominations is a simple majority; the presiding officer ruled against the point of order; the ruling was appealed; and because an appeal on a nondebatable question is itself nondebatable, the appeal was decided by a simple majority, which overturned the chair and established a precedent displacing the text. The majority did not present itself as breaking the rules; it presented itself as reading them correctly, on the view that the Senate's power to determine its own rules is a continuing power that no prior rule can entrench against a later majority. In April 2017 the same route extended the precedent to Supreme Court nominations. The maneuver has been repeatedly proposed for ordinary legislation and never successfully used there, and the last actual lowering of the threshold, in 1975, went through the ordinary amending route.

Four answers to the self-amendment problem. Prior authorization: an amending act is valid only if a rule in force at the time authorized it, so a self-amendment is valid only if the old rules already permitted it. Official practice: validity consists in the convergent treatment of the relevant officials, so the ultimate rule is whatever their practice settles on and a change is valid once they treat it as such -- the Hartian rule of recognition. Rupture: a change at the level of the ultimate rule is not a legal change at all but a break in the chain of validity, after which a new ultimate rule is simply presupposed -- the Kelsenian thought that a change not authorized by the existing basic norm is a revolution. Pseudo-problem: nothing forbids a single act from being governed by the rule it alters, so no vicious regress is generated and the appearance of paradox dissolves once rule and act are distinguished.

What I want is a mapping in both directions. First the case under the theory: say precisely what changed in 2013 -- the text of a rule, a precedent, or a practice -- and which of the four answers the majority's claim expresses and which the minority's objection expresses. Then say which answer best fits what actually happened, and what each of the other three must say about it. On prior authorization, was the 2013 precedent valid, and what follows for everything confirmed under it since? On official practice, can the minority coherently complain that the rules were broken, once the practice has settled the other way? On rupture, is amending a standing rule a change in the ultimate rule or a change authorized by it? On the pseudo-problem view, why did the majority need a route that avoided a vote on the rule at all? Then the theory under the case: does the precedent's durability, including its application by the party that objected to it, vindicate the practice answer and embarrass prior authorization? Does the maneuver's disuse on legislation show that the practice answer needs something the self-amendment problem does not supply, namely an account of when official convergence holds and when it fragments? Does the availability of the maneuver to any future majority, coupled with its disuse, support or undermine the claim that a change at this level is a rupture?

Close by saying where the problem is silent rather than forcing it to speak: whether it can, on its own resources, distinguish a rule-change valid because authorized from one valid because accepted; whether that distinction is one the participants themselves can draw; and whether the problem can say which of the four answers a system ought to adopt, as opposed to which one merely describes it.
