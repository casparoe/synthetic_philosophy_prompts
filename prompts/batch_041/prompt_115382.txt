Ok so I'm writing the final for my upper-level seminar on animal minds and moral status — third and fourth years mostly, two grad students sitting in, 90 minutes, closed book, no internet. I want a single essay question on the scale argument about insect sentience, i.e. the move where the sheer number of insects is supposed to swamp the fact that any given insect is probably not sentient, and I want it to actually discriminate. Every draft I've written so far can be aced by a student who recites 'bugs are everywhere, we don't know if they feel anything, so the expected amount of suffering is enormous' and then lists a few positions. Thats not what I'm after. I want them to have to run the argument and find where it gives.

Since they can't look anything up, the question has to stand on its own: put the setup inside the question and don't lean on particular figures, studies, or who-said-what. I'd much rather you invent a concrete case than have them recall a real one. Something in the neighbourhood of: a stipulated reliable method tells you the chance that a given insect is sentient, and the question is what that does to how you'd spend a fixed pot of money when the alternative is preventing one human from a year of severe pain. But design it yourself, and keep the numbers obviously hypothetical and round enough that nobody has to do real arithmetic.

Then give me three things: the question itself (one question, not three — I want depth over coverage), a model answer of around 650 words, and a marking scheme built on five must-hit points worth 4 marks each (20 total), with a one-line note per point on how candidates typically miss it, plus a short note on what separates an A from a B. I want that A/B line to turn on specific intellectual moves, not on polish or length.

Some things I'd like the question to force, and you can weave these in as you see fit: whether the probability that a given insect is sentient and the probability that the whole kind is sentient are doing the same work, what job the moral weight of a possibly-sentient insect's experience is actually doing in the sum, and whether the whole thing is best read as a reductio of insect-sentience scepticism or of expected-value reasoning itself. It definately shouldn't be answerable by 'we just don't know enough' — a good answer has to commit to something and defend the commitment. If a student could pass it by reciting a summary of the debate, the question is wrong, and I'd want you to fix it rather than paper over it.

No citations needed anywhere in this, and don't worry about the literature.
