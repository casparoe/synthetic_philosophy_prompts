I've been argueing with a couple of friends about Hegel's take on war, and I've ended up defending a version of it that I want you to try to shoot down. The view is roughly this: war, or at least the real possibility of war, isn't just a necessary evil that a state tolerates; it has a positive ethical function. It keeps a society from dissolving into private comfort and consumerism, it forces a people to treat its collective existence as something it might have to sacrifice for, and it prevents the kind of sclerosis that Hegel thought threatens any long period of peace. On this view, a world of permanent peace wouldn't just be unrealistic; it would risk a kind of ethical atrophy, a society that no longer knows what it would die for. I'm not asking for a reading list or citations, just the philosophical case. What would count against this position? I want the evidence, arguments, and historical or anthropological considerations that should move me, ranked from the ones that would really hurt the view to the ones that are merely awkward. Which of these would a committed Hegelian actually concede as counting against them, and which would they just dismiss as misunderstanding the view? And be honest about whether the position risks becoming unfalsifiable: if any peaceful society looks vibrant, the Hegelian can say the war possibility is still doing the work in the background, and if it looks stagnant, that just proves the point. Is that a fatal flaw, or is it just the normal cost of a view about long-run social dynamics?

One specific thing I've been stuck on, and I'd like you to take seriously as a possible defeater: imagine a society that gets all the supposed benefits of war without any war, through something like mandatory nonviolent national service, rotating competitive festivals, and a shared project of, say, ecological restoration or space exploration that demands real sacrifice and produces the same kind of collective seriousness and anti-sclerosis effect. If that society were stable and psychologically realistic, would it count against the Hegelian view? Is it a decisive counterexample or just a weird edge case? I'm also curious about the difference between actual war and the mere possibility of war: does the view need war to happen, or only to be a standing threat? If it's only the threat, what would count against that weaker version? Finally, what would you personally find most persuasive as a defeater, and what would you say to someone who thinks the whole Hegelian line is just a romantic gloss on violence? I'd like the answer to separate claims about what war does empirically from claims about what war means ethically, and to say where the view stands or falls with each.
