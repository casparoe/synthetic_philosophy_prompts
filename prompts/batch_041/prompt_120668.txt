For the past three hours I have been arguing with my friend Mira, a philosopher of economics who has just finished a postdoc on idealisation in economic models, and I have come away quite unable to reconstruct the rule she is actually running. The occasion was a reading group on Friedman's 1953 essay on methodology, the one whose central claim Samuelson nicknamed the F-twist, and we spent most of the evening on her verdicts about a long list of models she has refereed or taught. She holds, with no hesitation at all, that the following are perfectly legitimate pieces of idealisation: neglecting air resistance and treating the bob as a point mass in a pendulum model; treating the planets as point masses and ignoring every body but the sun and the planet; assuming that there are only two countries and two goods in a trade model; assuming a single representative household with fixed preferences; assuming that agents form rational expectations; and assuming that firms maximise profits even though the managers in the industries she works on plainly satisfice. She holds, and got rather heated about it, that the following are not legitimate: assuming in a model of pension sustainability that everyone lives forever; assuming that labour supply is completely inelastic in a model whose entire point is to estimate how taxes change labour supply; and assuming that buyers know the quality of every good in a model of a market for used cars. What defeated me is that she cheerfully conceded that the eternal-life pension model fits the historical data better than any of its rivals and still called it bad modelling, while conceding that managers do not maximise profits and calling that good modelling. At one point another student offered a distinction between assumptions that treat some factor as negligible, assumptions that merely mark out the domain within which a theory claims to apply, and assumptions that are pure heuristic scaffolding; Mira said that was exactly the right distinction and then applied it in a way I could not follow, and we have a bottle of wine riding on who can predict her next verdict.

Here is the case I want you to call. She has been sent, as a referee, a model of housing bubbles whose central assumption is that all buyers and sellers hold identical beliefs about future prices and have identical information, and which is used to explain why speculative bubbles form and why they eventually burst; the paper fits thirty years of price data better than any competing model in the literature, and the authors' defence of it is explicitly Friedmanite. I would like you to act as a philosopher of science who works on modelling and idealisation, and to think carefully before you commit to anything. Start by brainstorming the candidate rules that would reproduce her verdicts on the earlier cases, listing four or five and then discarding those that cannot survive contact with the whole record; then tell me which of the surviving rules her verdicts genuinely leave open, what each of them predicts about the housing-bubble paper, which verdict you would stake my bottle of wine on and why, and which case — drawn from her existing verdicts or invented — would best tell the surviving rules apart. I would also like you to say plainly where her verdicts look inconsistent, and whether the inconsistency can be repaired by a distinction she is entitled to draw or whether it exposes something she has not noticed about her own position. Please answer in British English.
