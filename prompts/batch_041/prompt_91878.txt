I sit on a citizens' commission that has been asked to draft a new charter of rights for our region, and the draft we are converging on tries to combine two commitments that a colleague insists cannot coexist. The first is a short list of protected personal spheres — decisions each person is to make for themselves, over which no majority may override them (what they wear, whom they associate with, what they read). The second is an efficiency clause: any policy change that leaves no one worse off and at least one person better off is to be adopted. My colleague, who did graduate work in social choice, keeps invoking Sen's liberal paradox and says the two commitments are formally incoherent together, at least once you let the agenda be reasonably open. I accept that the result exists; what I want from you is the honest accounting rather than the slogan.

Please lay out the most important implications of Sen's liberal paradox — for a charter like ours and for the wider questions the result bears on. I am after the implications that are genuinely load-bearing rather than decorative: what the theorem actually forces, as distinct from what people read into it; what it implies about whether rights can be represented at all as individual decisiveness over pairs of alternatives, and what the serious alternatives look like (I have heard, for instance, that one might recast rights as control over the available moves in a game rather than over outcomes); what it implies for constitutional design and for the general practice of carving out spheres of personal sovereignty; and whether the familiar claim that a Paretian liberal is impossible is really as strong as it is usually made out to be. Separate the implications that survive under any plausible formalization from those that are artifacts of one particular setup, and tell me which of the widely cited implications are in fact overstated.

Finally, be candid about how much weight your central verdict can bear — in particular on whether the paradox exposes a deep and general tension between individual rights and collective efficiency, or is largely an artifact of how rights were formalized in the first place — and say what would change your mind: which argument, which formal result, or which kind of counterexample would push you toward the opposite verdict, and where your reasoning is most exposed to error.
