I have been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and the argument I keep meeting there — sometimes as a puzzle about where to draw a line, sometimes as a proof that there are no heaps, no bald men, no persons, and no species — is the sorites. Every introduction to it I have found hands the reader a handful of labels and leaves them to work out which one describes their own view. I want a diagnostic instrument instead, and I would like you to write one.

Please produce a decision tree for the sorites and for vagueness generally, laid out as a flowchart that a reader can walk through one question at a time. Start every reader at the same place, with the package almost everyone accepts before they have thought about the paradox: (1) a single grain is not a heap; (2) no grain added to a non-heap ever turns it into a heap; (3) ten thousand grains certainly do make a heap. Classical reasoning cannot keep all three, and the reader would rather not abandon classical reasoning. The tree's job is to show that this package cannot be kept whole and that each way of giving something up corresponds to a recognizable position.

Build the tree from the logical space, not from the list of names. The branch nodes should be questions about the reader's commitments rather than questions about which label they prefer: whether a borderline sentence has a truth value at all; if it does, whether the boundary is fixed by the world, there to be discovered, or only by our conventions and use, and whether the fuzziness is in the territory or only in the map we draw of it; if it does not, whether what is missing is truth, knowledge, or meaning; whether a single context of utterance pins down a boundary even when the predicate as such has none; whether there is one correct way to make the predicate precise or many equally admissible ones; whether the predicate has any extension at all. Each node must be phrased as a question the reader can answer without already knowing where it leads.

Then locate the actual positions in the space you have built. At minimum: the view that vague predicates do have sharp boundaries, which we simply cannot know (usually called epistemicism); the view that borderline sentences lack a truth value, while the law of excluded middle survives because every admissible precisification agrees (usually called supervaluationism); the dual view that they have too many truth values rather than too few; the view that they have intermediate degrees of truth; the view that the boundary is fixed by the context of utterance and shifts as the conversation proceeds, in both the sharp-in-context and the boundaryless versions; and the view that the predicates are simply empty. Where a leaf is a standard position, name it; where a combination of answers is coherent but has no established name, or is defended by no one you know of, say so plainly and describe it in a sentence. If you use a position's name, do not worry about attributing it to a particular author or work.

Make sure the tree shows, on a single branch, exactly where epistemicism and supervaluationism part company, and that the question at that branch is one a reader could answer without already siding with either. For every leaf, add one sentence saying which of the three starting claims it gives up and at what cost.

Two further requirements. First, do not run the tree only on heaps and bald men: test each branch on at least one predicate from outside the textbook — whether a given record counts as personally identifiable information, whether a defendant is guilty beyond a reasonable doubt, whether a stretch of land is the territory of a species, whether a patient is clinically depressed — so that a reader can run the tree on a case that matters to them and find the same structure. Second, the tree must survive higher-order vagueness: wherever a node turns on a borderline distinction, say what happens when that distinction is itself borderline, and whether the position can stop the regress or must simply accept it.

Finally, be honest about what the tree cannot do. If two leaves differ only by a relabeling, or if a branch cannot separate two positions whose defenders insist they are distinct — for instance the epistemic view and the view on which the boundary is fixed by convention but happens to be unknowable — say so rather than inventing a question to keep them apart. For any combination of answers that nobody seems to occupy, say whether you think it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has explored it.

Keep the whole thing to six to nine branch nodes and roughly eight leaves, and make it usable as a handout for a reading group that has spent a few weeks on the paradox. Do not include a reading list or citations; I want the structure, not the scholarship.
