I'm in a metaphysics reading group that's been on dispositions for a few weeks and I keep getting into arguments with a friend in it, because I hand out verdicts on her cases and she says they can't all be right. Here's what I actually think. A jar of sugar cubes with a wizard's field rigged over it so that no sugar in the jar can ever dissolve the instant it touches water: I say that sugar is still soluble -- the field is just a lid on the water, it's not about the sugar. A lump of plastic that would never dissolve in anything, but a wizard would make it dissolve the moment it hit water: not soluble, that's the wizard, not the plastic. Same for wires: a live wire with a fink that cuts the current the instant you touch it is still live, and a dead wire with a fink that connects it the instant you touch it is still dead. A glass packed in foam so that it will never break in a million years is still fragile. So my rule so far seems to be: whether a thing is disposed to do something is about the thing itself, and whether it ever actually gets to do it is beside the point.

But then I don't think a diamond is fragile, even though I could smash one with a sledgehammer, and I do think a wine glass is fragile, and a sledgehammer would smash that too -- so "fragile" has to be about how a thing holds up under ordinary handling, not about what it does under any old conditions. And it gets worse. Sugar that only dissolves if the water has a trace mineral that's in all normal water: soluble. Sugar that only dissolves when a wizard has a spell running: not soluble. From the sugar's point of view those look identical -- in both, something outside the sugar has to be there -- so why does the mineral count and the spell not? And this is the one that really bothers me. My friend tells a joke that nobody minded five years ago, and now the same joke in the same words offends people, and I want to say the joke is now disposed to offend, even though the joke hasn't changed at all. But if a thing can gain a disposition from a change that's entirely outside it, I don't know why I was so sure the plastic plus the wizard wasn't soluble. One more, about people: a friend of mine would give to every cause a generous person would give to, but only because she was hypnotized into it, and I don't think she's generous; another friend is generous and just happens to have no money and never meets anyone in need, and I do think she's generous.

So is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? I keep half-formulating one -- something like "the disposition is the thing's own basis plus whatever background conditions we're willing to call normal, and the manifestation never has to happen" -- but "we're willing to call normal" is doing an awful lot of work there, and I suspect it's either circular or smuggling in a whole theory of which dispositions are the real ones. Please state the best version or versions of the principle precisley, say which of my verdicts forces each clause, tell me what it says about cases I haven't thought of (a key and a lock, maybe, or a trait that only ever shows up in one kind of situation), and be honest about which of my verdicts has to go if I want the principle simple. I'd rather loose a verdict than keep a Frankenstein principle, but tell me which one I'm losing and what the simpler view says instead.
