I host a philosophy podcast, and my next guest is a philosopher who defends what she calls a topology-first account of supertasks. Her central claim is that questions of the form "what state is the system in once the task is complete?" have no answer until one specifies a topology on the system's state space, and that the familiar disagreements over Thomson's lamp — that it ends up on, that it ends up off, that it ends up neither — are really disagreements about which topology is appropriate rather than disagreements about the lamp. Draft eight to ten interview questions that press this view instead of letting her restate it. At a minimum, please include one question on each of the following, and for each of those a follow-up tailored to the answer she is most likely to give:

(i) the conventionalist objection — if the verdict is fixed by our choice of topology, in what sense is there a fact about the lamp at all, and how does her position differ from the older complaint that the task simply fails to specify any final state;

(ii) whether a physical system can single out a topology on its own or whether the topology is always something we impose, and what turns on that answer;

(iii) the "no last action" worry — since the toggles are indexed by the natural numbers and there is no last natural number, the final state is not produced by any toggle, and I want to know how a topology-first account explains the presence of a state that no step produces;

(iv) whether her account draws a principled line between tasks that are physically impossible and tasks that are merely underdescribed, and where that line falls for something like a lamp that would have to be switched ever faster; and

(v) whether her account says the same thing about Zeno-style continuous motion, and if not, why the same topological apparatus yields a determinate answer there but not for the lamp.

I would also like two or three questions that introduce cases she has not, so far as I know, addressed — for instance a lamp that is on at step n exactly when n is prime, and a "block" lamp that is off for one step, on for the next two, off for the next three, on for the next four, and so on — asking in each case whether her account treats these differently from the ordinary alternating lamp, given that none of the three has a final toggle. Keep every question specific enough that a listener who knows the literature would find it substantive, and avoid anything that invites her to explain what a supertask is or to say what first drew her to the topic.
