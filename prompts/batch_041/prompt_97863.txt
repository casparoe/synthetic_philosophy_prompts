I sit on the benefits/coverage committee at a mid-size health insurer -- we're the ones who decide what the plan actually pays for -- and I keep tripping over the same thing, and I want someone to think it through with me properly rather than just name-drop the usual philosophers at me.

Here's the pattern that bugs me. We pay for drugs to push down a blood-pressure number. A few years back the main US heart bodies moved the cutoff down from 140/90 to 130/80, and tens of millions of people who felt fine and had been told they were healthy woke up with "hypertension" and a prescription, with nobody's body changing. We pay for glasses for near-sightedness, which is just your eye doing the wrong thing -- but age-related far-sightedness, which is the exact same kind of blurry-vision problem with the same fix, we call "normal aging" and mostly don't cover. We cover erectile-dysfunction drugs; we don't cover hearing aids for the hearing loss nearly everyone eventually gets. We cover antidepressants for depression -- and the diagnostic manual a while back dropped the old rule that said you couldn't call it depression if it started right after a bereavement, and then later turned around and made prolonged grief its own disorder. Homosexuality sat in that same manual as a disease for years and then didn't. Meanwhile I've got two live requests on my desk: one to cover a drug for a newly minted condition that's defined purely by a lab marker predicting a future stroke in people who feel perfectly fine, and one to cover a "longevity" drug whose entire pitch is that aging itself is a disease we should be treating.

So here's what I actually want. Tell me what theory of disease this whole apparatus is secretly assuming. Is it the naturalist one -- disease is just biological malfunction, the body failing to do what it's designed to do, facts not values? Is it the normativist one -- disease is whatever we decide is bad and worth fixing, so the line moves whenever our values or our tools move? Or is it some hybrid, like disease = a malfunction that's also harmful, which is what I half-suspect we're running on without admitting it? And be honest about the difference between what the practice strictly *needs* to presuppose and what it merely happens to fit, because I have a suspicion we could run this same coverage rulebook on two or three incompatible theories and nobody at the table would notice.

Then walk me through the mess: which rival theories our setup quietly rules out, where our own practice contradicts itself (I think the risk-factor-versus-disease thing is where it's worst -- is a risk factor for a disease itself a disease, or not?), and where the practice is genuinely underdetermined so that several theories would license the exact same coverage decisions. Finally, tell me what would visibly change on the ground if we swapped the theory out -- if we went strict naturalism, or strict normativism -- and whether "the cutoff moved overnight" is a bug or just what the normativist expects.

Please don't hand me a reading list or citations, I won't chase them down and I don't care who said it first. Just the reasoning.

Give it to me in these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:
1. "The practice, stated plainly" -- what we're actually doing, in my terms, no jargon (~150 words).
2. "What it has to presuppose" -- the implicit theory of disease, spelled out (~250 words).
3. "What it rules out" -- the rival theories the setup can't live with (~200 words).
4. "Where it contradicts itself" -- the risk-factor case and any other flip-flops (~250 words).
5. "Where it's underdetermined" -- what the practice leaves open (~150 words).
6. "What changes if we swap the theory" -- pure naturalism vs pure normativism, concretely (~200 words).
7. "Bottom line" -- the one or two sentences I'd say at the next comittee meeting (~80 words).
