I am reconstructing the account of rational choice held by a philosopher whose graduate seminar I have been attending, and I want to predict how she will judge a case she has not yet discussed. Her verdicts on the cases that have come up so far are these.

- Two granola bars, indistinguishable to the chooser in brand, flavor, and price: she says either choice is fully rational, requires no further reason, and betrays no defect. There is nothing the chooser is failing to see.
- A life devoted to music and a life devoted to mathematics, as options for one person: she insists these are neither equal in value nor ranked — that it would be a mistake to say either is "just as good as" the other — and that choosing either is rationally permissible.
- Two desserts differing in price by one cent: she treats the difference as negligible and the choice as rationally free.
- A chooser who resolves all ties by a standing habit — always the item on her left — and applies it without exception: she says the habit is not required but is legitimate, and that choices made through it are no less rational than any others.
- A chooser among incommensurable options who, asked to justify her pick, answers only that she "just chose one": she says this is unsatisfactory. There were real reasons on both sides, and the chooser should be able to say what drew her to the option she took, even though those reasons do not establish that it is better.

The new case: a woman is torn between two options she regards as incommensurable — taking a demanding position in a distant city, or remaining near a parent whose health is failing. She takes the position. Six months later, with no change in her information, her circumstances, or her values, she is offered the chance to reverse, and she does. Six months after that, again with nothing new, she reverses back, and then back again, indefinitely.

I want your prediction of her verdict on the woman's rationality, but I am less interested in the bare verdict than in the rule she is running. Set out the two or three rules that fit all five of her earlier verdicts equally well; say what each predicts about the new case; give me your best guess at which rule she is actually running, with your reasons; and name the further case that would most sharply separate the surviving rules. Be explicit about where the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new one, and take a definite position rather than surveying the options and leaving the choice to me.
