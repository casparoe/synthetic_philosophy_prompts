I'm in a third-year philosophy of perception module and we've spent two weeks on the arguement from illusion and disjunctivism, and I've managed to get myself completely tangled. The argument from illusion as we set it up goes like this: in an illusion you're aware of something that has a property the physical object lacks (the stick looks bent); whatever you're aware of in an illusion can't be the straight stick; so in illusion you're aware of something other than the physical object; and since an illusion can be subjectively indistinguishable from a veridical perception, the same must hold in the veridical case; so we never perceive physical objects. Our lecturer says the disjunctivist blocks this at the last move by denying that the good case and the bad case are experiences of the same fundamental kind — they share at most a 'highest common factor', and a highest common factor isn't a kind of experience, it's a disjunction of two. Fine. But I built the following out of the disjunctivist's own reason for saying that, and it seems to show his reason proves far too much. I can't find the bad step.

(1) A veridical perception of a tomato and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination are not experiences of the same fundamental kind. (2) The reason is that the perception has the tomato as a constituent of the experience, while the hallucination has no such constituent: they differ in nature. (3) If two experiences differ in nature, they differ in fundamental kind. (4) Now take a row of three qualitatively identical spoons and let me look at all three at once. (5) My experience of spoon 1 has spoon 1 as a constituent; my experience of spoon 2 has spoon 2. Different constituents, so different natures. (6) By (3), those two experiences are not of the same fundamental kind. (7) Nothing in this turned on spoons: for any two experiences with different objects there are different constituents, different natures, different kinds. Since hardly any two experiences share an object, hardly any two experiences are of the same fundamental kind. (8) So the disjunctivist's reason generelises into "every experience is of its own kind", and then the good case/bad case contrast — the one thing disjunctivism was supposed to be about — is just one instance of a difference that holds between everything, and the notion of a fundamental kind is doing no work at all.

My flatmate says the cost is obvious: (3) has to go, and if (3) goes then (2) can't deliver the denial in (1), so the argument from illusion runs unblocked and we're back to sense-data. But I really don't want to give up (7) — it seems flatly obvious to me that my experience of one spoon and my experience of the next spoon along are the same kind of experience, and if a theory says otherwise I think the theory has lost. So: which step do I cut, and what does cutting it cost me? If the answer is "keep (3), drop (7)", tell me what "same fundamental kind" is supposed to mean once we've said that; if it's "keep (7), drop (3)", tell me whether the disjunctivist has anything left to say to the argument from illusion. And if there's a third way — say, that (2) is just the wrong description of what the disjunctivist is claiming — price that one too, because I'd rather keep the whole thing if it can be kept.
