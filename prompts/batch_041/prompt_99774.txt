I'm a graduate student in a philosophy-of-literature reading group and I've been drafted to run the session on authorial intention. The trouble is that the usual survey — actual intentionalism, hypothetical intentionalism, anti-intentionalism, and the rest — is something everyone in the room has already read, so the discussion collapses into reciting labels within twenty minutes. What I want to hand out is a classification instrument that makes people commit to answers on cases before they can see which camp those answers put them in, and only afterwards names the position. So please build me a decision tree for that purpose — not an essay about the debate, a tree — one that starts from a commitment nearly everyone in the room can accept and terminates at a named position about what fixes the meaning of a text.

Construct the tree out of the logical space first and only then populate it with names. Every internal node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing where the branch leads, and every leaf a position. I want the following distinctions to carry the branching work: (i) whether the author's actual intention is constitutively relevant to meaning or merely evidentially relevant, as a clue to something fixed independently; (ii) if it is constitutive, whether an intention that fails to be realized in the text still fixes meaning, or only a successfully realized intention does; (iii) whether the intention in question is the author's communicative/illocutionary one or something broader, such as a design for the work's aesthetic effect; (iv) within the hypothetical-intentionalist family, exactly whose knowledge the suitably informed reader is supposed to possess — the author's known corpus, the genre conventions, or an idealized best hypothesis about what was meant — since I suspect this question alone fractures that family; (v) whether linguistic and literary conventions plus context can settle meaning with no intention anywhere in the picture; and (vi) the value-maximizing proposal that interpretation should select whichever reading makes the work best. The leaves should be named positions, and must include at least extreme or absolute actual intentionalism, moderate actual intentionalism, hypothetical intentionalism, conventionalism or anti-intentionalism, and the value-maximizing view; add whatever further leaf the space forces on you, and if the space contains a slot for a view on which the author's later, retrospective intention is allowed to count, or one on which we merely talk as if there were an author without believing it, tell me whether anyone actually occupies it or whether it is empty and why.

For the diagnostic cases, please use the following, which I've invented or adapted for the handout, and make sure at least six of them appear as nodes: a poem that turns out to have been produced by an author in a trance, or by automatic writing; a grocery list found in a dead stranger's coat, which a critic reads as an elegy; an unfinished novel whose first half is by a human author and whose second half was completed by a language model trained on their style; a text published under a pseudonym whose author later reveals the whole thing was a hoax and that the sincere reading was in fact the intended one; a sentence whose author later disavows their original meaning and now endorses a different reading; a translation that is the only surviving version of a lost original; an ironic passage that no contemporary reader recognizes as ironic; and a collaboratively written manifesto whose two authors intended opposite things. Where two of my leaves come apart only on cases you cannot construct, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a distinction to keep them apart. Finally, in a closing note, separate what is settled among people who actually work on this question from what remains genuinely contested, and flag any leaf whose name or attribution is itself disputed — I don't want a handout that presents one camp's taxonomy as the neutral map of the terrain.
