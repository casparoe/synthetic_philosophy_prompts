For two years I have met monthly with three people — a proof theorist, a molecular biologist, and a philosopher of biology — to argue about one claim, and we are no further along than at our first meeting. The claim is Hofstadter's: that the trick by which a formal system contrives to talk about itself and the trick by which a molecule contrives to make a copy of itself are not two analogies but one structure seen twice. His typogenetics was built to exhibit the second half of that. A toy alphabet of bases; strands that fold; enzymes that bind to a strand and then edit it, delete from it, insert into it, or copy it; and, if you search long enough, a strand that codes for enzymes which, acting on that very strand, yield another copy of it. That strand is supposed to stand to biology roughly as a Gödel sentence stands to arithmetic. I am a novelist, and the book I am writing has a self-copying text at its centre, so I have a stake in whether this is a theorem-shaped fact or a metaphor that dissolves under pressure.

What I want from you is not a verdict on the analogy but a diagnosis of the argument about it, which has settled into three fixed positions. The proof theorist holds that the load-bearing element is the closed loop — the diagonal, the fixed point — and that a string must be used twice over, once as an object that is operated on and once as the rule that operates, before anything deserves the name self-replication; a crystal, a photocopier, and an autocatalytic cycle all fail this test, and the self-reproducing automata tradition is the clearest exhibit, since a universal constructor needs a description that is read twice, once as instruction and once as data to be copied. The biologist denies that any such loop is required, offers templating without a code, autocatalytic sets, and self-splicing RNA, notes that in a real cell no molecule copies itself but only serves as a template for a polymerase, and concludes that self-replication is not a property of a system at all but a description we impose once we have decided where to draw the boundary around a subsystem; he adds, woundingly, that typogenetics only ever finds its self-replicating strand by search, so the system does not replicate itself, we replicate it. The philosopher of biology says the whole thing turns on the arbitrary code — Gödel numbering on one side, the genetic code on the other — and that without an interpreter assigning meaning to a sequence, there is no description to be copied and hence nothing for the analogy to be about.

My hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis, is that the impasse is an artefact of three different things being called the isomorphism: the arbitrary code, the dual use of one string as object and as rule, and the closed loop that results. Each of us treats one of these as the whole content of the claim, and they come apart in general, so we are not really disputing biology or logic but the identity conditions for saying that two structures are the same structure. Test that hypothesis against what each side actually says. If it holds, tell me what kind of disagreement this is — verbal, a hidden formal question, a disagreement about individuation rather than about replication, or a question that is ill-posed — and which of the three carriers can be shown dispensable by a case everyone already accepts. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, an independence result, a case, a change of question, or nothing, with reasons. I want the argument, not a reading list; do not give me citations, and do not tell me who said what. If my hypothesis is wrong, give me the better diagnosis and defend it against the three positions as I have stated them.
