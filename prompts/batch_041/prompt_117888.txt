My political-epistemology reading group has a move I can't beat. Someone says: each voter is like a thermometer in a room. Individual thermometers are junk — they read a degree high, a degree low — but if you take enough of them the errors cancel and the average nails the true temperature. Condorcet's theorem, they say, is just that: as long as each voter is a bit better than a coin flip and nobody's copying anyone else, a big enough majority is nearly certain to be right. From there they draw the practical conclusion: more voters is always epistemically better, so a large random sample voting on a verdict beats twelve jurors, and anything that shrinks the electorate shrinks the accuracy.

I think this is doing something sneaky but I can't say what. Can you audit it? Give me the correspondence table — what maps onto the thermometer, the temperature, the noise, the averaging, the "true reading" — then where the mapping holds and where it breaks. In particular I want the features of the thermometer picture that the argument quietly relies on but that voters don't obviously have, and a clear statement of which breaks are fatal to the "bigger electorate = more reliable verdict" conclusion and which are just complications you can patch.

Two things to fold in, because people in the group keep gesturing at them without saying them out loud:

1. Averaging thermometers works because the errors are independent and centered on the truth, not merely because they're noisy. There's a family of results where the crowd's error equals the average error of its members minus how spread out their guesses are — so the crowd gets smarter exactly as the guesses get more diverse, which is a different mechanism than the thermometer one (diversity, not cancellation). Does the thermometer analogy even name that mechanism, or does it hide it? And does it matter for the conclusion which one is doing the work?

2. Voters aren't sealed instruments. They read the same papers, they're correlated by whatever the loudest voice says, and any procedure that makes them better informed — deliberation, a feedback round where they see the group's running average, the sort of thing Delphi panels do — also makes them more correlated. So the remedy for ignorance looks like the poison for independence. If correlation is what kills the theorem, and information causes correlation, is there any way to get both, or is the whole picture incoherent as a defense of big electorates?

Finish with a verdict: does the thermometer analogy actually support the conclusion it's being used for, or was it carrying the conclusion the whole time? If it doesn't, what analogy would do the job better (a jury of witnesses? a poll? a market?), and does that one carry it any further than the thermometer did? Tell me how confident you are, and — more useful to me — what would change your mind: what result or argument would make you think the analogy survives the audit after all, or that I've rigged the case against it.
