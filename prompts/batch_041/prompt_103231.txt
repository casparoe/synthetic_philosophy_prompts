OK so I've been going back and forth with a friend about the old Friedman line — the one where a model's assumptions don't have to be realistic, only its predictions have to come out right. I get why its tempting, but I think two different things are getting smooshed together and I want you to pull them apart for me.

Here's the case I keep pressing. Model A leaves stuff out on purpose: no friction, no air resistance, agents all identical, whatever. It's false, but false in the direction of omission, and we can see how to add the missing stuff back and watch the predictions drift a little. Model B is false in a nastier way: it doesn't omit a cause, it inverts one. Say it treats the effect as the cause, or makes the causal engine of the whole phenomenon some variable that we're pretty sure is just along for the ride. Now suppose both models nail the same held-out data over the same regime, same accuracy, same robustness to small perturbations of the inputs. Is there a principled way to say A is a legitimate idealization and B is a lucky coincidence — or does the predictions-only view just have to shrug and call them equal? And if it has to call them equal, is that a reductio of the view, or can it be defended?

The part that really bugs me: if B's inverted assumption is doing no work, can't we strip it out and get the same predictions? And if we can, doesn't that show the model's success is independent of the assumption — which would undercut the whole defense? But if the assumption IS doing work, then it seems like it's shaping what counts as a good prediction, and then we're back to caring about the assumptions after all.

Please just explain the conceptual issue here, I don't want a list of who said what. Take the two-models case seriously and tell me where the real fault line is, and whether the predictions-only view has any non-question-begging way out. Concrete examples are welcome and you can invent them.
