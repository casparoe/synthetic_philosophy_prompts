Four times a year the small county historical society I direct mounts a new exhibition, and each time my two-person research staff and I must decide how the past on the walls is to be handled: a mill that closed, a farming community displaced to build the reservoir, the industrialist whose fortune paid for the library that still bears his name. A board of trustees signs off, donors and descendants often have a stake, and our research time is measured in weeks rather than years. The board recently adopted a mission line to the effect that the society exists to make the county more capable of acting well in the present, which I take to be a loose version of Nietzsche's demand that history serve life rather than mummify it; but when I ask what that means for a particular wall label, no one can say, and I suspect the board reads the mission as reassurance of the living rather than the vitality Nietzsche had in mind, which is not at all what the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life seems to mean.

Write me a procedure my staff and I can run on every new exhibition. It should be grounded in Nietzsche's three ways of relating to the past — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — and in what he says each of these does for life and how each turns against it. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops with a verdict, and the points at which it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Then run it on three cases: the industrialist's library, a dying craft tradition we want to keep alive, and a recent episode of violence that much of the county would rather not have reopened. At least one of the three verdicts should be one we will not like. Close by saying what the procedure cannot decide, and where it should be expected to misfire on a subject Nietzsche never imagined.
