I've somehow ended up as one of the two lay members on our hospital's clinical ethics committee -- I'm not an academic, I just read a lot and got volunteered -- and over the past year I've become convinced of something I can't stop chewing on: that there aren't really any moral principles, only judgments about particular cases. Not that principles are false exactly, more that they never actually do any of the work. Whenever we say "this case is decided by respect for autonomy," I think it's really the other way round -- we've already judged the case, and the principle is a tidy label we stick on it afterwards. I picked up the term moral particularism from somewhere, and I think that's roughly it, though the version I actually believe is my own: reasons are context-dependent, a feature that counts in favour of doing something in one situation can count against it in the next, and any principle that tries to ignore that is either so vague it decides nothing or so specific it only ever fits one case. Anyway, next month I've got to give a short talk to the full committee making this case and then take questions, and I want to be ready for it.

Here's who's in the room. The chair is a consultant nephrologist who likes decisions to be defensible on paper and gets twitchy when he can't point to a reason on a form. There's a palliative care nurse who's the most thoughtful person there and mainly cares whether patients are actually being heard. There's a hospital lawyer who has to sign off on anything controversial and thinks in terms of consistency, precedent, and what would we say if this ended up in the papers. There's a chaplain who is sympathetic to the idea that morality is more than rule-following but worries that with no rules anything goes. And one other lay member who's basically on my side but hasn't thought it through. We've got the four-principles card (autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice) taped to the wall and everyone reaches for it constantly -- that's really what I'm arguing against. The case I'll probably use to make it concrete: an elderly man with capacity refusing a treatment that would probably save his life, his daughter desperate and pushing hard, the team split down the middle, and the committee reaching straight for the checklist as usual.

I'd like you to act as a philosopher who works on moral theory but has spent alot of real time in rooms with clinicians and lawyers, and who can tell the difference between an objection that sounds good and one that actually bites. Think carefully and brainstorm a bit first about what this specific group would genuinely say to me, then write me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they'd do, each one phrased the way that particular person would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would, with a short reply I can give in about two minutes and an honest note on where the reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should refuse to give up, and which single reply has to land or the whole thing collapses. And include the one question I'm dreading -- the one I really hope nobody asks. Please don't give me reading lists or citations, I won't have time for them and they wouldn't help me in the room; just the arguments themselves. And if my version of the view has a hole in it, say so plainly instead of being polite about it.
