I'm a science-and-ideas journalist at work on a long feature about the philosophy of perception, and I keep circling back to an old thought experiment about two coffee tasters. Both men drink the same cup they have drunk for years, and both report that something has changed: the first is certain the coffee now tastes worse to him, the second is equally certain that his own palate has improved and the coffee tastes better. Each can describe his history and his present experience in loving detail, yet neither can produce anything that would let a third party adjudicate who is right about the direction of the change — or even establish that there is a fact of the matter about it. The puzzle is normally wheeled out to make trouble for the notion that experience has intrinsic, ineffable, introspectively accessible properties that we can compare across persons and report on with authority; it is the sort of case that makes one wonder whether such properties — qualia, if you like the word — are a posit we could simply do without.

I would like you to write a short story that dramatizes this puzzle rather than explaining it. Set it in a plausible near-future clinic — imagine something like the Meridian Institute for Sensory Recalibration — where affluent clients pay for elective adjustments to how the world tastes, sounds, or looks to them, marketed as a remedy for aesthetic burnout. Center the story on a counselor and two clients who have just come through the same procedure and who now disagree, in exactly the way the coffee tasters do, about whether their experience of a shared stimulus has improved or degraded, with no test in sight that could settle the question. Let the tension arise from the fact that the clients, the counselor, and the clinic's engineers all keep reaching for a vocabulary of inner properties that the situation refuses to underwrite, and end in a way that leaves the reader genuinely unsure whether the question of whether their experience changed, and in which direction, even has an answer — without ever letting a narrator step forward to announce the correct philosophical conclusion. I am less interested in a twist than in whether the fiction can make a reader feel the vertigo of not knowing what there would be to know.
