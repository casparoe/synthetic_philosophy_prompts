I'm a senior engineer on the safety team at a mid-sized AI company, with no formal training in philosophy — my background is distributed systems and far too many production incident reviews. Next month I have to defend an internal proposal to our safety review board, and I'd like a preparation sheet for the questions I'm actually going to get, not the ones I'd get from a different room.

The proposal, in brief. We want a low-impact constraint that we can actually implement and test. The design is that before taking any action, an agent simulates a null rollout — the trajectory the world would have followed had the agent never been switched on — and it may then alter only those state variables whose values in the real rollout differ from the null rollout by less than a small budget, with that budget spent across the whole episode rather than per step. We've been calling this the quiet-hand rule. Anything the agent touches outside its delegated variables has to be paid for out of that budget, and the accounting is mechanical.

The part that will get me into trouble is my claim about what this rule means. I think it makes impact a purely causal notion. On my view, how much you changed the world is a descriptive, counterfactual question: you compare two trajectories and subtract. It doesn't require a theory of value at all. Value enters at exactly one point, when we set the budget — how much change is acceptable. If that's right, then low-impact AI is an engineering problem with a tunable parameter rather than a moral one, and we can hand the parameter to the people whose job it is to argue about values.

The room I'm presenting to: two engineers from the evals team, who believe that anything not reducible to a benchmark number is a vibe, and who will want to know exactly how the delta gets measured and how the agent would game it; one decision theorist we retain as a consultant, who I'm told is unusually good on counterfactuals and reference policies; an applied ethicist who signs off on our deployments and has been skeptical of us before; and my director, who cares about schedule and headcount and will ask what this costs.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way each of those people would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is weakest, and an indication of what I should concede up front rather than fight. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks.

Please take a definite position rather than giving me a balanced tour: if my core claim is wrong, say so and give the reasons, and if it survives, tell me what it survives as — I would rather be told that the honest version is considerably weaker than the version I'm presenting than be left to infer it. I won't be able to do the philosophy on my feet, so I need to know which parts of my framing I should abandon before I walk in.
