I'm running a one-off 90-minute workshop at a local "creative day" thing next month and I need you to plan the session, not explain the idea to me — I've got the idea. The room is about twenty people, mixed: a few hobbyist songwriters, a couple of graphic designers, a retired structural engineer, my aunt. No background in philosophy or cognitive science whatsoever. The topic is the claim that creativity is mostly variation. There's a Hofstadter essay from the early eighties, the one collected in Metamagical Themas, where he says variations on a theme are the essence of imagination — a concept comes with a space of dimensions you can vary, I keep calling them knobs, and creative work is largely turning knobs and finding out that the interesting stuff is at the edges of the space.

So: what has to come first, second, third, and why — which piece is load-bearing for which other piece. I'm fairly sure "a concept is a space with knobs" has to land before "the edges are where the good stuff is", but I don't know what has to land before that, and I keep flip-flopping on whether "recognizability is what makes a variation a variation" should come early (it's the thing that stops the model collapsing into anything-goes) or late (it only bites once they've tried to generate variations themselves). Also tell me where the difference between twiddling a knob inside the space and re-cutting the space itself — changing which knobs exist — belongs, and whether it's even worth doing at this level.

The carrying example: I was going to use the twelve-bar blues, everyone's heard a thousand of them and can twiddle one in their head. But I worry it's the obvious one and they'll nod and file the whole thing under "music" and never transfer it. My backup is a recipe — carbonara, or a paper airplane design. Tell me which to use and give me the criteria (what the example has to have, what it has to lack) rather than just picking; if all three of mine are wrong, say so and tell me what to use. One hard constraint: the example has to be twiddlable live — the room should be able to produce three or four variations out loud in about two minutes, or the whole thing stays abstract.

Misconceptions I'm expecting, and I want to know exactly where in the sequence to head each one off and how: (a) creativity = breaking rules / thinking outside the box; (b) variation is derivative, the original is the real art; (c) if the space is handed to you there's nothing genuinely new happening; (d) if there are enough knobs then everything counts, so the theory says nothing. There are definately ones I haven't thought of — especially the one where someone concludes a machine could do this, which I don't want to have to fight in ninety minutes.

Then: one question I can ask at the end that shows me whether the model landed, not whether they can repeat my words back. And what to openly skip at this level — I know the mind-dependence of conceptual spaces is a swamp and I don't want to touch whether the space is "real", so a line on how to say I'm skipping it without looking like I'm dodging.
