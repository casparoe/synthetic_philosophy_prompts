I have been arguing about this at length with two friends, one a committed utilitarian and one a Kantian, and the argument has left me unable to state a view of my own. What I notice is that my reactions to actions whose distant consequences are opaque are not consistent with any position I can formulate, and I would like you to act as a philosopher who works on consequentialism, decision theory under uncertainty, and the epistemology of long-run effects. Please begin by brainstorming several candidate principles rather than committing to the first one that fits, then test them against my cases, and be explicit at the end about which of my verdicts your preferred principle forces me to give up. Here are the cases and my reactions, offered as gut verdicts rather than as conclusions I have argued for: donating to a charity that distributes insecticide-treated bed nets feels clearly good, and the objection that the child I save might grow up to do something terrible strikes me as no objection at all; voting in a national election feels worth doing even though the chance my vote is decisive is minuscule; buying a shirt from a factory whose workers I know labor in bad conditions feels not fine, even though I grant that the long-run effects of my purchase on those workers and their economy are something economists dispute; my individual carbon footprint feels like it matters, though its causal contribution is as negligible as my single vote; and taking a gamble with a tiny probability of an enormous catastrophe feels wrong even when the expected value is positive. I am also bothered by a pair I cannot reconcile: I dismiss the remote and speculative downstream effects of saving a life as irrelevant, yet I give weight to remote and speculative effects in the carbon and catastrophe cases, and I would not accept a politician's reasoning that a small present wrong is worth it for a vast good that may or may not materialize.

My own tentative proposal, which I suspect is too crude, is that one should act on the expected value of the consequences one can foresee with reasonable confidence and ignore merely speculative ripple effects. This seems to handle the bed nets and the sweatshop shirt, but it appears to license ignoring climate change and to permit the catastrophic gamble, which I do not want, and it leaves me unsure what to say about a city deciding whether to fund a program whose benefits would appear only in fifty years, about whether to have a child, and about whether to eat meat. Please state the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures the pattern of verdicts above, identifying for each clause the case that motivates it; then tell me what the resulting account predicts about those three further cases I have not settled, and whether it can accommodate my discomfort about the politician's reasoning or must instead treat that discomfort as a mistake. Think carefully and step by step, distinguish the various senses in which a consequence can be uncertain or remote, and where my verdicts cannot all be saved, say plainly which one you would revise and why.
