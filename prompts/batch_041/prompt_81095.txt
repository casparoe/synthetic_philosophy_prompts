I'm in my final year of a politics and philosophy degree, and the paper I'm supposed to be writing for our seminar on freedom is stuck. The seminar has spent the term on three families of view: freedom as the absence of interference, freedom as not being subject to anyone's arbitrary power, and freedom as self-mastery, or living under a law you give yourself. My problem is that when I apply these to the cases I actually care about, I keep producing verdicts that don't line up with any one of the three, and a friend in the seminar says that just means I haven't thought hard enough about which view I really hold. I don't think that's right — I think my reactions are tracking something, and I'd like you to work out what principle, or small set of principles, they add up to, and which of my verdicts I should give up. I should say I'm not a partisan of any of the three; I'm reporting my reactions, including the ones I distrust.

Here are my verdicts, going case by case. (A) My landlord. I rent month to month; he hasn't raised the rent in five years, repairs things the same week, and we get on fine. He could give me notice and I would have to be out in two months. I feel unfree here, and I don't think that's just ordinary worry about the future. (B) The identical flat next door, where a friend has a five-year lease with a rent cap and an eviction procedure her landlord has to win. The landlord's character is no different from mine. I say she's free and I'm not. (C) A case I've been chewing on: a woman claiming a benefit is assessed by a scoring system whose rules she can't see, and when it docks her payment there is no official who can explain the rule or overrule it. Suppose it has in fact always assessed her correctly, and suppose no human being decides her case at all. I say she's unfree — but I can't say who dominates her. (D) A statute on the books that would make her weekly meeting unlawful. Nobody has been prosecuted under it for decades and the police say they won't. I say she's unfree anyway. (E) A worker in a town with one employer, who has never been told she can't leave, is paid above the legal minimum, and could in principle move two hundred miles — but every job, every house and the only grocery store belong to the firm. I say unfree, though I'm not confident it's the same kind of unfreedom as in A. (F) A friend whose partner has never once forbidden her anything, but who I am fairly sure would make her life unbearable if she took the job in another city she was offered. She didn't apply, and says she no longer wants to. Unfree. (G) A member of a housing co-op, outvoted on a rule she opposed — say, a ban on pets — and now living under it. She had a vote, she can leave at the end of the year without penalty, and the same procedure can reverse the rule. I say she's free. (H) Me again, under police powers that would let an officer stop, search and detain me on thin grounds; where I live this almost never happens, and when it does there are courts, compensation and a press. I say I'm free. (I) A friend who has joined a religious order whose rule governs when she eats, sleeps and speaks, and whose superior can post her anywhere. She chose it, she can leave, and she says she has never been freer. I'm inclined to agree. My half-belief is that you are unfree to the extent that some other power can change what you can do without your having a say in it, whether or not it ever does, and that a constraint stops counting against you once it is genuinely answerable to you — through voice, or through an exit you could actually take rather than merely a formal one. I suspect that's a mash-up of the three views rather than a fourth one, and that it says something false about at least one of my cases, probably I or E.

Please give me your answer as a single JSON object, with no commentary outside it, using exactly these keys. "diagnosis": a string of at most 200 words saying what pattern you think my verdicts actually exhibit, in plain language. "principles": an array of two to four objects, each with "label" (a short name), "statement" (the principle stated precisely enough that someone who hasn't read my cases could apply it, at most 80 words), "verdicts_explained" (an array of case letters), "verdicts_contradicted" (an array of case letters), "unwanted_commitments" (a string: what else the principle forces me to accept about freedom, whether or not you think it is true), "verdicts_that_must_be_given_up" (an array of case letters), and "confidence" (a number between 0 and 1). "cases": an array of nine objects, one per case A through I, each with "case" (the letter), "my_verdict" (one of "free", "unfree", "unclear"), "what_is_doing_the_work" (a string of at most 40 words naming the feature of the case that seems to drive my reaction), "best_principle" (the label of the principle that handles it best), and "agree_with_me" (a boolean). "hard_pairs": an array of objects, each with "pair" (two case letters), "why_they_conflict" (a string), "which_verdict_you_would_overturn" (a letter), and "why" (a string); include at least the A-versus-H and C-versus-G conflicts if you think they are real. "new_cases": an array of three objects, each with "description" (a case I haven't listed, concrete enough to have a verdict), "predicted_verdict" (one of "free", "unfree", "unclear"), and "which_principles_this_separates" (an array of labels). "bottom_line": a string of at most 120 words giving the single principle you would tell me to adopt, or a plain statement that none of them survives my cases, in which case say what the closest surviving version of it looks like.
