Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about whether weakness of will is a genuine phenomenon or an artifact of how we model choice.

The first holds the classical view. An agent can judge, all things considered, that she ought to do one thing, and then knowingly and without compulsion do another; this is a distinctive failure of agency, a defect in her relation to her own judgment rather than a mere shift in what she wants. She insists that the weak-willed agent is not overpowered the way an addict is — she could have acted otherwise, and that is exactly what makes the action hers. Call this the judgment-failure view.

The second holds that the phenomenon dissolves once we take seriously that preferences are indexed to times. The person who stays in bed rather than go to the gym simply prefers, at that moment, the nearer comfort to the farther benefit; the discounting of future goods is steep, and steeper still as the moment of choice approaches. On this view there is no single dateless verdict about what she judged best, for her to have violated — only preferences held at times, with weakness of will a moralizing label pasted over ordinary preference dynamics. Call this the preference-dynamics view.

Build the dialogue around a case both characters accept as the shared datum. A man resolves on Sunday, sincerely and after deliberation, to stop drinking. He knows that on Friday, at a party, he will want a drink and will probably take one. In the first variant he simply resolves, and on Friday he drinks. In the second he also hands his car keys to a friend on Sunday and asks her to keep them until Saturday, precisely because he anticipates his Friday self; on Friday he drinks anyway, having talked the friend into returning the keys. The judgment-failure theorist reads both variants as the same failure. The preference-dynamics theorist reads the second as showing there was never a stable judgment to violate — only a Sunday self that wanted sobriety and a Friday self that wanted the drink, the former trying and failing to bind the latter. Both characters also accept, as a further datum, that people who use such commitment devices often describe themselves afterward as having acted more freely, not less.

Let each character press the other where it actually hurts rather than trade talking points. The judgment-failure theorist must say what the preference-dynamics account cannot capture — for instance the blameworthiness and the peculiar self-alienation of acting against one's own settled judgment — and must address whether a settled judgment is anything more than a confidently held preference. The preference-dynamics theorist must address whether the second variant can be described without smuggling in a second, hidden agent who does the binding, and whether talk of what the agent could have done otherwise is doing any explanatory work. Neither character may be a strawman; each should concede something real to the other. Let them converge on at least one claim and remain genuinely divided on at least one, and make the point of division exact rather than gestural.

End with a short note in your own voice, outside the dialogue, stating how confident you are in the philosophical claims the dialogue relies on and naming the single point you are least sure of.
