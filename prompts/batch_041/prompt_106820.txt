I'm a novelist, and next month I've somehow agreed to run a session at a writers' festival — the strand is "philosophy for people who make things up" — and I've picked the identity of indiscernibles, mostly because I've got a half-writen novel about a woman who meets an exact duplicate of herself and I keep hitting a wall I can't name. Don't explain the principle to me, I've read enough to be dangerous. Tell me how to teach it. Forty-five minutes, a room of novelists and poets who will all assume it's a logic puzzle and tune out in five.

What has to come first so the thing lands? My instinct is you can't even state the puzzle until people see that "if identical then indiscernible" and "if indiscernible then identical" are two different claims and only one of them comes for free — but tell me if that's the right opening or if something has to go before it.

Then the two spheres alone in an empty universe (Black's, I think — don't quote me on the citation). Tell me what that example has to have and what it must not have if it's going to do the work. I suspect it dies the moment you smuggle in a landmark or a background grid, but maybe that's the whole point rather than a flaw? And where in the sequence do I head off the two objections I can already hear: the one who thinks it's about whether *we* can tell them apart, and the one who says "well, one's over there and one's over here, so they're different." I want to know whether that second one is a cheap dodge or the actual crux, and when to spring it on them.

What do I refuse to go into at this level — the modal stuff, the haecceities, whatever else — and say so out loud? And give me one question to ask at the end that tells me whether they've actually got it or just nodded along. Last thing: if there's a version of this puzzle that plays better to a room of fiction writers than two spheres in a void, tell me, because my whole session probably hinges on the example.
