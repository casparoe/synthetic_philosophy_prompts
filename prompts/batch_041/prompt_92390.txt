So I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and an old colleague of mine roped me into guest-grading a stack of short answers for this undergrad seminar she runs on numbers and democracy. I want to be fair to these kids, so before I scrawl a score on this one I was hoping you'd sanity-check me. The question was: "Explain the Condorcet jury theorem, state the assumptions it depends on, and assess whether it gives us a good reason to trust large groups of ordinary people over small groups of experts." Here's what the student turned in:

The Condorcet jury theorem says that if you put a yes-or-no question to a group and each member has a better-than-even chance of getting it right, then the chance that the majority gets it right rises as you add more members, and it approaches certainty as the group gets large. So a big enough crowd is almost guaranteed to land on the correct answer. Two assumptions do the work: each voter is more likely than not to pick the correct option, and voters reach their judgments independently, so they aren't copying each other. If you relax the first assumption and let voters be worse than a coin flip, the majority actually gets less reliable as it grows, so the theorem isn't a blanket endorsement of any crowd.

The usual takeaway is that majority rule is a truth-tracking device: a large referendum is more likely to hit the right answer than a small panel of experts, even when the experts are individually smarter, as long as the ordinary voters are above chance. I think that's basically correct, and it's the same reason a big random poll beats asking a couple of pundits.

On the independence assumption, I read it as mainly ruling out collusion. As long as people make up their own minds and aren't literally coordinating, the theorem goes through. So it's fine if everyone reads the same newspaper and comes away with the same slant, since each of them still has a better-than-chance shot on their own.

Finally, the theorem isn't limited to factual questions. If enough people favor a policy, the majority is more likely to be right that it's the good policy, so it's also a defense of democracy on value questions, not just on facts.
