Somebody in my group chat handed me this argument and I cant find the exit. Setup first, since I had to get it straight myself. There's a four-rung ladder of creature-kinds (Dennett, I'm fairly sure, in one of his short books): Darwinian creatures are just built the way selection built them, so their errors get paid in dead bodies over generations; Skinnerian creatures can learn inside a single lifetime, but only by actually doing the thing and eating what comes back, so the error has to land on them; Popperian creatures can run the trial in-house -- model the situation, drop the bad option, never do it -- so the hypothesis dies and the creature doesn't; Gregorian creatures take on mind-tools that other people made (words, numerals, diagrams, procedures) and do a lot of their thinking with borrowed equipment.

Now the argument.

(1) A model only earns its keep if the world keeps correcting it, sooner or later; nothing else gives a model authority.
(2) The world corrects you in exactly two ways: by the consequences of what you actually do, and by the consequences of what you only simulate.
(3) Whatever you simulate comes out of your model, and your model is just a compressed record of how things went for you or your ancestors.
(4) So the simulated trial is made of the same stuff as the real trial -- it's a replay of material that correction already shaped.
(5) A replay you throw away is still just reinforcement, with the action withheld.
(6) So "the hypothesis dies instead of me" is not a new kind of mind; it's the Skinnerian kind with a gap between the doing and the paying.
(7) And the fourth rung is just the third rung plus borrowed tools, so if the third collapses into the second, the fourth collapses too -- a Gregorian creature is a creature that's been conditioned by a culture.
(8) So the ladder is one rung with a knob on it: hardwired vs. shaped-by-consequences, plus a very large shaping environment in our case.

I can't accept (8). The whole point of the ladder, for me, is that having language and notation is a difference in kind and not just a lot more conditioning -- and (8) says it's exactly that. It also looks like it proves way too much: if (6) is right, then everything I read about some model "not being able to do science because it can't let hypotheses die in its stead" is just a fancy way of saying it wasn't conditioned on the right distribution, and I don't believe that's all there is to it.

So which of (1)-(7) goes, and what does dropping it cost me? If I get to choose, I'd rather keep the fourth rung than the third. I cant tell whether the problem is in the plumbing or whether the thing is valid and I just have to eat it.
