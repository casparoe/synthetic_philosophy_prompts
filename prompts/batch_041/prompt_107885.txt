I am a first-year PhD student in political science sitting in on a seminar on rationality and democracy, and I have no background in epistemology. The instructor opened the term with a handout summarizing the dispute between conciliationists and steadfasters about peer disagreement. I can follow the prose, but I cannot tell how much of it is the state of play in the field and how much is his own spin, and I would rather find out now than after I have cited it in a paper. This is the handout, as he wrote it:

Two people who are equally intelligent and equally well informed look at the same evidence and reach opposite conclusions. Conciliationists say that in such a case you are required to split the difference: if you put the chance of rain at 80% and your peer puts it at 20%, the only rational credence is 50%. This is not a matter of courtesy but of strict rationality — your own judgment is just one item of evidence among others, and counting it twice is a mistake. Steadfasters deny the requirement: because you already took your peer's evidence into account when you made up your mind, learning that they disagree tells you nothing you did not already know, so you may keep your original credence. The real question between them is whether disagreement is itself evidence.

Conciliationism, however, is committed to something stronger than its defenders like to admit. Call it the parity principle: whenever two people have the same evidence and are equally reliable, reason requires them to arrive at the same credence — a view some call uniqueness. This is what forces the conciliationist to say that disagreement always signals that at least one party has made a mistake.

Neither position survives scrutiny. Conciliationism is self-defeating: if it is true, then a conciliationist who meets a steadfaster who is her equal must give up conciliationism, and since such people exist, the view cannot rationally be held by anyone who knows the literature. Steadfastness fares no better: it licenses pure stubbornness, since on its account the flat-earther who has never opened a physics book is as entitled to his view as the astrophysicist. The honest conclusion is that the epistemology of disagreement is a dead end, and that the interesting questions — how we should treat one another when we disagree — belong to politics, not to the theory of knowledge.
