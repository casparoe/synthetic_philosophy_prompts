I've spent enough time in the standard discussions of Aristotle on exchange to be bored by the usual move—he condemned usury, he wanted exchange to be proportional—and more interested in a tension between two of his own treatments of it. I'd like a purpose-built case that isolates that tension rather than a general essay, so here is the specification.

Construct a thought experiment in which Aristotle's two treatments of exchange deliver opposite verdicts on the same transaction. The first is his account of justice in exchange, on which a just exchange is one of proportional reciprocity and need is the real measure for which money is only a conventional stand-in; call its verdict V1. The second is his account of household management versus wealth-getting, on which acquisition aimed at provisioning a household is natural and bounded by the good life, while acquisition aimed at money for its own sake is unbounded and contrary to nature; call its verdict V2. I want a case in which V1 comes out "just" and V2 comes out "condemned"—and the split has to fall out of Aristotle's own commitments, not out of a caricature in which the money-lover is simply a villain.

Constraints. The scenario must be realistic and one paragraph long: a market in a Greek polis, no anachronisms, no exotic devices. It must isolate a single variable. Hold fixed the parties, the goods, the quantities, the local fairness of each exchange, and who ends up with what; vary only the end toward which the gain is directed—provisioning a household up to the point its needs are met versus accumulating without limit. Do not build the case on a loan at interest, which is the textbook illustration I'm trying to get past, and do not let either party's character carry the verdict; if the condemned party is prudent, generous, and likable, so much the better.

Then do the following, in order. (1) State each account's verdict and derive it from that account's own criterion rather than asserting it. (2) Say which feature of the case is doing the work, and be precise about the unit of assessment each verdict operates on. (3) Remove that feature and report what happens—do the two accounts collapse back into agreement, and if so, which one was silently borrowing from the other. (4) Give the strongest reconciliation a sympathetic Aristotelian would offer—most likely that the two passages answer different questions, one about a transaction and one about a way of life, so there is no contradiction, only two units of assessment—and then tell me whether that reconciliation actually succeeds or merely relabels the problem. (5) Finally, say what the case exposes about the role of money as the measure of proportionality, given that Aristotle treats money as conventional and subject to fluctuation: does his own account of the measure undermine the stability that proportional reciprocity requires?

What the case is for: I want to know whether the tension is a genuine inconsistency or a case of two different units of assessment, and whether Aristotle's ethics of exchange is better read as an ethics of fairness or an ethics of boundedness. A case that stipulates the verdicts instead of generating them from the two accounts fails the exercise, so show your work.
