I'm in a reading group on Davidson and we've been grinding through the radical interpretation stuff — the picture where you interpret a speaker from scratch, no dictionary, no translator, no prior knowledge of their beliefs, and you can only do it by working out meaning and belief simultaneously, on the assumption that they mostly hold true what you'd hold true in their situation, with the end product being a truth theory in Tarski's style. Anyway, we got into an arguement because I brought up a case I don't think anyone in the group had considered: the decipherment of Linear B.

Here's the case as I've got it (correct me if any of it is off, but I'm fairly confident): a few thousand clay tablets from Bronze Age palace archives on Crete and the mainland, all of them administrative — sheep, rations, chariot wheels, lists of women and children. No bilingual text, no Rosetta stone, no living speakers, nobody to ask. An American classicist, Alice Kober, spent the 1940s doing the unglamorous work — frequency counts, positional statistics, and a technique for spotting signs that share a consonant and differ in vowel — which established that the script was inflected and that certain signs were phonetically related, all without assigning a single sound value to anything. Then in 1952 an English architect, Michael Ventris, an amateur at this, built a grid of the signs on that structural basis, guessed the language might be Greek, and when he tried values taken from a few place names the thing cracked open — the tablets turned out to be an early form of Greek, older than Homer. His collaborator John Chadwick, a Cambridge classicist, worked out the phonology and grammar, and later tablet finds confirmed the readings. Meanwhile Linear A, the earlier script on the same island, still can't be read, and neither can the other Aegean scripts.

So what I want is the analysis run in both directions. First take the case through Davidson: who exactly is the interpreter here, what is the total evidence, what are the constraints — the interdefinition of belief and meaning, the assumption that the speaker is mostly right about his world, the demand that the theory of meaning be a truth theory yielding T-sentences — and say honestly which parts of the story the machinery actually describes. Then tell me where it breaks, and whether my suspicions hold up or are just amateur noise: (a) there are no speakers and no behaviour, only texts, so the simultaneous attribution of belief and meaning has nothing to grip — is Davidson's account even applicable to a dead corpus rather than a person? (b) the load-bearing early move was pure structural statistics, semantics-free, which looks like it contradicts the holism; (c) the hunch that it was Greek was an external input rather than something charity generated — the assumption that the tablets record familiar bureaucratic business seems to have done more work than any principle about mostly-true beliefs, and the fact that Linear A resists the identical treatment suggests the decisive ingredient was already knowing the language. Then the payoff question: does this case tell for or against the later Davidsonian line that there is no such thing as a language, if a language is supposed to be a shared convention — a decipherment looks like the limiting case of getting by without one — and does the fact that the decipherment was checkable, with predictions that came out right on tablets nobody had read yet, count against indeterminacy of interpretation, or is that just Quine's problem and not Davidson's? Keep the mapping tight — name the interpreter, name the evidence — and where the theory is silent, say so instead of patching it.
