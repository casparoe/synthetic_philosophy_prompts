I am drafting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in early modern moral philosophy, and I need one essay question on Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals," together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question must not be answerable by reciting the received view that Montaigne is an early cultural relativist who used the peoples of the New World to expose the ethnocentrism of Renaissance Europe.

That view is not false, but it is shallow, and I want the question to force students past it. The essay is credited with a relativist thesis — that we call a custom barbarous merely because it is not our own — and yet its rhetoric plainly evaluates. Montaigne admires the cannibals' society for its simplicity and its closeness to nature, and he condemns his own age for the cruelties his contemporaries commit under cover of religion. A student should therefore have to ask whether a thoroughgoing relativist can condemn anything at all, and whether Montaigne's admiration for the Tupinambá is a verdict delivered by a universal standard rather than an abstention from all standards.

To give the question a spine, I want it organized around a distinction that I will print on the examination paper itself, so that students must apply it rather than merely recall it. The distinction is between three positions that are routinely conflated: first, metaethical relativism, the claim that there is no standard of right and wrong independent of a society's own customs; second, a Pyrrhonian suspension of judgment, on which one neither affirms nor denies such a standard but withholds assent in order to cure dogmatism; and third, a naturalistic universalism, on which there is a standard — nature, or the uncorrupted simplicity of peoples who live close to it — against which both the cannibals and the Europeans can be measured. The question should require students to say which of these the essay enacts, or whether it holds them in unresolved tension, and to show what turns on the choice.

I would also like the question to press a harder point: that Montaigne's admiration may itself be a form of the ethnocentrism he attacks, an idealization of the cannibals as a foil for Europe, and that students must say whether this is a fatal objection or whether it can be turned to the essay's advantage.

Please supply the essay question itself, phrased so that it cannot be answered by a summary of the essay or of the secondary literature; a model answer of about 700 words; and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a total of twenty, and, for each point, a one-line note on the mistake that candidates typically make and that should cost them marks. The examination is closed-book and ninety minutes long; students have read the essay in translation and may refer to its arguments rather than to its wording; no citations or reading lists are expected, and the question should not demand any.
