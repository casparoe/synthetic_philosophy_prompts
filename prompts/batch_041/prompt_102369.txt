I run the pricing desk at a small prediction market that has recently begun listing contracts on conditional claims. Most are mundane — a contract that pays out if the incumbent declines to run and the challenger then wins the nomination, another that pays if a merger closes and the combined firm then hits a revenue target — and we have always priced them the obvious way, taking the price of an if-A-then-B contract to be our conditional credence in B given A. That shortcut has served us well enough on the simple cases, but I recently worked carefully through Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals, and I now suspect that what we are doing is not a benign approximation but something closer to an incoherence that we quietly import into every conditional we list. The worry bites hardest, I think, on the compound contracts our quants have been pushing — a conditional buried inside another conditional, a conditional sitting under a disjunction, or the pattern where a claim of the form "if A, then if B, then C" gets treated as interchangeable with "if A and B, then C" — and I want to straighten out our methodology before we list any more of them.

Please write me a decision procedure that my analysts — capable people, but not logicians — can actually run on a new indicative-conditional contract, grounded in what the triviality results genuinely establish rather than in the folklore that has grown up around them. I want the questions asked in order: what the first cut should be; how to tell the cases in which the conditional-probability shortcut is defensible from those in which it is not; what to do with nested, compound, and import-export-style conditionals; how to check that a whole book of conditional prices is jointly coherent rather than merely locally plausible; and where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is one of house policy rather than arithmetic. Constraints: an analyst should be able to run it in about twenty minutes per contract; the output should be either a price with a stated interval or an explicit refusal to list; and the whole thing should be auditable after the fact, which means the steps have to be written down rather than left as tacit craft.

Then run the procedure on three contracts — a plain one, a nested or import-export one, and one that you think we should decline to list at all — with your reasoning for each. Throughout, mark which steps are genuine constraints forced by the triviality results and which are pragmatic conventions we could reasonably set the other way, and be candid about the points where a price will reflect our priors and our risk appetite rather than anything the logic settles. If the procedure forces a conclusion I will not like — say, that a class of contracts we have been selling is not coherently priceable at all — I would rather hear it now than after the market has taken our money.
