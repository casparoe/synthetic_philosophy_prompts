Write me a short story — 2000 words is plenty, no need to go longer — set in a fictional secular republic that has just tightened up its neutrality rules. I want it to be about the friction between laïcité-style state neutrality and Anglo-American multiculturalism, but please don't give me the usual headscarf-in-a-classroom scene; that one's been done to death and you'll just be reassembling the parts of it you've seen. Instead, build the story around a piece of bureaucratic machinery and let the concepts come out of how people have to talk in order to get through it.

The machinery: the republic grants reasonable accommodations only to practices it can classify as matters of individual conscience — belief, not belonging. Communal and cultural practices get nothing; they're just customs, and the state isn't in the business of protecting customs. So there's a small exemptions board, a form, a hearing, an appeal.

Your central characters: a family from a minority community whose practice is at once religious, cultural, and irreducably communal — it only makes sense done together, on a calendar, in a particular building — and who therefore have to argue their way into a category that dosen't fit them. Give them a lawyer who is competent and quietly cynical about the vocabulary she has to use on their behalf.

Three things I want the story to do:

1. Put at least two incompatible justifications for the same rule into the mouths of sympathetic characters — someone who thinks neutrality is what makes citizens equal, and someone who thinks the rule is really about emancipation, pulling people out of communities that hold them back. Make me feel the force of both, and let the two justifications pull apart from each other rather than harmonizing.

2. Show the classification problem from inside: what the family has to say to fit, what they can't say, and the moment one of them realizes that winning would mean describing their own life in a way that isn't true of it.

3. If they get the exemption, let it have a cost inside the community. Once the state recognizes one faction as the authentic voice of the practice, someone else in that community — a woman running a different version of it, say — ends up worse off than before. Don't resolve whether the law was right.

Also: no villains, no saints; the official enforcing the rule should be decent and tired. And don't let anyone deliver a speech about liberal neutrality versus multiculturalism — if a character has to explain the idea, make them do it badly, mid-sentence, to somebody who isn't really listening. Keep the country unnamed and don't worry about getting any real legal system right; I care about the concepts, not the procedure.
