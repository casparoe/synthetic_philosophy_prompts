I'm in my final year of a philosophy degree and I'm taking a course on the theory of knowledge. Next week I have to defend my coursework essay at the seminar, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet rather than reassurance. My thesis is that the tracking theory's famous failure of closure is a defect not of sensitivity itself but of attaching sensitivity to beliefs rather than to methods, and that once methods are individuated by the reasons an agent could actually give for her belief, closure survives ordinary deductions: I know I have hands and I know I'm not a brain in a vat, because the second belief rests on inference from the first rather than on bare perception, and the abominable conjunction only appears when the method gets described causally at precisely the point where that description suits the argument. I also use a case of my own, an accountant who reads a ledger and concludes that it balances, where the nearest world in which it does not balance is one in which a forger has made it look as though it does: on the narrow description of her method she comes out sensitive, and on the wide description she does not. The audience is my seminar: two postgraduates who work on safety and on virtue epistemology, one who is a Bayesian and thinks 'knowledge' is not a natural kind, and my tutor, who is a contextualist about 'knows'. Tell me the objections I will actually get from those four, not the ones any tracking-theory paper attracts, in the order of how much damage they do, phrased the way each of them would put it, each with a short reply and a note on where the reply is soft, and finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks. Write it as continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
