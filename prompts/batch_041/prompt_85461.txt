I have been keeping a private ledger of verdicts about when several things amount to one thing, and I want to know what principle, if any, they express. The verdicts, as they come to me:

- A brick wall, mortar included: one object. A dry-stone wall, no mortar, stones merely stacked and wedged: also one object, held together by nothing but weight and friction.
- A heap of gravel at the roadside: not an object, only stones that happen to lie near one another. A dune of loose sand: an object, and I do feel the difference from the gravel heap, though both are nothing but loose grains at rest.
- A welded sculpture: one object. A heap of scrap in exactly the same shape and arrangement, never joined, only balanced: not an object. This is the pair I trust most.
- A candle flame, a whirlpool, a hurricane: each is one thing, though no part of any of them persists for long and nothing in them is fastened to anything.
- The drifting accumulation of plastic in the North Pacific, or the stretch of ocean between two currents: not things; names for regions.
- A cat: an object. The same cat a week after it dies, a stiffening corpse: still an object, I think, though an organicist I once argued with insisted that the moment the life goes, the cat and the corpse both go with it and only particles remain. The same cat's atoms scattered: no object.
- My left shoe together with the Eiffel Tower: not an object, obviously. But I cannot say what is wrong with it that is not equally wrong with the dune.
- A corporation: one thing. A traffic jam: one thing, at least while I am stuck in it. A marriage: a relation between two people, not a third thing alongside them.
- A puddle of rainwater: one thing. A shadow: not a thing. A rainbow: not a thing, though it is no less visible than the puddle.

My own guess at the principle is this: some things compose a further thing when they are caught up in a single process, so that what happens to one makes a difference to what the others do and the whole sustains some behavior of its own. I suspect this is either too loose or circular, and I would like to know which. Does it make the traffic jam and the hurricane into things while leaving the plastic accumulation and the stretch of ocean as regions, and if so, on what ground? Or does it merely restate the verdicts?

So: give me the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, each stated precisely enough to be tested, with the case that forces each clause. Then apply whichever you favor to cases I have not listed: a bale of trash compacted and wired, a slime mold before and after its separate cells gather into one body, the organs of the dead cat kept functioning for transplant, the wave that travels around a stadium, and the same dry-stone wall after it has been knocked down and the stones lie where they fell. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts I must surrender for the simplest principle that keeps most of the others, and which single verdict you would have me reconsider first.
