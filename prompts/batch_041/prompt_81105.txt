I recently took a job as the clinical ethicist at a small community hospital, after eight years at a large academic medical center where nearly every ethical question had a corresponding page in a policy manual — a rule, a flowchart, a list of precedents from earlier committee meetings. My new hospital has almost nothing written down, and part of my job is to draft our first real ethics policy, so I have been spending my evenings trying to figure out what such a document is even for. A colleague on our committee, who did graduate work in philosophy before going to medical school, has been arguing that the whole project rests on a mistake. Her position, as I understand it, is that there are no genuine, non-trivial moral principles: whether some consideration counts as a reason for or against an action depends on the rest of the situation, so a feature that tells in favor of doing something in one case can tell against doing it in another. What guides a good clinician, she says, is trained judgment about the particular case in front of her, not the application of a rule.

I would like you to lay out the most important implications of that position for how I should actually do this job. Does a written code have any legitimate role, or is it at best a crutch and at worst a distortion of the reasoning it is meant to support? What becomes of the demand that we treat like cases alike — is consistency still a real requirement, and if so, what does it consist in once there are no principles to be consistent with? Do our committee's past rulings carry any authority as precedent? Can we fairly hold a clinician accountable for departing from a policy? And how should we be training the new members who rotate onto the committee each year? One of my colleagues has proposed what he takes to be a compromise: a code made up entirely of default rules, each defeasible by features of the individual case, together with a requirement that whoever departs from a rule write down the reasons for doing so. I cannot tell whether that is a genuine position or a rulebook with extra steps, and I would like your view. I also want you to address the strongest objection I have come across, which is that the moral supervenes on the non-moral: two cases identical in every non-moral respect cannot differ morally, and so any particularist must either accept that there are principles after all — at least trivial ones, of the form: in a situation with exactly these descriptive features, do whatever is required — or else deny supervenience outright. Tell me whether that argument actually does the work generalists want it to do.

Please take a definite position rather than surveying the options. Tell me which of these alleged implications really follow from the view and which are overblown, and if you think the particularist is right about some things and wrong about others, draw the line precisely and defend where you put it. I would rather have a committed, argumentative answer I can disagree with than a balanced summary that leaves me where I started. My own training is in nursing rather than philosophy, so spell the arguments out rather than gesturing at them. No need to cite sources — I have no library access at the moment and no interest in a reading list. Write in American English.
