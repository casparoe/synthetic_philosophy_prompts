A colleague of mine from work sent me this blog post he read about something called "cognitive penetration" and asked me if it's accurate. I'm a backend engineer, I did one philosophy module in college about ten years ago, so I genuinely can't tell. Here's his summary of it, pasted more or less word for word:

"Perception is affected by what you already believe, want and expect -- that's cognitive penetration. Your eyes take in raw data, but the brain filters it through your priors, so what you actually see depends on what you think you know. The famous experiments: hungry people see food as bigger, people who just climbed a hill see it as steeper, people shown an ambiguous picture see whichever thing they were primed to expect. This kills the modular view of the mind -- Fodor said perceptual systems are encapsulated from the rest of cognition, and these results show they aren't. The upshot is that perception is theory-laden, so there's no neutral observation that can settle a scientific dispute, because the evidence you see is already shaped by the theory you hold. And it means you can't fix someone's false belief just by showing them the facts, since they'll perceive the facts in whatever way fits the belief they've got."

That last bit sounds way too strong to me, and I'm not sure the science part follows either, but the middle stuff sounds like things I've half-heard. Could you go through it and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong -- and whether the closing claims about scientific evidence and about not being able to correct beliefs actually follow from the parts that are true?
