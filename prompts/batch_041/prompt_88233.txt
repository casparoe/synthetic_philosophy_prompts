I belong to a small decision theory reading group that meets in a back room above a pub, and after last week's session two of the members, Alex and Bea, fell into an argument that outlasted our being asked to leave and is still simmering in the group chat. (1) Alex's opening claim was that disagreement between well-informed and rational people is inherently unstable. There is, he said, a theorem showing that if two agents share a prior over the same space of possibilities and each of them knows the other's posterior probability for some event — and knows that the other knows, and so on up the hierarchy — then those posteriors must be exactly equal; so a persistent disagreement between two informed people means that something has gone wrong somewhere: at least one of them is not reasoning correctly, or they are not really pooling the same evidence, or they are not really disagreeing about the same question. He added that this has practical teeth — what he called the common prior assumption is not merely a technical nicety — because if he finds himself permanently at odds with someone he regards as his epistemic equal, he takes the theorem to mean that he should shift his own probability towards hers, since the only alternative is to assume, without justification, that he is the one who has got it right. (2) Bea's reply was that the theorem is a conditional whose antecedent is never satisfied: real people do not share priors, the very existence of reasonable disagreement is evidence that they do not, and you cannot extract a requirement of rationality from an assumption that is simply false of the agents you are describing. (3) Alex answered that the common prior is not an arbitrary posit but follows from the thought that rational agents who have the same information ought to reach the same conclusion; Bea called this circular, since that is exactly the point at issue.

(4) She then pressed a further objection. The theorem, she said, also takes for granted that the two agents carve up the possibilities in the same way, working from the same list of hypotheses; if he is entertaining a possibility she has never considered, or if the two of them conceive of what could be the case differently, then they can disagree perfectly rationally, common prior or no, and the theorem simply does not reach them. (5) Alex conceded that the result is stated for a fixed space of states, but said that this only shows the parties must be talking about the same thing, which is a precondition of any disagreement worth the name; he added that common knowledge of the other's beliefs is in any case never achieved in practice, since it would require an infinite hierarchy of knowledge that no finite conversation can deliver, and that the theorem is therefore an idealisation telling you what to aim at rather than a prediction about actual people. (6) Bea replied that if the theorem holds only of an ideal case then it cannot be used to discipline anyone's actual credences, which is precisely what Alex wants to do with it; and she observed that the case they kept returning to — the two of them reading the same report and coming away with different views about whether a drug works — seemed to her to show the opposite of what Alex claimed, namely that two competent people can read the same evidence and reasonably differ, whatever a theorem about ideal agents may say. Alex replied that if they really did share a prior and each knew the other's confidence, then at most one of them could be rational, and that this is a hard saying but a true one.

I have deliberately stayed out of it and would like an outside verdict rather than more heat. Please take on the role of someone who works on both the formal theory of common priors and the epistemology of peer disagreement — a philosopher of probability, say, rather than a partisan of either camp — and think the whole thing through carefully before you commit to anything. Before judging, set out in your own words what each of the six moves is actually claiming, so that I can see you have understood the argument rather than pattern-matched it. Then adjudicate move by move: for each one, say whether it is clearly mistaken, defensible but overstated, or clearly right, and give a brief justification for the rating. Finally, tell me who has the better of the exchange overall, which of Bea's objections genuinely damage Alex's practical conclusion and which of Alex's points genuinely tell against Bea's dismissal, and what both of them are missing — in particular, I would like to know whether any version of Alex's advice to move towards one's epistemic equal survives Bea's objections, and what exactly it would have to be qualified by. Please write in American English, and do not bother with reading suggestions; I want the argument itself, worked through.
