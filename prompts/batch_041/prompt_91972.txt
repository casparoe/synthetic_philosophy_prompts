I have been reading the rationalist blogosphere for long enough that its standard moves have become familiar furniture, and one piece I keep returning to is the transitivity of better than. The received view treats better than as a strict ordering — irreflexive and transitive — and treats any preference structure that violates transitivity as not merely eccentric but exploitable, on the grounds that a being with a preference cycle can be drained of money by a sequence of trades it willingly accepts at every step and regrets at the end. Set against this is the spectrum argument: arrange a chain of cases in which each case differs from its neighbour by something negligible while the two ends differ enormously, and it becomes hard to deny that each adjacent case is at least as good as the next, and equally hard to accept that the endpoints are on a par — yet if better than is transitive, one of those denials has to give.

I want a joke built on precisely this tension, and to make sure it is about a concrete chain rather than a vague gesture at sorites, I will supply the chain myself. Take a life of unbroken mild contentment. Take a second life identical to it except for a single extra second of mild headache at noon on one day; take a third identical to the second but with two such seconds; and continue, adding one second of mild headache at a time, through a life containing a full year of continuous mild headache (still mild, still bearable) and onward, until you arrive at a life of unremitting severe agony. Every step from one life to the next looks like a change no one could reasonably care about; the two ends are as far apart as anything in ethics. That is the chain. Feel free to let the joke wander off this particular ladder into a preference cycle, a sequence of trades, a demon, a barista, or whatever device makes the exploitability vivid.

The joke has to be funny to people who actually know the debate: the punchline should turn on the content — on the intransitivity itself, on the money-pump consequence, on the fact that preserving transitivity forces an endpoint nobody wants, or on some other structural feature of the problem — and not on the mere mention of a philosopher's name or a general air of nerdiness. Someone who knows the material should laugh and recognize the move; someone who does not should at least be able to follow the mechanism after one sentence of explanation. Keep it short enough to tell aloud at a meetup, and do not worry about citations or references to particular papers.
