Two people in my reading group have been going at each other about the Löbian obstacle for like four months now and I want them to just write the damn joint statement instead of re-litigating it every week. Here's the setup so you know what they're on about: the obstacle is the thing where an agent that only acts on what it can prove, and that has to reason about its own proofs — to predict what it's going to do, or to check that a self-modified successor of itself is safe — can't consistently hold the general principle that whatever it proves is true. Löb's theorem is what bites: a consistent theory can't prove "if I prove φ then φ" for any φ it doesn't already prove. So its self-trust gets stuck at whatever it can already prove, which is a weird and annoying place to be stuck. Camp A (the wall people) says that's a real, inescapable limit: no consistent proof-based agent can fully trust itself or a successor it builds, and every proposed workaround just relocates the problem one level up. Camp B (the artifact people) says it's a byproduct of one very idealized design — deductive certainty + a uniform reflection principle + self-reference — and that a realistic bounded agent with uncertainty about its own reliability just walks around it.

What I actually want them to fight over on paper, because I think it's the crux neither of them will state cleanly: Camp B keeps saying that if you just bound the proof length — the agent trusts only proofs below some fixed size n, of which there are finitely many, so it can go through them one at a time and verify the lot — then self-trust comes back for free, and the whole obstacle is a resource problem dressed up as a logical one. Camp A says that bound has to keep growing the moment the agent has to reason about a successor, since proving things about the successor's proofs takes longer proofs than the successor's own, so it's still a wall, just a wall that moves. Also make them settle: whether deductive certainty is essential to the obstacle or just a modeling choice, and what a logically-uncertain agent that holds its own conclusions with some probability actually gets and loses; whether "never reason about your own reasoning at all" is a genuine dodge or just an agent that can't improve itself; and whether the blocked principle ever costs an agent a concrete action it would otherwise take, or whether it's vacuous in practice.
