I am a programme officer at a grantmaking foundation that distributes roughly two hundred grants a year, mostly between £20,000 and £500,000, in farmed animal welfare. The proposals we see run from measures that spare a very large number of animals a mild, chronic discomfort, to measures that spare a small number a severe, acute pain; a growing share also claim gains in positive welfare — enrichment, comfort, the chance to forage — which our trustees are increasingly inclined to fund. Our caseworkers have no philosophical training, each proposal must be assessed in about an hour, and the funding committee requires a written justification for every score.

The trustees have adopted, in outline, a position we have been calling lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and I need to turn it into something the caseworkers can actually run. As we have stated it, the position has four clauses: (i) pain is the only thing bad in itself, and pleasure at best a tie-breaker; (ii) harms fall into a band structure we already use, A to E, where band E is roughly the severity of a day of untreated agony; (iii) a single band E harm is worse than any number of harms in bands A to D, however large the number — the lexical claim; (iv) below the threshold, harms in bands A to D aggregate against one another in the ordinary way, so a great many small harms can outweigh a few moderate ones.

Please write the procedure. Give me the questions to put to a proposal in order, what answer to each would be sufficient to move on, where the procedure stops with a refusal, and where it must hand the decision to the committee because the question is one of values rather than facts. It should be something a caseworker can run from a spreadsheet in an hour, and it should say plainly which of its steps are judgments dressed up as arithmetic. Then run it on three proposals: (a) a scheme improving the housing of two million birds from band B to band A; (b) a scheme ending a band E practice for six hundred animals; (c) a proposal that adds enrichment, scored as a gain in positive welfare, to a system that continues to inflict band D harms on the animals it enriches. I expect the procedure to rank (b) first, and I would rather be told if it does not.

There are two questions I want the procedure to answer rather than dodge, because they are where I expect it to break. First, composition: a proposal may impose a very large number of individually sub-threshold micro-harms, each imperceptible on its own, which jointly amount to agony. Does the lexical claim treat that as one band E harm or as many band A harms — and does your procedure's answer follow from the stated position, or is it a stipulation you are adding? Second, offset: clause (i) says pleasure is at best a tie-breaker, but clause (iv) lets small harms aggregate; can any quantity of positive welfare in case (c) offset a band D harm, and if your procedure says no, which clause is doing the work?

Finally, tell me where the procedure will misfire, what it cannot decide, and whether the sharpness of the A-to-E boundary is doing real work or merely concealing a value choice we ought to be making openly.
