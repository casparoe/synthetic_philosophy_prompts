I'm a second-year PhD student in a philosophy of mathematics seminar, and I keep getting frustrated by how quickly my classmates reduce intuitionism to a one-line slogan — classical logic with the law of excluded middle deleted — and then move on. I want to feel the force of the view from the inside rather than from the outside, so I've decided to write a piece of fiction for our end-of-term reading. I'd like you to act as a novelist who is also a competent philosopher of mathematics and to write that story for me, thinking carefully about the logic at every step instead of gesturing at it.

Here is the premise I want you to build on. Set the story in a mid-sized city whose local culture has internalized a constructivist ethic that reaches well beyond the university: to assert that something exists is to have produced it and to be able to exhibit it; to assert that one thing or another is the case is to have settled which; and the claim that it is impossible for something to be otherwise — the double negation, so to speak — is treated as a genuinely weaker claim than the claim that it is so. I want the characters to feel that gap in their daily lives rather than merely know it as a rule. A city planner, for instance, might be able to prove that a bridge cannot fail to be needed without yet having any bridge; a detective might establish that the culprit cannot be nobody while still being unable to name anyone; a grieving parent might be certain that it cannot be that the child is gone and yet not be entitled to say the child is present. Build one such situation — pick whichever character and stakes you find most fertile — into a real story with a beginning, a complication, and an ending, rather than an allegory in which the ideas simply lecture the reader. Let the consequences of the constructive rules drive the plot, and let at least one character be tempted to cheat by reasoning classically.

After the story, add a short reflective afterword, addressed to me rather than to the characters, that (a) names which features of intuitionistic mathematics the story was dramatizing and marks where the dramatization is faithful and where it distorts, (b) explains why an intuitionist would regard the gap between the double negation and the plain assertion, and the insistence on actually producing a witness, as matters of meaning rather than mere caution, and (c) flags any point where I would be likely to smuggle in a classical assumption without noticing. Please begin by brainstorming three or four possible scenes and telling me which you chose and why before you write the story itself. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words across the story and the afterword, and write in American English.
