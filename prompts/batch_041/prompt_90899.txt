I'm in a reading group that has spent the term on proposals to replace political judgment with market aggregation, and I've drawn the case report this week. My case is the Policy Analysis Market, the DARPA project that was killed in the summer of 2003. Here are the facts I want you to work from, so that nothing in your answer hangs on details you can't check: PAM was to be a small futures exchange run out of DARPA's Information Awareness Office, on which traders would buy and sell contracts on political and economic developments in the Middle East; the design originated with a private research firm, and the rationale was the standard one, that market prices aggregate dispersed information better than expert panels or polls do. The project was cancelled within about a day of a Senate press conference at which two senators attacked it as a government-run market in assassination and terrorism — the outcry was touched off by sample interface screens that listed, among many humdrum items, an assassination and a coup as possible contract types — and the head of the office that housed it resigned soon afterward. I don't want you to relitigate who was to blame or what exactly was on those screens. Stipulate with me that the informational case for the market was sound, and that a good part of the opposition was a moral reaction rather than a factual claim, and analyze the episode.

The theory I want you to run it through is futarchy, in Robin Hanson's sense of it: the polity votes on values — it settles, democratically, on some measurable welfare metric — and then uses markets rather than votes to settle beliefs, so that a proposal is adopted if and only if conditional markets predict a higher value of the metric under adoption than under non-adoption. Two features of that proposal matter here. First, it is a decision rule and not merely a forecasting tool, and its two halves come apart: PAM was all beliefs and no values, and it bound no one, since nobody's policy depended on its prices. Second, its slogan is that we vote on values and bet on beliefs, which presupposes that the values at stake are already settled and that only the beliefs are in dispute. So the first thing I want from you is a disciplined mapping. Was PAM a futarchy in embryo, a partial implementation, or something futarchy would not recognize as its own? Under futarchy's description, who are the players — the traders, the agency, the oversight committees, the press, the public — what are their options and payoffs, and what is each of them doing? Then tell me honestly where the theory goes silent or misdescribes the episode. My own suspicion, which I'd like you to test rather than adopt, is that the interesting failure here is not informational but legitimatory: what killed PAM was a dispute about whether certain things ought to be priced at all, and about what it means for a state to be the counterparty to such a market, and that is a dispute about values, not beliefs. If I'm right, say what it costs futarchy — does the episode show that values and beliefs cannot be cleanly separated, and if so, is that fatal to the proposal or a constraint a better design could meet? Also take up the incentive objection the critics gestured at, that anyone in a position to cause the priced event has a reason to trade on it, and say whether futarchy's conditional markets inherit that problem or defuse it by construction.

Finally, I want the case to run back the other way, theory tested by episode. Futarchy is a normative proposal that says almost nothing about how a polity could ever get from here to there, and this episode is one of the few real attempts to put prediction markets inside a government; it lasted about a day. Is that a confirmation of the theory, an anomaly for it, or a case it never anticipated? In particular: could futarchy's own adoption ever be decided by a futarchy, and if not, what follows about its account of its own feasibility? And would the futarchy version of the same exercise — conditional markets on the welfare consequences of a war-and-occupation decision of the sort the United States was making that summer — have been any less vulnerable to the objection that killed PAM, or does the objection attach to any price set on death, whatever the mechanism? Write it in American English as continuous prose, roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, the way you'd write a section of a seminar paper: committed where the theory earns commitment, explicit about where it is silent rather than forcing it to speak. I don't need citations or a reading list; I know the literature and want your argument.
