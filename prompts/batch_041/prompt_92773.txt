I am a third-year undergraduate in philosophy and biology, and this term I am also working as a research assistant for the clinical ethics and coverage advisory committee at our university teaching hospital. The committee meets twice a month and reviews roughly half a dozen requests for interventions that sit near the line between treating a condition and enhancing a person: growth hormone for a child with idiopathic short stature, stimulant medication for a resident who wants to study for board exams, testosterone for age-related decline, and occasionally requests for elective procedures whose main appeal is competitive advantage. We have twenty minutes per case, the members are clinicians, a chaplain, an administrator, and one lay representative, and our recommendations go to the insurer and sometimes shape what the patient can access. What we lack is a defensible, repeatable procedure. I do not want an essay on the therapy/enhancement distinction, and I especially do not want a reading list; I want something the committee can actually run in the meeting, with the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, the points where the process stops with a no, and the points where it must hand the matter to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts.

My seminar has left me suspicious of any procedure that treats disease as a natural kind or normal function as a purely statistical fact. The distinction I want you to operationalize is between four modes of intervention: restoration, which returns someone to a functional level they previously had; compensation, which offsets a stable deficit relative to a reference class; augmentation, which raises capacity above the person's own prior baseline while remaining within what is typical for that reference class; and optimization, which raises capacity beyond the typical range or secures a positional advantage over others. I also want the procedure to confront two problems that our committee keeps hitting. The first is the counterfactual baseline problem: for many conditions we cannot know what the person's function would have been without the intervention, and for children we cannot know what they would have chosen. The second is the positional problem: some interventions help only or mainly because others do not have them, so their value depends on distribution. Please build the procedure around these ideas, but make it usable by people who have not read the theory. It should say explicitly which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped, and it should mark the places where the procedure will misfire.

Then run the procedure on three requests and show your work. First, recombinant growth hormone for a ten-year-old with idiopathic short stature, predicted adult height below the first percentile, no identifiable pathology, and parents who are worried about social treatment. Second, modafinil for a healthy internal-medicine resident whose sleep is disrupted by night rotations and who wants it for board preparation; the resident says it would restore the focus that sleep loss has taken away. Third, deep brain stimulation for a patient with treatment-resistant depression who also hopes the device will leave them sharper and more productive than they were before the depression began. For at least one of these, I want the procedure to return an answer I will not like, and I want you to say why the procedure nevertheless commits us to it. End with a short section on what the procedure cannot decide, including any case where the committee should refuse to give a recommendation rather than pretend the procedure has settled it.
