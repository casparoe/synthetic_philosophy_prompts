I am putting together a position statement on animal minds for a workshop and would like it audited for consistency before I circulate it. I genuinely do not know which parts I would abandon if pressed, so please do not treat any of them as the obvious weak link. Here is where I stand.

(1) Welfare is exhausted by experience. Nothing is intrinsically good or bad for a creature except what it feels; pain and pleasure, and states sufficiently like them, are the whole of the matter.

(2) Moral realism. There are facts of the matter about what is good or bad for a creature which do not depend on what anyone believes or feels about them.

(3) Sentience comes in degrees. There is a continuum running from a creature with a rich, temporally extended mental life down to one with only the faintest and most fragmentary flicker of experience, and no non-arbitrary point on that continuum at which experience begins.

(4) Even so, if you press me for the probability that an insect has experience at all, I will put it well below one half. I am more confident that a fish feels pain than that a beetle does.

(5) The scale argument is sound in its broad shape. Because insects are so staggeringly numerous, the total quantity of insect welfare at stake could plausibly exceed that of all vertebrate animals combined, so insect welfare deserves substantial weight in our practical decisions — far more than it usually receives. I take insect-welfare research to be among the most important neglected causes I could support.

(6) I am not a fanatic. I think it is a mistake to let minuscule probabilities of enormous stakes dominate practical reasoning: I would not reorganise my life around a one-in-a-billion chance of an outcome of astronomical value, and I am suspicious of arguments whose practical upshot changes dramatically once a few zeros are added to a headcount.

(7) Obligation is bounded by our epistemic position. To the extent that a creature's sentience is permanently inscrutable to us, the demand it places on us is correspondingly weakened; we cannot be required to organise our lives around matters we have no way of assessing.

(8) When a wasp blunders into my kitchen, I kill it, and I do not think I have done anything wrong. I would not think better of someone who spent five minutes coaxing it out of the window.

Please audit this. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and how severely? Which pairs only look like tensions and dissolve once the terms are made precise? For each genuine tension, say what would have to be given up or restated, what the cheapest repair would be, and what that repair would cost me elsewhere in the list. Finally, tell me which single revision would resolve the most, and whether the package as it stands is coherent enough to circulate. Please write your answer in British English.
