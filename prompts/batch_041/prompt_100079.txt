I am presenting at a departmental colloquium a paper whose thesis is that Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is routinely invoked as a vindication of utilitarianism but in fact establishes something considerably weaker: that when each individual's preferences and the social preference are all representable by von Neumann–Morgenstern expected utility, and the social preference respects unanimous individual preference, the social utility function is an affine combination of the individual utility functions; and that the further step to a utilitarian criterion depends on a premise about interpersonal comparability, and about what the weights mean, that the theorem does not itself supply. My positive proposal is that the slide from the theorem to the claim that rational social choice must be utilitarian equivocates between aggregating over persons and aggregating over states of the world, and that the premise the theorem leaves out should be supplied statewise, ex post, rather than ex ante.

The audience is a social choice and decision theory seminar. Three of them know the representation theorems and the axioms of expected utility cold and will hold me to exactly what the theorem does and does not say. Two are welfare economists broadly sympathetic to Harsanyi who suspect that the utilitarian reading is perfectly defensible and that I am demolishing a straw man. One is a Rawlsian who will ask why anyone needed a theorem to see the point. One works on the ex-ante/ex-post distinction and will press me with cases in which ex-ante expected-utility aggregation conflicts with a statewise Pareto condition. Prepare my question-period prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that member of the audience would phrase it, each with a reply I can deliver in two minutes and a candid note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one objection I am hoping nobody raises.

Keep the prep sheet honest about the state of the literature: distinguish what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading of Harsanyi, or of the ex-ante/ex-post question, as though it were the consensus — where the experts disagree, say that they disagree and characterize the disagreement rather than resolving it by fiat. End the response with a single final line of the form \answer{...} naming, in a few words, the objection that would do me the most damage if I walked in unprepared for it.
