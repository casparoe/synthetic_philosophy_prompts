Last night I got drawn into the longest argument I have been part of in years, and it was over a thought experiment that a friend of mine has been refining for months. I was in the room for all of it and came away genuinely unable to say who had the better of it, so I would like the exchange adjudicated properly.

The set-up he insists we accept is this. Take a living human brain and replace its neurons one at a time with silicon devices stipulated to be input-output equivalent to the neurons they replace: same outputs for the same inputs, same connections, same modulation. Nothing else about the person is altered, and the person is awake and behaving normally throughout. He adds two stipulations, because he says the standard version of the puzzle is too easy. First, the chips are not analogue all the way down: each runs in discrete clock cycles and matches the original dynamics only over intervals of a few milliseconds, so the fine temporal grain of the biological process is not preserved. Second, every excised neuron is kept alive in a dish, where it continues to do whatever neurons do when isolated.

The argument went roughly like this.

Anna: no single step changes anything that matters. If the person is still there after the first replacement — and she must be, since one cell out of billions cannot be the difference between a subject and no subject — then she is still there after the second, and by the same reasoning after the last. Refusing the conclusion is like insisting that a man who loses one hair at a time must go bald at some particular hair. It is sorites reasoning run backwards.

Ben: that only shows that if you grant the conclusion at each step you get it at the end. The real question is whether being the same person is the sort of thing that can survive a swap of the underlying stuff at all. Consider a photocopier: each copy is caused by the previous one, and after a thousand generations you have a page causally descended from the original, yet nobody thinks the last page is the original. So causal continuity plus information preservation cannot be sufficient for identity.

Anna: the photocopy case is disanalogous, because there the chain passes through a thousand distinct objects, each newly made, whereas here one continuing system merely swaps components. Ben: that is precisely the point at issue. Calling it one continuing system is what you were supposed to establish; you have defined the system as whatever persists, so of course it persists.

Anna: then take the discrete clock cycles. If that mattered, there would have to be some threshold of temporal resolution below which a subject vanishes, and nothing downstream could possibly detect the difference, so nothing psychological can turn on it. Ben: I am not claiming the discreteness kills anyone. I am claiming it exposes what you are actually preserving, which is a functional profile. And a profile can be run twice. Run the whole replacement process on two machines in parallel and you get two people, each of whom would sincerely claim to be the original. A criterion that cannot distinguish one from two is not a criterion of identity.

Anna: duplication is a different case; the actual process never branches. Ben: then identity is being secured by a fact — no branching — that has nothing to do with the psychology. So either psychology is doing the work, in which case duplication is a fatal problem, or something non-psychological is doing the work, and then you owe us an account of what it is; my money is on the biological continuity you were so keen to throw away.

Please go through this move by move and tell me who is right about which specific point, and what each of them is missing. I do not want a survey of the positions on offer: I want a committed verdict, including a definite answer on whether the person survives the process as described and on whether either of the two stipulations changes the answer, and I want the reasoning that forces that verdict rather than a list of considerations on both sides.
