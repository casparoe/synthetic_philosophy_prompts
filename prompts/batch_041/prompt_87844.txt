I'm a science reporter and I've somehow been voluntold to run a brown-bag lunch at the office — forty-five minutes, no slides, a dozen reporters who cover health and tech, can skim a study abstract, have never taken a stats course. I picked the reference class problem. They don't know the name and I'd honestly rather they leave with the thing itself than the phrase, so don't explain it to me, plan the session: what has to be in place before it even looks like a problem to them, the order the pieces have to come in and why that order and not some other, the one example that carries the whole forty-five minutes and what that example has to have and lack, the misconceptions they'll walk in with or form along the way and the exact point in the sequence where each gets headed off, one question I can put to the room at the end that tells me whether it landed, and what I should openly refuse to get into at this level.

Here's my thinking so far, argue with it. My carrier was going to be a risk calculator: I put a hypothetical patient up — sixty-year-old smoker with a family history — the calculator spits out a 12% chance of a heart attack in ten years, and then I show that the 12% is doing almost all its work through a choice of class that nobody in the room is making consciously, and that if I ask twelve reporters to say what the class is, I get twelve different answers and they're all defensible. Is that the right thing to hang it on, or does it quietly teach them that the problem is about medical stats? Second: I want to end on the claim that Sleeping Beauty is just the reference class problem wearing a costume — I read that somewhere, I half-beleive it, tell me if it survives contact or if I'm about to say something embarrassing in front of the desk. And a third thing I can't settle: do I have to make them care about the frequentist/subjectivist split first, or is that a tangent that eats the session? Half the room is Bayesian by temperament without knowing the word, and I can't tell if that's an asset or a landmine. My guesses at the misconceptions, for what they're worth: "you just pick the narrowest class you can" and "a probability of a single event is meaningless." Keep the whole thing under 700 words.
