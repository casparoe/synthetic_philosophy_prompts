I would like your comments on a draft essay. Please be a demanding reader: I want to know where the argument is weak, where it overreaches, and whether the central claim is genuinely interesting or merely a familiar complaint in new clothes. Assume I am writing for readers who already know the method under discussion, so you need not explain it back to me; what I need is an assessment of whether my criticism of it lands.

Draft essay, provisional title: The Residual Figure: Why the Criteria of Authenticity Cannot Identify Anyone

1. The method and its promise. The criteria of authenticity are a family of tests used to sort Gospel material into the earlier and the later: the criterion of embarrassment (material that would have embarrassed the early church is unlikely to have been invented); the criterion of dissimilarity (material that cannot be derived either from the Jewish setting or from the church's own interests is privileged); multiple attestation (material appearing in several independent strands is privileged); coherence (material consistent with what has already been accepted); and linguistic tests for a Semitic substratum. The criteria are meant to be applied to small units of tradition and to combine, so that a unit surviving several tests is judged more likely to be authentic and the survivors can be assembled into a portrait of the figure behind the texts.

2. The familiar objection. It is often said that the criteria are not independent: embarrassment and dissimilarity are both, at bottom, tests of what is anomalous relative to presumed tendencies, and multiple attestation, the one genuinely independent test, probes transmission rather than origin. I grant this, but do not think it decisive, because the tests can be combined through a joint likelihood rather than by multiplying separate ratios, and a joint likelihood need not factor. The real objection, I claim, lies elsewhere.

3. The residual-figure argument. Every criterion selects for anomaly. A unit passes because it is unlike the surrounding Judaism or unlike the early church's interests — because it is, relative to its context, surprising. But surprise is a relation, not an intrinsic property of the unit, and the relation holds to exactly the context that the method sets aside. Worse, an individual is individuated in part by their engagement with that context: what makes a person that person is a pattern of accepting, resisting, and reshaping the conventions they inherit. Subtract the conventions and the pattern vanishes with them. The method is therefore built to return a figure defined only negatively, by what he was not, and a negatively defined figure cannot be identified with any particular individual: two candidates embedded in the same world, differing only in which of its conventions each happened to defy, would yield the same output. The more successfully the criteria work, the thinner the figure becomes; carried to completion they would leave nothing, since any human being is continuous with their world in more ways than they depart from it.

4. A repair and its cost. One might try to escape by abandoning unit-level criteria for an inference to the best explanation over whole lives: treat the criteria as likelihoods, place a prior over what a figure of that time and place might plausibly be, and update. But the posterior is then dominated by the prior, and the prior must encode precisely the contextual background the criteria were meant to neutralize. The repair does not escape the problem; it exposes that the method's output is a function of the historian's antecedent imagination of the period, dressed as evidence.

5. Conclusion. The usual question — whether the criteria are reliable — is the wrong one. Even ideal criteria would answer only what about the figure was unlike his world, which is not the same as who he was.

Please comment on the following, in order of importance to me. First, is the argument in section 3 sound, or does it prove too much? In particular, can you build the strongest reply on behalf of the method — for instance, that a pattern of engagement with a context is itself detectable and need not be subtracted with the context? Second, is section 4 coherent, or have I conflated a prior's being necessary with its being dominant? Third, is the essay's payoff genuinely different from the familiar complaint in section 2, or have I dressed up the same point in new language? Fourth, would the essay be stronger if it worked through a single criterion in concrete detail rather than treating the family as a whole — and if so, which one? Finally, flag any place where I attribute to the method a commitment its practitioners would not accept.
