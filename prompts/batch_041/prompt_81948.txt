I'm in a metaphysics reading group — we meet in a pub on Thursdays, which this argument has ruined two of — and we've been on material constitution for a few weeks. One of the members brought in an argument I can't shake, and the case is my aunt's, so I know it's clean. She bought a lump of clay at a craft stall, kept it in a drawer as a paperweight for about three years, then my cousin squeezed it into a little horse, and then a week later it got rained on and slumped back into a shapeless lump. Nothing was added and nothing was taken away at any point; the same clay sat in that drawer the whole time. Here's the argument, roughly as he wrote it up. (1) If a and b are one and the same thing, then whatever is true of a is true of b — that's just what being one thing amounts to. (2) Three years ago the lump of clay existed; three years ago the horse did not. (3) So there is something true of the lump — that it existed three years ago — that is not true of the horse. (4) So the lump and the horse are two things and not one; they merely happen to be made of the same clay and to sit in the same place. (5) Nothing in 1-4 is special to clay or to horses: the recipe runs whenever some matter gets arranged into a new thing. (6) So run it again on the paperweight, on the lump, on the horse, and on any arbitrary way of carving up that same clay, and you don't get two objects in the drawer, you get a crowd of them, with no principled line between the real ones and the made-up ones. And here's the bit I keep chewing on: (7) the same rule runs backwards too. The clay was soft three years ago and it's hard now; if soft and hard are genuine properties of the thing itself rather than just relations it bears to times, then one and the same lump has two properties it can't have at once, so the lump from back then and the lump from today must be two things after all — which would mean nothing survives any change at all, the lump included.

He says the moral is that constitution without identity is incoherent and we have to pick a side: either there are no statues, only clay arranged statuewise, or premise 1 is too strong as stated and there's no fact of the matter about how many things are in that drawer. I don't want either, but I also dont want to just wave my hands at it. So: tell me which numbered step is the one to give up, say honestly why it looked fine when he wrote it up, and then price the going fixes — relativizing properties to times, saying identity is sortal-relative (same clay, different statue), saying the statue is just the clay under a description and there's nothing over and above it, or biting the bullet on coincident objects and giving a principled account of which ones there are. I'd most like to keep 1, since a version of identity without indiscernibility sounds to me like a different subject than identity, but tell me what that costs. No reading lists please — I've got plenty and they never tell me which step is wrong; I want to know where the argument actually slips, if it does, and what I pay to say so.
