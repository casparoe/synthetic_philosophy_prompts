I am preparing a recorded conversation for a series on the history of philosophy of science, and the next episode is with a scholar whose book defends a deliberately revisionary reading of Otto Neurath's visual education work. On her account, the picture-statistics project — the Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics and its later ISOTYPE form — is not a popularization sideline but the practical completion of Neurath's anti-foundationalism: the point at which his coherentism, his physicalism, and his insistence that knowledge is a social and institutional achievement all cash out in a working notation. She also holds the stronger claim that ISOTYPE should be treated as a genuine language in its own right, though she grants that it is not a full language in the sense in which a first-order language is, and she is fond of describing it as a helping or auxiliary notation that was never intended to replace verbal language.

Write me eight to ten interview questions for this conversation, together with the follow-ups they are most likely to need. I want the sequence to build, so that her recurring move — retreating to the claim that the notation was only ever a helping language, not a replacement for words — becomes progressively harder to use as a general shield. Each question should come with a one-line note on what it is designed to expose, and where a question has an obvious standard reply, the follow-up should be keyed to that reply and should press past it rather than restating the original objection.

The set must include at least the following, though you should order them so that the pressure accumulates rather than arriving as a list of disconnected objections. One question must put the anti-foundationalism and the visual program into direct tension: if protocol statements are revisable, if there is no tabula rasa, and if Neurath's boat is the right image for inquiry, what entitles the pictogram to present social facts as neutral with respect to interpretation and culture? Her likely reply is that the transformation from source data to finished chart is an explicit and accountable act of interpretation rather than a concealed one; the follow-up should ask what distinguishes an accountable transformation from an unaccountable one, and whether that distinction can be drawn without appealing to exactly the sort of privileged vantage point her reading denies. A second question must press the logical poverty of the notation: how it handles negation, conditionals, quantified claims about non-typical cases, and modality, and whether that poverty is a limitation of the notation or a feature of the restricted domain it was built for. Her likely reply is that it was built for statistical facts, not for argument generally; the follow-up should ask whether statistical fact is a natural kind of content or a category constituted by the notation's own constraints. A third question must take up the transformer role and the division of labour between data, transformer, and chart, asking whether inserting a trained human interpreter between the source and the display is a concession to the theory-ladenness of observation that Neurath elsewhere resists, or an institutional answer to it that relocates the problem rather than solving it. A fourth must ask about the unity of science: whether a notation that cannot be rendered sentence-by-sentence into the physicalist thing-language can serve the unity project at all, or whether, on her reading, unity has to be relocated from a shared language to a shared practice and a shared encyclopedia. A fifth must situate the program in Red Vienna and in adult education, pressing the charge that the claim of universal readability is an empirical hypothesis, an ideological commitment, or both, and that the typical figure in a pictogram is a normative construct rather than a neutral one. A sixth must confront reception history: the same graphic system was taken up across politically opposed regimes and adapted to opposed purposes, and the question is whether that uptake supports the universality thesis or shows the notation to be a flexible rhetorical instrument whose apparent transparency is itself a persuasive effect. The sequence should end with a question about what would count against her reading — what archival or conceptual evidence would show that the visual work really was a side project, or that it betrays rather than completes the coherentism — and a follow-up asking what she would have to give up elsewhere in her interpretation if the picture language turned out to be ineliminably dependent on the verbal commentary that accompanied it in the museum setting.

Avoid questions that merely invite her to summarize the view, recount how she came to it, or speculate about what Neurath would think of contemporary data visualization. Do not ask for citations, archival references, or reading lists.
