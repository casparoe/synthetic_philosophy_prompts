I'd like a case analysis, not a survey of positions. Take the career of continental drift: in the standard telling, Wegener argued from 1912 that the continents had once been joined and had since separated; the case rested on the fit of the coastlines, the same fossil species and rock sequences turning up on opposite shores, and glacial deposits in what are now tropical latitudes; the idea was nevertheless rejected by most geologists for a generation, chiefly because no known force could move continents; and in the 1960s, once seafloor spreading and the magnetic striping of the ocean floor were worked out, the same idea became the organizing framework of the earth sciences, without anyone who had rejected it in 1930 regarding themselves as having been merely careless. I gather historians have complicated the "no mechanism" story; if that matters to your analysis, say so, but do not let the analysis hinge on it.

Run that episode through the two pragmatist accounts of truth, and let the traffic go in both directions. On Peirce's side: the meaning of a concept is exhausted by the practical bearings we can conceive it to have, reality is what it is regardless of our opinion of it, and truth is the opinion that inquiry is fated to settle on in the end, however long that takes. On James's side: truth is not a stagnant property but something that happens to an idea, an idea becomes true in the course of being verified, and the true is what it is good for us to believe, for definite and assignable reasons.

Keep three questions strictly apart, since I think most discussions collapse them: (a) was drift true in 1930; (b) was it reasonable for a geologist in 1930 to believe it; (c) could anyone in 1930 have known it to be true. Say what each pragmatist is committed to on each question, and whether either can answer all three without fudging.

Then press where I expect the mapping to strain. First, the mechanism objection: the rejection was not that drift failed to pay off in practice but that it lacked a causal story, so ask whether either theory can distinguish a belief that has not paid off yet from a belief that is unsupported. Second, the cash-value problem running backwards: drift had almost no practical payoff in 1930 and enormous payoff after 1960, since plate tectonics now underpins seismology and mineral and hydrocarbon exploration. If truth is fixed by what is expedient in our thinking, does the timing of acceptance start to look like it tracks usefulness rather than evidence, and is that a reductio of James or just the ordinary fact that evidence accumulates? Third, convergence versus consensus: Peirce's indefinitely deferred end of inquiry never arrives, so any present consensus can be relabelled provisional. Is that a virtue of fallibilism or a way of making the theory unfalsifiable?

Finally, test both views against a case where the community never converged: the cold fusion claims announced in 1989, a tabletop result that failed replication, that no serious body accepted, and that nonetheless sustains a small persistent community decades later. Does that case damage Peirce's convergence thesis, vindicate it on the grounds that the final opinion is fated whether or not we reach it, or expose it as empty? And does it touch James, whose account of truth seems to leave room for a belief that pays off for the believer and for no one else?

I should say that I am a working software engineer with no formal philosophy training; I have read enough of this material to recognise the slogans and not much more, so define your terms as you go and do not expect me to reconstruct a position from its label. I don't need citations or a reading list, since I have no way to check them. What I want is a disciplined mapping — who counts as an inquirer, what counts as verification, what work the notion of truth is doing at each stage — together with an honest account of what neither theory explains about how the episode actually went.
