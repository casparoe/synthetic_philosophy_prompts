You are an expert in two fields at once: the history of comparative economic systems — in particular the interwar socialist calculation debate — and distributed systems, control theory, and the theory of decentralized optimization. I want you to work as a scrupulous translator between them, not as a popularizer of either.

Context: I am a distributed systems engineer. A close friend is a political philosopher, and we spent most of an evening arguing about whether a socialist economy could allocate capital goods and labor rationally — whether it could, as he put it, calculate. I kept reaching for my own vocabulary: nodes that know only their local state, message passing, bandwidth, consensus, single points of failure, optimization over a global objective. He kept insisting I was collapsing two different problems into one, and I suspect he is right but could not say exactly how. Before you write anything, brainstorm: lay out the moving parts of the debate and of my vocabulary side by side, sketch the candidate mappings, and only then commit to the ones you can defend.

Your task has two parts.

First, reconstruct the central positions — Mises's original calculation argument; Hayek's knowledge-based reformulation, with its emphasis on the dispersal of knowledge of particular circumstances and on the price system as a communication and discovery mechanism; and Lange's trial-and-error market socialism, including his later suggestion that a computer could in principle perform the coordinating role of the market — and restate each, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually use: local versus global state; message complexity and bandwidth; consensus and coordination protocols; sharding; single points of failure; the difference between computing an allocation and discovering one; exploration versus exploitation; incentive compatibility and the principal-agent problem; convergence of a distributed optimization procedure. Be explicit about which economic concept you are rendering as which computational one, and flag every place where the rendering is approximate.

Second, give me the residue report. For each position, tell me what translates cleanly, what translates only under a distortion (and name the distortion), and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Then tell me what to make of the residue: is it a defect of my framework — something distributed systems simply cannot express — or a sign that the original economic claim is vaguer or less substantive than it sounds? I am especially interested in whether the Hayekian emphasis on discovery, and the separate question of who holds the authority to decide, survive translation, or whether they are precisely what my vocabulary drops.

Two warnings. Do not let the target vocabulary smuggle in its own assumptions and then present the result as a translation: if, for instance, you assume there is a well-defined global objective to be optimized, say so, and say what that assumption presupposes economically. And do not pad the answer with textbook summaries of either debate — I want the mapping and the residue, in the weeds.
