I've been defending a line in my dissertation that subsidiarity is a real normative principle and not just a preference for decentralization, i.e. that decisions should sit at the lowest level that can handle them competently, and that this constrains what higher levels may legitimately do rather than merely describing how they should behave when it suits them. What I want from you is the other side: what would count against this? Which cases (the German Länder and the federal role in education, Swiss cantonal divergence on, say, tuition or naturalization, the EU's own subsidiarity test, US federalism fights over things like the drinking age or the ACA) actually push back on the principle rather than just illustrating it, and which are only awkward for it? I'd like the defeaters ranked, and I want you to be honest about which ones a committed subsidiarist would themselves concede versus wave away. Also tell me straight whether the principle as I've stated it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, since "the lowest level that can handle it competently" can be read as deferring to whoever gets to decide what competent means. If it has, say whether that's a fatal problem for a moral principle or just the cost of any competence-based standard. I'd be most moved by anything showing the principle collapses into a rival (say, plain democratic legitimacy or efficiency) rather than doing independent work.
