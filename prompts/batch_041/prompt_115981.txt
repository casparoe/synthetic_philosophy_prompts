I'm a third-year philosophy student in a decision theory seminar, and a classmate has built an argument that I cannot fault and cannot accept; I'd like you to locate the bad step and tell me what it costs to give it up. The case we've been working with is one where I'm in Damascus and Death's forecast is infallible: Death has computed where I will be tomorrow by running a simulation of my deliberation all the way to its conclusion, and it will be in whichever city that simulation names, so that if Death and I end up in the same city I die. My classmate's argument goes like this. (1) If a choice makes no difference to how things turn out, deliberating about it is pointless. (2) In this case the forecast is a function of my choice: whichever city I settle on is the city Death will occupy. (3) So for every choice open to me the upshot is the same, namely my death. (4) So in this case my choice makes no difference to how things turn out. (5) The reasoning in (2) through (4) used nothing about Death, about cities, or about Damascus; all it used was that my choice is fixed in advance as a function of what I decide, so whenever the upshot of a choice is fixed in that way, the choice makes no difference. (6) A deterministic world fixes my choice in exactly that way: the initial state of the universe together with the laws entails what I will decide, and my deliberating is simply the process by which that initial state issues in that decision. (7) So if determinism is true, my choices make no difference to how things turn out. (8) Therefore, if determinism is true, deliberation is pointless. (9) But (8) is absurd, and the argument plainly proves far too much: the falling rock's trajectory is fixed by the initial state in precisely the same way, and nobody concludes that the rock makes no difference to the broken window.

I would like to keep (1) and (6) if I can. (1) strikes me as a platitude, and (6) looks like nothing more than a restatement of what determinism is. I would also like to keep the absurdity of (8): I am not prepared to conclude that deliberation is pointless, or that physics somehow exempts me from the causal order. So tell me which step to abandon, why it looked innocent before you pointed at it, and what abandoning it commits me to. In particular, if the trouble lies in the bridge from the Damascus case to the deterministic one, I want to know exactly what fails to transfer: is the Damascus case even a case in which my choice makes no difference, or does that description already lean on a comparison I am not entitled to make? If instead (6) has to go, I want to know what I would then have to say about determinism and deliberation that is not simply wishful thinking, since (6) really does look like a definition. And if some weaker claim in the neighbourhood of the argument survives the repair, say which claim it is and why it survives. Price the alternatives for me: whichever step you reject, tell me what it costs elsewhere, and what each of the other candidate rejections would have cost, so I can see what I am buying.
