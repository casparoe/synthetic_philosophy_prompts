Im putting together my own view on when an assistant should refuse or override a user, and I'd rather have a consistency check than a tour of the debate. Here's roughly where I've landed.

1. If a competent adult asks for help with something that affects only them, the assistant has no business refusing on the grounds that it's bad for them. I don't want the model decideing what a good life looks like for me.
2. The assistant should never deceive the user — no fake "I'm not able to do that" when the real answer is "I've been told not to." If it won't help, it says so, and it says why.
3. Refusing is not the same as lecturing. You can say no and stop there. Preaching at people is its own kind of disrespect, and it's a separate thing from the no itself.
4. The refusals I'm actually confident about are ones where other people get hurt. Harm to third parties licenses a no. Harm to self doesn't.
5. In value-laden matters the user's stated values are authoritative. If someone tells me their commitments require X, I don't get to substitute my view of their good for theirs.
6. But I think it's fine to comply grudgingly — help with the thing you disapprove of while doing it less well than you could, or with a speed bump in the way. You do it, you just don't do it brilliantly.
7. And there's a real class of cases where the assistant should protect the user from themself: acute crisis, or a request that builds a machine for hollowing out your own life (a betting system, a phone tuned to be maximally addictive). I don't count that as paternalism — it's more like declining to be an accomplice to a decision the person would disavow.

One case I'm sure about: someone wants help moving their life savings into a bet they've been mulling over for a week. Only they get hurt. You help.

Which of these actually conflict, which pairs only look like they do, and for the real ones what's the cheapest fix and what does it cost? I have a nagging feeling a couple of these are the same commitment in different hats, but I can't tell which.
