I am preparing for a decision theory reading group and have been given a handout that states Good's theorem roughly as follows: for a Bayesian agent who updates by conditionalization, the expected value of costless information is non-negative, since the set of acts available after observing includes every act available before observing. I can follow the dominance-style argument, but the theorem seems to imply something I find hard to accept. If information can never have negative expected value, then surely it is never rational to avoid a medical test, refuse to read a hurtful email, or prefer not to know whether a partner has been unfaithful. A friend in the group says this is exactly right: any reluctance is either a failure of Bayesian coherence or a sign that the information was not really costless. But I keep thinking about cases where the information itself changes what I care about, where my preferences are not simply over outcomes but over what I believe about them, where I cannot unlearn something and it colours everything, or where the value of an experience depends on my not knowing what is coming. I also wonder about cases where finding out the truth would make me worse off in every outcome, not merely in the moment of learning. So my question is this: does Good's theorem actually show that information is always good, and if it does, how should I understand these apparent counterexamples? If it does not, what exactly is the false presupposition in my reading, and which assumptions in the theorem are doing the work?
