I am a novelist midway through a long project, and I have come to think of the book not as a sequence of decisions but as a set of dials on a single concept — point of view, tense, narrative distance, the proportion of scene to summary, the order in which revelations arrive, the density of the prose — which I can twiddle one at a time to see what the story becomes. This is close to Hofstadter's picture of creativity, in which the interesting move is a variation run on an existing concept rather than an invention ex nihilo, and the fertility of a concept lies in the richness of the neighbourhood its knobs can reach. I want to spend my remaining effort on the knobs that actually pay. Please rank the following candidate dimensions of a novel from most to least fertile for producing variations that are genuinely new rather than merely rearranged: (1) the temporal stance of the narration, whether the book is told from inside the events or from a vantage after they are over; (2) the epistemic gap between narrator and reader, who knows what and when; (3) the unit at which I revise, whether sentence, scene, or whole architecture; (4) the constraint regime, rules the prose must obey, such as a restricted vocabulary or a ban on interiority; (5) the degree of formal repetition, how literally the book revisits its own central scene; and (6) the register of the voice, the diction and rhythm of the sentences. For each, say what kind of variation it opens up, how far a variation can travel before the underlying concept stops being recognizable as the same book, and whether it is fertile alone or only in combination with another knob. Then identify which one is really a dial on a different machine — a change that does not vary the concept but replaces it — since I would rather not mistake that for craft.
