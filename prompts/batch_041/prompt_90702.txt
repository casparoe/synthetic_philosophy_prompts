I retired from the bench a few years back (physical chemistry, forty years of it) and I've been sitting in on a philosophy reading group ever since — last month we did a paper on conditionals and I'm still chewing on it.

Here's what grabbed me. Everyones intuition is that the probability of "if A then B" is just the probability of B given A. But the author showed — with a proof that looks like it would fit on a postcard — that you can't have that for any ordinary proposition. Assume the equation holds and add one more step that seems completely innocent, and the whole probability space goes trivial: everything flattens. Our discussion leader kept saying "triviality" like it was a magic word, and I wanted to actually see the machinery, so I worked through the steps myself.

I'd like a poem about it, and I want the form to do the work, not just decorate the idea. Specifically a villanelle, nineteen lines, with these two refrains:

1. the naive identification — the probability you give the conditional is the probabilty of the consequent given the antecedent;
2. the collapse — assume that and the probabilities go trivial, the space flattens out.

The reason I want a villanelle is that the refrains keep coming back whether you want them to or not, and in this proof the first one keeps coming back too: you assume it, you derive nonsense, you back off, and there it is again. The two lines should finally land side by side in the closing quatrain, the way the proof makes the premise and its self-destruction meet. Someone who knows the result should be able to tell which refrain is the assumption and which is the consequence, and shouldn't wince at the argument the poem traces. Someone who's never heard of it should still get a decent poem.

Don't name the philosopher or the paper — I know who it is and I'd rather the poem stand on its own. No more than nineteen lines, please, and write it in American English.
