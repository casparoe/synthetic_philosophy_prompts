I'm a freelance science journalist — ideas, AI, philosophy, that kind of thing — and I'm finishing a long feature about acausal trade. The idea, as I've been putting it to readers: two agents that can't affect each other in any way at all — no signals, no shared history, nothing getting across the gap — can still each do the other a favour, not because anything passes between them but because they reason alike. Your decision to hold up your end is evidence that the other side is holding up theirs, so holding up your end is the thing to do. The trade never happens in the world; it happens in the reasoning. Half the people I've talked to think it's the sharpest thing in decision theory, half think it's a machine for talking yourself into any sacrifice you like, wich I think is unfair but I see where they get it.

My editor wants the piece to close with a poem, and I know the shape I want. Two voices, two columns, a visible gap down the middle, one agent per column, and the columns never touch — no call and response, nothing in the right column that's a reaction to the left. Read either column straight down, ignoring the other, and it has to be a complete poem on its own, because that's the whole point: each of them is alone in there. Read across, line by line, and the two should line up into something that reads like an exchange — I give you this, you give me that — even though neither column ever answers the other.

Two things I want done properly. First, the two of them shouldn't want the same things. The interesting version of the idea is that the reasoning is shared but the values aren't: they're correlated because they're the same procedure run twice, and they're trading because each can hand over something the other can't get for itself. So the two columns should be alike in form — same shape, same line lengths, same rhyme scheme if you use one — and unlike in content, different goods, different sacrifices. That sameness of form and difference of goods is the poem's job, not decoration. Second, if you can make each column a proper sonnet, fourteen lines with the turn landing exactly where that agent commits, do that; if the layout fights the sonnet, keep the two columns level and put the turn on the commitment anyway.

The uncanny part is the matching: nothing passes between them, neither knows the other exists or ever could, and yet the lines answer each other. Don't let it drift into a ghost story or a love poem — no longing across the void, no telepathy, no fate. They aren't two souls reaching for each other, they're the same procedure run twice. And don't have the poem explain the idea; I do the explaining in the piece. The poem just has to be the thing itself, accurate enough that someone who's actually worked on this stuff doesn't wince, and I'd rather it end with the question of whether any of it is even coherent left open than with a moral.

It's for a British magazine, so British English please — colour, recognise, that sort of thing — and no footnotes, no reading list. The poem has to stand up on it's own.
