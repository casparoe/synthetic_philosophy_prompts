I am a novelist, and a friend who directs a graduate writing workshop has asked me to take a single session of it — ninety minutes, some fifteen students, all of them fiction writers, none of them trained in philosophy, most of them quicker to argue than to grant a distinction — on a question I have never managed to settle for myself: whether a forgery can be as good as the original. What I want from you is not an account of the problem but a plan for teaching it.

My reason for wanting to teach it is that I hold two convictions which seem to me to sit badly together. A copy of one of my novels is, for everything I care about, my novel; I have never felt that a reader who bought a cheap reprint had read a lesser book, or had failed to read the book at all. Yet I accept without argument that a painting copied so exactly that neither eye nor instrument could tell the two apart would be a lesser thing than the canvas it copies, and that a gallery would be right to say so and right to care. I want the students to feel that tension as a live one rather than as a paradox to be admired, and then to see what would have to be true for either side of it to be defensible.

Plan the session for me. I would like you to work out:

- the order in which the ideas must be introduced, and the dependencies among them — which distinction has to be in place before the next can even be stated, which ones I should refuse to state early even though the students will reach for them, and where the sequence would break if I got the order wrong;

- the single case that should carry the whole session: what it must have, what it must conspicuously lack, and whether the case everyone reaches for — the master forger's fake old master — can bear that weight, or whether its very familiarity lets the students off the hook;

- the misconceptions the students will bring with them or form along the way, in the order they are likely to surface, and the exact point in the sequence at which I should head each one off, separating the ones I should pre-empt from the ones I should let them walk into and climb back out of;

- one question I can put to them at the close that would separate a student who has understood the problem from one who has merely been persuaded that forgers are frauds;

- and what I should openly decline to take up in ninety minutes, together with the sentence I can offer them to justify the omission.

The room has a whiteboard and nothing else; the students will have read nothing in advance, and I do not want a reading list. I do not need the argument made to me — I need to know how to put it in front of them so that it arrives as a problem about where value can live, rather than as a curiosity about clever criminals.
