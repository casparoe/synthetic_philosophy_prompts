I have recently joined the architecture review committee of a company that builds autonomous systems, and I am the member responsible for writing up the reasoning behind each decision. The committee has four years of rulings behind it and the engineers here treat them as settled law, so before Thursday I need to know both what we should decide about the proposal now in front of us and how much our own record actually determines.

The record, as it stands:

1. Approved: a scheduling assistant whose objective at every step is to do whatever the operator on duty would most approve of. The operator sees each candidate action and its predicted consequences before it is taken, may halt the episode at any moment, and the assistant's actions are cheap to undo. Episodes last minutes.

2. Approved: a laboratory robot whose objective is to minimize a quantity our staff specified directly and can check mechanically, namely idle time. Episodes are short, a physical button zeroes its score for the episode, and a supervisor inspects its output between episodes.

3. Rejected: a trading agent that maximizes a fixed utility function over portfolio states, written by our own staff with no learned component. Once a session begins it cannot be interrupted, and its positions take weeks to unwind and cannot be unwound at all in adverse conditions.

4. Rejected: a content-ranking system whose objective is a reward model trained on human ratings and then frozen, so that after training nothing in the loop consults a person. It has no interrupt, and the effects of a ranking decision persist for months.

The first of these designs is what we here call approval-directed; the others are goal-directed.

The proposal before us: a planning and procurement agent that will negotiate contracts and commit the company to engagements lasting weeks, many of them irreversible. Its objective is to do what our director of operations would most approve of. She cannot evaluate its decisions in real time, so the agent acts on a learned model of her judgments, refitted weekly from her ratings of sampled past decisions and from the corrections she issues; the proposal's authors argue that this model is simply her judgments in compressed form. The design includes a kill switch: any of three designated people can halt the agent, the agent's score is unaffected by being halted, and testing found no behavior aimed at preventing or delaying a halt. In retrospective testing, the agent's model of the director agreed with her judgments on 97% of sampled decisions; among the disagreements, the agent had done something she endorsed once she saw it but said she would not have authorized in advance.

What I need from you: the ruling we should reach if we want to be consistent with our own record, with the closest precedent named and the differences that matter stated; the rule the committee appears to be running, together with the other rules that fit all four rulings just as well, and any that the record leaves silent on this proposal; your own best guess, with reasons; the one or two further cases that would tell those rules apart, described concretely enough that I could put one of them on next month's agenda; and, if the record genuinely underdetermines the decision, a plain statement to that effect, with the principle we would be adopting under each choice.
