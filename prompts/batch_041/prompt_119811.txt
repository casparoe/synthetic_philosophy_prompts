I am trying to work through a passage in Thomas Aquinas's Summa Theologiae, II-II, q. 64, a. 1, the article on whether it is a sin to kill dumb animals or plants. The second objection runs: "Further, murder is a sin because it deprives a man of life. Now life is common to all animals and plants. Hence for the same reason it is apparently a sin to slay dumb animals and plants." The body of the article answers:

"There is no sin in using a thing for the purpose for which it is. Now the order of things is such that the imperfect are for the perfect, even as in the process of generation nature proceeds from imperfection to perfection. Hence it is that just as in the generation of a man there is first a living thing, then an animal, and lastly a man, so too things, like the plants, which merely have life, are all alike for animals, and all animals are for man. Wherefore it is not unlawful if man use plants for the good of animals, and animals for the good of man, as the Philosopher states (Polit. i, 3)."

And the reply to that second objection:

"Dumb animals and plants are devoid of the life of reason whereby to set themselves in motion; they are moved, as it were by another, by a kind of natural impulse, a sign of which is that they are naturally enslaved and accommodated to the uses of others."

What I cannot get a grip on is the shape of the argument in the body of the article, and I would like help reconstructing it rather than a general summary of medieval views about animals. In the English translation I have at hand, the passage reads as though it were moving very quickly from a claim about how things come to be to a claim about what they are for, and I want to know exactly where the normative conclusion is supposed to come from.

Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the argument as a numbered sequence of premises and conclusion, making explicit every premise the conclusion needs but the text leaves tacit. The conclusion I take to be something like: it is not unlawful for human beings to kill plants and animals for their own use. I want to see precisely which steps are load-bearing.

2. The move from the order of generation to the order of ends — from "first a living thing, then an animal, and lastly a man" to "plants are for animals and animals are for man" — looks to me like an inference from a temporal sequence to a normative-teleological hierarchy. Is that what it is? If so, is the argument simply fallacious, or is the generation series meant as evidence for an independent metaphysical claim (that the less perfect exists for the sake of the more perfect) which then carries the weight? If the latter, what is supposed to support that independent claim here: does the text offer anything, or does it function as an axiom?

3. "There is no sin in using a thing for the purpose for which it is" seems to presuppose that things have purposes "for which they are." Is this premise analytic, a piece of natural teleology, or a claim about God's ordinance? And does the argument's conclusion require the strong reading (animals exist for man's sake, by nature) or only the weaker reading (it is permissible to use them as though they did)?

4. In the reply to the second objection, Aquinas does not say that animals' lives matter less because they are less vivid or less complex; he says they lack "the life of reason" and are moved "as it were by another," and he treats being "naturally enslaved and accommodated to the uses of others" as a sign of this. Does that answer the objection as stated, which was about life as such, or does it substitute a different criterion of what makes killing wrong? If the latter, what exactly is the criterion, and is it a criterion the objection could have been reframed to meet?

5. Finally, I am reading this alongside the later article in the same question on killing in self-defense, where Aquinas argues that an act of self-defense can have two effects — the preservation of one's own life, which is intended, and the aggressor's death, which is beside the intention — and that the act is licit when the intention is directed at the former rather than the latter. There the permission seems to rest on what is intended rather than on the metaphysical rank of what is killed; here it seems to rest on rank. Are these two strategies simply sitting side by side in the same question, or does the intention-based analysis in the self-defense article actually depend on the ordering claim made here — for instance, in how the unjust aggressor is located within or outside the order of things? I would like to know which parts of the later double-effect framework are independent of the teleological hierarchy asserted in this passage and which are not.

Please answer by reconstructing the argument and addressing these points in order. I am not looking for sources to consult; I want the reasoning laid out and the weak joints identified.
