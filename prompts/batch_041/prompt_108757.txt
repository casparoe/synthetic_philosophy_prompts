I am the designated sceptic on the investment committee of a mid-sized pension fund. Before we commit to a large contrarian position — one that takes the other side of a liquid, widely followed market — I am expected to present the case against our doing so. We take roughly half a dozen such decisions a year. Each is large enough that being wrong would be visible to our members, and I have at most a working day to prepare: I cannot commission research, and I am not a sector specialist. The committee may overrule me and the chief investment officer signs off either way, but the minute records whether I was satisfied, so the procedure needs to yield a defensible verdict rather than a mood.

Please turn the no-trade results in the economics of information into a decision procedure I can actually run. The intuition to operationalise is this: in a world of common priors, rational agents, an initially efficient allocation and no aggregate uncertainty, speculative trade does not occur, because the mere willingness of someone to take the other side is itself informative and the trade unravels. When we propose a contrarian position, we are therefore implicitly claiming that one of those assumptions fails. A usable procedure must force us to say which one fails, and must treat our inability to name one as a reason to stop. A companion point belongs in the procedure too: if prices already reflected all costly information, no one would bear the cost of gathering it — so any edge we claim must be compensation for a real cost or risk we are carrying, and we should be able to name that cost or risk.

The procedure should include:

- the questions in order, and what counts as a sufficient answer to each;
- the list of non-informational reasons a counterparty might trade that the framework allows — forced or mechanical flows, hedging, tax, mandate and index constraints, agency and career incentives, plain noise — together with a test for distinguishing a genuine instance from a story we have told ourselves;
- a rule for the reverse direction: when our own motive for trading is non-informational, so that our conviction carries no evidential weight;
- a rule for the second-order case: what we may and may not infer when a market fails to move on news we regard as important, and how to tell whether a price is functioning as a forecast or merely as a record of who was compelled to sell;
- the points where the procedure stops with a no, and the points where it must hand the decision to a person, because the question concerns our tolerance for a particular kind of risk rather than the facts;
- an honest note on which steps are judgement wearing the costume of procedure.

Then run it on three proposals:

1. Shorting a large, heavily held company on the thesis that the market is underestimating the probability of a regulatory crackdown.
2. Buying into a sharp fall in a widely held share, on the thesis that the fall was driven by a leveraged fund being forced to liquidate rather than by any news about the company.
3. Taking our largest position of the year on the strength of a private conversation with someone who works in the industry, on which we believe we know something the market does not.

At least one of these should receive a verdict I will not like, and you should say plainly which and why. Do not supply reading lists, citations or names of papers: write from what you know, and where a result is standard, state it in your own words. Answer in British English.
