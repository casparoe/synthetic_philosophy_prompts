I am reconstructing Aquinas's position on tyrannicide as a case law, and I need you to predict how he would decide a new case. Treat the following verdicts as the settled track record our seminar has attributed to him, and assume they are internally consistent.

Case 1: A duke with no dynastic or legal claim seizes the throne by force, dissolves the old council, and rules for his private enrichment. The legitimate king's loyalists, acting under the legitimate king's explicit commission, kill the usurper. Aquinas's verdict: not murder; lawful.

Case 2: A hereditary king, validly crowned and acknowledged, becomes a tyrant by abuse. He imposes ruinous taxes, imprisons opponents without cause, and corrupts the courts, but he does not wage war on his people as a whole. A private nobleman, with no public commission from any assembly or superior, assassinates him in order to restore good government. Aquinas's verdict: murder; not lawful.

Case 3: A city groans under a tyrannical governor. A revolt is likely to succeed, but it will cause a short civil war with many deaths. If the citizens wait, the governor's tyranny will continue, but with fewer deaths over the same period. Aquinas's verdict: the revolt is seditious and unlawful, because it would inflict greater harm on the community than the tyranny it removes.

Case 4: The same legitimate king from Case 2 is deposed by the senate and the traditional assembly of the realm, acting through the kingdom's established procedures. Aquinas's verdict: lawful; not sedition.

Case 5: A tyrannical regime cannot be overthrown without a foreign invasion and a long, bloody war. Aquinas's verdict: the people should tolerate the regime, avoid rebellion, and seek divine remedy rather than attempt overthrow.

Now the new case: A legitimate hereditary monarch has become a tyrant by abuse. He has not yet ordered mass killings, but he is packing the courts, arresting opponents, and preparing to name his brother, a notoriously brutal general, as successor. The kingdom's traditional assembly has been dissolved by the monarch, but its members still exist and claim to represent the realm. A group of military officers and judges, without any formal commission from the assembly because it cannot safely meet, plan to assassinate the monarch. The plot is likely to succeed. If it succeeds, the brother may seize power with the army and ignite a civil war; if they wait, the monarch will likely complete his consolidation and the brother will succeed peacefully, leading to a long tyranny with many deaths. The officers intend, immediately after the assassination, to restore the assembly and hold elections.

Predict Aquinas's verdict on the assassination. Identify the candidate rules that fit the earlier verdicts, say what each predicts for this new case, give your best guess with reasons for preferring it, and name the further case that would best tell the candidate rules apart. Be specific about which features of the new case—legitimacy of title, presence or absence of public authority, proportionality, scandal, and the common good—do the work. Answer in American English.
