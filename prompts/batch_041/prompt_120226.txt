I chair the ethics committee at a residential center for adults with intellectual and developmental disabilities, and we are rewriting our policy on decision-making: today a court-appointed guardian consents on the resident's behalf to nearly everything, from dental work to where the resident lives. Some families argue that certain residents can take in a reason once it is given but cannot generate or weigh reasons for themselves, and that being directed is therefore genuinely good for those residents rather than merely convenient for staff; others reply that this is Aristotle's argument for natural slavery in modern dress and want supported decision-making as the default, with guardianship only as a last resort. Draft the memo I will circulate before the vote: it should recommend a default and a concrete implementation timeline, and it should engage the families' premise on its merits rather than treating it as paternalism to be managed. Say what would have to be true of a person for the premise to hold; whether the relevant capacity is a fact about the person or a judgment about whose reasons get counted; and whether the test can do the work the families need, given how much harder it has always been to apply the old distinction between those suited by nature to be ruled and those merely kept down by convention than to state it. Grant the strongest consideration on the losing side, and say what would make our decision the wrong one. No citations needed — argue it out.
