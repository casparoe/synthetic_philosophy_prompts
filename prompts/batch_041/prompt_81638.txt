Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about whether generations are real, and I've decided I'd rather settle it against a novel than against another round of shouting. I'd like your help doing that.

Her position, roughly: she designs surveys for a living, and she thinks cohorts are real enough to be worth measuring. People born within a few years of each other meet the same formative conditions at the same age — a recession at twenty-two, a new technology at fifteen — and those shared conditions leave durable marks on outlook and behaviour, whether or not the popular labels are tidy. My position: "Boomer", "Millennial" and "Gen Z" are marketing categories that have been reified. The labels are unstable, the boundaries move whenever a demographer wants a headline, and the same birth years have been sold to advertisers under three or four different names. I'll grant her that something cohort-shaped is going on; my claim is that "generation" as it is used in public life picks out nothing stable enough to be a kind.

Now the test case, which is why I'm writing. Read Douglas Coupland's novel *Generation X: Tales for an Accelerated Culture* (1991) through this dispute. You'll need the following, and I'd rather you weigh it as evidence than treat it as trivia. The book is a framed narrative: three friends in their twenties — Andy, who narrates, Dag, and Claire — live in cheap bungalows in the Coachella Valley and tell one another stories, some about their families and some invented, over a long picnic and then across a year. Andy calls his bartending job a McJob. The chapters are titled like slogans; one of them is "I Am Not a Target Market". The phrase "Generation X" was in circulation long before the novel — a photographer's post-war photo-essay, a 1964 book about British youth, a punk band — and Coupland has said his title came from the last chapter of Paul Fussell's *Class*, where X named a social category rather than an age band. The characters in the novel are in their twenties in 1990 but were born in the late 1950s and early 1960s, which by the standard definition makes them younger boomers. The cohort had other names before this one stuck, including Baby Busters and the 13th Generation. And it is often said that the novel's success handed the cohort to the advertisers it mocks.

Two theoretical resources to bring to it, both of which I'd like you to state in your own words rather than cite. First, the account of kinds on which a kind is real when a cluster of properties is reliably co-instantiated because some mechanism holds the cluster together — and the question whether anything plays that role for a cohort. Second, the account on which classifications are not descriptions but interventions: a label applied to people changes them, so the kind is manufactured by the naming and then loops back into the data. Third, keep in view that in any single time series age, period and cohort advance together, so a "generation effect" cannot be separated from an age effect or a period effect without assumptions that are doing most of the work.

Please write the reading with these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

**Summary** (about 100 words): my thesis about what the novel shows, stated flatly enough that I could disagree with it.

**What is actually at stake** (about 200 words): the three senses of "generation" in play — a genealogical step between parents and children, a demographic birth band, and a cohort with a shared formative experience and something like a common outlook — which of them the argument with my friend is really about, and which of them the novel can bear on at all.

**The case that the novel makes generations real** (about 350 words): the devices, scenes and characters that support my friend — where shared conditions visibly produce a shared stance, and what it would take for that to count as a kind rather than a coincidence.

**The case that the novel dissolves them** (about 350 words): the naming, the prior names, the mis-dated cohort, the refusal of definition built into the X itself, and the marketing afterlife — where the book is evidence that the label made the kind rather than the reverse.

**Where the reading strains** (about 200 words): what this novel cannot show, and where I would be overreading if I leaned on it — including whether a book about three middle-class Americans in the desert can be evidence about a birth cohort at all.

**What the novel supplies that the argument cannot** (about 150 words).

**Verdict** (about 150 words): which of us the novel supports, whether it should move me, and if the answer is split, which half of my position survives.

Two constraints. Don't quote the novel — I don't want to be checking page numbers, and paraphrase will do. And don't pad the answer with a reading list or citations; I want the argument, not a bibliography.
