I have no academic background in any of this — I work as a delivery driver and have spent the past few years reading about prisons in the evenings — but I have been keeping a private list of cases and my reactions to them, and I have reached the point where my reactions seem to contradict one another. What I want from you is the principle, or a small set of competing principles, that would account for them, together with an honest account of which of my reactions would have to be abandoned. Please do not tell me what the literature says I should think; tell me what my own verdicts add up to.

Here are my verdicts.

A man beat a stranger half to death outside a pub, for no reason, shows no remorse, and the assessment says he is likely to do it again. Holding him for a very long time feels right to me, and I would be uneasy if he were released simply because a fixed term had run out.

A woman embezzled from a small firm over several years, taking enough to ruin the owner's retirement. She has repaid all of it, lost her career, is genuinely ashamed, and is judged at no risk of repeating it. A long prison sentence feels wrong here — I would want something short, or unpaid work, or a heavy fine. But the harm she caused was enormous and lasting, and the people she harmed do not get their years back.

A driver who was drunk killed a child at a crossing. What matters most to me is that he never drives again, and that nobody asks the family to say they forgive him. I notice I do not much care what his sentence is, which bothers me, because in the other cases I care about sentences a great deal.

A sixteen-year-old broke into a garden shed and took the tools. A meeting with the owner and a repair bill feels exactly right; prison would be grotesque.

A man convicted of murder at nineteen is now sixty, has been a model prisoner for decades, and nobody seriously claims he is dangerous. Keeping him locked up feels wrong to me. The crime has not changed; only the man has.

A man has served his full sentence, and I am told he will very probably kill again. Locking him up again feels right to me. But I can see that this is not punishment for what he did, it is detention for what he might do, and I cannot tell whether I am allowed to want both this and the previous verdict.

I also read about a maximum-security prison in Norway, called Halden, where the staff are unarmed, there are no gun towers or barbed wire, and the place is laid out like a small village, with ordinary kitchens shared between a dozen cells and woodland around the buildings. Everything in me says this is better than a prison where people are shut in a cell for twenty-three hours a day. Yet if prison is chiefly about making someone suffer in proportion to what they did, Halden looks like a soft failure, and I do not think it is a failure at all.

Two further cases I cannot settle on my own. A prisoner refuses every course, every job, and every offer of help, and asks only to be left alone. Under the view that prisons should rehabilitate people, he is a problem; under the view that they should punish, he is not. And a man whose violence is caused by a condition that could be treated, where treating him would also stop the violence: I cannot tell whether I am looking at medicine or at punishment.

Is there a single account of what prison is for that yields all of these verdicts, or nearly all? If the only way to keep them all is to let the account grow complicated — say, that what someone deserves sets a ceiling, and how dangerous they are decides where under that ceiling they sit, with something further added for the people they harmed — then I want that stated plainly, clause by clause, with the case that forces each clause. If a simpler account is available at the price of one of my verdicts, I want to know which verdict I would be giving up, and what the simpler account predicts for a case I have not listed: a prisoner who is no danger to anyone, who has not been rehabilitated in any sense, and who is released into a town with no job, no housing, and the family of his victim living on the same street.

Finally, please be explicit about how much of your answer is settled among people who actually work on prisons and punishment and how much is genuinely contested, and say which side you are taking where it is contested. I would far rather be told that a question is open than be handed a confident answer that turns out to be one party's position.
