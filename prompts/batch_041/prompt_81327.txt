Our housing co-op has spent three meetings arguing about a single bylaw, and the argument has drifted well past the bylaw. I have been taking minutes rather than taking sides, and I would like you to referee the exchange on its merits rather than tell us how to vote.

The proposal is that each household must take one four-hour maintenance shift per month, with a fixed fine for a missed shift and no exceptions other than a documented medical one. Ruth and Malik have been going back and forth.

1. Ruth: The case for a bright line is that discretion is where unfairness hides. 'Reasonable excuse' sounds like a standard but functions as a second, unwritten rule, namely whatever the person granting the excuse happens to consider reasonable; and the households that get the benefit of the doubt are the ones who talk like the deciders. A fixed rule with a fixed penalty is a levelling device: it takes judgment out of the hands of whoever is holding the clipboard.

2. Malik: Bright lines manufacture offenders. Any 'always X or pay' rule applied to a diverse group guarantees that the fines fall hardest on households with irregular lives — shift workers, carers, people with unpredictable health — and not because they care less about the norm. Discretion is not a leak in the rule; it is how the rule stays attached to its purpose, which was a decent building to live in, not a punishment schedule.

3. Ruth: That is an argument for better rules, not for vagueness. If the schedule penalises shift workers, fix the schedule. And discretion does not remove the burden; it relocates it, onto the household that needs the exception, which must now plead its case to a neighbour every month. A rule is at least something you can plan around.

4. Malik: Here is a test I keep turning over. Ask what happens when everyone complies perfectly. A norm whose point is coordination — which side of the path to walk on, which day the bins go out — becomes invisible and costless the moment compliance is universal. A norm whose point is to sort members into the good ones and the rest cannot survive universal compliance: if every household did its shift and paid its fine, the rule would have nothing left to do, and the people most attached to it would start finding other faults in the same neighbours. So the tell is this: norms that go quiet under full compliance are working for all of us; norms that need visible failure to keep going are working for some of us.

5. Ruth: That is too neat. Plenty of ranking norms go quiet when everyone complies — family expectations about who visits whom at the holidays work exactly that way, and nobody experiences them as ranking. And the test cannot be confined to a co-op; read as a claim about communities generally, it convicts any society that has ever asked anything of anyone of doing rank work. Meanwhile plenty of coordination norms cannot be complied with fully, because they are open-ended, like keeping the common areas tidy. Worse, by your test the roster is coordination — the building does need cleaning and somebody has to know who is doing it — and your test also makes enforcement fatigue itself evidence of injustice, which cannot be right, since fatigue is also just what happens when people do not want to do their share.

6. Malik: The fatigue is not the test; who carries it is. When we ran the old informal arrangement, the exceptions we granted went almost entirely to the same three households, and they were granted by the same two people, in private. Whatever we call that, it was a rule.

I cannot tell whether Malik's test is a genuine criterion or a slogan with a good instinct behind it, and I cannot tell how much of Ruth's reply actually lands. Please adjudicate move by move: for each of the six, say what is right in it and where it overreaches, and rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right. Then say who has the better of the exchange overall and why, and what, if anything, both of them are missing — including anything that, if true, would change the verdict.
