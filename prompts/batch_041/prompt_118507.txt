I am putting together a seminar on moral particularism and I would rather end on a literary text than on another article. The text I have settled on is Melville's Billy Budd, Sailor, left unfinished at his death in 1891 and published posthumously some thirty years later, because the whole novella turns on a single act of judgement. Captain Vere convenes a drumhead court-martial after Billy, unable to answer Claggart's false charge because of his stammer, strikes him dead; Vere concedes Billy's lack of intent, yet presses the court to convict on the letter of the Articles of War, and Billy is hanged. What I want is a reading of the novella through the particularist/generalist dispute, not a plot summary.

Take the particularist case in its strongest form and then test it: does the story dramatise the claim that moral reasons are context-dependent all the way down, so that the features which make Billy's act innocent — Claggart's malice, the provocation, the absence of any mutinous design, the stammer that silenced him — are invisible to any principle, and Vere's appeal to the law is not a moral error but a category error? Or does it cut the other way, showing that a man with Vere's responsibilities has no option but to follow a rule, and that the particularist's fine perception is a luxury of those who do not have to answer for a fleet? I would like the two particularist theses kept apart, as Dancy keeps them: holism about reasons, on which a feature that is a reason for an action in one case may be no reason, or a reason against, in another; and the further denial that there are any true moral principles at all. Does the novella support both, only the first, or neither? Press the standard generalist replies as well — that a sufficiently hedged or default principle can absorb holism without surrendering codifiability, that particularism degenerates into a judgement by intuition that cannot be argued with, and that principles may be indispensable even if no principle is exceptionless — and say which of them the story seems to anticipate.

One thing I cannot settle, and would like you to: the novella does not end with the court's verdict but with three short chapters — Vere's death and his last words, an official gazette account that turns the facts upside down, and a ballad attributed to one of the crew — and I cannot decide whether that multiplication of perspectives is the literary form of particularism, a refusal to deliver a general verdict because there is none to deliver, or merely Melville hedging because he could not make the case either way. Tell me which scenes to put in front of the seminar, what the story shows that Dancy's arguments only assert, and where the particularist reading distorts the book — where an honest critic would have to admit that Vere's predicament is about institutional roles, martial law and the fear of mutiny, and not about the metaphysics of moral reasons at all.
