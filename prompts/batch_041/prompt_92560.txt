I have twice recently been told what the Kelly criterion proves, and the two occasions pulled in opposite directions: once to argue that a single, non-repeatable opportunity — staking a large share of my savings in a deal with a clearly positive expected value and a substantial chance of losing the whole stake — should be declined; and once to argue that anyone staking less than the full Kelly fraction on a favourable, repeatable gamble is behaving irrationally and leaving money on the table. In both cases the entire argument rested on a one-liner, that the Kelly criterion is provably optimal, and in neither case could anyone tell me what it was supposed to be optimal at.

I would like a menu of the distinct theses that line could be asserting, ordered from the trivial to the strong, with a verdict on each: which readings are theorems, and under exactly which assumptions (repetition, independence, known probabilities, proportional staking, perfect divisibility of the bankroll, an infinite horizon); which are substantive decision-theoretic commitments dressed up as theorems — in particular, whether 'optimal' adds anything at all to 'maximises expected log wealth', and what independent case there is for log wealth rather than some other function of wealth; which are heuristics for a world in which the edge is unknown and the future is not a sequence of identical trials; and which are simply false, such as the claims that Kelly staking cannot be ruined, that it dominates its rivals, or that the criterion transfers unchanged to a bet that will never come round again. For each reading, say what it would actually license in the two cases above, and in particular whether the cautious reading needs the assumption that the gamble is one of a long run of similar gambles, and whether the aggressive reading needs the bankroll to be large, divisible, and repeatedly staked. The slogan that 'rationality is geometric rather than arithmetic' sometimes travels with the first one; separate what is arithmetical in that claim from what is a claim about how an agent should choose. Lay the readings out side by side rather than defending one of them, and be explicit about where the line stops being a theorem and starts being a value judgement.
