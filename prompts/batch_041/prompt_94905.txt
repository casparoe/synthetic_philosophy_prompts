I am a novelist, about two-thirds of the way through a book whose central character is offered the chance to learn any single fact about his own life, and I have discovered that the hardest part of writing him is deciding which facts he should want and which he should refuse. That difficulty has left me with a set of intuitions about the value of information that I would like you to treat as data. Please take up the role of a professional philosopher and decision theorist, and treat me as a subject who has reported his verdicts on a battery of cases rather than as someone with a theory he wants defended. Here they are. A free weather forecast on the morning of an outing I can still cancel or rearrange: I want it, obviously. A friend who has read the novel I am halfway through offers to tell me how it ends, and I will finish it tonight in any case: I would pay him a small sum to keep quiet. A coin already flipped to settle a bet I have locked in and cannot alter: I am entirely indifferent to being told which way it fell. A free test for a condition that, caught now, I can be treated for: I want it. A free test for a condition that is untreatable and will kill me in about four years, but that would let me stop working and spend the time deliberately: I want it. A free test that would name, to the day, when and how I will die, which nothing I do can change: I do not want it, and would pay to avoid it, even though I concede it might let me plan. Learning whether a long-dead great-uncle's scandal was true, a thing I can do nothing about and that would only recolor my memory of childhood: I do not want it. And then the case I keep going back and forth on: whether I will eventually lose my marriage, in a world where I cannot change the outcome, only know it in advance.

Now, I have read enough to know that a result — I believe it is called Good's theorem — seems to make half of these verdicts irrational. As I understand it: for anyone who maximizes expected utility, the expected value of costless information is never negative, because a decision-maker who may observe before acting can always do at least as well as one who acts blind; the observation can be ignored if it does not help and acted upon if it does. Evaluated before the fact, then, there should be no such thing as information I prefer not to have. Yet several of my verdicts say exactly that. My own tentative principle, which I suspect is too crude, is this: information is welcome exactly insofar as it can change what I do, and unwelcome insofar as it can only change how I feel. But two of my verdicts break it. The untreatable-but-plannable test and the untreatable-and-unplannable test look like cases of the same shape — bad news I cannot reverse — and I want the first while refusing the second; and worse, the name-the-day test also seems to leave me room to act, since I could quit my job and travel, yet I still refuse it, while the four-years test, which leaves me exactly that room, I accept. So either the principle must be more delicate than action-relevance, or one of my verdicts has to go, and I cannot yet see which.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of rival principles, stated precisely enough that I can test them against cases I have not yet considered. Please begin by brainstorming the axes along which these cases might differ before you commit to an answer — whether the information bears on an action or only on experience, whether it changes the state of the world or only my representation of it, whether the dread it induces is itself part of the outcome, whether I am permitted to have a preference about my own beliefs, and whether the theorem's expected qualifier is doing real work — and then say which of these axes actually earns its keep. Think carefully about the theorem's scope, and about whether my verdicts require me to abandon expected-utility maximization or only to widen what counts as an outcome. For each clause of your principle, tell me which case in my list motivates it. Then apply your principle to at least three cases I have not listed — I would particularly like one in which the information is free and action-relevant yet you predict I should still refuse it, and one in which I should accept news I know will hurt — and finally be honest about which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and why you would rather keep the principle than the verdict. I don't need citations or a reading list; I need the shape of the thing.
