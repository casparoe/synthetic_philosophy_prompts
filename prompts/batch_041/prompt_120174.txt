I want to practice defending a claim that is difficult to state without sounding mystical: that human flexibility differs in kind from mechanical rigidity, and not merely in the depth of the machinery. Please play the opposing side — a committed mechanist who holds that the specter of sphexishness cannot be escaped, because every human routine, including our routines for revising routines, is itself a routine. Make him sharp rather than a strawman: he may concede that the standard wasp story is probably folklore, that the difference in degree is enormous, and that the regress argument is not a proof, but he must never concede the main point. Stay in character until I tell you to stop.

Every turn, including your first, should consist of exactly five numbered points, each one a sentence or two, with no preamble and no closing summary. Use American English.

Here is my opening move. The usual illustration of sphexishness is an interruption: move the caterpillar an inch and the wasp restarts the provisioning sequence from the top. In my department there is a standing practice — the chair reads the previous meeting's minutes aloud, and nothing may be voted on until that is finished. Last month the minutes were lost, so the meeting could not start; after twenty minutes of silence we adjourned. By the interruption test we look exactly like the wasp. But we all knew something was wrong, two of us said out loud that the rule was idiotic, and the following month we changed it — and when we changed it, we did not restart the sequence, we abolished it. The wasp cannot complain about its own routine, and it cannot amend the rule that governs it. So tell me what we were doing that a sufficiently complex machine could not do; and if your answer is that the complaint and the amendment were themselves triggered routines, tell me what would count as evidence against that, because I suspect your view cannot be wrong.
