For a year now, two friends of mine in a reading group on the foundations of mathematics have been arguing past each other about Russell's paradox, and they have finally agreed to stop scoring points and write something together. One of them is a classical set theorist of the iterative-conception stripe: for her the paradox is a genuine reductio, and its lesson is that there is no set of all sets and no set of all sets that are not members of themselves — that a predicate can only collect the members of a set already formed, so that comprehension has to be restricted to the members of a given set. On her view the cumulative hierarchy is not a patch on the naive principle that every predicate determines a set, but the discovery of what we meant by 'set' all along. The other is a dialetheist: he holds that the derivation is valid, that its premises are true, and that its conclusion is a true contradiction — the Russell set both is and is not a member of itself — so the fault lies not with the naive principle but with the rule that a contradiction entails anything whatever, and hence with the inference from 'A or B' together with 'not A' to 'B', which delivers that rule. For him, calling the derivation a paradox rather than a proof is itself a piece of classical prejudice.

What I want from you is the joint statement the two of them could actually sign: not a transcript of the argument, not a verdict for one side, but the document a good-faith adversarial collaboration should have produced. Begin with a single sentence both would put their names to, stating what is and is not in dispute. Then a numbered section of agreed claims — that the derivation is valid and turns on no fallacy or ambiguity; that the predicate 'is not a member of itself' is perfectly well-formed; that restricting comprehension to the members of an already-formed set yields a strictly weaker principle than the naive one, whatever else may be said about it; that a treatment which handled the set-theoretic and the semantic paradoxes together would be a virtue in a theory, if it could be had; and whatever else they can honestly both accept, including about what the paradox does not show. Then a disagreements table, one row per live disagreement, with columns for: the claim in neutral wording; the classical theorist's wording of her own side; the dialetheist's wording of his; the crux, that is, the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them; the case, argument, or result both agree would bear on that crux; and each side's prediction about how it comes out. I expect the cruxes to include whether an inconsistency-tolerant theory can recover the standard theorems without smuggling back the classical reasoning it rejects — the dialetheist will want to cite results in naive set theory that he says settle questions the classical theory leaves open, the continuum hypothesis among them, and the classical theorist will want to say that a theory which proves a proposition and its negation has settled nothing; whether the non-triviality of such a theory can be established without classical metatheory; and whether the paradoxes of set theory and of truth really do stand or fall together.

Where a disagreement turns out to be merely verbal — I suspect at least one does, over what 'there is a set' is being used to mean, or over what it is to revise logic — say so plainly, and say which wording each of them should abandon. Close with a short section both can sign on what would actually move the debate, and what each of them would count as evidence against his or her own position. Neither of them is impressed by the fact that working mathematicians use standard set theory, so keep the agreed claims to things they would accept on the merits; and since they are writing for each other rather than for a journal, there is no need for references or reading lists.
