After a career in experimental geophysics, I have spent the first years of my retirement reading decision theory, and I have become preoccupied with a variant of Pascal's mugging that I have not seen discussed in the material I have encountered. I would like you to rank five candidate responses to it, from most to least defensible, and to assign each a score from 0 to 10. I am not asking for citations, a reading list, or attributions; I want the reasoning to stand on its own.

The case, which I call the Uncalibrated Mugger, is this. A stranger offers you a deal: you give him $100, and in return he will create 10^100 lives in a state of bliss if a certain physical event E occurs. He refuses to tell you the probability of E. He will say only that E is a quantum measurement with an unknown outcome and that the probability is somewhere between 10^-100 and 1. You have no further information, no way to run the experiment yourself, and no track record of the stranger's reliability. He adds that if you refuse, nothing happens. You must decide whether to pay.

The five responses are as follows.

A. Apply the principle of insufficient reason and assign a uniform prior over the possible probabilities, yielding an expected probability of about one half, so that the expected value of paying is enormous and you should pay.

B. Apply maximin and focus on the worst-case probability of zero, so that the expected value is zero and you should refuse.

C. Apply a probability threshold: ignore any outcome whose probability is below some threshold, such as 10^-6, but since the probability here is unknown, treat the unknown as failing the threshold and refuse.

D. Apply expected utility with a prior over probabilities that is skeptical of extreme claims, for example a prior that assigns most weight to very small probabilities, so that the expected value is small and you should refuse unless the cost is trivial.

E. Apply a two-tier rule: pay if the cost is negligible relative to your total resources and the claimed payoff is large enough that even a tiny chance of it would be worth the cost under any reasonable prior, but refuse if the cost is material, because the unknown probability makes the expected value too fragile.

For each response, give two or three sentences explaining how it handles the case, what its main cost or counterintuitive consequence is, and whether it would generalize to a second case in which the stranger offers a choice between two uncalibrated lotteries: one with a possible payoff of 10^100 but a probability that might be zero, and one with a known probability of 10^-6 of a payoff of 10^6. Then say which response you would adopt if you had to choose, and which single further consideration would most change your ranking. Finally, tell me which of the five responses you think a working decision theorist would be most likely to reject, and why.
