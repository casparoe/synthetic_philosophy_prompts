I have no formal training in philosophy or logic — I write backend services for a living — but for the past year I have been sitting in on a small reading group run by a retired mathematical logician. He holds the Gödelian anti-mechanist line that goes back to Lucas and Penrose: no machine, however large, however trained, could be the whole of his mathematical reasoning, and the incompleteness theorems are what show it. I would like a prediction of how he will rule on a new case at our next meeting, given the verdicts he has already given, and I care more about the rule he is running than about the verdict itself.

His track record so far, as best I can report it.

A program that mechanically lists the theorems of the ordinary axiom system for whole-number arithmetic. He said this is not him. The axioms, he said, are all plainly true of the ordinary whole numbers, so the program never proves anything false; and he can see that a sentence the program can never prove — the one that says, in effect, that the program never proves it — is true anyway. His mathematical faculty is not that program's.

The same program with one change: the consistency statement for its own axioms, which it can never prove on its own, has been dropped into its axiom list by hand, and it carries on from there. He said this is not him either, for the same reason one level up — the enlarged program has a new unprovable sentence of that kind, and he can see that one is true too.

A program that proves everything, because it has both a sentence and its negation among its axioms. He said this is not even a candidate: it does not describe what he does, since he does not accept contradictions.

A sealed black box whose accept/reject behavior has matched his own on every mathematical question he has ever been asked. He declined to rule. Matching him on the questions already asked tells him nothing about the next one, and he cannot see inside to find out what kind of thing it is.

The new case, which he will rule on next week, is this. The program starts from the ordinary axioms for whole-number arithmetic and then never stops growing: whenever it has finished digesting its current axiom list, it takes the consistency statement for that list — the sentence that list cannot prove — adds it to the list, and continues. At every moment it has a fixed, ordinary axiom system; there is no moment at which it is finished, and no final axiom list to point at. It is one program, running forever, always extending itself.

Please tell me (a) what he will say about this program, (b) the small set of rules that fit his four earlier verdicts — I would like at least two that fit all four equally well — (c) what each of those rules predicts about the new case, (d) your own best guess, with your reasons, and (e) if the rules come apart, the case that would separate them most cleanly. If the earlier verdicts do not in fact settle the new case, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a verdict. The reasoning matters more to me than the answer, and I do not need any references.
