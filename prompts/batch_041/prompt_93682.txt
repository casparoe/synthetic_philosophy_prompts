I'm a software engineer with no background in philosophy, and I'm trying to referee an argument that has broken out on my team. We make risky calls constantly — which experiments to run, which rare failures are worth spending money to prevent — and we settle disputes by comparing plain expected values: each outcome's payoff multiplied by its probability, summed. A colleague has started arguing for a different rule. His rule ranks an option's outcomes from worst to best, takes the chance of doing at least as well as each one, and pushes those chances through a fixed curve — the same curve in every decision, leaving a 0% and a 100% chance where they are — that squeezes chances near the good end and stretches chances near the bad end; the weight he gives an outcome is the gap between its stretched chance and the stretched chance of the outcome just below it. On his rule, someone can consistently take $45 in hand over a coin flip between $0 and $100, and also take $900 in hand over a 1-in-100 shot at $100,000, even though the plain average favors the gamble both times.

Write a dialogue between the two of them, each trying to convince the other that his rule is the one a rational person should use. Neither should be a strawman, and each should answer the other's actual arguments rather than talk past them. I especially want them to fight over three things: (1) whether the colleague's rule can be exploited by someone who offers him a sequence of bets, one at a time, each of which he would accept; (2) whether expected value is genuinely neutral about risk or just hides a risk attitude inside the numbers it calls payoffs; and (3) whether there is any principled way to choose the curve, or whether it can only be fitted after the fact to whatever choices someone already happens to make. Give them enough rounds to press these points properly.
