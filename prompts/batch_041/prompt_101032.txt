I'm a science journalist, mostly covering physics and the philosophy of science, and I'm trying to write a piece about the weird afterlife of the OPERA faster-than-light neutrino result — you know the one, the 2011 Gran Sasso experiment that seemed to show neutrinos arriving about 60 nanoseconds early, which a lot of people half-believed for a few months until it turned out to be a bad fiber-optic connection and a clock problem. What I want to do is run the whole episode through the so-called dogmatism paradox, the old puzzle that says if you know some proposition, then any evidence you later encounter against it must be misleading evidence, so a knower is entitled to ignore it. I know the rough shape of the paradox and I dont need a bibliography — what I need is a careful mapping. Who in this story is supposed to be the dogmatist? Is it the community of physicists who knew special relativity, or the OPERA collaboration who knew their own apparatus, or both? What exactly counts as the evidence against the known proposition — the raw timing anomaly, or the anomaly plus the failure of every systematic check? And what does the paradox predict they should have done that they actually didn't do? Because from where I sit, the interesting thing is that nobody simply ignored the result; they took it seriously enough to run it down, and yet they also didn't treat it as a genuine refutation of relativity. So I want to know whether that counts as a refutation of the paradox, or a case where the paradox never got a grip because the relevant knowledge wasn't really present, or a case that shows the paradox is about a kind of ideal knower that no actual scientific community resembles.

The part I'm really stuck on is a distinction I've been kicking around, between an anomaly that is a defeater and an anomaly that is merely a surprise. My hunch is that the OPERA result was a surprise for a long time and only became a defeater once the systematic errors were ruled out, and by then the alleged evidence had evaporated anyway. But I don't know if that distinction is doing real work or just redescribing the paradox. So please use it, or tell me it collapses. I'd also like you to say where the paradox is silent rather than forcing it to speak: is it a normative claim about rational belief, a descriptive claim about how knowers actually treat counterevidence, or a bit of both that shouldn't be mixed? And what does the episode show about the paradox's hidden assumptions — for example, that evidence against a known proposition is always misleading, or that a knower can't simultaneously take a challenge seriously and still count as knowing? I'm not looking for a verdict on who was right about relativity; I'm looking for a case analysis that runs both ways, theory illuminating the episode and the episode testing the theory. If the paradox comes out looking less like a paradox and more like a warning about logical omniscience, that's a fine answer. I just want the mapping to be disciplined — who knows what, what the options were, what the evidence was, and where the theory runs out of things to say.
