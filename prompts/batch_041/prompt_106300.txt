I'm a high school junior, and for my school's philosophy club I've been asked to write out my position on mathematics so the other members can interrogate it. Before I present it I'd like a real audit rather than a pat on the back, because I can't tell how much of it actually fits together. Here is roughly where I've landed. (1) I'm a realist about mathematics: propositions such as 'there are infinitely many prime numbers' and 'the square root of two is irrational' are true or false independently of what any person or culture happens to believe, and they would have been true even if no one had ever done arithmetic. (2) I'm a naturalist: the only things that exist are things in the natural, spatiotemporal world, and I have no patience for souls, spirits, or other spooky additions to the inventory of reality. (3) I think we genuinely know some mathematical truths — I know that there are infinitely many primes, and I take that to be knowledge, not just a conditioned reflex to say 'true' when the teacher asks. (4) I think all knowledge ultimately rests on some reliable connection between the knower and the fact known: if I know a fact, there has to be a causal or at least counterfactual dependency running from the fact to my belief, the sort of thing that makes my belief track the fact instead of merely happening to be right. (5) I think the things mathematics is about — numbers, sets, functions — are abstract: they aren't located anywhere in space or time, and they don't cause anything to happen. (6) I think mathematical truth is really a matter of what follows from axioms by logic, so 'there are infinitely many primes' is true relative to the standard axioms. (7) Yet I don't think mathematical truth is merely relative or a matter of convention: if two people adopt different axiom systems and disagree, at most one of them can be right about what is actually true, because I believe there is a fact of the matter.

I suspect that some of these can't all be true at once, but I honestly can't tell which pairs are in genuine conflict and which only look like they are. For instance, (1) and (5) seem to sit badly with (2), though maybe that's just my own narrow idea of what counts as 'natural'; and (6) and (7) feel as though they are fighting each other even though each seems obviously correct when I consider it on its own; and (3) and (4) together make me nervous for reasons I can't quite put into words. Please go through the set and tell me, ranked by severity, which commitments actually pull against each other and how badly; which apparent conflicts dissolve once the claims are stated more carefully, together with the careful statement that dissolves them; and, for the genuine conflicts, the cheapest revision and what it would cost me to make it. Don't just lay out the menu of options and leave the choice to me — I want you to commit to a verdict about which single commitment I should be prepared to give up first, to say what I would be losing by giving it up, and to tell me plainly if you think the whole package is actually tenable and I'm worrying about nothing.
