You probably know the story: the female digger wasp drags a paralyzed cricket back to her burrow, leaves it outside while she goes in to inspect the nest, and if you take the cricket and move it a few inches while she's inside, she'll just go retrieve it and then do the whole inspection routine again from the top, and you can do this to her over and over, she never catches on. Dennett's the one who made it famous (I think he's quoting an older book by a guy named Wooldridge), and Hofstadter coined "sphexish" for that kind of rigid, mindless-looking behavior. So the group has split and hasn't budged. Camp A: the wasp is a mirror for us — it shows that behavior can look flexible and purposefull while the machinery underneath is a fixed script, and our own freedom is just a fancier version of the same trick, so stop being precious about it. Camp B: the example is a red herring twice over. First, the entomology has moved on — I think it was someone named Keijzer who pointed out that the wasp's behavior does adapt after enough repetitions, and that re-inspecting a nest you just disturbed is arguably just sensible, so the premise is shaky. Second, and more importantly, even a perfectly rigid wasp wouldn't tell us anything, because freedom isn't flexibility of behavior, it's being responsive to reasons, and no amount of extra gears in the machine gets you that. Same three points every week, nobody moves, and at this point I care less about who's right than about what kind of fight this even is.
