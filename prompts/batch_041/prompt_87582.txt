I run a scrappy little interview series for grad students in history and STS, and next week's guest is an archivist-theorist — she's not famous, so don't worry about who she is, I'm going to hand you her whole position and I want the questions to be about *that*, not about whatever you already associate with the phrase "archives and power."

Her line, roughly: what a document means is fixed less by what it says or by what its author wanted than by its chain of custody — who physically held it, who had the power to destroy it and chose not to, who eventually decided it was worth keeping, and who was never in a position to produce one in the first place. Call it the custody thesis if you need a label. Two consequences she draws from it: (a) a file that survived a purge is evidentially different from an otherwise identical file that was never at risk, and (b) digitization doesn't repair archival silence, it launders it — the scanner inherits the original selection, then presents the result as a neutral "complete archive," so the gaps get harder to notice, not easier. Her stock deflection when people push back is: "custody is a causal claim, not an epistemic one — I'm telling you where the document came from, not what you're allowed to believe." She's also oddly dismissive of the "just read against the grain and recover the subaltern's own voice from the margins" move, which I think is where she's most vulnerable.

Give me about nine or ten questions. I don't want her restating the thesis for the fourth time; I want questions that make her commit to things. In particular I want her pressed on: whether the thesis collapses into "every source is equally compromised so nothing is usable"; a counterexample I think she can't wave away, namely a shoebox of family letters kept by people with zero institutional power that survived precisely because no one with power wanted it; and a case where new imaging techniques recovered text that had been physically erased or scraped off — does that count as repairing a silence or as laundering it, given her own view?

Lay it out like this, in this order: "Openers" — two questions, one line each on why they're not throwaway; "Custody, stated plainly" — three questions that force her to say what the thesis actually rules *out*, each followed by the answer she'll most likely give and the follow-up that answer invites; "Where it breaks" — three questions built on specific counterexamples, same answer-plus-follow-up treatment; "Digitization" — two questions, same treatment; and "The one she can't deflect" — one closing question with no follow-up, where "custody is a causal claim, not an epistemic one" is simply unavailable to her. Keep each question under forty words; the follow-ups can run longer.
