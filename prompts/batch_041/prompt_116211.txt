I'm running a reading group on Borges's "The Lottery in Babylon" — the one in Ficciones where the Company's lottery starts as a small game and ends up governing every detail of life, with punishments and rewards, and by the end nobody's sure the Company even exists and the narrator says Babylon is just an infinite game of chance. I want to read it through the St. Petersburg paradox and the philosophical fight about expected value. The hook is obvious, but I'm more interested in the deeper parallel: the story takes a world where chance is the whole order and asks what that does to the idea that a rational person should maximize expected value. So does the story dramatize the paradox as a real problem for expected-value reasoning, or does it show the paradox is an artifact of an idealization (infinite bankroll, infinite time, unbounded utility) that Babylon makes literal and then makes horrible? Take the paradox seriously: Bernoulli's log-utility fix and Menger's objection that any unbounded utility can be re-paradoxed; the point that the expectation is only infinite because we let the casino have unlimited funds and the player live forever; the variant where the series is only conditionally convergent so the expected value doesn't exist at all (you can rearrange the terms to get any sum); and the difference between the average over many parallel players and the average over one player's repeated plays, where the latter can look grim even when the former is infinite. Then read the story against that. Where does the story illuminate the paradox — e.g., the slave who steals a ticket whose penalty is having his tongue burned, which is also the penalty for stealing a ticket, so nobody can say whether he's punished for the theft or for the draw, which is a nice image of a decision procedure that's lost its grip on the difference between what's deserved and what's drawn? Where does it resist the reading? Does the story take a position on whether the lottery is just or unjust, or does it refuse the question? And what does the story add to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone don't? Keep it faithful in both directions — don't reduce the story to an illustration of the paradox, and don't distort the paradox to fit the story. End with a last line starting "Bottom line:" that gives one sentence saying whether the story comes down on the side of the paradox being a genuine problem for expected-value reasoning or of its being dissolved.
