I've been co-teaching a seminar with a social psychologist for three years now — moral foundations theory, him on the psychology, me on the ethics — and the same fight happens every October and neither of us has moved a milimeter. His line: the psych shows that liberals and conservatives just have different moral palettes, that the so-called binding foundations (loyalty, authority, sanctity) are moral intuitions every bit as much as care and fairness, and that when philosophers call conservatives wrong they're only privileging their own palette. My line: a description of what people feel isn't a premise about what's true, and anyway I suspect the questionnaire is picking up ingroup preference and calling it morality.

What gets me is that the standard philosophical rebuttal — the is/ought point — doesn't move him at all, and his empirical replies don't move me, and I've started to think were not even disagreeing about the same thing. So I don't want a verdict on the theory, I want a diagnosis of the impasse. Which of these is doing the actual work?

(a) A verbal dispute over "moral". He means anything that triggers a moral emotion; I mean anything that could be justified. One word, two questions.

(b) A real empirical fight about whether the factors are distinct and cross-culturally robust, or whether the instrument is measuring ingroup favoritism and calling the residual morality. His grad student rewrote the sanctity items to be about, say, food taboos among secular vegetarians and got the same factor out; he says vindication, I say it's disgust sensitivity and nothing more.

(c) A metaethical fight about whether intuitions are evidence at all — in which case "who's right" presupposes a reliabilist moral epistemology neither of us has ever argued for, and the whole debate is downstream of a commitment we haven't examined.

(d) Bedrock disagreement about whether persistent moral disagreement is better explained by error or by genuine pluralism.

Here's the cut I keep trying to sell him and can't: treat a foundation not as a belief but as an attentional mechanism — what you notice in a situation before you've reasoned about it. On that reading, the claim that conservatives have foundations liberals lack doesn't mean they hold extra moral beliefs; it means they perceive features of situations that liberals are blind to, and the is/ought objection, which is about beliefs and truth, simply doesn't reach it. He thinks that's a distinction without a difference. I think it's the whole ballgame.

So: is that the right diagnosis, or am I misdescribing his side? And given your answer, what would actually count as progress here — a distinction, a study, a change of question? Or is this one of those impasses where the honest answer is that nothing would, and I should stop losing Octobers to it.
