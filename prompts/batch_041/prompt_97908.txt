Here's the question I set:

"Ranke is the historian everyone cites for the ideal of showing the past as it actually was, and Carr and Hayden White are the two everyone cites for the claim that this ideal is impossible. (1) What did Ranke's dictum mean in its own context? (2) State Carr's objection to the ideal of objectivity in history, and state White's. (3) Are Carr and White making the same objection or different ones? (4) Does the debate show that objective history is impossible, or is the real disagreement about something else? Answer all four."

And here's the answer:

"Ranke's famous line, usually rendered as saying the historian should just show how things actually were, is basically a positivist manifesto: stick to the documents, don't let philosophy or politics bend the record. It was aimed at the Hegelians, who forced the facts into a preconceived scheme. So Ranke's ideal is presuppositionless history — the sources, and nothing but the sources.

Carr's objection, in his lectures on history, is that this is naive. Facts don't come with labels on them. The historian has to select which facts matter, and that selection is guided by the problems of the present. A fact only becomes a historical fact when a historian picks it up. So the historian is always in the frame and pure neutrality is a myth.

White's objection is the same in spirit but more radical. In Metahistory he argues that historians impose narrative form on the past, and that this form is essentially literary. The historian emplots events as romance, tragedy, comedy, or satire, and that choice comes before any evidence. For White, history is really a continuous dialogue between the historian and his facts, except that he denies the dialogue ever reaches a stable answer — so the past itself is finally inaccessible and history turns out to be a kind of fiction.

So Carr and White are making the same basic point — the historian can't be neutral, the facts don't speak for themselves — with White just pushing it further. Both show that the historian always brings something to the table, so the ideal of objective history is dead. The real question underneath is whether we can ever get outside our own perspective, and both of them answer no."
