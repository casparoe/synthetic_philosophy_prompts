I spent thirty-five years as a geochemist and hung it up a couple of years back; since then I've been teaching myself philosophy, epistemology first and now aesthetics, and something about judged competitions has been nagging at me.

Here's the pattern. Anywhere you can't just put a stopwatch on the run or measure the jump — diving, gymnastics, figure skating, dressage, wine shows, dog shows — we do more or less the same thing. A panel of trained judges watches the same performance, each one writes down a number, we usually toss the highest and lowest and average the rest, and the medal goes to whoever comes out on top. In some of them the judges arn't allowed to talk to each other during the round; in some their names are kept secret; and a judge whose marks keep landing far from the panel average can get hauled up on suspicion of bias. Several of them score artistic impression as a seperate mark sitting right next to the technical one. Then the very same federation that just handed out a gold medal on the average of five people's opinions will turn around and say, without blinking, that taste is subjective and there's no arguing about it — and the broadcast will put the audience vote up on the screen beside the judges' scores as if those were two readings of the same quantity.

What I want to know is what the whole apparatus is committed to. As a scientist I was taught that when you can't measure a thing directly you take repeated measurements and average them, on the assumption that the errors are independent and shake out — but here the instruments are all trained on the same rulebook and all looking at the same event, so I don't see how the errors could be independent, and yet the scoring is built as though they are. So: does the practice presuppose that there's a fact of the matter about how good the routine was, and that a judge's mark reports that fact rather than reporting the judge? Does it need that, or could you run the identical apparatus while thinking taste is just whatever a trained community converges on, or that the marks are expressions of approval that happen to be coordinated? And the mark is testimony, isn't it — one person telling the rest of us what's beautiful — but I keep reading that you can't get a real aesthetic judgement out of somebody else's say-so, that they can tell you a thing is beautiful without making you see it. If that's right, the medal is being awarded on testimony that's supposedly no good for the purpose. Which views does the practice actually rule out, which does it merely sit comfortably with, and where is it simply underdetermined? And if you swapped out whatever it presupposes for one of the rivals, what would have to change on the floor — would there still be medals, would there still be a reason to drop the high and low mark, would the bias investigation still make sense?

Give it back to me as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and nothing to wrap it up at the end.
