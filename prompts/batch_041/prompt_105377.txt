I am the mayor of a mid-sized city, and six weeks from now I will stand at the edge of what used to be the Eastmoor district and deliver the formal apology my council voted nine to four to make. Sixty years ago the city cleared Eastmoor under an urban renewal program: roughly fourteen hundred households were relocated, most of them to neighborhoods farther from work; the assessments that justified the clearance called the district blighted without consulting anyone who lived there; the compensation paid was well below what the properties were worth; and the replacement housing promised at the time was never built. I need the text of the speech, to be read aloud in about seven minutes.

The advisory group that prepared the ceremony has given me a short brief, and I have found it more demanding than I expected. It distinguishes three things an official apology can be doing, which it says can come apart. First, record: fixing in public the account of what was done, by whom, to whom, and that it was wrong. Second, responsibility: accepting an obligation that outlives the ceremony, and being able to say what will now be different. Third, reconciliation: the repair of a relationship, which requires the other party's participation and cannot be declared from a podium. The brief adds two warnings. An apology that reaches for reconciliation before record and responsibility are established converts the victims' loss into the apologizer's relief. And a promise of repair that the body has not authorized converts the record itself into a new falsehood. It also insists that the clearance is not simply over — the dispersal, the lost equity, the neighborhood that never re-formed are still running — so a speech that speaks only in the past tense will be describing something that did not happen.

Some things you should know about the room. The Eastmoor descendants' association will be there in force, and it has said publicly that a ceremony without a commitment to a fund and a memorial is not an apology but a plaque. My council has authorized the ceremony and a marker; it has not authorized a fund, and four members who voted no have asked that the speech say the apology is not a judgment on those who acted in good faith at the time. The historical report is firm on the facts above, but it does not establish whether the city knew, when it made the promise, that the replacement housing would never be built; I don't want to assert knowledge the record doesn't support, and I don't want to launder the wrong into a mistake either. The association is itself split on whether I should ask for forgiveness at all; some of its members say that asking hands the burden back to them. My grandfather sat on the council that approved the clearance and voted for it, and I have not decided whether that belongs in the speech.

Write the speech. Plain language: the audience includes people who were children when this happened and people who were not born yet, and it includes residents of the housing that now stands on the site. Do not name any philosopher or theory, and do not quote from the report or from any other document — the distinctions have to do their work in ordinary words. Finish with two or three sentences addressed to me alone, not to the room, saying which of the competing demands you decided to sacrifice and why.
