I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I'm setting the final, which is closed-book and runs three hours. We spent three weeks on Berkeley, and every year the same thing happens: students can recite the standard objection to the master argument — that Berkeley slides from the harmless claim that you cannot do the conceiving without conceiving to the loaded claim that the thing cannot exist unconceived, so the argument tells us only about the act of thinking and nothing about the world — and then they stop, with nothing to say about whether the argument can be repaired, what a repaired version would look like, or whether it is doing any independent work in Berkeley's system at all. I want a single essay question that makes that recitation insufficient. Specifically, it should force students to confront three things: first, whether the master argument is independent of Berkeley's thesis that for sensible things, to exist is to be perceived, or whether it silently presupposes that thesis and so begs the question; second, whether the argument, even if it succeeds on its own terms, delivers mind-dependence or only mind-relatedness — since the materialist can cheerfully grant that we cannot think of a tree with no relation at all to any mind while insisting that the tree is not itself a mental item, on the grounds that what we conceive is a tree that would be perceived were anyone there, whereas Berkeley needs the stronger conclusion; and third, whether the appeal to divine perception rescues the argument or merely relocates the burden onto theism, so that the master argument alone cannot carry the weight Berkeley places on it.

A colleague of mine reads the argument quite differently, and I want his reading built into the question rather than left as a footnote. On his view, the master argument is not a conceivability-to-possibility inference at all but a claim about the identity conditions of sensible things: a sensible thing is nothing over and above the ideas that compose it, so that the phrase "an unperceived tree" fails to pick out a possible object of thought in roughly the way that "a round square" fails to pick out a possible object of thought — the failure is not psychological but semantic, and that is why no act of conceiving can get a grip on it. He thinks this rescues the argument from the equivocation charge. I think it merely relocates the circularity, since the identity claim is a compressed statement of the conclusion it is supposed to support. I want the question to make students take a side on that disagreement and defend it, rather than split the difference.

Please write the essay question, a model answer of roughly 600 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 marks. Have the question itself state the constraint for students — 90 minutes, about 600 words — and make clear that they are not expected to cite commentators or locate passages, only to argue. The marking scheme should name the specific moves a top answer must make, with the marks for each, and for each one a one-line note on how students typically get it wrong; it should also make explicit what a competent but merely recitative answer can and cannot earn, so that the A/B boundary falls between two identifiable kinds of answer rather than being a matter of polish. The model answer should paraphrase rather than quote, and should not depend on section numbers or page references.
