I'm a philosophy graduate student sitting in on a seminar on conditionals, and I would like help auditing an analogy that has escaped the seminar and is now doing real argumentative work in a friend's hands. The picture — introduced by our instructor as a heuristic, but treated by my friend as a proof — is the conditional bet. To grasp what it is to say that if A then B, imagine a bet that runs only if A obtains: you put down a stake; if A and B both turn out true you collect your winnings; if A turns out true and B false you forfeit the stake; and if A turns out false the bet is simply called off and your stake is returned as though nothing had happened. My friend's argument runs in two steps. First, the fair price of such a bet is fixed by the probability of B given A, so the probability of the conditional simply is the conditional probability — I believe this is the thesis associated with Adams. Second, a bet is not the sort of thing that can be true or false, so to assert a conditional is not to state a proposition but to place a conditional bet. He takes the single analogy to deliver both conclusions at once, and he grows impatient when I object that the probability equation and the claim that conditionals are propositions cannot both be true — that this is exactly what Lewis's triviality results are meant to establish, although I have never been able to state the result cleanly myself. His reply is that the objection merely shows conditionals are not propositions, which was his point all along.

Act as an expert in the semantics of conditionals and in the probability theory surrounding them, and give me a careful audit of the analogy rather than a survey of the literature. Before you settle on a verdict, take a moment to work out for yourself what the betting picture is actually doing at each step, and flag anywhere my summary of the dialectic is itself confused. Then give me, in order: a correspondence table, in which you say what plays the role of the bettor, the stake, the conditions under which the bet is live, the payoff in each case, and the fair price, with a note for each entry on whether the target really contains anything answering to it; the points at which the mapping breaks, sorted into those that matter for my friend's two conclusions and those that are merely picturesque; the features the analogy quietly imports from the source side that the target may lack — I am especially interested in what it assumes about how the object behaves when the antecedent is false, and about whether bets can be combined, negated, and hedged into portfolios; and finally whether either of his conclusions survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument all along. If you think a different comparison, or a device that is not a comparison at all, would do the work better, say what it is and why. Be concrete, and where the issues turn technical, show the reasoning rather than asserting a verdict.
