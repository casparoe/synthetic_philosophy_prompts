I'm assembling teaching material on how concepts drift when they are copied between neighbouring practices, and I need one worked example rather than a general discussion. The setting I have in mind is a small, self-contained microdomain of the kind where everyone knows everyone: a single workshop, laboratory, or guild whose members share a vocabulary and can tell, without hesitating, whether a term is being used correctly.

Take a concept that begins life in one part of such a microdomain with a narrow, operational job — it picks out something you can point at, measure, or act on, and its boundaries are fixed by what the work requires. It is then carried into an adjacent part of the same microdomain, where it is used as though it still meant the same thing, but the new context gives it a different job: it now classifies, or licenses an inference, or marks a status, rather than picking out the original thing. The copy survives the journey, but its extension has shifted, and the two uses can no longer both be right about the same borderline case. What I want is a case where the shift is not simple ambiguity (one word, two dictionary senses, no real dispute), and not mere vagueness (one sense, fuzzy edges), but genuine slippage: the second use inherits the authority of the first while quietly changing what the term covers, so that people reasoning in the two uses talk past each other without noticing.

Please do three things.

First, invent the microdomain and specify it tightly enough that the example stands on its own: the practice, the people, the two sub-parts, and the term. Give me the term's original job and its original boundaries, stated as the practitioners themselves would state them, and then the borrowed job and the new boundaries. I should be able to see exactly which case falls on one side under the old use and the other side under the new one.

Second, give me the example in motion: a short scene or exchange in which two competent users of the term, each internally consistent, reach incompatible conclusions about one concrete case, and neither can see why the other is being obtuse. Show where each would say the other has simply misused the word, and why that accusation is unfair from the other's position.

Third, diagnose the slippage. Is the drift driven by the new job the term has to do, by an analogy that was apt at the centre and misleading at the edges, by a change in what counts as evidence in the new sub-part, or by something else? Say what the two uses actually disagree about — a fact, a standard, or a starting intuition — and whether anything short of splitting the term in two would resolve it. If splitting it would work, say what the two resulting terms would be and what each would lose; if it would not, say why the concept is genuinely fluid and the drift is not an error at all.

Keep the example small and concrete. I would rather have one well-specified case with its seams visible than a survey of many, and I would rather the term be one whose two uses are both defensible than one where the second use is plainly a mistake.
