I'm an undergraduate taking a course on Wittgenstein and the philosophy of psychology, and I have been trying to work out what the clinical practices I see while volunteering actually commit us to. On the ward where I work, the pain protocol is stated flatly: a patient's pain is whatever the patient reports it to be, and the 0–10 number they give is charted as the measurement of it. Nobody checks the report against anything, on the understanding that there is nothing to check it against; the numbers are then aggregated into ward-level quality figures, so that a shift can be said to have done well or badly by the average score it produced. The same hospital runs a very different protocol for suicide risk: the patient is asked about their intentions in much the same way, but the answer is treated as one fallible piece of evidence, weighed against a structured assessment and against the patient's behavior, and it can be overridden. And where a patient cannot speak — advanced dementia, a preverbal child — the same ward scores pain from behavior instead: facial expression, guarding, breathing, and similar signs, entered in the same field of the chart and treated as a pain score in the same sense.

What does this pattern presuppose about the mind? Does the first protocol presuppose a Cartesian picture, on which pain is a private object and the report is an incorrigible description of it, or is it better read in the Wittgensteinian way, on which the report is an expression rather than a description and its authority belongs to the grammar of the practice rather than to a privileged route to an inner object — and if so, what is the practice still committed to? Does charting the number as a measurement, comparable across patients and usable as an institutional score, quietly reintroduce the private object, or does it presuppose something else? Do the behavioral scales presuppose that the same state is present and merely reached by a different evidential route, or a different conception of what the criteria for the state are? Is the suicide-risk protocol, which treats the report as defeasible evidence, inconsistent with the pain protocol, or does the difference follow from something other than the metaphysics of mind — the stakes, the possibility of deception, the direction of fit between report and state? And which parts of the pattern are strict commitments of a single theory, which are merely well fitted by several incompatible ones, and what would have to change, and what would be lost, if the ward adopted the suicide-risk model across the board?
