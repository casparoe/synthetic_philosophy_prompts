I write long-form features on ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am at the reporting stage on a piece about the politics of the archive — about the fact that the historical record is not merely incomplete but systematically shaped, and that the shape tends to track power. My editor has given me an unusual brief. Rather than another essay lamenting that the voiceless go unrecorded, I am to come out of the reporting with an actual agenda: a defensible answer to where a fixed pot of money and attention should go first, and why. The occasion is concrete. A mid-sized foundation has told me it will commit roughly eight million dollars over four years to archival work and wants to know which problems in this area are worth solving first. So the reader you are writing for is a program officer who will read your answer once and then have to defend it to a board.

To keep us both honest, let me fix a taxonomy rather than let you reach for the familiar one. I have come to think there are four distinct ways a past can fail to reach us, and that they call for different remedies:

1. No record was ever made — the events were never written down, or survive only in perishable or non-textual media.
2. A record was made but not kept — it was destroyed, discarded, deaccessioned, or simply never collected, whether through neglect or by design.
3. A record was kept but is unreachable — it sits in a basement, an unprocessed backlog, a proprietary database, a dead format, or a jurisdiction that will not let you see it.
4. A record survives but speaks in the wrong voice — the only trace of a person's life or testimony is a powerful party's summary, translation, or transcription of it, so that the past is present but distorted rather than absent.

I want you to rank these four by where marginal effort should go if the goal is to make the historical record less unjust, and then, within the top-ranked one, to name the two or three concrete lines of work you would actually fund. Defend the ordering rather than the list. I want the criteria you are using made explicit — upstreamness (which silence, if addressed, unlocks work on the others), tractability with money, technology, and institutional will as they actually are, neglectedness, and how much of the historical profession's interpretive work is currently bottlenecked on each — along with the trade-offs between those criteria and what evidence over the next four years would move an item up or down. Note also where the taxonomy itself may be doing illicit work: if two of my four collapse into one, or if there is a fifth kind of silence I have missed that would change the ranking, say so and adjust rather than ignoring it.

Two constraints. I do not need a reading list or a survey of who has said what; I need an argument I can quote and a program officer can act on, so keep the reasoning self-contained. And resist the temptation to conclude that all four matter and it depends. Someone with a fixed budget cannot fund a category. Take a position.

End with a final line, on its own line, in exactly this form: Verdict: [your full ranking, best first, as digits separated by ' > '] — [the single line of work you would fund first, in no more than fifteen words].
