I'm three weeks into a metaphysics seminar, and we've just finished the unit on the necessity of origin — the Kripkean thesis that a person's origin is essential to them, so that I could not have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones I in fact did. I thought I had followed the argument well enough, but two things now make me suspect that I have it backwards, and I would rather be corrected than keep walking around with the wrong version of the view in my head.

The first is my cousin and his brother, who are identical twins. They came from the same sperm and the same egg — the fertilised egg simply divided — and yet they are plainly two people, with different jobs, different temperaments, and no sense that they are one another. But if my origin is what makes me the individual I am, then two individuals who share an origin ought to be a single individual, and here they manifestly are not. The second is that my mother likes to say, only half joking, that I was an accident, that she and my father had meant to wait several more years, and that a slightly more careful month would have meant no me at all. So the union of that particular sperm and that particular egg looks like the most fragile of contingencies, and it seems to me that something cannot be essential to me and at the same time be something that so easily might never have happened. Either the thesis is false or I am misreading it, and I would like to know which. Could you set out what the thesis actually claims, and say what it does and does not rule out in these two cases — in particular, whether either case is a genuine counterexample or whether each simply shows that I have been assuming something the thesis never asserted?
