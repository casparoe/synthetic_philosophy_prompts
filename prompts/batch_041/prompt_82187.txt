Ok so I've been going down a bit of a Newcomb's problem rabbit hole and I want your help sorting out which arguments actually carry weight. Quick setup in case it matters: there's a predictor, two boxes, one transparent with $1000 in it, one opaque with either $1M or nothing inside, and you get to take both boxes or just the opaque one. The predictor is known to be reliable and fills the boxes before you choose. Standard stuff.

Here's my thing. I've collected a bunch of considerations that get thrown around in this debate — some from the usual back-and-forth, some I've sort of cobbeled together myself — and I want to know which ones should actually move someone who's currently a two-boxer (which is roughly where I lean, but I'm not confident). I don't want a survey of the literature or a reading list, I want your honest weighting.

So: rate each of these on how much it should move a thoughtful two-boxer, and in which direction, then sort them into tiers. Don't hedge everything into the middle — I want to know which ones are doing real work and which are noise.

1. The straightforward correlation argument: the predictor is reliable and fills the boxes before you choose, so your choice is evidence about what's in the opaque box; taking both means you're probably in the branch where it's empty.

2. The causalist reply: the contents are already fixed by the time you choose, your action can't causally reach back and change them, so the opaque box holds whatever it holds whether you take one box or two — so take both, it's a free extra grand.

3. Reframe: the predictor isn't really predicting your action so much as your decision procedure. So the real question isn't "which box" but "what kind of agent to be," and by the time you're standing in the room that's already settled by what kind of agent you are.

4. The medical/statistical analogy: a gene that causes both a taste for cigarettes and lung cancer makes smoking correlate with cancer without smoking causing it. Same move here — the predictor's reliability might be pure correlation with no influence either way, so the correlation argument in (1) never earns you causation.

5. Ensemble thinking: imagine running the game a thousand times with your policy held fixed. The one-boxing version of you ends up rich; the two-boxing version picks up an extra $1000 in the runs where the box was empty but misses all the big paydays. So pick the policy that wins across the ensemble.

6. The "it's really a metaphysics question" line: a perfectly reliable predictor of your choice seems to presuppose that your choice was determined or known in advance, so the whole case may be smuggling in a picture where "you could have taken the other box" is just false. If that's right, the decision-theoretic framing is a distraction.

7. The reliability wrinkle: the case only bites at very high reliability. At 50% or 60% the calculus for a cautious person flips, so part of the disagreement is really about which reliability regime we're all quietly imagining.

Format the answer like this, in this order:

- **Tiers**: the tier list up front, top tier down to bottom, each item on one line with a few words on direction (toward one-boxing, toward two-boxing, or neither). Keep it compact.
- **Per item**: go through them in your tier order, 2–4 sentences each, saying what's actually doing the work and where it overreaches. Maybe 500 words total.
- **Least confident call**: which placement you'd most expect me to push back on, and what would flip it. A short paragraph.
