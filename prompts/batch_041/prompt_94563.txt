I moderate a monthly discussion circle — most of us are engineers or retired teachers, nobody with any formal training in philosophy — and this year we've been circling the question of whether the brain is a computer. We always end up in the same ditch: one person says the brain is obviously a computer because it takes in information, stores it, and puts out behavior; someone else says computers only shuffle symbols around and never mean anything while a brain does; and then we go around again. What finally got us somewhere was a distinction we cooked up ourselves, which we've been calling backwards versus forwards implementation. A backwards implementation is one you can always construct after the fact: you watch what the brain does and then choose an assignment of its physical states to the states of some machine so that the two march in step. A forwards implementation is one fixed in advance: you specify the machine, and the physical system's own wiring forces the assignment, so that the machine's next state is the state the system actually goes to, and the state it would go to if you poked it a certain way. Our hunch is that the only interesting version of the claim that the brain is a computer is the forwards one, and that the backwards version is true of a bucket of water, a rock, and the weather, which makes it worthless.

I'd like to close the year with a written exam, since the group asked for something that would make us commit to a position instead of trading slogans. So please write me a single essay question, pitched at people who have read widely but have no technical background, that cannot be answered by reciting the standard functionalism-versus-the-Chinese-room material. It should force the writer to say what it would even mean for the brain to implement a computation, to take a stand on whether the backwards/forwards distinction (or something better in its place) survives scrutiny, and to decide whether "is the brain a computer?" is finally an empirical question, a conceptual one, or not a real question at all. Please also give me a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme totaling 20 points that names the specific moves a top answer has to make — and, for each move, the specific way a middling answer typically misses it, so that I can tell a student who understands the issue from one who has merely memorized the labels. One thing I want the question to force: it should make the writer confront a case where the forwards/backwards line looks shaky, namely a system whose own causal wiring can only be identified by first assuming which computation it implements. Write everything in American English.
