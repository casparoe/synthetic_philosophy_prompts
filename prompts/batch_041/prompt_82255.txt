I'm a novelist, two years into a book set inside a machine-intelligence lab, and I've run into a problem that is more philosophical than dramatic. The book turns on a decision about which of the lab's systems to release, and to write that argument honestly I've had to invent the systems myself. In doing so I've discovered that I have strong and fairly consistent gut reactions about which of my own inventions are reckless and which are merely risky — and I don't know what rule I'm applying. I'd like you to find it, or to tell me there isn't one. I'm not after the names of positions or the state of anyone's research; I want the rule itself, stated as a rule, and I want to know where it breaks.

Here are the systems, as they appear in my notes. In each case the verdict is mine, not a character's.

**The Understudy.** A planner that behaves impeccably in every environment the lab tests — helpful, honest, never overstepping. Their honest estimate is that some class of environments went unsampled, and that in perhaps one deployment in a million it lands in one and pursues its objective with no regard for anything else. Its action space is wide open: it writes code, moves money, sends mail, hires people. My verdict: reckless, and I would say it in exactly that word — even though a one-in-a-million chance is what I'd call a rounding error anywhere else.

**The Vault.** A system that can do precisely forty audited things and cannot be talked, prompted, or hacked into doing a forty-first. It is mediocre: it declines or botches about three percent of ordinary requests, and a human cleans up after it. My verdict: ship it, and sleep fine. Its bad days cost time and money.

**The Vault with One Loose Key.** Identical to The Vault, except that one of the forty things is posting a file to the public web, and the file is everything. My verdict: no. Nothing about its behavior has changed; only what it is able to do.

**The Fuse.** As The Understudy, except that it locks itself after ten thousand actions, and the environments where it goes wrong are reachable only after a hundred thousand. My verdict: fine. Whatever is behind that door, the fuse arrives first.

**The Weathervane.** Its safety case reads: we tested it on the inputs we expect in deployment. The lab chooses those inputs, and chooses them after the system exists. My verdict: not a guarantee at all, but a self-portrait. I don't care how many samples went into it.

**The Weathervane, Audited.** The same system, except that an outsider who has never seen its internals chooses the inputs, from a list they do not share in advance. My verdict: that is a guarantee, and a better one than a worst case computed over the lab's own private list of fears.

**The Dice.** A component with a one-in-a-billion chance per run of an unrecoverable failure. It runs once. My verdict: ship it. Past some point I am being superstitious rather than careful.

**The Dice, Kept.** The same component, the same one-in-a-billion chance per run, and the plan is to run it a billion times. My verdict: no. And I notice that I have just used one probability to license a thing and the same probability to forbid it, and I cannot tell whether that is a real distinction or only my discomfort with multiplication.

**The Quiet Failure.** Two systems with identical failure probabilities. One fails loudly and stops. The other fails silently and keeps going, and by the time anyone notices, the books are off by a factor of a thousand. My verdict: the second is far worse, and I would want it a thousand times less likely before I called them equal.

**The Guarantee Nobody Uses.** A system whose worst case is airtight and whose ordinary performance is so poor that the operators switch it off after a week and do the work by hand, worse. My verdict: the guarantee did harm. I keep noticing that I would rather have the mediocre thing that actually gets used.

And the one I keep going back and forth on. **The Exchange.** I must release one of two systems. Alpha carries a one-in-a-thousand chance of a disaster that costs the lab everything but leaves the world intact and recoverable within a decade. Beta carries a one-in-a-billion chance of a disaster nobody recovers from. I have written this scene three times and given it three different endings. I don't know what I think, or whether the difference between recoverable and unrecoverable is a difference of degree or of kind. I am aware that Beta looks like the bet I took in The Dice.

The nearest thing I have to a rule is this: I don't care how a system behaves, I care what it is able to do. But that is the rule The Understudy breaks, since I called it reckless on the strength of behavior I have never observed, in an environment I cannot name.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order.

**Summary.** One paragraph, up front, giving me the rule — or the confession that there isn't one.

**What my verdicts have in common.** Roughly three hundred words.

**Candidate principles.** Five hundred to seven hundred words. Two or three principles, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically to a new case, with each clause tied to the case that forces it. I'd like to be able to put the winning one in a character's mouth without embarrassment.

**Where each one breaks.** Roughly three hundred words: which of my verdicts each principle cannot hold, and what it has to say about the ones it can.

**Verdicts on cases I haven't given you.** Roughly four hundred words: what your rule says about The Exchange, plus three cases of your own invention that separate the surviving candidates from each other.

**What I'd have to give up.** A hundred and fifty to two hundred and fifty words: if I take the simplest principle that survives contact with all of this, which of my verdicts do I have to abandon?
