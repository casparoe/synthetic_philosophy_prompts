I am a data engineer with twelve years on ingest pipelines, and I have just been seconded to a project that is migrating a large paper archive — parish registers, institutional minute books, case files, a great deal of correspondence — into a live database with a schema, a validation layer, retention rules, and access tiers. The historians on the project keep saying that the migration is itself an act of power rather than a neutral change of format. I want to know whether that is a substantive claim or a figure of speech.

Take the central claims of the literature on archives and power and restate each of them in the vocabulary I actually think in: schemas and columns, ingest validation, retention and deletion policy, provenance fields, access tiers, indexes, NULL semantics, and the difference between a record that was never created and one that was created and later destroyed. Restate these claims one at a time, as faithfully as you can, and without quietly weakening them into things my team already does as a matter of routine:

- that the record is an artefact rather than a trace;
- that absence is produced rather than merely suffered;
- that the ordering of an archive — its series, its finding aids, its categories — constitutes rather than describes;
- that whoever holds the power to restrict, delete, or decline to create is doing something to the past, not merely declining to do something;
- that the reader is a further author of the record.

Then give me the residue report. Which of these translate cleanly into my vocabulary? Which survive only in a weakened form — and where they do, mark the weakening rather than presenting the weakened version as the translation. Which have no counterpart at all? And where my vocabulary makes a claim look like an ordinary engineering defect that a better pipeline would fix, say whether that is a genuine reduction or an artefact of the vocabulary.

Finally, the question I actually care about. My pipeline will drop rows that fail validation; retention will delete certain classes of record on a schedule; provenance will record the last writer and not the first; some facts will end up in a free-text notes field and therefore be unqueryable. Are the archival silences anything over and above missing data with a non-random missingness mechanism — absence correlated with the very thing that is absent — or is the talk of power doing work that a statistician's vocabulary already does better? If it is more, say precisely what the extra is, and whether that extra is something my vocabulary cannot express or something the literature has not succeeded in stating.
