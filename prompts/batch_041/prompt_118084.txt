For several years I have been part of a reading group on the moral status of animals, and one dispute keeps returning without either side yielding. One party appeals to comparative neuroanatomy. It observes that brain mass, brain mass relative to body mass, and the encephalisation quotient built on that ratio have all been used as proxies for cognitive capacity, and it points to the more recent proposal that what matters is the absolute number of neurons, together with the suggestion that counting neurons rather than weighing brains reorders the ranking, so that some comparatively small-brained birds come out much closer to mammals than their brain mass alone would indicate. The conclusion drawn is that, if moral status admits of degrees, such measures are our best available proxy for where a creature stands. The other party replies that moral status turns on sentience, that sentience is not a matter of degree in the way cognitive capacity is, and that comparative neuroanatomy therefore cannot bear on moral status even if every empirical claim it makes is sound. A third view, to which I am inclined, holds that both sides skip a step: no one has specified which capacities are supposed to matter, or how possessing a capacity to a degree is supposed to generate a degree of moral status.

My hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but three superimposed on one another: an empirical question about whether any anatomical measure actually predicts the capacities we care about, across taxa; a normative question about which psychological features ground moral status and whether status is a threshold or a scale; and a conceptual question about what is meant by calling something morally relevant, whether a necessary condition, a sufficient condition, a tiebreaker, or a weight in a calculation. Is that the right diagnosis, or is there a unity to the dispute that I am failing to see? If the diagnosis is right, tell me which of the three questions is actually doing the work in the impasse, and what progress would look like for each: a piece of evidence, an argument, a distinction, or a decision to stop asking the question in this form. And if the impasse is instead the familiar one of conflicting bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for, say so plainly and explain why none of the above would move it. Please answer in British English.
