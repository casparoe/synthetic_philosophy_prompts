I am assembling materials for a seminar on how a reasoner should behave when it cannot check everything, and for this piece I want a story rather than an exposition. The idea to stage is the presumption of independence: an agent that must assign probabilities to many claims at once cannot verify each one, so it adopts a default rule — treat two events or quantities as independent unless a dependence between them can actually be derived, so that the estimate for their conjunction is simply the product of the separate estimates, and no correlation is admitted merely because it seems plausible or because a pattern is felt to be there. The rule has real virtues: it is indifferent to the agent's hunches, it keeps the whole assignment internally consistent, and it is the sort of discipline that survives an agent's awareness of its own wishful thinking. Its cost is that where a genuine dependence exists but cannot be proved — two events with a hidden common cause, two quantities that are in fact one quantity under two names — the rule is confidently wrong, and wrong in a way that no amount of internal checking can expose.

Write the story so that this tension is the plot and not the subject: characters with something at stake, an institution that has adopted the rule and defends it, and at least one person who is certain of a dependence she cannot demonstrate. Set it wherever it works — a pricing desk, a weather office, a parish, a marriage. Let no character explain the principle; let the reader watch it work.

Then add an afterword of no more than 150 words saying where the story takes liberties with the idea, which reading of it the story assumes — a reading a defender of the rule might well reject — and what would change your mind about whether the story is fair to the rule: the argument or consideration that would force you to rewrite the central conflict.

The story should be no longer than 900 words, and the whole response under 1,100.
