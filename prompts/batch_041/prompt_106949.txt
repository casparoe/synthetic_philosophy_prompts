I am trying to understand the closing argument of Gödel's essay on relativity theory and idealistic philosophy, and I would like help working through it. I do not have the original at hand, so what follows is my own paraphrase, not a quotation; if the paraphrase distorts the argument, say so before answering.

As I reconstruct it, the argument runs roughly as follows. In the rotating cosmological model Gödel constructed, there are worldlines that close back on themselves, so that a traveler could in principle return to his own past. But the philosophically important feature is not the time travel; it is that the model admits no global time coordinate whose level surfaces could be read as a succession of presents. An objective lapse of time, on this view, would require precisely that: a privileged partition of events into past, present, and future, with one slice after another coming into existence and passing away. The laws of nature, as relativity presents them and as this solution illustrates, single out no such partition. Gödel concludes that an objective lapse of time is not merely absent from this model but incompatible with the laws of nature, and that what we call the passage of time therefore belongs to the observer rather than to the world. He takes this to favor idealism over materialism.

What I want help with is the inferential structure. First, why should the absence of a privileged present in one cosmological model bear on the actual world? Is the thought that the model is physically possible, hence the laws of nature do not require a privileged present, hence our experience of one cannot be grounded in the laws and must be grounded in something non-physical? Or is a stronger claim at work? Second, how is the conclusion supposed to favor idealism rather than a block-universe materialism, which also denies passage but seems to make time more thing-like rather than less? Third, what work is done by the closed timelike curves as opposed to the absence of a global time function: is the time-travel feature essential to the philosophical conclusion, or a byproduct of the model? Fourth, I have a vague sense that Gödel thought the argument would survive even in a universe that did possess a global time function, so long as no slice were privileged; if that is right, reconstruct the argument in that form and tell me whether it comes out stronger or weaker.

Please walk through the moves in order, mark where the argument is doing real work and where it is sliding, and tell me which single premise, if false, would do the most damage.
