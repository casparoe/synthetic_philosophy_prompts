I host a small interview podcast about philosophy, and I am looking for help preparing an episode. My background is in software engineering; I have no formal training in philosophy, so while I can follow an argument once it is laid out, I want the questions to be concrete and case-driven rather than dependent on terminology I might misuse on air.

Next week's guest is a philosopher who has been developing a distinctive position on moral testimony — that is, on what is going on when you take someone's word for it about a moral matter. I would like you to draft the interview. Here is the position as I understand it, and I would like the questions to be built around these three claims rather than around the topic in general.

First, he holds that testimony with moral content can transmit knowledge and justified belief just as testimony about ordinary matters can: if someone you have good reason to trust tells you that a particular policy is unjust, you can come to know that it is, and the common feeling that you must work moral questions out for yourself is a prejudice rather than a requirement.

Second, he does not extend this to every case. He separates moral questions that are not yours to settle — whether a distant country's policy is unjust, whether some historical figure deserves condemnation — from questions that are yours to settle because you are the one who has to act: whether to forgive someone who hurt you, how much of your income to give away, whether to keep a promise. On the first sort of question he thinks deference is unproblematic; on the second he thinks it damages the act itself, so that an act performed on someone else's say-so is not fully the agent's own.

Third, and this is the part I find most interesting and least familiar, he denies that the second sort of question is simply harder to know about. His claim is that for those questions the correct answer partly depends on what the agent is willing to commit to, so that there is a class of moral questions that are settled by the person answering rather than discovered by her. Testimony cannot reach those questions, on his account, not because testimony is unreliable but because there is nothing there to transmit until the agent commits.

What I need is eight to ten interview questions. At least one should press on where the line between the two sorts of moral question actually falls — give him cases rather than asking him to state a criterion. At least one should press on the third claim, in particular on whether it makes the answer to a moral question depend on what someone happens to be willing to stand behind, and on how that differs from simply saying that she should decide for herself. At least one should press on what moral expertise amounts to on his view: if the expert's advantage lies in attending to a situation better rather than in knowing more moral facts, can that advantage be passed on by testimony at all, and if it cannot, does his first claim survive?

Please add three further things. First, a question about a case in which two sources the agent reasonably trusts give conflicting moral testimony, since that seems to be where the first and second claims pull against each other. Second, a question designed to make the reply that his view simply gives the deferring agent an excuse harder to use, assuming he answers that the excuse is a feature rather than a defect. Third, a closing question about what would change his mind.

For each main question, include a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give, and where you can see the standard reply coming, write the follow-up so that it does not let him stop there. Please keep the questions free of jargon where a plain formulation will do, and do not include questions that ask him to restate the view, questions about how he came to work on this, or requests for reading recommendations.
