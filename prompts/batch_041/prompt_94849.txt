I sit on the statistical review panel of a journal. For any submission that turns on a group comparison, we issue one of two dispositions before the paper goes to referees: the headline claim may stand as written, or the claim must be restated around the stratified analysis. Our dispositions run to a sentence or two and are formulaic, so authors have learned not to read much into the wording; the record is the record. I have to write the disposition for a new submission, and I want to predict what we will do, on the assumption that we are trying to be consistent with ourselves.

Act as a methodologist who works at the intersection of causal inference and the philosophy of statistics, and who adjudicates this kind of dispute for a living. Begin by enumerating the candidate rules that our record is consistent with — resist settling on one immediately — then reason carefully about which rule is best supported, and commit to a prediction with a stated level of confidence.

Our recent dispositions:

1. A cohort study reported that a drug looked protective in the pooled sample but harmful within every stratum of a severity score recorded at screening, before any exposure, and named in the analysis plan. Disposition: claim restated around the stratified analysis.

2. A study of a workplace policy reported a benefit pooled and a harm within every band of a job-grade variable recorded at intake and named in the analysis plan. Disposition: claim restated.

3. A regional evaluation reported a program as effective in the pooled comparison and ineffective within every stratum of a deprivation index fixed before the program began and named in the analysis plan. Disposition: claim restated.

4. A submission reported a pooled benefit that reversed within a subgroup defined after the fact by a measure taken after treatment had begun. Disposition: claim stands as written.

The new submission is a randomized trial of a team-training program: roughly ten thousand teams, a continuous outcome measured at twelve months, and a mean difference as the reported contrast. Pooled, the program improves the outcome; within every stratum of the variable the authors call baseline team climate, it worsens the outcome. The methods section, which the disposition will not mention, records that the baseline climate survey was administered at the three-month visit, three months into the program; assignment was randomized and the survey played no part in it. The variable was named in the analysis plan as a pre-specified stratifier, and the share of teams falling in each climate stratum differs substantially between the arms.

Give me the disposition you predict; the candidate rules our record is consistent with, each with its prediction here; your best guess and the reasons for preferring it; the case we could plausibly see next that would best discriminate between the surviving rules; and an honest statement of what would change your mind — which reading of our precedents, or which fact about the new submission, would flip your prediction — together with the step in your reasoning you regard as most likely to fail. If the disposition that best fits our record and the disposition that is simply correct come apart, say so, and say which you would defend.
