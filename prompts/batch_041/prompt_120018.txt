A friend of mine argues that a region's right to secede is simply the collective form of an individual's right to run her own life: if no one may conscript me into a life I reject, then no majority may hold a people inside a state it rejects, and a referendum won by the secessionists settles the matter, the rest of the state having no veto over another's self-determination. He goes further and says this is precisely why the costs to the remainder of the state — lost revenue, an unfriendly neighbour, unsettled borders — cannot count against secession, since we do not let third parties veto a person's choices. He treats the individual-autonomy analogy as carrying the whole argument. I want an audit rather than an endorsement: set out the correspondences explicitly (a table is fine), saying what stands in for the individual, for her life, for her body, for the coercion, and for the options she is entitled to; then say where the mapping fails, and sort the failures into those that bear on his conclusion and those that are merely picturesque. Does the conclusion survive a repaired version of the analogy, and would some other analogy — divorce, a club, a dissolving partnership — carry more of the weight? Close by stating how confident you are in each of your main claims and identify the point you are least sure of.
