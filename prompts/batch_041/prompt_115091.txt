Before I read another argument about what the absent-minded driver shows, I want the requirements list first: state what any adequate decision theory must deliver when it is applied to agents with imperfect recall, with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restating the question.

I have been reading rationalist blogs for over a decade, so I know the standard material: the driver who cannot tell the first intersection from the second, the one-third mixed strategy, the claim that causal decision theory mishandles the case because the action at the first intersection is correlated with the action at the second, and the appeal to choosing a policy rather than an action. I do not need the puzzle explained. I need the spec.

Fix adequacy relative to this purpose: I want a theory I could actually use as a bounded agent, not merely one that resolves the puzzle. So the requirements should cover the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must respect (action versus policy, choice versus prediction, what the agent knows at the moment of action versus what it knows at the moment of policy selection, imperfect recall versus unawareness), and the puzzles it must dissolve rather than merely accommodate. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude existing candidates.

Then stress-test the list against a variant I have not seen discussed. The driver can leave a durable mark at an intersection, and can see marks left by its past selves, but still cannot remember whether it is the one who left a given mark. Does the mark function as memory, as a commitment device, or as something else? What must a theory say about it, and does the answer change which requirements are non-negotiable?

Finally, a one-line verdict per family you take to be standard — the ones that treat an action as evidence about what the agent will do, the ones that treat it as a cause, and the ones that evaluate policies rather than actions — on which requirement each fails, if any. Keep that part short; the criteria are the deliverable.
