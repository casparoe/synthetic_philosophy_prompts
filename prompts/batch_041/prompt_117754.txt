I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about whether constructive mathematics is a doctrine about numbers or only a doctrine about proofs, and he left me holding an argument I cannot dismantle. What I want is a diagnosis rather than reassurance: which step fails, why it looked so plausible, and what each possible repair costs.

(1) A proof of "A or B" must yield a proof of A or a proof of B; that is just what the connectives mean.
(2) So a proof of "A or not-A" would have to decide A.
(3) Intuitionistic arithmetic does not prove that schema; and there are statements independent of the usual formal systems — take S to be the claim that a particular computation halts, or any arithmetic statement of the kind known to be independent.
(4) Even so, "not (A or not-A)" is refutable intuitionistically, since either disjunct would immediately reinstate the disjunction; hence "not not (A or not-A)" is a theorem while "A or not-A" is not.
(5) So for such an S, neither S nor its negation can be established, and their disjunction cannot be established either — and it is not merely that we do not yet know which disjunct holds, since the disjunction cannot even be refuted.
(6) A statement that can be neither established nor refuted has no truth value: nothing makes it true and nothing makes it false.
(7) Constructive mathematics therefore leaves some of its own statements without truth value; it records what can be proved rather than what is the case.

I would rather keep (1) through (4), and I would rather not concede that constructive arithmetic is about different objects than ordinary arithmetic, but my friend's parting shot was that I cannot have both. Tell me which step to abandon, why it looked airtight, and what the alternatives cost; and separate what is settled among people who work on intuitionistic logic from what is genuinely contested, rather than presenting a contested account of constructive truth as the consensus.
