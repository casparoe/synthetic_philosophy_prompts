ok so I've got to give a short talk at a seminar next month on marriage — what it's for, why the state bothers with it at all — and I want to get my own view straight before I embarrass myself in front of people who've actually read the literature. Here's roughly where I've landed:

(A) What makes a marriage a marriage is that it's a comprehensive union of two people — not just affection, not just a shared house and a joint bank account, but a union of their whole lives, bodies and sexuality included. Two best friends who live together for fifty years and care for each other till the end aren't married, however much they love each other; there's no union of that kind between them.

(B) The reason the state is in the marriage business at all is kids. Marriage gets legal recognition because a stable committed unit is the best place to raise children, and the state has an interest in that. Take children out of the picture and I honestly can't see why the state would care who lives with whom.

(C) So same-sex couples should be able to marry, obviously. They raise kids, they adopt, they have kids from earlier relationships, and their kids deserve exactly the same legal security as anyone else's.

(D) A marriage between two people who are never going to have kids and aren't raising any is a real marriage, full stop. Nobody's marriage is less of a marriage for being childless, and the state should recognize it on exactly the same terms as any other.

(E) The state shouldn't be ranking ways of life. It should recognize marriages, not grade them, and it shouldn't be in the business of declaring some intimate arrangements better than others.

(F) Two sisters who've shared a house and looked after each other for forty years aren't married and shouldn't be able to marry — but it does seem to me they should be able to get the legal package if they want it (visiting rights, inheritance, medical decisions), and marriage shouldn't be the only door to that stuff.
