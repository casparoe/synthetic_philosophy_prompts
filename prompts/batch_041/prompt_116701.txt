I have been trying to reconstruct Keynes's account of probability for a seminar I am preparing, and I keep arriving at a conclusion that strikes me as too strong to be correct. On my reading, Keynes holds that probability is a logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, and that in a great many cases — investment decisions being his recurring example — the resulting probabilities cannot be measured numerically. He also insists that when the evidential basis is thin, rational conduct falls back on convention and on animal spirits rather than on calculation. Does this not commit him to the view that whenever a probability cannot be assigned a number, rival probability judgments are equally defensible and there is no rational basis for preferring one estimate to another? And if that is right, is his emphasis on uncertainty not flatly inconsistent with the confidence he places in statistical and actuarial reasoning whenever the evidence happens to be abundant? I suspect I have misread him somewhere, but I cannot locate the mistake. What I want is a diagnosis rather than a summary: what Keynes actually claims about probabilities that cannot be measured, what that implies for the rationality of choosing among competing probability judgments, and whether the alleged inconsistency between his treatment of uncertainty and his treatment of statistical inference survives once the misreading is corrected. Please answer in American English.
