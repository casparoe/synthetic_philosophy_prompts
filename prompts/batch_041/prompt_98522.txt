Can you give me a gut-level explanation of why thought experiments in science are supposed to yield real knowledge, instead of just being vivid restatements of arguments we already had? I get the destructive ones -- if your premises contradict, something has to give, and a hypothetical makes that vivid. What I don't get is the constructive ones, where imagining a case is somehow supposed to tell us something positive about how nature actually is. Here's a little one I made up to try to pin it down: imagine two universes that are identical in every observable respect, down to every event, but in one the regularities are backed by laws and in the other they just happen to hold forever by cosmic coincidence. The thought experiment seems to deliver the verdict that these are genuinely seperate possibilities, and so that laws are something over and above the regularities. Did I actually learn something about what's possible there, or did I smuggle in the conclusion by imagining the two universes differently in the first place? And more generally, whats the actual mechanism by which a constructive thought experiment could be more than a definition in disguise -- do any of them really pull it off, or does the whole thing collapse into "it's just an argument, and the argument's key premise was empirical all along"?
