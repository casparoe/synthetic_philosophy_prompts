The case. A parliamentary democracy has the following six provisions in its constitution and standing orders:

B. There is no limit on service in the cabinet or in the appointed upper house. Ministers and appointees hold office at the prime minister's pleasure, may serve indefinitely, and former lower-house members are routinely appointed to both.
C. A Citizens' Review Panel of 120 people is drawn by stratified lot from the electoral roll for a single nine-month term. Service is paid, and no one may serve twice in a lifetime. Anyone employed in politics, government, or lobbying during the previous five years is excluded from the pool. The Panel's recommendations go to the lower house, which must vote them up or down without amendment and without committee stage.
D. Judges hold office until seventy and may be removed only for misconduct.
E. The central bank's governors serve eight-year terms, renewable once; the statute states that the length of the term is meant to insulate them from electoral pressure.
F. A person who has served on the Citizens' Review Panel may not seek or hold elected office for five years afterwards.

What puzzles me is this. We cap legislative service on the ground that long tenure in office is corrupting or insulating, and then we prize long, secure tenure as the condition of trustworthy judgment in judges and central bankers. We select the Panel by lot precisely because we want people who owe nothing and have nothing to gain, and then we bar them from carrying what they learned into ordinary politics — and exclude anyone with political experience from the pool in the first place, as though knowing how the system works were a contaminant rather than a qualification. Meanwhile the reward for a decade in the chamber is an unlimited appointment to the cabinet.
