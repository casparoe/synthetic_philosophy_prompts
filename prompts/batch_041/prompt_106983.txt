I retired a couple of years ago after thirty-odd years in behavioural ecology — birdsong, mate choice, honest-signal stuff — and I've been sitting in on philosophy seminars ever since like a man who's wandered into the wrong building and decided to stay. I'm applying for one of those one-year fellowships for people switching fields, and I need a two-page research proposal. I've got a half-formed idea I want you to turn into something real, and I'd rather you tell me honestly if it's no good.

Here's the idea. The whole debate about whether AI systems can be honest or deceptive seems to get stuck on intention. Everyone seems to agree that deceiving someone means intending to make them believe something false, and then they spend all their time arguing about whether a language model has intentions, and it goes nowhere. We never defined it that way in my old field. A cuckoo chick doesn't intend anything, and it's still the textbook case of a deceptive signal. The call is deceptive because it's built, by selection, to have the effect of misleading the receiver — that's the whole job of it. Honesty, on that view, is a property of a signalling system, not of a mind. So my hunch is that if we define AI deception the way signalling theory does — functionally, in terms of what the system is designed or disposed to bring about in the receiver — a lot of the current muddle about machine "beliefs" and "intentions" just dissolves, and the real questions get sharper.

But there's a catch I can't get past, which is what the proposal should be built around. With an animal I can point to a selection story that tells me what the signal is for; with a model there's no lineage, no fitness, no generation of cuckoos. Maybe training is the analogue of selection, maybe it isn't, and if it isn't then the analogy collapses and I've got nothing. I don't know which way that goes, and I suspect that's the actual research question.

So: write me the proposal. I want the question stated so precisely that it has a determinate answer and you can say what that answer would have to look like — not just "explore the analogy" — and I want you to show why the intent-centred accounts genuinely leave it open rather than merely disagreeing with me. Give me the method: what I'd actually do, month by month, across a year, on a fellowship with no lab and no data collection — this is a reading-and-writing project and I need to see that it's a year's worth of work and not a fortnight's. Tell me what would count as the project failing, and what the strongest version of the sceptical reply is (that the whole biological framing is a category error and I should have stayed in my lane). And be realistic about scope: say what I should cut, and give me something to show at the end that isn't just a pile of notes. Don't bother with a bibliography or reading list, I'll fill those in myself — just talk about the positions and arguments. I've got one or two typos in here I'm sure, ignore them, my hands aren't what they were.
