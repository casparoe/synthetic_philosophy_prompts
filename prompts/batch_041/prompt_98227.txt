I've been going back and forth with a mate of mine who's doing a PhD in political theory, and he keeps leaning on an analogy I cant quite knock down. The gist of it: a political community is a family. He reckons this is the deep Confucian insight — that you learn to be good first at home, as a son or a daughter, and everything larger is just that writ large. The ruler is a father to the people, the citizen owes something like filial respect, and the whole "we're autonomous individuals who consent" picture is a Western fiction; the self isn't a bare chooser sitting underneath its roles, it just *is* the son, the friend, the subject. From there he slides to a conclusion I really don't like — that it's fine for the state to treat us a bit like children: paternalism, guidance, a duty of obedience, less of this business about rights and exit. I'd rather stress-test the analogy than just splutter at him, so lay it out for me. Give me the correspondence first — what plays the role of the parent, the child, the siblings, the household economy, the bond itself — then tell me where the mapping breaks down.

What I'm really after is the stuff the family picture quietly imports that a polity doesn't obviously have: that you don't choose your parents and can't really leave them, the built-in asymmetry, the fact that love there is supposed to be non-voluntary and unconditional, that the parent is meant to be wiser, that the relationship is valued for its own sake rather than for what it delivers. Sort out which of those matter for the work he's making the analogy do and which are just scenery. Then the payoff: does the paternalist conclusion actually survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? And be fair to him — I know the same comparison gets wheeled out the other way round, to argue the state should *care* for citizens like family rather than boss them about; so say which direction, if either, the analogy genuinely supports. If there's a better analogy for what he's actually getting at, I'd like to hear it.
