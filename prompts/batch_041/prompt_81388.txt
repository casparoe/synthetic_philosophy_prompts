I am a high school student preparing a paper for our philosophy club, and I have constructed an argument that I now worry proves far too much. I would like you to diagnose it rather than simply tell me which premise is unpopular.

(1) If an agent genuinely ought to perform an action, then the agent can perform it; the principle that ought implies can is correct.
(2) Many morally urgent decisions are instances of problems in the class called NP-complete, or at least are as computationally hard as such problems. Examples include allocating scarce medical resources under many simultaneous constraints, drawing fair legislative districts, scheduling essential services, and checking a very long mathematical proof.
(3) For NP-complete problems, no efficient general algorithm is known, and the standard conjecture is that none exists. Every known exact method requires time that grows explosively as the problem gets larger.
(4) Human beings are finite agents with limited time, memory, and computational power.
(5) Therefore, for sufficiently large instances of these morally urgent problems, no human being can find an optimal solution, or even a guaranteed correct solution, within the time available.
(6) By (1), if no one can do it, no one ought to do it.
(7) So, for sufficiently large instances, there are no moral obligations to allocate resources fairly, draw districts fairly, schedule services well, or verify proofs correctly.
(8) But that conclusion is absurd. A hospital administrator who assigns patients by dartboard, or a legislature that draws districts by random lot, is not thereby beyond moral criticism.

I would rather keep (1), (2), and (4) if I can. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? If the problem is an ambiguity in can or in ought, say exactly where the ambiguity enters and how to restate the premises without making the argument trivial. If I have to give up (1), what is the best replacement, and what does that replacement cost me in other cases where the principle that ought implies can seems clearly right?
