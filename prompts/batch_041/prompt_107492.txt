Here's the practice I want to poke at. Every year the big world happiness rankings come out, and the core of them is basically this: they ask people to imagine a ladder, top rung is the best possible life they could have, bottom rung the worst, and to say which rung they're on right now. Then the country's score is just the average of everyone's rung number, over a few years to smooth it out. Nobody asks if you're happy, or whether your life is meaningful, or whether you got what you deserved -- they ask you to judge your life as a whole, then they take the mean and sort the countries.

So what does that little machine presuppose about wellbeing? Reconstruct the implicit theory for me. A few specific things I keep chewing on:

- The mean. Does averaging assume that one person's 7 is the same chunk of wellbeing as another person's 7? That's an interpersonal-comparability assumption, and I want to know how load-bearing it is -- could you run the ranking while denying it, or does the whole thing collapse?
- The ladder format. Does putting everyone on one scale presuppose that wellbeing is a single dimension, and does it quietly rule out pluralist views, or views where what matters is desire-satisfaction rather than a self-report?
- The life-as-a-whole framing. Using a reflective judgment rather than a mood measure seems to assume the reflective judgment is the authoritative one -- some kind of first-person authority. What happens if you drop that?

Then the half I actually care about: does the ranking practice presuppose that it's the right thing for a country to try to climb? Imagine a stats office decides to go for number one. What would it have to assume -- that the score is a valid proxy for what really matters, that a government may legitimately pursue aggregate satisfaction, and whose satisfaction, that gaming it wouldn't just defeat the point? People tell me a country should ignore these tables because they measure the wrong thing, and other people say chase them because they measure the right thing; I want to know what each side is assuming without saying so.

One more thing, a variant a think-tank floated that I can't find anyone discussing: instead of a plain average, weight each person's rung by how much they say they care about their country's rank, so the people who really want to win count for more. Take it seriously for a minute -- what does the weighted version presuppose that the plain mean doesn't, and is it any more or less defensible?

Keep straight what the practice strictly requires vs. what it just happens to fit, and tell me where it's underdetermined -- where several rival theories of wellbeing would all happily license the same ranking. No reading list, just the argument. And definately push back if I've set this up wrong.
