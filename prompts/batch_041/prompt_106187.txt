I'm a software engineer who runs a small podcast on computation and cognition, and I have no formal philosophy training — my day job is distributed systems, and my reading about formal systems is entirely hobbyist. Next month's guest is a researcher who has spent several years building variants of the typogenetics system from Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979). In the original, strands are sequences over four letters (A, C, G, T), a strand is translated into an enzyme via a lookup table that maps pairs of letters to a small set of operations — cutting a strand, deleting a segment, switching two segments, moving a segment, copying a strand, inserting material — and the interesting cases are strands whose translated enzymes, acting in combination, rebuild the original strand. My guest has modified both the operation set and the translation table so that the system can express, from within itself, claims about which strands are producible, and he argues that this makes typogenetics a genuine instance of the same structure that appears in Gödel's incompleteness results, and further that this is the right way to understand how DNA self-replication relates to self-reference in formal systems.

I want to interview him without simply letting him restate the analogy in fresh words. Please draft eight to ten questions, each followed by a separate follow-up line keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. The questions should press on the following, and should be ordered so that they become progressively harder to deflect: (1) what exactly is claimed to be isomorphic to what — if the claim is that there is a structure-preserving map between his typogenetic system and some formal system, then what are the objects and relations on each side, and what would count as breaking the map; (2) whether the self-replication in his system is genuine replication or a simulation of it, and what test would distinguish the two; (3) why Gödelian self-reference is the relevant comparison at all, given that his system achieves self-reference through a hand-built translation table rather than through arithmetization, and whether that difference is fatal to the analogy or merely a difference in implementation; (4) whether the meaning of a strand in his system is intrinsic to the system or assigned by us as observers, and how that bears on the strength of the claim; and (5) what the typogenetics case is supposed to tell us about minds, or about the relation between syntax and semantics, that a clever puzzle would not. Where a standard evasion exists — for instance, dismissing a worry as an implementation detail, or saying the analogy is a research program rather than a claim — the follow-up should close that escape rather than restate the original question.
