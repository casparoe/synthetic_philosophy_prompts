I am a novelist. My current book is set in a small Atlantic fishing town — invented, but assembled from a dozen real ones — and its plot turns on a single night in 1919, when a cluster of houses on the eastern spit burned and the families who lived there were resettled inland. The town remembers that night with unusual precision: the date, the names of the dead, the identity of the men who profited, and a set of quiet annual observances that have been kept for a century. The record is thinner and less useful: a fire marshal's report attributing the blaze to an overturned lamp, two letters from the cannery's agent that are scrupulous in saying nothing, and a land transfer that went through with improbable speed. I have drafted a short essay to run as a coda to the novel, and I would like your comments on it. Here is the draft.

The usual advice given to a novelist who works from a real place is to subordinate memory to the archive: memory is soft, interested, and endlessly revised, while the archive is hard, indifferent, and fixed. I no longer believe the archive deserves that deference, and I want to say why without pretending the alternative is simple. The archive is not the past; it is the residue of the past that someone with an interest in keeping paper chose to keep. To treat what survives as the neutral baseline against which memory is measured is to mistake a sample for a population — and a sample selected, in every case that matters to me, by the party with the most to gain from the selection.

Memory, meanwhile, is not a rival archive. It is a practice, and its claims are of two kinds that are usually run together. It claims that certain things happened; it also claims that the community which remembers them is the kind of community that would have been wronged in that way and would have borne it as it did. The second claim is not a proposition about 1919 that could be checked and found false. The first is. Much confusion follows from answering the first question with the authority that belongs only to the second.

When the two conflict, I want to defend a limited asymmetry: the burden of proof should fall on the one who proposes to correct the community, not on the community itself. Two reasons. First, the costs of correction are borne by the corrected while the benefits — a cleaner record, a more defensible history — accrue largely to the corrector, so a symmetric standard quietly subsidises the outsider. Second, the silences of the record are themselves evidence: the letters that say nothing, the transfer that moved too fast, are not gaps in the neutral sense but the trace of a decision to leave no trace. To read them as merely insufficient is to let the absence do the work of the presence.

I do not want the asymmetry to be absolute, and here the essay gets harder. Memory can be self-serving; a community can need a villain as much as a family can need an alibi; and the very features that make memory durable — repetition, ritual, communal rehearsal — are the features that let a later generation's needs settle into what feels like the plain shape of the event. So I try to state the conditions under which the record should win: when the correction costs the community nothing it is entitled to keep; when the record's silence can be explained without appeal to suppression; and when the memory's stability can be shown to be a product of the telling rather than of the event. Whether those conditions are ever jointly satisfied I leave open.

The objection I cannot shake is that this is a licence for convenient falsehood dressed as humility. My reply is that the asymmetry I defend is an asymmetry of burden and not of truth — it tells us who must argue, not who must win — but I am aware that in practice the two are rarely kept apart.

I would like comments of three kinds. First, on accuracy: are the distinctions I draw sound, and do I anywhere confuse a claim about what the evidence shows with a claim about who is entitled to speak? Second, on interest: is this more than a restatement of a familiar position, and where does the argument go slack? Third, on the crux: the whole essay rests on the claim that the burden of proof should lie with the corrector. Tell me whether that survives the objection in my last paragraph, and if it does not, identify the weakest joint and what would have to change to repair it. Please also state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point in your response that you are least sure of.
