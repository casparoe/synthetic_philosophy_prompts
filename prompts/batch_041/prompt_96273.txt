I am a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind, and I am working up a seminar paper on the extended mind thesis. A postdoctoral colleague in my department, who is broadly sympathetic to the view, offered me the following thumbnail when I asked what the thesis comes to:

'Clark and Chalmers's extended mind thesis is the claim that the mind is not confined to the brain. The engine of the argument is the parity principle: if a process carried out beyond the skin does the kind of job that, performed inside the head, we would not think twice about calling cognitive, then it is a cognitive process. Their illustration is Otto, an Alzheimer's patient who writes everything down and consults his notebook automatically and without hesitation; the notebook plays the role that biological memory plays in the rest of us, so Otto's memory literally includes it. The moral generalises: cognition is spread across whatever an agent happens to be coupled with, and the boundary between mind and world is accordingly arbitrary. A notebook, a smartphone, or a search engine can be part of your mind just as much as a neuron can. That is the real force of the thesis — not merely that such things help us think, but that they are part of the thinking.'

I would like to know how much of this I can take at face value. Please work through it claim by claim and tell me, for each, whether a philosopher working in this area would accept it as stated, treat it as a harmless simplification, or reject it as a misrepresentation — and, where the sketch has quietly run together two claims that the position keeps apart, or asserted as a consequence of the view something its defenders would disown, say so explicitly and explain what turns on the difference. A one-line verdict on the sketch as a whole would also help.

Two further things, from the same exercise. First, the sketch is silent on what distinguishes Otto's notebook from the notebook in which I jot a shopping list, and I suspect that silence is carrying a good deal of weight; tell me what constraints the original argument places on the extension, and whether the sketch's formulation can be repaired without abandoning the thesis. Second, I am trying to use the view in a section on shared or institutional memory — a couple's joint calendar, a laboratory's communal notebook — and I want to know whether the sketch's criterion, applied consistently, would make one such resource part of several minds at once, and whether that would be a genuine implication of the extended mind thesis or a reductio of the sketch's way of putting it.
