I teach a 75-minute session of an upper-level undergrad seminar on Wittgenstein, about 14 students, and I want an in-class activity that makes them actually feel two things rather than hear me announce them: that a word's meaning is its use inside a shared activity, and that a category like "game" holds together by criss-crossing similarities with no common core. I'd much rather they walk into both themselves before I name either one.

Rough shape I have in mind, change it if you can do better: first they sort a deck of description cards (practices and activities, some obviously games, some clearly not, plenty of borderline ones) into game / not-game, and each pair has to write down the single criterion they used — the criteria should visibly collide when we compare them. Then in the second half they have to teach a partner to use an invented word by using it inside a shared task, no definitions and no "this is what it means" pointing at a sample, and a third student who only watched the training tries to work out what the word picks out. I want that second part to make them feel that the outsider can't recover the rule from watching, because nothing like an inner definition is being handed over, just a practice they were trained into.

Give me the rules, materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, the two or three things that'll predictably go wrong the first time (and how to salvage them), and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the ideas. Be honest in the debrief about which feature of the ideas the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply myself, and seperate what's actually settled about these ideas from what's genuinely contested among people who know Wittgenstein well — don't hand my students a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
