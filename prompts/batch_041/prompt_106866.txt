I'm a graduate student in early modern philosophy of science, and I've spent the last several months working through Kepler's astrological writings — not the planetary laws, the astrology — and I've come to hold a view I'll call Kepler's reformed astrology. Roughly: celestial configurations have a real, non-arbitrary significance, but that significance is grounded in harmonic and geometric ratios rather than in physical causation; the planets do not push on bodies, they act on the soul, which is innately structured to respond to harmonic proportion, so that a configuration functions as a sign rather than a cause. On this view the rational core of astrology is the aspects — the angular relations among the planets — while the twelve houses, the twelve signs of the zodiac, and the detailed prediction of particular events are conventional or superstitious accretions that a reformer ought to discard. I find this attractive partly because of a line I came across in the Third-Party Intervention, where Kepler writes that "the geometry or harmony of aspects is not between the stars in the sky, but is located rather down here on earth in the point that collects all these rays" — it seems to me to relocate the whole business from the heavens into the soul in a way that might survive the collapse of the traditional cosmos.

I'd like you to act as a historian and philosopher of early modern science — someone who knows Kepler's corpus and also the modern philosophy of science — and to think carefully before answering. Set the position out in your own words first, briefly, so I can see you've got it, and then tell me what would count against it. I don't want a general essay on whether astrology is bunk; I want a map of this particular position's exposure. Which observations, arguments, intuitions, or formal results should move me, and which would count decisively as opposed to merely awkward? I'm especially interested in the internal stresses: whether the line between aspects and houses can actually be held (both seem to lean on conventions — why should the soul be responsive to angular ratios but not to the division of the sky into twelve?), whether "significative rather than causal" does any real work or merely insulates the view from testing, and how much of the position's credibility rests on doctrines about the soul and about harmony that I'd have to accept separately. Tell me honestly what Kepler's own defenders would concede as counting against the view, not only what critics would say, and whether the position risks anything at all — whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, whether that is a defect here or simply the price of a view of this kind. For what it's worth, I'd personally be most moved by a demonstration that the aspects are just as conventional as the houses, so if that argument can be made, make it; if it can't be made, say so plainly.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long letter to a student — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no numbered lists, no tables — and in American English.
