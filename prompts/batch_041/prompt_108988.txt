I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on the foundations of rational choice under risk, and before we assess any particular model I want a specification of what a model of this kind must deliver. Treat the target as a normative theory of rational risk attitude: it is meant to tell us which preferences over risky prospects are rationally defensible, not to predict how people in fact choose. Write me the desiderata, with the consideration that motivates each, organised under four headings: the choice patterns and cases the theory has to get right; the distinctions it must not collapse; the formal constraints on the preference relation; and the arguments or results it must not contradict.

I want at least the following on the list, each paired with the case or argument that puts it there. First, the Allais-type patterns, and what they show about the sure-thing principle. Second, stochastic dominance, and the minimal thought that a prospect better in every state should be preferred. Third, the reduction of compound lotteries, and whether a rational agent must be indifferent to how a risk is staged. Fourth, the distinction between risk aversion and diminishing marginal utility of money, and whether the former can be an attitude in its own right rather than a byproduct of the latter. Fifth, the fact that one and the same agent may buy insurance and buy lottery tickets. Sixth, a constraint I care about more than the standard axioms: that the theory not let modest and plausible small-stakes risk aversion inflate into absurd large-stakes behaviour — the calibration argument that an expected-utility maximiser who turns down a small fifty-fifty bet with a slight edge is thereby committed to turning down some large fifty-fifty bet with an enormous edge. Seventh, dynamic consistency, together with the possibility that a risk-averse agent might rationally wish to tie her own future hands. Eighth, the treatment of the St. Petersburg-style case with unbounded payoffs. Ninth, whether the theory may declare ambiguity, in the sense of unknown probabilities, out of scope, or is required to extend to it. And tenth, a distinction of my own that I suspect the usual lists ignore: between aversion to a mean-preserving spread of a prospect one already holds and the simpler preference for a sure thing over a lottery of equal or greater expected value; I want to know whether these can come apart in a rational agent, and if they can, which of the two the term risk aversion ought to name.

For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a commitment of one camp; I suspect that the sure-thing principle and the reduction of compound lotteries are camp preferences rather than genuine demands, and I would like to be told if that is right. Flag any pair, or larger set, of the requirements that cannot be satisfied together, and say which single requirement, if retained, does the most to rule out the popular models. Finally, briefly: how do classical expected utility theory and the risk-weighted expected utility model fare against the list, and does any third contender do better on the requirements you have marked non-negotiable? Keep the weight on the requirements and their motivating cases; the scorecard is secondary.
