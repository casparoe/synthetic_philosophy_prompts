I am preparing a graduate seminar on the epistemology of mathematics, and I have agreed to vet a draft chapter on Benacerraf's dilemma for a handbook aimed at strong undergraduates in philosophy and mathematics. The author is a colleague from the mathematics department — careful, widely read, but not a specialist in this area — and I would like an audit of his central section before the chapter goes to press. I reproduce it below as it stands.

"The dilemma has two parts. The first is the identification problem. There is no fact of the matter whether the number 3 is the set {∅, {∅}, {∅, {∅}}} or the set {{{∅}}}, since both set-theoretic constructions reproduce arithmetic perfectly; so there is nothing that the number 3 really is, and platonism about numbers collapses. The second part is the epistemological problem. Benacerraf asks us to hold two requirements together. Our semantics for mathematical statements must be continuous with our semantics for ordinary statements about middle-sized objects: 'there are infinitely many primes' has to be true in the same sense, and for the same kind of reason, as 'there are infinitely many chairs' — it has to be made true by the objects it talks about. And our account of mathematical knowledge must be continuous with a general theory of knowledge, which for Benacerraf means the causal theory: to know something is to stand in the right causal relation to it. Platonism can meet the first requirement but not the second, because numbers are causally inert and so can never enter into the causal chains that produce knowledge. Any account that meets the second — say, one that identifies mathematical truth with provability from axioms — meets it only by giving up the first, since on such an account mathematical statements are not made true by any objects at all. So no interpretation of mathematics satisfies both requirements, and platonism is refuted. The moral, which Benacerraf draws explicitly, is that mathematical statements do not describe a realm of objects: they are instruments for organising our experience, and the apparent reference to numbers is a mere manner of speaking. Two replies are worth noting. First, the causal requirement proves far too much, since we also cannot causally interact with the past or with quarks, and we do not conclude that history and particle physics are impossible. Second, structuralism dissolves the dilemma: if mathematics is about structures rather than objects, there are no causally inert objects to be known in the first place."
