I am grading the mid-term scripts for a second-year course on causal inference in the social sciences and would like a detailed second opinion on one answer. The question is worth 20 marks, 12 for part (a) and 8 for part (b). Please give the script a mark out of 20, split between the two parts, and then a point-by-point commentary: for each part, say what the answer gets right; identify the sentence or sentences carrying any error and explain why the step fails; say what a full-mark answer would have said instead; note anything important that is missing; and state how many marks you would withhold for each defect and why. Please flag any point on which your judgement is arguable, since I have to justify the mark to the student.

Question

In 2019 a university department compared two revision programmes for its introductory statistics module. Programme A ran weekly small-group tutorials. Programme B was a set of online problem sets with automated feedback. Students were allocated to programmes by the departmental office, not at random. The table gives pass rates for the two programmes, broken down by whether the student was sitting the module for the first time or retaking it after a previous failure.

- First-time sitters: A 150/200 (75%); B 70/100 (70%)
- Retakers: A 35/100 (35%); B 3/10 (30%)
- All students: A 185/300 (61.7%); B 73/110 (66.4%)

(a) Explain how the pattern in the table arises. What must be assumed about the breakdown variable if the within-stratum comparison is to be read causally? What, if anything, does the unstratified comparison tell us?

(b) The comparison was repeated the following year. On this occasion the only breakdown available was whether the student had completed the optional online workbook. Programme A had actively encouraged the workbook; Programme B had not. Among students who completed the workbook, the two programmes had almost identical pass rates, and the same was true among those who did not. Advise the department on what may and may not be concluded.

Student's answer

(a) Both stratified comparisons favour Programme A: 75% against 70% among first-time sitters, and 35% against 30% among retakers, so A is five points ahead in each stratum. The overall figures run the other way, 61.7% for A against 66.4% for B. This is Simpson's paradox: the direction of the association between programme and outcome depends on whether we look at the marginal or the conditional table. The reversal is entirely a matter of composition. Retakers are much less likely to pass, and Programme A took almost all of them (100 of its 300 students, against 10 of B's 110), while B's intake was almost entirely first-time sitters. The decomposition makes this explicit: the composition term is about −9.7 percentage points and the programme term about +5 points, which together give the −4.7 points actually observed.

Since the difference in the marginal table is an artefact of how students were allocated, it is spurious, and the conditional comparison is the one that carries causal information. Simpson's paradox is at bottom just confounding: the breakdown variable is a common cause of the allocation and of the outcome, so conditioning on it closes the back-door path. In general the rule is to adjust for any variable that is associated with both the treatment and the outcome; once all such variables have been adjusted for, whatever association remains is the causal effect. Prior attainment is the obvious such variable here, and since it is recorded for everyone, the within-stratum comparison gives us the causal effect of A over B, which is about five percentage points.

The unstratified comparison has no causal interpretation at all. It records only what happened to the two groups as they were constituted, and since the groups were not comparable to begin with, the figure cannot be used for anything.

(b) The following year's comparison is the same situation, so the same rule applies: we should adjust for workbook completion, since that is the variable that differs between the two groups. Once we condition on it, the difference between the programmes disappears in both strata, so the apparent advantage of A was again a matter of composition rather than of anything the programme itself did. Programme A's students were more likely to complete the workbook, and completion is associated with passing, so A looked better without being better. The department should conclude that the two programmes are equivalent, and that it can economise by using B so long as workbook completion rates can be kept up.
