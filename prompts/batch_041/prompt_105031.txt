My friend is a philosophy grad student and he keeps summarizing Black's two-spheres thought experiment like this: 'Leibniz's Law says that if two things are indiscernible then they're identical. Black's spheres show this is false. Imagine a universe with nothing in it but two iron spheres, same size, same mass, same color, same temperature, arranged perfectly symmetrically. They're two distinct objects but they have all the same properties, and no possible observation could tell them apart. So identity has to be a bare fact over and above the properties, and Leibniz's Law is refuted. It also proves space has to be absolute, because if space were just relations between things, there'd be no difference between the left sphere and the right one. And it's basically the same point as the principle of sufficient reason, since God would have no reason to put one sphere on the left rather than the right.' I think he's onto something, but some of this sounds off. Go sentence by sentence: what would a metaphysician who works on this actually accept, what would they wince at, and what's just wrong? And do the absolute-space and sufficient-reason conclusions follow from anything true in what he said, or are those separate issues?
