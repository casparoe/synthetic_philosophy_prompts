I've been going around in circles in a reading group about mechanical rigidity — the thing people have in mind when they say an animal or a machine is just running a script — and I'd rather have a principle than another round of examples. Let me put down the cases I have verdicts about. A digger wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold while she goes in to inspect, and then pulls it down; if you nudge the cricket a few inches away while she is inside, she comes back out, drags it to the threshold again, and goes in to check again, and you can keep her at it as long as you keep moving the cricket. That is the clearest case of the rigid thing I know, and I want to say she is sphexish through and through — though I have a vague memory that the story is not quite as clean as the telling of it, and that if you disturb things more aggressively she simply goes off and finds another cricket, which makes me wonder whether the rigidity is in her or in the way I have described her. A thermostat cycling a furnace is more fixed still, but I resist calling it rigid in the same sense: it seems wrong to say the thermostat is in the grip of a routine, as if there were anything it was like to be stuck. My commute, which I drive on autopilot, is not rigid at all — if the road is closed I take another one without breaking stride. A colleague who checks the deadbolt thirty times a night, knows perfectly well that it is locked, and cannot stop himself seems rigid in a way much closer to the wasp than to my commute, even though he is aware of what he is doing and the wasp presumably is not. A chess engine that opens with the same line every game does not feel rigid to me in the slightest, and neither does a monomaniac who will change his job, his city, and his friends to get one promotion, though those two are flexible in such different ways that I suspect I am using one word for two things. The case I keep going back and forth on is a woman I know whose honesty is so well established that in twenty years I have never once seen her deliberate about whether to shade the truth; she just tells it. That looks like a groove worn smooth, and it also looks like the opposite of a groove. The half-formulated principle I keep saying to myself is that rigidity is a matter of whether the agent would have done otherwise had the world been relevantly different, but that seems to make the wasp flexible, since she does track where the cricket is, and to make the engine rigid, since it would respond to any variation of the position — so I assume it is the wrong principle, and I would like to know what the right one is.

What I want from you is a principle, or two or three competing principles stated precisely enough to disagree with each other, that captures these verdicts: each clause of the principle traced back to the case that forces it, your own verdicts on cases I have not raised, and a frank account of which of my verdicts the principle cannot save and what it costs me to give that verdict up. Three things I care about especially. First, does your principle make rigidity a feature of the shape of the behavior, of the mechanism that produces it, or of the agent's capacity to have done otherwise, and which of those readings actually fits the cases rather than just sounding right? Second, the wasp's case is supposed to bear on free will, so tell me what your principle implies about her — whether she is unfree in the way that matters, or merely unfree in a way that has been confused with it — and then whether extending the principle to me leaves most of my ordinary day sphexish, and if so whether that is a threat to my freedom or only a description of it. Third, and this is the one I would most like answered plainly: is the honest woman a wasp, an engine, or something the vocabulary was not built for? Please write in American English.
