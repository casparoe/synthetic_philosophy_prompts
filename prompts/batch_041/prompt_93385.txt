I'm writing the summer paper for our final-year decision theory module and I want one long question on risk-weighted expected utility — Buchak's version — worth 30 marks, roughly 45 minutes of work. The trouble is that every version of this question I've set before gets answered with a recital of the Allais paradox, so this year I want the whole thing pinned to a concrete set-up the students have to actually work through, and I'd like you to draft the question itself, a model answer of about 700–800 words, and a marking scheme.

Here's the set-up I want it built round. Take the RW EU representation: for a gamble whose outcomes are x₁ ≤ x₂ ≤ … ≤ xₙ with probabilities p₁, …, pₙ, with a utility function u and a risk function r (where r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1), the risk-weighted value is

u(x₁) + Σᵢ₌₂…ₙ [u(xᵢ) − u(xᵢ₋₁)] · r(pᵢ + pᵢ₊₁ + … + pₙ).

Use linear utility, u(x) = x, so that nothing can hide in the curvature of u, and hand them r(p) = 2p − p².

Part (a) should be a straight calculation. Gamble A pays £100 with probability 0.6 and nothing otherwise; gamble B pays £60 for sure. Have them compute both risk-weighted values, say which is preferred, and work out A's expected value — the point being that this agent takes a mean-preserving gamble over its certain equivalent.

Part (b) is the diagnosis. r is concave, and I want the question to make them confront the fact that this on its own tells you nothing about whether the agent is risk-averse, and to force out the correct condition in terms of r and the 45° line. They should be able to get it from a two-outcome gamble and then say why it holds generally. I want the standard mistake — "concave means risk-averse" — to be the thing this part is fishing for.

Part (c) is the real crux and I want it genuinely in the weeds. Introduce a rival model that weights each branch by r applied to that branch's own probability, then renormalises the weights so they sum to one (call it whatever you like). Have them run the branch-splitting test: compare the gamble "£100 with probability 0.5, else £0" against the very same lottery written with the £100 branch split into two branches of probability 0.25 each. The rival model gives the two different values, so it violates stochastic dominance, since the two lotteries are the same distribution; the RW EU model gives the same value for both, and they should say why the cumulative form is what buys that. Then push them further: does passing the dominance test actually vindicate RW EU, or does it show that the "risk function" is really a probability weighting in disguise, so that the promised separation of beliefs from attitudes to risk is doing less work than advertised? I want a real answer to that, not a fence-sit.

For the marking scheme: out of 30, broken into must-hit points with the marks for each, plus a one-liner for each point on how candidates typically drop it. Keep the numbers exact so it's actually usable at the marking table. And make sure the question can't be answered by summarising — if someone could score full marks without doing the calculations in (a) and (c), it's the wrong question, and I'd rather you rewrite it than leave that hole in.
