I have just taken over as the history editor at an educational publisher, with final sign-off on our upper-secondary world-history titles, and I need a decision procedure for a problem that has become mine rather than merely academic. In a single term, three chapters have been formally challenged: the one on the Trojan War, where a reviewer says our phrasing concedes more than the evidence will bear; the one on medieval Britain, where a teacher objects that we present Arthur as though he were a documented king; and the one on ancient Israel, where a parent and a member of our editorial board have complained in opposite directions. My authors are good teachers, not specialists in the ancient world, none of them can tell me how to decide, claim by claim, what a textbook is entitled to assert, and I have weeks rather than months and no budget for a specialist on every chapter. It has also stopped being abstract for me personally, since the same book is the one my own child brings home.

What I want is a procedure my authors and I can actually run on a manuscript before it goes to the printer. For any narrative in a school text it should return a verdict on each separable part of that narrative — not on the story as a whole, since a story is rarely all of one piece — placing each part in one of four categories: we may present it as history; as history with acknowledged uncertainty; as a legend that may have a historical kernel; or as a legend making no historical claim. I want the questions asked in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a verdict, and where it must be handed upward — to me, to a scholarly reviewer, or to the board — because what is at issue is a community's identity or a political judgement rather than a question of evidence. Bear in mind that our books are adopted by state boards, so a wrong call has consequences well beyond the page.

The procedure must make operational several distinctions that I can state but cannot myself turn into tests: between an event having occurred and a narrative about it being accurate; between a tradition functioning as testimony and material remains functioning as evidence of a different kind; between the absence of evidence and the evidence of absence; and between a story that preserves no accurate detail and a story that preserves the fact that a community organised its identity around it. It should be usable by an author who cites no scholarship in the text itself, and it should be honest about where it will misfire.

Then run it on the three chapters — the Trojan War, Arthur, and the Exodus — and on one case of a different shape: a modern nation's founding story that professional historians have quietly revised but that schools still teach as fact. Include at least one case where your procedure returns a verdict I will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot settle.
