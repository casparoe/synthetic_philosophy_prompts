A county near me recently started using a single risk score, with a single cutoff, to decide two different things: whether someone is held in jail before trial, and whether someone gets one of a small number of slots in an intensive support program. The company that sells the tool calls it fair because it doesn't use race as an input and because a given score means the same thing no matter which group you're in. A public defender I know says it can't be fair, because at that same cutoff a much larger share of one group gets flagged as likely to reoffend when they wouldn't have, and those people sit in jail. Both of them are talking about fairness, and neither seems confused about the facts. I'd like you to work out what theory of fairness this whole arrangement assumes — including the part where one score and one cutoff are made to serve two decisions with very different stakes — and then tell me how much of that theory the practice actually needs. Which parts are strictly required, which other theories of fairness would equally well explain why the county does what it does, and what would have to change if it adopted a different one? Please answer in American English.
