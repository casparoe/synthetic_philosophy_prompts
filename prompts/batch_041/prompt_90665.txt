In a meeting last week a colleague proposed a study of whether a language model's reports about its own knowledge are calibrated: whether the questions it says it is confident about are in fact the ones it answers correctly. The proposal was dismissed with a sentence I have now heard used to close several such conversations — 'It's just next-token prediction' — the implication being that there is nothing there to calibrate.

I want a careful inventory of what that sentence can be taken to claim. Please set out the distinct precise claims it could be making, ordered from the nearly trivial to the radical, and for each say whether it is true, whether it is even the sort of claim that admits of truth and falsity, and whether it would actually support the conclusion that the proposed study is confused. Three classes interest me in particular: readings on which the sentence is true but has no bearing on the dispute; readings on which it does real work and is therefore contestable; and any reading on which the training objective itself rules out the states the study would be trying to measure.

The same sentence has been used in my hearing to argue that the model cannot be mistaken: that when it asserts something false it is not in error but merely unrepresentative of its training distribution. If one of your readings secures that conclusion, say which; if none does, say where the inference breaks. I would also like to know what work the word 'just' is doing, since the force of the objection seems to sit there rather than in the rest of the sentence.

Lay the readings out as a list I can circulate before the next meeting, with a verdict attached to each.
