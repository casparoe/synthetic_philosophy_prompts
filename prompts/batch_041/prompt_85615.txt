I write a column about ideas — philosophy, ethics, the arguments people use on one another — and I have spent years reporting on the debates over humanitarian intervention and regime change. In those debates one analogy does an enormous amount of work and almost never gets examined. It is the Pottery Barn rule: "You break it, you own it," the formulation that Bob Woodward reported Colin Powell and Richard Armitage used privately as they weighed what toppling Saddam Hussein would commit the United States to, and that has been pressed into service in nearly every intervention argument since, from the claims about whether the powers that intervened in Libya in 2011 thereby acquired responsibility for the collapse that followed, to the retrospective verdicts on Kosovo.

The analogy is compact, and it is being asked to license a specific conclusion: that whoever destroys a regime inherits an obligation to stay, to rebuild, to pay, and to answer for what comes next. I want an audit of it rather than another opinion about intervention.

Begin with the correspondence, made explicit — a table is fine. What plays the role of the shopper, the vase, the store, the shopkeeper, the price, and the act of paying? Then tell me where the mapping fails, and, crucially, which failures matter for the conclusion the analogy is used to support and which are mere decoration. A few pressures occur to me, though I don't want to rig the exercise:

- the vase is inert; a state is made of people with purposes of their own, and the breaking of a regime is not a single event but a process that continues, and can accelerate, after the breaker has left;
- there is no shopkeeper and no price list: the debt is open-ended, denominated in lives, money and legitimacy, and payable over decades by a public that never agreed to the purchase;
- in the store, owning is a transfer of title with defined rights; owning a broken state confers liabilities without rights, and the people inside it did not consent to being owned;
- the rule presupposes a determinate breaker, but interventions are coalitions whose membership, and whose governing parties, keep changing;
- the vase was already cracked, and the shopper did not back into it by accident;
- and the store rule is about having caused the damage, not about whether the world is better or worse for it. The whole counterfactual question — would these people have been better off if we had never come? — is bracketed by the analogy.

Then the question I actually care about: does the conclusion — that the intervener owes a long-term commitment — survive once those disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy quietly carrying the conclusion the whole way? Bear in mind that the rule cuts both ways: the same maxim tells a power that cannot afford to own the broken thing not to break it, which is the anti-intervention reading. And if you think a better analogy is available — bankruptcy receivership, a trustee's fiduciary duty, a surgeon who may not abandon a patient mid-operation, a parent — say which one, and whether it escapes the disanalogies or merely relocates them.
