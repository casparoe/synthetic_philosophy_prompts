I have recently changed my mind about the family of cases usually gathered under Pascal's mugging, and I would like the position I now hold stress-tested rather than explained back to me.

Some context for why this has stopped being academic. A few months ago I was one of three people deciding whether a small organization I belong to should commit a substantial share of its reserves to a proposal whose entire case rested on a very small chance of a very large benefit. I reasoned in the way I am about to describe, we declined, and I have been uneasy ever since — not because I think we decided wrongly, but because I cannot say what would have shown the reasoning to be wrong.

The position. I take expected-utility maximization to be the correct criterion for ordinary decisions, and I do not want to give it up. What I now deny is that it applies uniformly across all probabilities. Below some scale — I cannot say exactly where, and I suspect the exact location does not matter — I hold that a branch's contribution to expected value should be treated as nil, however the payoff is described, so that the decision is made as though the branch were absent; above that scale, ordinary expected-utility reasoning governs. I also hold that this is not merely a pragmatic concession to our computational limits. My thought is that a credence of the order of one in a very large number, held toward a specific and elaborate scenario, is not the kind of attitude that can be multiplied by a payoff at all: it is bookkeeping that we mistake for belief, and the mistake stays invisible until the payoff is astronomical. I call this the negligibility floor.

I want to know how exposed this is. Please do not give me a survey of the literature or a catalogue of famous replies; I want the position's exposure map.

1. What would count against it — which arguments, intuitions, formal results, or cases — and of those, which should be decisive and which merely awkward? Rank them by how much they should move me, and say plainly where you think the position actually breaks rather than merely creaks.

2. Which of these would a defender of my position concede as genuine costs, and which would they dismiss — and are the dismissals legitimate? I am more interested in what the view has to give up than in what its critics say about it.

3. What does the position predict or forbid that its rivals do not? The rivals I have in mind are bounding the utility function, and abandoning maximization for something like a maximin or precautionary rule. If my position and theirs agree on every case I can actually describe, I would rather hear that than a fabricated contrast.

4. Is there a version of the position that risks nothing at all — qualified until no case could count against it? If so, is that a defect of the position, or a consequence of its being a claim about how to deliberate rather than about what is worth wanting?

5. Two pressures I cannot classify, and which I would especially like you to sort into fatal, awkward, or harmless. The first is that the floor makes my decisions discontinuous in my credences: a credence of one in an enormous number and a credence of one in a merely very large number are treated alike until a boundary is crossed, and I cannot say why the boundary sits where it does — nor, worse, why a mugger who simply raises the probability just above it does not reappear with his threat intact, which would make my position a cousin of the bounded-utility view I rejected as arbitrary. The second is that whether a branch falls below the floor seems to depend on how I carve up the possibilities rather than on the world or on my credences. Here is the case that made me notice it: suppose there are a trillion independent situations, each carrying a one-in-a-trillion chance of an enormous harm. The chance that at least one occurs is near certainty, and by my own lights that near-certain harm should weigh heavily; but each situation, taken on its own, sits below the floor, and my position tells me to ignore each. I can save the view by applying the floor to the total chance of an outcome rather than to the chance contributed by each branch — but then the advice I give depends on how finely the world is described, which is not something the world or my credences settle. I suspect these two pressures are one problem wearing two faces, but I cannot see the face they share.

If it turns out that the position is well insulated and that this is a defect, tell me so; I would rather have that than reassurance.
