I teach a 300-level social choice / judgment aggregation class and I'm putting together the final. Every year my discursive-dilemma question gets the same canned answer — the three-person P, Q, "P and Q" example, majority on each proposition, contradiction, then a stock paragraph on premise-based versus conclusion-based voting — and I'm bored of grading it. I want ONE essay question (60 min, closed book, no notes), a model answer, and a marking scheme. Please make the question self-contained: everything it needs has to be spelled out in the question itself, and the model answer shouldn't lean on any reading or citation (my students can't look stuff up anyway).

Here's the scenario I want the question built around. A five-person tenure committe votes on three propositions:

P: the candidate's research meets our bar.
Q: the candidate's teaching meets our bar.
R: the candidate should be tenured.

By the committee's own rules, R holds exactly when P and Q both hold (nobody gets tenure who fails either bar). The votes:

1: P yes, Q yes, R yes
2: P yes, Q no, R no
3: P no, Q yes, R no
4: P yes, Q yes, R yes
5: P no, Q no, R no

I also want to drop in a rule I invented so they can't pattern-match it. Call it the chair's sequence: first take a majority vote on P; if P fails, R fails. If P passes, then take a majority vote on Q among only the members who voted P yes; if that sub-majority passes Q, R passes, otherwise R fails. (The chair sells it as a neutral compromise between premise- and conclusion-based voting. It isn't.)

The question should make them: (a) work out the plain majority result on each proposition and say exactly what's wrong with it; (b) give the committee's decision under premise-based voting, conclusion-based voting, and the chair's sequence; (c) assess the chair's rule — is it order-dependent, i.e. does it matter whether you start with P or with Q? Have them either produce a profile where the two orders give different decisions or argue that it can't happen, and say which standard properties the rule keeps and which it breaks; and (d) pick a procedure and defend it against the strongest objection to it. I want the crux to be that the procedure choice by itself flips who gets tenure, so "what the committee decided" is partly a fact about the rule and not only about the votes.

Write the question, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific moves that should cost marks. For part (c), be careful and honest about whether such a profile actually exists — I dont want to set a task that can't be done.
