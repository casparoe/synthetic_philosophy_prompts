I'm about two thirds through a novel set in a market town in the English midlands at the end of the eighteenth century, and I've hit a wall that isn't really a plot problem. My protagonist is a servant, then a labourer's wife, then a widow, and she leaves almost nothing behind. The only places she surfaces in the record are a handful of parish examinations — the ones where an overseer writes down what someone said in order to fix which parish has to pay for them — and a coroner's inquest into the death of a child. In both cases its her speech, but in the clerk's spelling, the clerk's grammar, and the clerk's idea of what was worth writing down. And here's the thing I keep circling: every so often a detail survives the translation that doesn't belong to the clerk at all. A named dog. A complaint about a particular hat. A grudge against a specific neighbour, recorded for no legal reason whatsoever. Those scraps are the only sentences in the whole file I'm confident came from her, and I don't know what to do with them. A friend of mine who's a social historian says use them and stop agonising, and another friend says the opposite — that inventing an interior life for someone who never got to write one down is a second act of taking, dressed up as sympathy. (My own comeback, which I doubt survives contact with a real argument, is that the historian's context is a story too, just one with footnotes.)

I've been reading around it and I keep landing on Saidiya Hartman, who proposed what she calls critical fabulation: when the archive gives you a person only as a scrap, you write the story anyway, but you write it as a story that can't be told as history, keeping the seams and the impossibility visible rather than pretending you've recovered anybody. I like it, but before I decide anything I've been trying to sort the silences into seperate kinds. Roughly: silences of destruction (nobody wrote it down, or somebody made sure it got burned), silences of classification (she's in the file, but only as a category that isn't hers — a pauper, a nuisance, a body), and silences of collision (she appears only at the moment she touched the state: the examination, the inquest, the removal order). My suspicion is that the right response is different for each kind, and that alot of the shouting about archival silence is people answering one kind with a remedy built for another. Underneath all of it there's a worry I can't shake: if I make her vivid and particular, am I quietly saying the other nine names on that page don't matter? Any single story I tell is a choice, and the choice is a kind of verdict on everyone I didn't choose.

So what I want is an essay that takes a position. Not a survey of the debate — I've definately read enough surveys. Weigh the case for reading against the grain, the case for refusing to fill the silence at all, and the fabulation route, then commit to one. And I want the verdict to actually answer the hard part, which is what the test of legitimacy is: whether it happened, or fidelity to her, or what the story does to the person reading it. My hunch is that the answer isn't "never invent" but something closer to "you may invent, but only in a way that keeps the seams showing and that's addressed to her rather than to the reader's appetite for her voice" — push on that, and if it turns out to be a cop-out, say so and give me the better position instead, including on the nine names I didn't pick. One request about the shape of it: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous prose, the way you'd write a long letter. Put the verdict in the first paragraph and then earn it, rather than making me wait until the end.
