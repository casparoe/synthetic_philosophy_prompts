I run a small press that reprints local history and family papers, and I keep having to decide what a text means — for a footnote, a caption, a title on a spine — and my decisions do not hang together. I have no philosophical training, and I do not want a reading list or the names of scholars who have written about this; I want the rule, or the smallest set of rules, that actually produces my verdicts, and an honest account of the verdicts it cannot save.

Here are the verdicts, as plainly as I can put them.

My grandmother's poem was printed in a church booklet in 1961 with a printer's error: the line reads 'we counted the goats coming home', while her manuscript says 'boats'. I reprint 'boats' and consider 'goats' simply not part of the poem. Yet in a later note she said she had come to prefer the goats, and that the poem had always secretly been about them; on that basis I have reprinted it as 'goats' in a new edition, and I think that is right too — the poem changed when she took the error up rather than corrected it.

In a letter from the 1890s, my great-great-uncle asks his brother to 'send the boy with the corn'. In that valley 'corn' meant oats; he had spent two years in the city and, as his diary makes clear, he meant maize. I still say the letter asks for oats.

A songwriter I published insists that his line about never having learned to swim is about his brother's drowning. Every reader I have asked takes it as being about cowardice, and I print the cowardice reading; I think his gloss is either wrong or beside the point.

A will in our archive leaves 'the orchard to my eldest son'. The testator plainly meant the younger, whom he had raised as his eldest after a first wife's death, and everyone knew it. I think the orchard goes to the actual eldest, and that this is the correct outcome, not merely the legally enforceable one.

A Lithuanian neighbour's letter says 'I have no interest in the house'. He meant he had no ownership stake; a stranger reading it cold would take it as indifference. In the book I gloss it as ownership, but I do not think the stranger is simply mistaken about what the sentence says.

An anonymous diary from an attic, no author to be found: I read it at face value. But if a second diary turned up showing the first was written in a private code, I would say its sentences never meant what they appear to mean, and that I had been reading a cipher as if it were English.

I once published a short piece I had generated as a joke with a text generator, under my own name. I think it means whatever a careful reader takes from it; my private associations while assembling it count for nothing, even though I am, in some sense, its author. Likewise a town history written by a committee: there is no one whose intention could settle anything, and the text still means something.

My provisional rule was: a text means what its author intended, except where the intention is unavailable, in which case it means what a competent reader would take it to mean. That rule already fails on the letter, the song, and the will, so I am not attached to it.

What I cannot settle: my grandmother's later claim that the poem is about her sister, when every detail in it fits her brother and she was very old when she said it. I do not know whether to defer to her, as the author, or to the text, and I would like to be told which, on the principle you give me.

So: state the principle or principles precisely, tell me which case motivates each clause, say what it predicts for two cases of your own devising that I have not listed — one where you expect it to be obvious and one where it is strained — and tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up if no single principle saves them all. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning; no preamble, and do not restate my question.
